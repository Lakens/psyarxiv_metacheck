Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Abstract

Affective inertia refers to the degree to which affect carries over from one moment to the next and is typically modeled as a fixed, person-specific parameter. However, emerging evidence suggests that it may vary across time and context. Social context is a likely source of such variation, as people often rely on others to regulate their emotions, but whether inertia varies with everyday social context has rarely been tested. We examined whether momentary social context moderates affective valence inertia in two experience-sampling studies. Study 1 included 315 adolescents and young adults from a Brazilian high-risk cohort (2020-2021), and Study 2 included 803 adults from Austria, Germany, and Italy (2020). Social context was measured as being with others at each prompt in Study 1 and as social interaction since the previous prompt in Study 2. In both studies, Bayesian multilevel autoregressive models showed that social context was associated with more positive momentary valence and weaker overall valence inertia.

Piecewise models further estimate positive and negative valence inertia separately, showing that social context was associated with weaker negative valence inertia in both studies, whereas its moderation of positive valence inertia was not credible in either study. Although the difference between these two moderation effects was only credible in Study 2, both studies linked social context to a weaker negative affect inertia. Overall, our results suggest a context-sensitive view of affective inertia and suggest that treating inertia as a fixed parameter may overlook meaningful within-person variation.

Affective Inertia Varies With Momentary Social Context: Evidence From Two Experience-

Sampling Studies

Emotion dysregulation is a transdiagnostic process in psychopathology (Aldao et al., 2010; Cludius et al., 2020). Such dysregulation manifests in the temporal dynamics of affect in daily life, that is, in how affective states unfold over time (Houben et al., 2015; Kuppens & Verduyn, 2015). A key component of these dynamics is how quickly affect returns to baseline after a perturbation, which can be quantified by affective inertia, the extent to which affect at one moment predicts affect at the next (De Haan-Rietdijk et al., 2016; Kuppens et al., 2010 Kuppens et al., , 2012)).

Higher inertia indicates greater affective persistence and slower recovery after perturbations.

Previous research has linked higher affective inertia, especially in negative affect, to psychological maladjustment, lower well-being, and depressive symptoms (Houben et al., 2015; Koval et al., 2016; Kuppens et al., 2010 Kuppens et al., , 2012)).

Traditional multilevel autoregressive models usually estimate one inertia parameter for each person and assume that it remains constant across the assessment period (De Haan-Rietdijk et al., 2016). However, this assumption may overlook meaningful within-person variation in inertia.

Evidence suggests that inertia can vary depending on prior affective states (De Haan-Rietdijk et al., 2016) and change over time (Bringmann et al., 2017). These findings suggest that inertia may not be a fixed characteristic of an individual, but may vary systematically with momentary states and conditions. Accordingly, recent literature review has emphasized that affective inertia may be context-sensitive (Shao & Ong, 2026).

Social context may be an important source of within person variation in inertia. Consistent with this possibility, an experimental experience-sampling study found lower threat-related affective inertia during anticipation of a social-evaluative stressor (Koval & Kuppens, 2012). Interpersonal emotion regulation perspective suggests that people often seek help from others to regulate their emotions (Zaki & Williams, 2013). Daily life studies also show that being alone versus with others is associated not only with the momentary affective valence, but also with affective variability among adolescents (van Roekel et al., 2015; Walsh et al., 2024). However, it remains unclear whether the inertia differs across everyday social contexts.

Furthermore, the functional meaning of affective inertia may depend on which affect persists.

Meta-analysis found that inertia is more strongly associated with lower psychological well-being for negative than positive affects (Houben et al., 2015). In contrast, another study found that higher positive affect inertia prospectively predicted fewer depressive symptoms and better treatment response (Höhn et al., 2013). The relationship between social processes and the persistence of negative and positive affects may also differ. In two experience-sampling studies, social sharing was associated with shorter duration of anger, fear, and sadness (Brans et al., 2013). In contrast, sharing a positive experience was associated with longer positive affect duration (Hovasapian & Levine, 2018). Although these studies examined active social sharing and emotion duration rather than social context and inertia, they provide a basis for estimating negative and positive valence inertia separately.

The current work examined whether social context moderates affective inertia in two independent Ecological Momentary Assessment (EMA) samples. Study 1 operationalized social context as current social accompaniment in a Brazilian sample of adolescents and young adults.

Study 2 operationalized social context as social interaction since the previous completed EMA assessment in a European adult sample. In each study, we first examined whether social context was associated with current affective valence and overall valence inertia. We then used a piecewise autoregressive model that allowed us to estimate positive and negative valence inertia separately and compared their moderation by social context. Given the limited prior evidence directly examining whether everyday social context moderates affective inertia, we did not specify directional hypotheses regarding these moderation effects.

Study 1: Brazilian High-Risk Cohort Study Method

Participants and Procedure

Participants were selected from the Brazilian High-Risk Cohort for Mental Health Conditions (BHRC) (Salum et al., 2015 (Salum et al., , 2025;; Silva Junior et al., 2026), a school-based longitudinal study conducted in São Paulo and Porto Alegre, Brazil. The cohort oversampled children at higher risk for mental disorders. Detailed recruitment and sampling procedures have been described elsewhere (Salum et al., 2015 (Salum et al., , 2025)). The analytic EMA sample included 200 participants (63.5%) from the risk-enriched stratum and 115 (36.5%) from the randomly selected community stratum. Sampling weights were not applied because our aim was to examine within-person associations among the study participants rather than estimate associations in the general population. Sex assigned at birth and race/skin color were reported by a biological parent at cohort baseline. Race/skin color categories were White, Black, Brown (pardo), Indigenous, and Asian (Silva Junior et al., 2026). The present study was conducted during the COVID-19 pandemic, with data collected from April 2020 through November 2021 using a smartphone application developed in-house. For up to 28 days, participants were scheduled to receive up to five prompts per day: one fixed morning prompt at 9:00 a.m. and four pseudorandom daytime prompts distributed from 11:00 a.m. to 9:00 p.m. Momentary affective valence and social context were assessed at all five daily prompts, while prior-night sleep was assessed exclusively during the fixed morning prompt. The current analyses focused on the momentary affect and social context reports. All parents and young people above 18 years of age signed informed consent, and children provided verbal assent. The study was approved by the Brazilian National Ethics in Research Commission (CAAE 13852413.0.1001.5327 and 74563817.7.1001.5327).

Measures

Social accompaniment. At each EMA assessment, participants reported whether they were alone or with other people. Responses were coded 0 for alone and 1 for with others.

Momentary affective valence.

At each EMA assessment, participants rated their current feelings on three valence items: sad versus happy, anxious versus relaxed, and irritable versus calm (Merikangas et al., 2019; Silva Junior et al., 2026). Each item was rated on a 7-point scale and recoded from -3 to +3, with 0 as the neutral midpoint. Higher scores indicated more positive valence, whereas lower scores indicated more negative valence. The three items were averaged within each assessment to create a momentary affective composite. Within-person and betweenperson omega were .74 and .94, respectively (see Supplemental Material).

Data Analysis

Lagged variables were constructed between adjacent scheduled prompts within the same assessment day. Overnight transitions were excluded, and elapsed intervals were required to be greater than 0 and no longer than 12 hours. Both the omnibus and piecewise models used 4,790 within-day transitions from 315 participants.

Results

Analytic Sample

The sample included 315 participants. At their first EMA assessment, participants were 19.74 years old on average (SD = 1.91; range = 15.45-24.31); 59.7% were female, and 63.5% were drawn from the high-risk stratum (Table 1). These participants provided 9,158 EMA assessments with complete valence data, with a median of 19 assessments per participant (range = 2-134). Of the 4,790 within-day transitions, 1,163 (24.3%) began with negative valence.

Omnibus Valence Inertia

The omnibus model showed credible valence inertia (φ = .362, 95% CrI [.312, .411]; see Table 2). Current social accompaniment was associated with more positive momentary valence (b = 0.219, 95% CrI [0.145, 0.294]). Social accompaniment at the previous prompt was not credibly associated with current valence (λ = -0.006, 95% CrI [-0.064, 0.052]). The interaction between current social accompaniment and lagged valence was negative (m = -0.109, 95% CrI [-0.218, -0.006]), suggesting overall valence inertia was lower when participants were with others.

Piecewise Valence Inertia

The piecewise model showed credible inertia in both positive and negative valence (φ pos = .557, 95% CrI [.490, .626]; φ neg = .476, 95% CrI [.340, .605]; see Table 2). Current accompaniment was associated with more positive valence (b = 0.191, 95% CrI [0.046, 0.337]). Accompaniment did not credibly moderate positive valence inertia (m pos = -0.016, 95% CrI [-0.142, 0.109]), whereas it credibly weakened negative valence inertia (m neg = -0.260, 95% CrI [-0.497, -0.025]).

This suggests that negative valence at the previous assessment was less strongly associated with current valence when participants were with others at the current assessment. However, the direct contrast between the two moderation parameters included zero (Δ m = -0.244, 95% CrI [-0.542, 0.050]).

Sensitivity Analyses

Elapsed-time sensitivity analyses showed the same pattern of results across the omnibus and piecewise models (see Supplemental Material).

Discussion

Study 1 found that participants reported more positive current valence and showed weaker overall valence inertia when they were with others than when they were alone. In the piecewise model, social accompaniment was credibly associated with weaker negative valence inertia, whereas its moderation of positive valence inertia remained uncertain. However, the credible interval for the direct contrast between the two moderation parameters included zero, so Study 1 did not establish that social accompaniment moderated negative valence inertia more strongly than positive valence inertia.

These findings motivated Study 2 in three aspects. First, Study 1 assessed social accompaniment as a momentary state at the time of each prompt, whereas valence inertia involves the carryover of valence between the previous and current prompts. Study 2 therefore examined whether the moderation effect would be replicated when social context was measured over this transition interval by assessing whether social interaction had occurred since the previous prompt. Second, the direct contrast in Study 1 was also estimated from a data structure in which only 69 participants contributed negative valence transitions in both accompaniment conditions. Study 2 used a larger sample and provided more usable within-day transitions, providing more information for the direct comparison between negative and positive valence moderation.

Finally, Study 2 used an independent European adult sample and different measures of valence and social context to examine whether the Study 1 findings would be replicated beyond a single Brazilian cohort and its specific measures, thereby providing a conceptual replication.

Study 2: European Adult Sample Method

Participants and Procedure

Study 2 used a publicly available EMA dataset from a multinational project conducted during the COVID-19 pandemic (Forbes et al., 2023; Pronizius et al., 2024). The project included two EMA bursts conducted during two different lockdown periods. This study analyzed the first burst, which included participants from Austria, Germany, and Italy. The second burst only included a smaller Austrian subsample from the first burst and was not included because it did not provide an independent replication sample.

Participants completed 5 EMA surveys per day for 7 consecutive days, which were conducted through the MovisensXS smartphone application. Four of the prompts were scheduled within a semi random time window between 10:00 am and 8:00 pm, and the fifth assessment was initiated by participants before bedtime. All participants provided electronic informed consent. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the University of Vienna.

Measures

Momentary affective valence. Momentary affective valence was assessed using two bipolar visual analogue items, unwell versus well, and dissatisfied versus satisfied, each rated from 0 to 100 (Pronizius et al., 2024; Wilhelm & Schoebi, 2007). The two items were averaged at each assessment, with higher scores indicating more positive valence. We rescaled the mean score from 0 to 100 to -1 to +1 using (score -50) / 50. Thus, -1 represented the negative endpoint, 0 on the rescaled score represented the neutral midpoint, and +1 represented the positive endpoint.

Social interaction.

At each assessment, participants reported whether they had engaged in an uninterrupted social interaction lasting more than 2 minutes since the last prompt. Interactions could occur in person, by telephone or video call, or through continuous text-based communication. Responses were coded 0 for no interaction and 1 for one or more interactions.

Data Analysis

Study 2 used the same Bayesian two-level random-slope autoregressive models as Study 1.

Lagged variables were constructed between adjacent scheduled prompts within the same assessment day. Overnight transitions were excluded, and elapsed intervals were required to be greater than 0 and no longer than 12 hours. Both the omnibus and piecewise models used 14,152 within-day transitions from 803 participants.

Results

Analytic Sample

The sample included 803 participants. Of these participants, 522 lived in Austria, 251 in Italy, and 30 in Germany. Participants were 31.87 years old on average (SD = 11.86, range = 18-80).

The sample included 559 women and 243 men, with one participant having missing gender information (Table 1). These participants provided 21,869 EMA assessments with complete valence data, with a median of 28 assessments per participant (range = 18-35). Of the 14,152 within-day transitions, 3,610 (25.5%) began with negative valence.

Omnibus Valence Inertia

The omnibus model showed credible valence inertia (φ = .257, 95% CrI [.235, .279]; See Table 3). Social interaction was associated with more positive current valence (b = .050, 95% CrI [.036, .063]). Lagged social interaction was also credibly associated with more positive current valence (λ = .018, 95% CrI [.006, .031]). The interaction between social interaction and lagged valence was credibly negative (m = -.106, 95% CrI [-.157, -.056]), indicating that overall valence inertia was lower when social interaction had occurred since the previous prompt. Thus, the omnibus social-context moderation observed in Study 1 was replicated in Study 2.

Piecewise Valence Inertia

The piecewise model showed credible positive-and negative-valence inertia ( φ pos = .422, 95% CrI [.388, .457]; φ neg = .200, 95% CrI [.135, .262]; See Table 3). Social interaction was associated with more positive current valence (b = 0.063, 95% CrI [0.036, 0.089]). Social interaction did not credibly moderate positive-valence inertia (mpos = -0.056, 95% CrI [-0.118, 0.007]), whereas it credibly weakened negative-valence inertia (mneg = -0.277, 95% CrI [-0.420, -0.138]). The direct contrast between the moderation parameters excluded zero (Δm = -0.221, 95% CrI [-0.396, -0.050]). Study 2 therefore provided evidence that social interaction moderated negative valence inertia more strongly than positive valence inertia.

Sensitivity Analyses

Elapsed-time sensitivity analyses also showed the same pattern of results across the omnibus and piecewise models (see Supplemental Material).

Discussion

Study 2 replicated the omnibus pattern observed in Study 1. When participants had social interactions after the previous assessment, they reported a more positive current valence and weaker overall valence inertia. In the piecewise model, there is a credible association between social interaction and weaker negative valence inertia, but there is no credible moderating effect on positive valence inertia. Unlike Study 1, however, the direct contrast between the two moderation parameters excluded zero, indicating that social interaction moderated negative valence inertia more strongly than positive valence inertia.

General Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first study to examine whether affective inertia varies across everyday social contexts. Three findings emerged across two independent EMA samples. First, social context was associated with more positive current affective valence and weaker overall affective valence inertia in both studies. Second, when positive and negative valence were examined separately, social context was associated with weaker negative valence inertia in both studies, whereas it did not credibly moderate positive valence inertia in either study. Third, stronger moderation of negative than positive valence inertia was supported only in Study 2, where the direct contrast between the two moderation parameters excluded zero. Taken together, everyday social context was associated not only with more positive momentary valence but also with less carryover of negative valence.

Our findings support a context-sensitive view of affective inertia.

By including a time-varying moderator of inertia, our study substantively contributes to the literature, which has traditionally relied on a single, context-independent inertia estimate (De Haan-Rietdijk et al., 2016). This result also provides empirical support for Shao and Ong's (2026) conceptual view that affective inertia can vary within persons across contexts rather than remain constant throughout the assessment period. This conclusion is strengthened by the consistency of the findings across two samples that differed in developmental stage, with adolescents and young adults in Study 1 and adults in Study 2, and in measurement. Study 1 assessed whether participants were with others at the current prompt, whereas Study 2 measured whether social interaction had occurred since the last prompt, and the two studies used different valence items. The overall moderation effect nonetheless emerged in both samples, suggesting that it is robust across developmental stages and measurement approaches.

Critically, the association between social context and affective inertia may differ by valence. The piecewise analyses showed that social context was associated with weaker negative valence inertia in both studies. The positive valence moderation estimates were in the same direction, suggesting weaker positive valence inertia in social contexts, but neither estimate was credible.

Although these parameters index regions of a single bipolar scale rather than independent affect dimensions, this pattern is theoretically relevant because the persistence of affective states may serve different psychological functions depending on valence. Meta-analytic evidence suggests that while high inertia in both positive and negative affect is linked to lower psychological wellbeing, this association is stronger for negative affect (Houben et al., 2015). Laboratory studies found similar results, showing that the association between negative affect inertia and psychological maladjustment is more robust and pronounced than positive affect inertia (Koval et al., 2016). Thus, the association between social context and weaker negative valence inertia warrants particular attention, as it concerns the type of affective persistence most consistently linked to psychological maladjustment. However, only Study 2 supported stronger moderation of negative than positive valence inertia. In Study 1, 24.3% of transitions began with negative valence. And only 69 of the 315 participants (21.9%) contributed to such transitions both when alone and with others, limiting the precision of the estimated moderation of negative valence inertia. The two samples also differed in developmental stage, and social contexts may carry different regulatory meanings for adolescents and adults (Crone & Dahl, 2012), so the betweenstudy difference may reflect developmental differences as well as data coverage. Overall, evidence for valence asymmetry remains preliminary.

An important question raised by these findings is why social context was associated with weaker negative valence inertia but did not clearly moderate positive valence inertia. This pattern is consistent with work on interpersonal emotion regulation, which suggest that people often turn to others for help regulating their emotions by seeking support and sharing emotional experiences (Marroquín, 2011; Rimé, 2009; Zaki & Williams, 2013). These processes may play different roles at different valences. For negative affect, social contact may facilitate emotional regulation by providing support or helping individuals reappraise their current situation (Marroquín, 2011; Reeck et al., 2016). Social Baseline Theory further suggests that staying close to familiar and trusted others may reduce perceived risk and decrease the effort required to respond to situational demands (Coan & Sbarra, 2015). Consistent with these views, previous EMA study found that sharing anger, fear, and sadness is associated with shorter durations of these emotions (Brans et al., 2013). These perspectives are grounded in attachment theory, which holds that proximity to trusted others is a primary means of regulating distress across the lifespan (Sbarra & Hazan, 2008). This framing also underlines the clinical relevance of the findings, as impaired use of others for emotion regulation is a transdiagnostic feature of psychopathology (Mikulincer & Shaver, 2012). At the same time, the finding that social context did not credibly moderate positive valence inertia does not necessarily mean that social processes are unrelated to the maintenance of positive affect. The role of positive-event sharing may depend on whether the sharing actually occurs and how the listener responds, rather than solely on the presence of others or general social interaction (Gable et al., 2004; Gable & Reis, 2010; Hovasapian & Levine, 2018). Taken together, existing literature suggests that social processes may shape the persistence of negative and positive affect differently, which may help explain the different findings for negative and positive valence inertia.

Constraints on Generality

Five limitations should be considered. First, the social context and valence measures used in both studies were relatively broad. The binary social context measurement cannot distinguish between interaction partners, relationship types, interaction quality, support, conflict, or responsiveness, and therefore cannot determine which interpersonal processes are associated with weaker valence inertia. Nor can it rule out that changes in activity or circumstances that accompany being with others, rather than interpersonal processes, account for the weaker association. Future EMA research should further measure interaction partners, shared activities, and perceived quality of interactions to determine how different types of social contact are associated with affective inertia and whether these associations vary by valence. Second, the current analyses focused on whether social context moderated affective inertia and did not examine whether prior affect predicted subsequent social contact. Prior affect may shape whether individuals seek or avoid social contact, which could partly contribute to the observed associations. Social context may also influence how participants answer EMA questions or whether they respond at all, potentially affecting the estimates. Future research could examine both the reciprocal associations between affect and social context and possible differences in EMA responding across contexts. Third, this study did not examine the outcomes associated with weaker negative valence inertia in social contexts. Therefore, reduced negative valence inertia does not by itself indicate that participants had better subsequent functioning. To examine the functional significance of this pattern, future research should investigate whether weaker negative valence inertia is consistent with situational demands and regulatory goals, and whether it is followed by lower subsequent distress, fewer psychopathological symptoms, greater well-being or better daily functioning. Fourth, the actual time intervals between adjacent EMA prompts are not equidistant.

To examine whether unequal time intervals affect our conclusions, we conducted elapsed-time sensitivity analyses, and the results were consistent with the main analysis. Although continuoustime models can handle irregular time intervals, current standard implementations cannot directly estimate within-person time-varying moderation of drift parameters. This analysis would require a custom model and simulation studies to confirm whether the parameters can be reliably recovered. Future methodological work should develop and validate tools for fitting such continuous-time moderation models. Fifth, although Study 2 extended the findings to an independent adult sample, Study 1's high proportion of participants from the high-risk stratum (63.5%) may limit its generalizability. In addition, both studies were conducted during the COVID-19 pandemic. Public health restrictions have changed not only how often people spend time alone or with others, but also the meaning of this experience.

For example, being alone may reflect more of a forced isolation than a personal choice, while social interaction may be restricted to a more limited relationship. Therefore, future research should replicate these studies in more typical settings to confirm the generalizability of our findings.

Conclusion

Across two independent EMA samples, momentary social context was concurrently associated with more positive affective valence and weaker overall affective inertia. Both studies also found weaker negative valence inertia in social contexts, although stronger moderation of negative than positive valence inertia was supported only in Study 2. Together, these findings indicate that within-person variation in affective inertia is associated with momentary social context. Future research should identify the interpersonal processes underlying this variation and determine its implications for psychological adjustment.
