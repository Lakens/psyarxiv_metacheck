What we found

We found 6 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Objective: Although neighborhood disadvantage has been linked to intimate partner violence (IPV) in younger and middle-aged adults, its role in late-life IPV is understudied. This study examined whether perceived neighborhood disorder (PND) and zip code-level social vulnerability (i.e., subjective and objective attributes of neighborhood disadvantage) are associated with psychological and physical IPV perpetration and victimization in adults 60 and older. Method: Participants in committed relationships (N = 380) completed an online survey assessing past-year aggression, PND, loneliness, and the zip code of their primary residence. Zip codes were linked to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention's Social Vulnerability Index (SVI). Hierarchical logistic regression models estimated associations of PND and overall SVI with each IPV outcome. Exploratory analyses replaced the overall SVI with its four themes and the total PND with its four subscales. Results: Greater PND was associated with increased odds of physical, but not psychological, IPV perpetration and victimization. Greater social vulnerability was associated with greater odds of psychological aggression perpetration and victimization, as well as physical aggression victimization. These associations remained significant after false discovery rate correction. Conclusions: Both subjective and objective aspects of neighborhood disadvantage are linked to IPV in older adults' committed relationships, even after controlling for sociodemographic characteristics and loneliness as an individual risk factor. These results suggest the need for geographically targeted allocation of IPV resources to socially vulnerable neighborhoods, as well as for late-life IPV screening research that considers the neighborhood environment.

Perceived Neighborhood Disorder, Social Vulnerability, and Intimate Partner Violence Among Adults over 60

Annual prevalence rates of physical intimate partner violence (IPV) among older adults are estimated to be 2.6%, with the pooled annual prevalence rate of any form of IPV being 10.6% (Chen & Li, 2026). This is concerning considering that by 2030, all baby boomers will be at least 65 years old and comprise 1 in 5 U.S. residents (U.S. Census Bureau, 2018), and that IPV in late life is associated with poorer physical, psychological, and social consequences (Chen & Li, 2026; Pathak et al., 2019). In younger and middle-aged persons, research indicates that, in addition to individual risk factors (e.g., earlier experiences of violence), objective and subjective aspects of the neighborhoods people live in are linked to the experience of IPV (Beyer et al., 2015; Voith, 2019). For instance, even after controlling for other variables (e.g., prior violence, economic distress, demographic characteristics), the likelihood of adult women experiencing IPV is over one-third higher in neighborhoods with concentrated disadvantage (e.g., poverty; Benson et al., 2003). Yet, despite the growing population of aging adults and evidence that neighborhood factors are important risk factors for IPV in younger populations, limited research has examined their role as risk factors for IPV in later life. Therefore, the current study aims to examine whether subjective (i.e., perceived neighborhood disorder) and objective (i.e., zip code-based social vulnerability) neighborhood factors are linked to psychological and physical IPV perpetration and victimization in adults aged 60 and older.

Intimate Partner Violence in Aging Adults

Two of the most common forms of IPV are psychological aggression (e.g., insulting or swearing at a partner) and physical aggression (e.g., pushing or slapping a partner). Partners in a relationship can both perpetrate aggression and be the victim of the other person's aggression.

Among older adults, the pooled lifetime prevalence rate of IPV is 15.1%, with psychological aggression being considerably more common than physical aggression (Chen & Li, 2026; Warmling et al., 2017). Moreover, aggression in couples is often bidirectional (Lysova et al., 2025; Machado et al., 2024; Warmling et al., 2021), which makes it important to examine both perpetration and victimization. Similar to their younger counterparts, individual-level risk factors for IPV among aging adults include sociodemographic characteristics, such as belonging to an ethnic minority, being aged 55-69, being female, and having low income, unemployment, and lower education (Gerino et al., 2018; Warmling et al., 2017). Because many of these individual characteristics (e.g., income, race/ethnicity) are also linked to where people live, it is important to determine whether neighborhood characteristics are associated with IPV above and beyond individual risk factors.

In older women, IPV is associated with frailty, gastrointestinal, and respiratory health issues (Pathak et al., 2019). More broadly, IPV in later life has been linked to health problems, elder abuse victimization, and medication use (Chen & Li, 2026). In a sample of older Indigenous persons, those who reported physical aggression victimization were more likely to be depressed (Roh et al., 2016), and a review of risk and protective factors for IPV in older adults found that social support is a main protective factor (Gerino et al., 2018). Additionally, persons experiencing IPV at a later age face a lack of available resources and evidence-based interventions tailored to their age range. A narrative review by Pathak and colleagues (2019) found no randomized controlled trials of interventions for older women experiencing IPV, and few observational studies describing the service use of older women experiencing IPV. Older women were less likely to use housing, financial, employment, case management, substance abuse, and counseling services than younger women (Pathak et al., 2019). Moreover, unsupportive responses from professionals, family members, friends, and community organizations, financial dependence on their partner, or caregiving obligations can act as barriers to help-seeking (Pathak et al., 2019).

Therefore, as many of these resources and relationships are local, the communities older adults live in may affect both their risk for IPV and their ability to respond to it.

Neighborhood Attributes and Aging Adults' Well-Being

A person's neighborhood may be particularly important in later life as many older adults age in place, and after retirement, or with declining mobility, spend more of their lives in and around their homes (Glass & Balfour, 2003; Horgas et al., 1998). The existing literature identifies two primary neighborhood-related domains that are relevant to greater well-being and flourishing among aging adults: the physical condition of the neighborhood and the social environment within it (Padeiro et al., 2022; Schepers et al., 2025). Physical features such as natural areas with trees, plants, and water; cleanliness; good paths; the absence of barriers; benches; and safe intersections are positively associated with older adults' well-being, and natural areas also provide an enjoyable space for social interaction (Padeiro et al., 2022). Along with the physical order of the neighborhood, a sense of community and a supportive social environment are critical to older adults' well-being. A sense of neighborhood community benefits older adults as it provides mutual assistance and social ties (Padeiro et al., 2022). The quality of neighborhood services, social capital, and social cohesion are linked to older adult well-being through mutual neighborly support (e.g., neighbors doing each other favors and helping each other with tasks), which may reduce worries about future difficulties (Cramm et al., 2013). Additionally, neighborhood security and the service environment have been related to greater well-being for older adults (Padeiro et al., 2022). These physical and social domains closely correspond to the two dimensions of perceived neighborhood disorder: physical disorder (e.g., vandalism), and social disorder (e.g., crime; Ross & Mirowsky, 1999). Perceived disorder, in turn, has been linked to poorer mental health (e.g., depression) and fewer positive interactions with one's spouse in middle-aged and older adults (Baranyi et al., 2020; Cho & Townsend, 2025). Given the central role of neighborhood in older adults' daily lives, the same conditions that undermine well-being may also increase the risk for IPV in this population.

Neighborhood Effects on Intimate Partner Violence

Several theories explain why neighborhood conditions may be linked to IPV. Social disorganization theory posits that structural disadvantage (e.g., concentrated poverty) weakens social ties and thus neighbors' willingness to intervene in or sanction violence (Browning, 2002).

Consistent with that, neighborhood-level factors have been linked to dating violence in adolescents (Rothman et al., 2011). Community norms are one mechanism underlying this association. According to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC, 2025), community norms (e.g., implicit acceptance of aggression) are an important mechanism linking neighborhood disadvantage and IPV. Consistent with this explanation, exposure to violence in a person's community has been linked with viewing aggression as acceptable (McMahon et al., 2009; Pittman, 2023). Stress-based perspectives suggest that stressors associated with neighborhood disadvantage (e.g., employment instability, financial strain) amplify relationship conflict and subsequently the likelihood of aggression (Benson et al., 2003; Masi & Santantonio, 2026). For example, in a sample of young adults, Copp et al. (2015) found that anger and depression partially mediated the association between neighborhood disadvantage and IPV perpetration. Lastly, disadvantaged neighborhoods may offer fewer resources and services to persons experiencing IPV. Coupled with support systems that face similar constraints, fewer resources create greater barriers to leaving or coping with a relationship, in which IPV occurs (Williams et al., 2018; Wright, 2015).

Research with younger and middle-aged adults identifies neighborhood composition and demographic factors as risk factors for IPV (Beyer et al., 2015). Specifically, living in disadvantaged neighborhoods characterized by higher unemployment, economic hardship, and residential stability due to inability to move is associated with increased IPV risk (Beyer et al., 2015; Gracia et al., 2021; Yakubovich et al., 2020). The risk is especially high in areas with more households led by single mothers with underage children (Benson et al., 2003; Beyer et al., 2015).

Additionally, lower social cohesion, higher crime, community violence, increased rurality, and greater density of alcohol outlets (bars, restaurants, and off-premise sites) correlate with higher IPV rates (Beyer et al., 2015; Cunradi et al., 2011; Gracia et al., 2026; Voith et al., 2021).

Neighborhood disadvantage can be measured objectively or subjectively. Objective indices such as the CDC's Social Vulnerability Index (SVI) use census indicators of socioeconomic status, household characteristics, racial and ethnic minority status, and housing type and transportation to assess to what extent a community is at risk of external harm (e.g., from natural disasters or disease outbreaks; CDC, 2024). For instance, results of a systematic review, showed that neighborhood social vulnerability was associated with worse health outcomes (Higginbotham et al., 2025). Perceived neighborhood disorder captures residents' subjective experiences of the extent to which social order (e.g., crime) and physical order (e.g., abandoned buildings) of their neighborhood have broken down (Ross & Mirowsky, 1999). Although related, objective and subjective indices are not interchangeable, as residents of the same community may perceive their neighborhood differently. Moreover, subjective indices of neighborhood quality also appear to be more consistently associated with intrapersonal outcomes such as loneliness (Schepers et al., 2025), and Choi et al. (2024) found that perceived neighborhood disorder mediates the link between neighborhood poverty and hopelessness. However, both subjective and objective indices of neighborhood quality have been linked to experiencing IPV (Beyer et al., 2015; Copp et al., 2015; Thulin et al., 2021). To our knowledge, few studies have examined the link between IPV and subjective and objective neighborhood characteristics simultaneously in older adults despite researchers calling attention to it (Pillemer et al., 2016). This limits researchers' ability to identify which older adults are most at risk and where prevention and intervention resources are most needed.

Present Study

The goal of the present investigation was to examine whether perceived neighborhood disorder and zip code-based social vulnerability were associated with psychological and physical aggression perpetration and victimization in adults 60 years and older. Specifically, we hypothesized that subjective perception of neighborhood disorder would be associated with greater odds of psychological and physical IPV perpetration and victimization (H1) and that social vulnerability would be associated with greater odds of psychological and physical IPV perpetration and victimization (H2). Lastly, to examine which dimensions of social vulnerability and perceived neighborhood disorder accounted for their associations with IPV, we conducted exploratory analyses with the four SVI themes and the four perceived neighborhood disorder subscales.

Methods

Participants

To be eligible for the study, participants had to reside in the United States, be 60 years of age or older, and be in a committed relationship (i.e., married, engaged, or cohabitating for at least six months). The final sample consisted of 385 participants (Nwomen = 221) who were, on average, 65.75 years old (SD = 4.85 years). Of those 385 participants, 89.1% identified as White, 5.7% as Black, 2.6% as Asian or Asian American, 0.3% as American Indian/Alaskan Native, and 2.4% as Mixed Race or other. Moreover, 3.1% reported their ethnicity as Hispanic. The majority (86.8%) of participants were married, 0.3% were engaged, and 13% were cohabitating for at least six months. Furthermore, most participants had received some form of higher education: 15.1% reported some college, 14.5% had a two-year degree, 34.3% had a four-year degree, 20.8% had a master's degree, and 5.2% had a doctoral degree. Additionally, 9.7% had a high school diploma or GED, and one participant (0.3%) reported completing grade 11 but did not complete high school.

The mean annual combined family income was $100,640.15 (SD = 101,198.74), with a median of $80,000. Five participants had missing data on perceived neighborhood disorder (n=1) or social vulnerability (n=4), resulting in an analytic sample of 380 participants.

Procedure

Participants were recruited via Amazon Mechanical Turk (MTurk) and Prolific to complete an online survey assessing relationship functioning, mental health, and substance use between August 2023 and July 2024. Both MTurk and Prolific are online marketplaces where participants complete various tasks posted by researchers, for which they are compensated. For the current study, participants first completed a brief screener survey to verify age, relationship status, and U.S. residency, for which MTurk and Prolific participants received $.05 and $.14, respectively. Participants who met eligibility criteria were then given the opportunity to complete the main survey. Both groups received the same explanation that the purpose of the study was to understand the link between relationships and substance use behavior. Of the 370 MTurk workers who completed the screener survey, 129 met eligibility criteria and 106 completed the main survey. Of the 403 Prolific users who completed the screener survey, 345 met eligibility criteria and 341 completed the main survey. After removing participants who failed attention checks, provided information that was inconsistent with their screener responses, or submitted duplicate responses, as well as those whose responses appeared to have been made by bots or, on Prolific, whose data appeared to be non-independent, the final sample consisted of 96 MTurk workers and 289 Prolific participants. Participants received $10 for completing the main survey. All procedures were approved by the University of [MASKED FOR BLIND REVIEW] Institutional Review Board.

Measures

Aggression

Physical and psychological IPV perpetration and victimization during the prior 12 months were assessed using the Revised Conflict Tactics Scale (CTS2; Straus et al., 1996). Participants completed the 8-item psychological and 12-item physical subscales to indicate how often in the last twelve months they engaged in or experienced specific acts of physical or psychological aggression, using an 8-point scale (0 = "This has never happened" to6 = "More than 20 times" or7 = "Not in the past year, but it did happen before"), with higher scores reflecting a greater frequency of IPV behaviors (e.g., "I kicked my partner"). Results showed that in the analytic sample, 7.4% (n=28) and 6.6% (n=25) of participants reported physical aggression in the last year by a partner and by themselves, respectively. Additionally, 68.4% (n=260) of participants reported psychological aggression by their partner in the last year. Moreover, 70.0% (n=266) of participants reported psychological aggression by themselves in the last year. Cronbach's alpha ranged from .661 to .835 for the different scales.

Perceived Neighborhood Disorder

Subjective neighborhood disorder was measured using the 15-item Perceived Neighborhood Disorder scale (PND; Ross & Mirowsky, 1999). On a scale from 1 ("Strongly disagree") to 4 ("Strongly agree"), participants rated the extent to which their neighborhood is characterized by order and disorder, respectively (e.g., "There is a lot of crime in my neighborhood"). In addition to a global neighborhood disorder score, four subtotals can be computed (perceived physical disorder, perceived physical order, perceived social disorder, perceived social order). Higher scores indicate greater perceived disorder. In the current investigation, we used the total score in the main analyses and four subtotals in the supplemental analyses. Reliability in the current study was .910.

Social Vulnerability

Objective neighborhood-level vulnerability was assessed by linking participants' self-reported zip codes of their primary residence to the CDC's Social Vulnerability Index (SVI). The SVI is a place-based index that uses demographic and socioeconomic indicators to identify communities that may be more vulnerable to hazards and other community-level stressors (CDC, 2024).

Specifically, we used the US-wide 2022 SVI for ZIP Code Tabulation Areas (ZCTAs), which is based on the 2018-2022 American Community Survey, and ranks each area relative to all other areas in the United States. All 385 participants in the present study reported 375 unique zip codes.

Ten zip codes were reported by two participants, and no zip code was reported by more than two participants. The SVI combines 16 census variables into four themes: socioeconomic status (e.g., percentage of persons below 150% of the federal poverty level, unemployment rate, percentage of adults [25+] without a high school diploma), household characteristics (e.g., percentage of residents aged 65 and older, percentage of single-parent households), racial and ethnic minority status, and housing type and transportation (e.g., crowding, households without a vehicle). We used the overall SVI percentile ranking (0-1) as the objective indicator of neighborhood quality in the main analyses and four theme percentile rankings in supplementary analyses. Higher scores indicate greater social vulnerability.

Loneliness

Loneliness was assessed using the 20-item UCLA Loneliness Scale (Russell, 1996). On a scale from 1 ("Never") to 4 ("Always"), participants responded to questions such as "How often do you feel alone?" or "How often do you feel left out?" Higher scores indicate greater loneliness. Cronbach's alpha in the current study was .95.

Analytic Approach

Given the low base rates and positive skew typically observed for CTS2 aggression variables, all four IPV variables were operationalized as dichotomous prevalence indicators rather than frequency scores. Specifically, consistent with recommendations by Straus et al. (1996), participants were classified according to whether any aggression occurred (0 = no aggression,1 = any aggression) in the past year. This allowed the full sample to be retained in analyses and avoided the loss of power that would result from restricting analyses to participants who reported aggression. Additionally, we included age, sex, education, income, and race/ethnicity as covariates given that sociodemographic characteristics have been shown to be associated with IPV risk and a person's exposure to neighborhood conditions (e.g., Gunarathne et al., 2023). Because 89.1% of participants identified as White and only 3.1% identified as Hispanic, race and ethnicity were combined into a single dichotomous variable, such that0 = non-Hispanic White and1 = non-White (e.g., Black, Asian) or Hispanic (n = 48). Given prior research that loneliness is associated with perceptions of one's neighborhood (Schepers et al., 2025) and IPV (Joksimovic et al., 2025), loneliness was included as a covariate.

To evaluate the link between subjective and objective neighborhood quality and IPV, four hierarchical binary logistic regression models were estimated in SPSS 31.0. Psychological and physical aggression perpetration and victimization were regressed on the demographic covariates (step 1), loneliness (step 2), perceived neighborhood disorder (step 3), and the overall SVI ranking (step 4). For ease of interpretation, odds ratios for the SVI are reported for a 10percentile-point increase. To account for multiple testing, we used the adaptive linear step-up procedure by Benjamini et al. (2006) with a false discovery rate (FDR) of .05 across the eight tests of the neighborhood measures (i.e., perceived neighborhood disorder and total SVI predicting each IPV outcome). To identify influential cases, we examined standardized residuals, leverage values, and Cook's distance for each model. In two instances, participants had Cook's distance values of 1.02 and 1.03. Because these values were close to the conventional cutoff, all participants were retained and models were re-estimated without these two participants as a sensitivity analysis. Several participants also had standardized residuals greater than 3 in absolute value, most of whom had reported physical aggression despite low predicted probabilities, which is expected given the low base rates of physical aggression.

Additionally, we conducted four supplementary analyses. First, to examine which dimensions of social vulnerability accounted for its associations with IPV, we replaced the overall SVI with the four SVI theme percentile rankings (socioeconomic status, household characteristics, racial and ethnic minority status, housing type and transportation). Second, to examine to what extent perceived social and physical order and disorder accounted for perceived neighborhood disorder's association with IPV, we replaced the total PND score with the four subscales. Third, because physical aggression perpetration (n=25) and victimization (n=28) were reported by fewer than 30 participants, we re-estimated the physical aggression models using Firth's (1993) penalized logistic regression, which reduces small-sample bias in logistic regression estimates. Fourth, given that loneliness was is associated with both perceptions of one's neighborhood and IPV, we re-estimated all four models without loneliness. The study was not pre-registered. Results

Overall, bivariate correlations were mostly in the expected direction (see Table 1). All four IPV variables were significantly and positively correlated with one another (rs ≥ .15; ps ≤ .004), indicating that experiencing one form of aggression increased the likelihood of experiencing other forms of aggression. Total perceived neighborhood disorder was positively associated with all four IPV variables (rs ≥ .11, ps ≤ .026). The total SVI was positively associated with psychological aggression perpetration and victimization as well as physical aggression victimization (rs ≥ .14, all ps ≤ .007), but not physical aggression perpetration (r =.03, p = .523).

The overall models for psychological and physical aggression perpetration as well as psychological and physical aggression victimization were significant (all χ²s [8] ≥ 20.09, ps ≤ .010, Nagelkerke R²s ≥ .073; see Table 2). Demographic covariates (step 1) did not explain any significant amount of variance in any of the IPV outcomes (χ²s [5] ≤ 9.57, ps ≥ .088). Loneliness (step 2) improved model fit for psychological aggression perpetration and victimization and physical aggression victimization (χ²s [1] ≥ 6.87, ps ≤ .009) but not physical aggression perpetration (χ² [1] = 1.76, p = .185). Perceived neighborhood disorder (step 3) improved model fit for physical (χ²s [1] ≥ 9.22, ps ≤ .002), but not psychological aggression (χ²s [1] ≤ 3.76, ps ≥ .052) perpetration or victimization. Adding the total SVI (step 4) improved model fit for psychological aggression perpetration and victimization (χ²s [1] ≥ 4.94, ps ≤ .026) and physical aggression victimization (χ² [1] = 5.74, p = .017), but not physical aggression perpetration (χ² [1] = .04, p = .846).

In the final models, perceived neighborhood disorder was significantly associated with increased odds of physical aggression perpetration (OR = 1.11, 95% CI [1.05, 1.18], p < .001) and victimization (OR = 1.07, 95% CI [1.01, 1.12], p = .019), but not with psychological aggression perpetration or victimization, which is partially consistent with H1. Living in neighborhoods with greater social vulnerability was associated with increased odds of psychological aggression perpetration (OR = 1.13, 95% CI [1.01, 1.25], p = .026) and victimization (OR = 1.14, 95% CI [1.03, 1.27], p = .012), as well as physical aggression victimization (OR = 1.30, 95% CI [1.03, 1.64], p = .027) for every 10-percentile-point increase in social vulnerability, but not with physical aggression perpetration, which is partially consistent with H2. All five associations remained significant after controlling the false discovery rate. No other neighborhood effects were significant. In exploratory analyses, when the four SVI themes (socioeconomic status, household characteristics, racial and ethnic minority status, housing type and transportation) replaced the overall SVI, the racial and ethnic minority status theme was associated with increased odds of psychological aggression perpetration (OR = 1.14, 95% CI [1.00, 1.29], p = .043), and the household characteristics theme was associated with increased odds of psychological aggression victimization (OR = 1.16, 95% CI [1.03, 1.32], p = .014; see Table $1). When the four PND subscales replaced the total score, perceived physical disorder was associated with increased odds of all four IPV outcomes (ORs ≥ 1.22, ps < .05), and perceived social disorder was associated with increased odds of physical aggression perpetration (OR = 1.30, 95% CI [1.03, 1.65], p = .030; see Table S2). Firth's penalized likelihood logistic regressions yielded virtually unchanged estimates in the physical aggression models (see Table S3). When loneliness was not included as a covariate, perceived neighborhood disorder improved model fit for psychological aggression perpetration and victimization (χ²s [1] ≥ 5.13, ps ≤ .024) but was not associated with either outcome once the overall SVI was added (ps ≥ .067; see Table S4). Lastly, excluding two cases with Cook's distance greater than 1 did not change any significant associations.

Discussion

The goal of this study was to examine the link between subjective and objective attributes of neighborhood disadvantage and the experience of IPV in adults over 60. Results partially supported the hypotheses. Specifically, partially consistent with H2, living in neighborhoods with greater social vulnerability was associated with greater odds of experiencing psychological aggression perpetration and victimization as well as physical aggression victimization, and partially consistent with H1, higher levels of perceived neighborhood disorder were associated with greater odds of experiencing physical aggression perpetration and victimization, but not psychological aggression.

The present results indicate that how people experience their neighborhood may matter for physical IPV above and beyond objective neighborhood characteristics. Subjective measures of neighborhood quality may capture more than census-based structural and organizational characteristics of an area, such as a sense of community, perceived safety, and everyday stress.

For instance, Schepers et al. (2025) found that subjective (vs. objective) neighborhood quality was more strongly associated with loneliness. Moreover, longitudinal research evidence suggests that social cohesion can buffer the effects of neighborhood deprivation on mental health (Fone et al., 2014). In other words, two people living in the same zip code may experience their environment differently depending on both individual factors (e.g., connectedness to the neighborhood due to having grown up there) and proximal experiences of safety, belonging, and social cohesion (e.g., close ties between neighbors on one street but not another). In the present study, the association between perceived neighborhood disorder and physical aggression remained significant even after accounting for demographic factors, loneliness, and objective social vulnerability. This is consistent with research on younger populations that perceived neighborhood disorder was associated with IPV perpetration above and beyond census-based neighborhood disadvantage (Copp et al., 2015).

In the final models, perceived neighborhood disorder was only linked to reports of physical aggression, but not psychological aggression, suggesting that older adults' experience of their immediate neighborhood may be more relevant to behavioral disinhibition than to the presence of antagonistic or conflictual interactions with one's romantic partner per se. Psychological IPV was highly prevalent in the present sample and likely reflects relationshiplevel interactions that arise across a relatively broad range of neighborhoods. In contrast, physical aggression may be more dependent on contexts that do not condemn loss of control over one's actions during conflict -or do so to a lesser extent. Limited social control and fewer social sanctions in disordered neighborhoods might weaken the social constraints that would otherwise discourage physical aggression (Browning, 2002; Rothman et al., 2011). Notably, perceived neighborhood disorder improved model fit for both outcomes when loneliness was not included; however, these associations were no longer significant once overall SVI was added, which suggests that the link between perceived neighborhood disorder and psychological aggression overlaps with loneliness and objective social vulnerability.

The present results are also consistent with research showing that community violence is more common in neighborhoods with higher levels of disorder (O'Brien & Ciomek, 2023).

Exposure to community violence has been associated with beliefs that make aggression more acceptable or justified, and those who are exposed to community violence are more likely to engage in physically aggressive behaviors and feel justified doing so (McMahon et al., 2009; Pittman, 2023). Consistent with that, the CDC (2025) lists exposure to neighborhood violence and community norms related to violence as potential mechanisms that facilitate IPV in disadvantaged neighborhoods. These mechanisms may be less relevant to psychological aggression, which can occur privately and without the same degree of social constraint. In other words, perceived neighborhood disorder may not simply be related to greater relationship conflict among older adults but may be specific to contextual conditions relevant to the enactment of physical aggression. However, exploratory analyses with the PND subscales showed that perceived physical disorder was associated with greater odds of all four IPV outcomes, whereas perceived social disorder was only associated with physical aggression perpetration. Therefore, the social control and violence mechanisms described above should be considered with caution.

An alternative explanation might be the substantially different prevalence rates of psychological and physical IPV in the current sample. Because IPV was conceptualized using a dichotomous yes/no framework, the high base rate of psychological aggression might not differentiate between those who experienced more severe or frequent versus more minor or infrequent instances of psychological aggression. As such, perceived neighborhood disorder may be associated with frequency and severity of psychological aggression without predicting whether or not psychological aggression occurs. In contrast, the lower prevalence and greater behavioral distinctiveness of physical aggression may have allowed for clearer differentiation among participants.

The present results also indicate a link between social vulnerability and odds of psychological aggression perpetration and victimization as well as physical aggression victimization. This association was significant even after controlling for annual income, educational attainment, and race/ethnicity, and suggests that neighborhood quality is linked to IPV above and beyond individual risk factors. Older adults living in socially vulnerable neighborhoods are more likely to encounter barriers, such as limited economic resources and poorer health (Higginbotham et al., 2025), which can increase dependence on an intimate partner with no alternative options even if aggression is a frequent occurrence in the relationship. Additionally, chronic stress related to neighborhood disadvantage (e.g., employment instability, subjective financial strain) may worsen relational conflict and increase the likelihood of psychological aggression (Benson et al., 2003; Masi & Santantonio, 2026). This may be further exacerbated by unstable or weak support networks (e.g., friends, family) as they tend to live in similar communities and therefore may have limited ability to provide financial or practical support to help an older adult experiencing IPV (Voith et al., 2021).

Additionally, for physical aggression victimization, social vulnerability was associated with victimization, but not perpetration. One explanation might be that physical aggression perpetration depends more on individual risk factors (e.g., previous experience of IPV, normalization of violence) than on structural neighborhood characteristics (e.g., Hammett et al., 2021). In other words, availability of neighborhood-level resources may be more relevant to older adults' ability to leave a relationship characterized by physical aggression than the perpetration of physical aggression itself. That said, in light of the small number of participants endorsing physical aggression in their relationship, the present findings should be considered with caution and replicated with a larger sample. Supplementary analyses suggest that the SVI household characteristics theme was associated with psychological aggression victimization. This is consistent with prior research that the risk of experiencing IPV increases if a person resides in neighborhoods with greater singlemother households and households with children (Benson et al., 2003; Beyer et al., 2015). As such, residents in these neighborhoods likely face greater demand for resources, which may constrain the support available to older adults experiencing IPV (Wright, 2015). However, the household characteristics theme also includes the percentage of residents aged 65 and older, residents aged 17 and younger, persons with a disability, and persons with limited English proficiency (CDC, 2024, which makes it difficult to attribute this association to any one indicator.

Moreover, the SVI minority status theme was associated with greater odds of reporting psychological aggression perpetration. Similar to research with younger populations, this likely reflects limited access to IPV-related services and support in neighborhoods with higher rates of non-White residents (Williams et al., 2018). Given that none of the themes were associated with physical aggression and the heterogeneous nature of indicators that make up each SVI theme, these findings should be interpreted with caution. The finding that the socioeconomic theme was not uniquely associated with any of the IPV outcomes is in contrast to previous literature that shows positive associations between poverty or low SES and psychological IPV (Ahmadabadi et al., 2020; Gerino et al., 2018; Hammett et al., 2022; Warmling et al., 2017). This might be due to the relatively high individual socioeconomic status of the present sample (e.g., median of 16 years of education), even though participants' zip codes were, on average, at the 66 th percentile of the overall SVI. Future research should ensure that a study sample covers the full spectrum of socioeconomic status.

Implications

The results show that both subjective and objective measures of neighborhood disadvantage are associated with higher odds of experiencing psychological or physical aggression among people over age 60, supporting the view that relationships, aging, and wellbeing are linked to the environmental context in which people live. Understanding the links between neighborhood factors and IPV will aid in reframing IPV as not just an individual issue but also a community and societal concern that warrants intervention at both the individual level (e.g., primary care providers who assess for and address IPV in aging adults) and policy level (e.g., addressing socioeconomic disadvantage, funding research on IPV in aging adults, advocacy and resources for victims of psychological and physical IPV).

On the individual level, the US Preventive Services Task Force (USPSTF) recommends routine IPV screening by clinicians for women of reproductive age, but this recommendation does not extend to older adults and the Task Force concluded that the evidence did not suffice to assess the benefits and harms of screening older adults for abuse and neglect (US Preventive Services Task Force et al., 2025). A study examining clinicians' attitudes toward screening for IPV in older populations found that clinicians' understanding of late-life IPV and the relevance of screening for older populations varied significantly (Dichter et al., 2025). These findings suggest that additional research on screening for IPV in older populations is needed to demonstrate benefits and efficacy, as is greater clinician education on late-life IPV. Specifically, the present findings suggest that examining neighborhood quality may help clinicians identify older adults at greater risk for IPV.

Although clinical trials have examined the efficacy of IPV interventions, most target younger populations; few are tailored to older adults (Pathak et al., 2019). For example, a recent study on an online intervention targeting IPV in perinatal women, Strength for U in Relationship Empowerment (SURE), showed gains in positive affect and perceived emotional support (Johnson et al., 2026). Further research is needed to expand this area of focus to include older populations.

Additionally, while best practice in public health involves matching community need with resource allocation (CDC, 2024), with IPV-related resource allocation specifically, areas with higher percentages of White residents tend to be over-resourced, and areas with higher percentages of low-income, Black, or younger residents tend to be under-resourced (Williams et al., 2018). While individual-level interventions such as anger management and communication skills training may be effective (Cheng et al., 2021; Karakurt et al., 2016), IPV prevention and intervention resources need to be targeted where IPV is most concentrated, requiring geographically targeted resource allocation to neighborhoods with the highest social vulnerability.

Limitations and Future Directions

The present study has several limitations. First, the study's data are cross-sectional and do not include participants' experiences over time, thereby limiting the ability to make causal inferences. Second, the final sample had limited representation of people of color and was lacking in accurate portrayals of diversity, with 89.1% of participants identifying as White. Third, related to the previous point, the sample reported a median of 16 years of education, which is higher than the approximate median of 13 years estimated by the National Center for Education Statistics (2023). Fourth, the data were self-reported and therefore may be limited by recall, social desirability, and other biases. Fifth, the present study used a convenience sample recruited via MTurk and Prolific; therefore, the data may not be representative of the general population.

Future studies should consider other data collection methods that better represent the general population and include a larger sample of people of color. Sixth, few participants reported physical aggression perpetration or victimization, which resulted in a low number of events per predictor in the physical aggression models. However, Firth's penalized logistic regressions yielded virtually unchanged estimates. Seventh, IPV was assessed with only one partner's report and operationalized as a dichotomous prevalence indicator, which does not capture both partners' perspectives or the frequency and severity of aggression. Eighth, loneliness was included as a covariate, although it may also be a pathway through which neighborhood characteristics are linked to IPV, which cannot be examined with cross-sectional data. Finally, using the Social Vulnerability Index as a measure of objective neighborhood characteristics is a limitation because socioeconomic status varies within a single zip code. Future studies would benefit from using indices such as the Area Deprivation Index as objective measures of neighborhood disadvantage at the census block level, as it better captures the nuances of socioeconomic variability within zip codes.

Conclusions

Despite these limitations, this study provides important insight into the relationship between neighborhood attributes and IPV in adults over 60, a growing public health concern that has been previously under-researched. Specifically, results suggest that subjective and objective indices of neighborhood disadvantage are both linked to the experience of physical or psychological IPV in older adults. This highlights the need to view late-life IPV as a societal, not just individual, concern, and to advocate for further research on IPV screening in older adults in connection to their contextual neighborhood environment and interventions tailored to their populations, along with greater clinician education on the prevalence and severity of late-life IPV and geographically targeted resource allocation.
