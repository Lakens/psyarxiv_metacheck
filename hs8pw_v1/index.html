Files that do not belong in the repository

We left out 52 files that software creates by itself, not the authors: Jupyter checkpoints (3), operating system (49). They hold nothing a reader can use, but they clutter the repository, and software can mistake them for real files (for example a ._ copy of a .csv for data).

Remove these: Operating system files: macOS adds a __MACOSX folder and a ._ copy of every file it puts in a zip, and .DS_Store files to folders; Windows adds Thumbs.db and desktop.ini. On a Mac, create zips from the command line with zip -r -X, or remove them afterwards with zip -d archive.zip "__MACOSX/*" "*.DS_Store". RStudio session files (.Rproj.user/) hold open tabs and unsaved edits; R history (.Rhistory, .Rapp.history) lists the commands typed in a session and can hold file paths and passwords; Jupyter checkpoints (.ipynb_checkpoints/) are automatic copies of notebooks; compiled Python files (__pycache__/, .pyc) are recreated when the code runs; open-document lock files (~$...docx, .~lock...#) are left while a document is open; editor backups (file~, .bak, .swp) and temporary files (.tmp, .temp, an interrupted save of R’s workspace, .RDataTmp) are copies a program made by itself.

Keep only on purpose: version control folders (.git, .svn, .hg) hold the project’s history, which can include earlier versions of files and data that was later removed; editor settings (.idea, .vscode) can hold paths on your own computer. A .gitignore based on GitHub’s templates (https://github.com/github/gitignore) keeps them out of a repository.

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Full text

Abstract

What factors govern and predict an individual's random behavior? Under many views, human random behavior arises from a grab bag of group-level heuristics (e.g., the representativeness heuristic, the law of small numbers, etc.). Contrary to this view, we present evidence that random behavior arises from highly idiosyncratic processes, borne out even with minimal amounts of data. Over 50,000 people generated random sequences of (a) coin flips, (b) dice rolls, and (c) locations in a 3-by-3 grid. Importantly, participants submitted only 8-12 responses in each task. This very small window minimizes many factors that might create individual differences in random behavior on their own, such as differences in working memory or attention. Yet, individuals demonstrated consistent behavior across these three different tasks, as reflected both by their sequence features and by a simple computational model of random behavior. Thus, these results indicate that, while some general heuristics guide random behavior, there also exist unique, idiosyncratic patterns that warrant attention -raising broader questions about how random behavior is realized in the human mind.

Introduction

Among the most pervasive and well-documented biases in human cognition is our supposed inability to behave randomly. When asked to generate sequences of random coin flips, numbers, locations, and so on, humans fall prey to a variety of biases that lead to non-random behavior. For example, ask a person to generate a sequence of coin flips and they will rarely repeat responses on consecutive trials (i.e., they will avoid generating two heads or two tails in a row); ask a person to predict what will come next in a series of coin flips following 7 heads in a row and they will likely guess tails (i.e., the gambler's fallacy); and so on (for review, see Bar-Hillel & Wagenaar, 1991; Falk & Konold, 1997; Nickerson, 2002; Hahn & Warren, 2009).

Canonically, researchers believe such biases reflect a variety of heuristics. Perhaps the most famous example of this is the representativeness heuristic, wherein people judge the randomness of an event or predict its next outcome based on how well it matches their prototype of a random sequence (Kahneman & Tversky, 1972; Reimers et al., 2018; see also the law of small numbers, Tversky & Kahneman, 1971). A variety of later work expands upon this idea, proposing that judgments of local representativeness operate over a subject's short-term memory window (e.g., Kareev, 1992; Rapoport & Budescu, 1997; Kubovy & Gilden, 1991), and that perhaps the limits of working memory explain such biases (Hahn & Warren, 2009; Warren et al., 2018; see also Icard, 2021; Boger et al., 2027).

Although these biases reflect real and meaningful aspects of human random behavior, researchers often imply (and in some cases, explicitly say) that their underlying mechanisms are homogeneous across people. In other words, it is often assumed that random behavior arises from group-level heuristics. For example, Falk et al. (2009) take this for granted, writing that " [b]ecause of people's shared subjective-randomness image, human-generated patterns meant to be random may resemble each other more than truly random patterns do"; in a review of random behavior, Nickerson (2002) notes that individuals' "performance, as a group, has been evaluated in terms of the expected properties of a random set of sequences" (emphasis added); and so on.

While the notion of "subjective randomness" often refers to group-level heuristics, recent evidence suggests that random behavior might be idiosyncratic -i.e., that different people show different signatures of random behavior. For example, Schulz et al. (2012) found individual differences in random behavior insofar as (a) a model fit to one person's random sequence performed better at predicting another random sequence from that same person than a model fit to a different person's random sequence, and (b) a model given two random sequences from one person and a third random sequence from a separate person correctly identifies the 'foreign' sequence at a high rate. Later work from Schulz et al. (2021) found that such patterns remained stable when re-tested after one week (see also Peters et al., 2007; Maes et al., 2011 for instances of test-retest or simple cross-task reliability in random behavior). More recently, Boger et al. (2025) demonstrated idiosyncratic random behavior not only across a variety of tasks (e.g., random number generation, one-dimensional random location generation, and two-dimensional random location generation) but also over the span of one full year. Thus, whereas the predominant view in the literature assumes uniformity in random behavior, some recent results point to the possibility of individuality in random behavior. Of course, the existence of individual differences in random behavior is not, in itself, incompatible with the existence of population-level biases in such behavior. Nevertheless, it is worth appreciating that these biases vary considerably across individuals, such that the most effective model of random behavior is one that takes into account an individual's idiosyncratic 'parameters' for each of several distinct biases or heuristics.

Are different people random in different ways? How quickly do idiosyncratic patterns in random behavior emerge? And is there meaningful variability across a much broader cross-section of the population? To approach these questions, we analyzed a massive dataset containing individual random behavior across three tasks. In April 2022, The Pudding -a data journalism websitepublished an article analyzing a finding (from Gauvrit et al., 2017) about how random behavior varies across the lifespan. This article included a demo where people generate short sequences of coin flips, dice rolls, and locations in a 3-by-3 grid. Impressively, the article collected random sequences across three different tasks for over 50,000 people, making it an ideal dataset for testing idiosyncratic random behavior. Perhaps more importantly, for our purposes, these sequences were at most 12 items long in the case of coin flips, and only 10 items long in the other two tasks (Figure 1). Thus, the tasks present a marked departure from typical laboratory tasks, which often contain several hundred trials for a given participant. These very short sequences should (in theory) minimize individual differences in random behavior. After all, with only a few responses, there is little to analyze in any sequence, and even less to analyze in a fashion that might distinguish one person's random behavior from another's. Furthermore, long laboratory tasks (of the kind in Boger et al., 2025) invite possible individual differences unrelated to randomizing ability itself. For example, in tasks with hundreds of trials, differences in working memory or attention span may manifest as differences in randomization. The present work mitigates these concerns insofar as the short sequences tested here impose minimal memory demands. With these limited data, and with various other sources of individual differences minimized, are individual signatures of random behavior still traceable?

Here, we demonstrate that the answer is yes. Individual differences in random behavior arose not only in simple emergent properties of the sequence (e.g., the frequency of repetition), but also in a simple computational model of random behavior. This model generalized across tasks: For example, observing only a few random coin flips from a given participant was enough to predict idiosyncratic random behavior in the other two tasks. Thus, our work provides unusually large-scale evidence for individual variability in random behavior.

Methods

Data collection

Our data come from demos in The Pudding's April 2022 article on random behavior (https://pudding.cool/2022/04/random/). We thank Russell Samora (a writer for The Pudding and an author of that article) for sharing the raw sequences with us. The Pudding stopped logging people's random sequences on August 1, 2022 -at which point they had already received over 50,000 responses. Given the nature of this 'experiment', the demos did not collect many variables that are often collected in typical experiments. For example, the demos did not collect any identifying information about the 'participants'; the demos did not log response times for each sequence (or each item in the sequence); and so on. Rather, the data contain only (1) the subject's self-reported age (this was collected for the purpose of age-prediction in The Pudding's article), (2) their sequence of random coin flips, (3) their sequence of random dice rolls, and (4) their sequence of random 3-by-3 grid locations. (Given that it is unrelated to our core questions -and that there is no way to verify if this information is correct -we do not report anything related to the participants' age. Preliminary analyses found no relationship between age and any of the relevant statistics reported below, in line with The Pudding article's conclusion.)

In total, we received data from 52,708 people.

Preregistration and code availability

We did not preregister any aspects of this study. The data already existed at the time of the idea's inception. However, our analysis methods and models mirror previous work (e.g., Boger et al., 2025; Boger et al., 2027; see also Angelike & Musch, 2025). All data and analysis scripts are available on our OSF repository (https://osf.io/vs5fp/); and readers can experience the 'tasks' for themselves on The Pudding (https://pudding.cool/2022/04/random/). (We highly recommend doing this, not least because the article itself is interesting and beautifully done. Raw sequences are also available on The Pudding's GitHub page for this article https://github.com/the-pudding/data/tree/master/random.)

Procedure

In The Pudding's article, participants provided a sequence of (up to) 12 random coin flips, 10 random dice rolls, and 10 random locations in a 3-by-3 grid. Each participant completed the tasks in this order. In the coin flipping task, participants had access to their most recent choice (i.e., heads or tails). In the dice rolls task, participants had access to a full history of their choices (i.e., their selection on all previous trials). Finally, in the grid task, participants had no access to any history of their previous choices. (After completing these three tasks, the article guessed the participant's age, at which point the participant input their real age with a slider.)

Exclusion criteria

First, we excluded participants who did not submit sequences that were at least 8 items in all three tasks. This excluded 2,281 people, leaving us with 50,427 participants.

Next, we excluded participants who exhibited obviously "non-random" behavior in any of the three tasks. This excluded very few people, and all results hold regardless of these exclusions (see also Boger et al., 2025 for discussion of exclusions in studies of individual differences in random behavior). First, we excluded participants whose coin flips contained all heads or all tails; if a fair coin was flipped 8 times, this would occur 0.8% of the time. Next, we excluded participants whose dice rolls contained any given value 6 or more times; if a fair die was rolled 8 times, this would occur 0.3% of the time. Finally, we excluded participants whose grid sequence contained any given value 5 or more times; for 8 uniform draws from a set of 9 items, this would occur 0.6% of the time. We applied these same bounds to all sequences (i.e., even ones with more than 8 responses). If a participant was excluded in any task, we excluded their data from all tasks (as we wanted to examine cross-task reliability). In total, this excluded 1,044 additional participants (2.07%), leaving us with 49,383 participants total.

Computational model

To analyze our data, we fit a simple computational model to subjects' sequences in the three tasks. Our model adapts the one proposed by Angelike & Musch (2025), which is itself a variation of a prior model (Boger et al., 2025). The model proposed by Angelike & Musch (2025) contains three parameters: (1) a 'stay' parameter, which adjusts the probability of repeating a choice, (2) a 'distance' parameter, which adjusts the probability of selecting choices that are nearby (or faraway) from the most recent choice in the response space, and (3) a 'cycling' parameter, which adjusts the probability of selecting a choice as a function of how recently it was last selected. In this work, we simplified this model and used only the distance parameter. We decided not to use the other two parameters for a variety of reasons. First, using a three-parameter model for sequences as short as 8 items causes instability in parameter estimates (and overfitting). Second, the parameters definitionally overlap in certain cases (e.g., a participant who repeats choices often will have a high 'stay' parameter and a low 'distance' parameter); longer sequences tease apart this overlap in ways that help distinguish the parameters from one another. Finally, the coin flipping task exacerbates both of these issues: With only two possible responses, every response is either a 'stay' with distance 0, or a 'switch' with distance 1. In other words, parameter estimates are especially erratic for the coin flips. Thus, we decided to use only the distance parameter here. (We believe other models might produce even stronger performance on the below metrics -this model is just one possible implementation.)

Our one-parameter model worked as follows. Using a nonlinear program solver for maximum likelihood estimation from the MATLAB optimization toolbox, we fit a model to each participant in each task. The model starts with uniform probabilities across all choices (the baseline to which we compare our full model). The model attempts to predict the next item in the sequence as a function of its 'distance' to the most recent choice (i.e., the distance between the most recent choice and a candidate next choice), starting from the second response (given that the first choice has no predecessor). For coin flips, distance is always 1 (alternating) or 0 (repeating). In the other two tasks, 'distance' means Euclidean distance between choices (e.g., the distance between the top-left and bottom-center locations would be ). 1 The distance matrix is multiplied by the 5 distance parameter, which is bounded [-1, 1]. This bound follows previous approaches (e.g., Boger et al., 2025; Angelike & Musch, 2025; Boger et al., 2027), and indeed most participants' parameters fell within these bounds (i.e., very few experience compression because of these bounds, as the 5th percentile parameter value is -0.80, and the 95th percentile is 0.94). Positive values of the distance parameter indicate that the subject prefers to choose faraway options; negative values indicate a preference for nearby options; and a value of 0 indicates no preference (i.e., uniform randomness). Finally, a softmax function is applied to convert the predictions into a probability distribution for the next choice.

Results

Do participants display idiosyncratic random behavior, even in these short sequences? First, we examined this with regard to individual stability in emergent properties across tasks. Specifically, we examined whether a participant's frequency of repeating a choice (i.e., selecting the same choice on consecutive trials) in one task predicted their frequency of repeating in another task; and whether the same was true for their average distance between choices (i.e., Euclidean distance between consecutive choices).

We performed 1,000 subsamples where, in each sample, we randomly selected 200 participants (approximately the sample size used in other, similar studies; e.g., Boger et al., 2025) and then computed the cross-task Pearson correlation for that subset. This provides a more stringent test for stability and ensures that any observed patterns hold not only in the full, 50,000-person sample but also in randomly selected slices of that sample (i.e., a sample size one might usually find in an online experiment). We applied this method to all tests below. First, we observed within-subject, cross-task correlations in repeats; median r(coin flips, dice rolls) = 0.23, 95% subsampling interval = [0.09, 0.37], median r(coin flips, grid locations) = 0.17, 95% subsampling interval = [0.02, 0.31], median r(dice rolls, grid locations) = 0.33, 95% subsampling interval = [0.18, 0.47]. A similar pattern arose when considering distance between choices; median r(coin flips, dice rolls) = 0.20, 95% subsampling interval = [0.05, 0.34], median r(coin flips, grid locations) = 0.12, 95% subsampling interval = [-0.03, 0.25], median r(dice rolls, grid locations) = 0.33, 95% subsampling interval = [0.19, 0.45]. 2 At first, these results might seem underwhelming, but they are quite strong, especially given (a) how much the tasks differ from one another and (b) how short the sequences are. For example, 'distance' in the coin flipping task is functionally equivalent to repeats; still, there is evidence for a relationship between distance in the coin flipping task and distance in the much richer grid locations task. (More generally, the relationships between the coin flipping task and either of the other two tasks may be more informative than the relationship between the dice rolling task and the grid location task, given that these latter two tasks are more similar in response format than the coin flipping task is to either.) Furthermore, with sequences as short as 8 choices, some participants might not repeat at all when generating dice rolls or grid locations -yet, we observed substantive correlations across these tasks.

As expected given the correlation in average distance, we observed within-subject, cross-task consistency in the model's distance parameter; median r(coin flips, dice rolls) = 0.21, 95% subsampling interval = [0.06, 0.33], median r(coin flips, grid locations) = 0.13, 95% subsampling interval = [-0.01, 0.26], median r(dice rolls, grid locations) = 0.31, 95% subsampling interval = [0.18, 0.43]foot_2 (Figure 2A).

But does the model also predict individual behavior across tasks? Our next set of analyses asked this question by comparing the model's subject-specific, cross-task predictive power to the model's subject-general, within-task predictive power. In other words, we used each subject's parameters in one task to predict their choices in another task, and compared this to a similar leave-one-out baseline as described above. However, the leave-one-out baseline used other subjects' parameters in the to-be-predicted task. For example, we compared the accuracy of the subject-specific coin flipping models in the dice rolling task to the accuracy of other subjects' dice rolling parameters on the dice rolling task. Surprisingly, even here we observed evidence for idiosyncratic behavior; train dice test coin median difference = 2.14%, 95% subsampling interval = [0.11%, 4.00%], train grid test coin median difference = 1.40%, 95% subsampling interval = [-0.78%, 3.34%], train coin test dice median difference = 1.48%, 95% subsampling interval = [0.21%, 2.70%], train grid test dice median difference = 2.08%, 95% subsampling interval = [0.73%, 3.43%], train coin test grid median difference = 0.81%, 95% subsampling interval = [-0.19%, 1.80%], train dice test grid median difference = 1.66%, 95% subsampling interval = [0.57%, 2.66%] (Figure 3A).

We further tested this cross-task predictability with a forced-choice model identification task, inspired by the approach of Schulz et al. (2012). In our analysis, pairs of subjects -and their corresponding sequences within a given domain -were selected at random. Then, we asked the model to 'identify' which subject generated a sequence in a held-out domain. For example, after observing two subjects' sequences of coin flips, could the model identify who generated a sequence of dice rolls? Identification accuracy followed a simple procedure wherein a 'correct guess' occurred when the model (trained on the observed domain's data) assigned a higher log likelihood to the correct subject (in the held-out domain). Here too, we observed evidence for individual stability; given dice identify coin median accuracy = 54.5%, 95% subsampling interval = [47.5%, 61.5%], given grid identify coin median accuracy = 52.5%, 95% subsampling interval = [46.0%, 60.0%], given coin identify dice median accuracy = 53.0%, 95% subsampling interval = [47.0%, 60.0%], given grid identify dice median accuracy = 56.3%, 95% subsampling interval = [49.0%, 63.0%], given coin identify grid median accuracy = 53.0%, 95% subsampling interval = [46.0%, 59.5%], given dice identify grid median accuracy = 58.0%, 95% subsampling interval = [52.0%, 64.5%] (Figure 3B).

Relative to chance-level performance (of 50%), this may seem underwhelming at first. Indeed, many of these subsampling intervals -both in the forced-choice accuracy and in the cross-task prediction -include chance-level performance (though they would reach significance in a less stringent, full-sample test relative to chance). However, for the forced choice case, Schulz et al. (2012)'s performance provides a reasonable baseline; they reach 88% accuracy, but this is with 300-trial sequences, a richer response space (numbers 1-9), within-task comparisons, and a substantially more complex model. In other words, given the dramatically shorter sequences, smaller response spaces, cross-task comparisons, and simpler model, the above-chance performance we observe here is noteworthy. Together, these two analyses provide evidence for idiosyncratic random behavior in ways that extend across tasks.

General discussion

Does random behavior emerge from homogeneous group-level heuristics or individual-level preferences? Whereas the extant literature largely assumes the former, some recent studies point to the latter (e.g., Schulz et al., 2012; Schulz et al., 2021; Boger et al., 2025). Here, we build on this possibility by demonstrating that idiosyncratic random behavior emerges (a) in a massive online sample of over 50,000 people and (b) with minimal input from each individual (both in terms of the number of trials, and in some cases, in terms of the number of possible responses).

The tasks examined here stand in stark contrast to the longer laboratory tasks typically used to study individual differences in random behavior (often containing hundreds of trials). Longer laboratory tasks open the door to a variety of individual differences in behavior unrelated to randomness itself. For example, perhaps some subjects simply have longer attention spans than others, leading to strong individual differences later in the tasks; perhaps some subjects have larger working memory capacities than others, allowing them to better monitor their own choices; and so on. In other words, prior results could in principle be explained by the fact that, when generating long sequences, participants vary in their ability to maintain focus over an extended period of time, rather than in their ability to randomize per se. In this way, the extremely short sequences tested here provide a minimal test for individual stability. Even with a substantial reduction in the factors that might induce individual differences in random behavior (i.e., differences in attention span or working memory), we found idiosyncratic behavior across tasks. Furthermore, our work presents data from three separate tasks, which allows us to explore the effect of randomness itself over and above task-specific factors.

Along these lines, the observed cross-task stability is especially notable given the fact that the three tasks differ not only in their format, but also in their feedback structure. In the coin flipping task, subjects had access to their most recent choice; in the dice rolling task, subjects had access to all of their previous choices; and in the grid locations task, subjects had access to none of their previous choices. This, again, presents an opportunity for the tasks to come apart for reasons orthogonal to random behavior itself. Yet, despite this difference, we found evidence for individuality in random behavior -suggesting that individual differences in random behavior are unlikely to be reducible to potential differences in working memory capacity or monitoring.

The results here should not be taken as definitive evidence against group-level heuristics at play in random behavior. Indeed, it seems likely that there are general biases and heuristics involved -in line with our proposal. Even so, these results suggest that there may be individual variability in the usage or application of those heuristics. In other words, it may not be that one either does or does not exhibit any given bias but instead that people vary continuously in the extent to which they exhibit that bias -and, thus, that the ideal way to understand and model random behavior may be to treat various known biases as 'traits' that vary in magnitude. Such a view would stand in contrast with the extant literature, which usually assumes a relative uniformity in the design of these heuristics.

Concluding remarks

Collectively, these findings invite a shift in how we think about "subjective randomness." Rather than reflecting a single shared heuristic applied uniformly across people, subjective randomness may instead consist of stable but individually tuned biases -different people approximating randomness in systematically different ways. Even with only a small amount of input, and across tasks with different structures and feedback, these signatures remain detectable. Randomness, in other words, is not merely something people broadly fail to produce; it is something they fail to produce in their own unique ways. Further understanding these idiosyncratic biases may provide insight into how random behavior is realized in the first place.
