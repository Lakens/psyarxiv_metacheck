How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

Abstract

Human language comprehension is remarkably robust-we are good at grasping what speakers and writers intend and can even understand them when their productions are not perfectly well-formed. But it is also susceptible to breakdown: we sometimes don't reach any possible interpretation of the linguistic input and even arrive at interpretations which are incompatible with the literal meaning. We argue that the most promising overall theory of both robustness and breakdown in language comprehension and processing is the Noisy Channel theory, which integrates the roles of probabilistic inference and cognitive (e.g., memory) constraints in language understanding and provides a unified account across many levels of language representation. We articulate the key components and commitments of this theory and review the substantial body of evidence which is consistent with its predictions. We further identify distinctive novel predictions of the Noisy Channel theory and show that it often provides a straightforward explanation for long-standing puzzles. We conclude by reviewing open questions and future directions for the theory.

1 Introduction

Humans are adept at using language to communicate with one another. However, the apparent ease of understanding what our conversation partner is saying belies a task that is not at all trivial. Language is noisy: errors and disfluencies on the speaker side, background environmental noise during the conversation, and memory limitations in the listener all can make language hard to understand. With respect to spoken language, it is estimated that approximately 6% of uttered words are disfluent (Bortfeld et al., 2001; Fox Tree, 1995; Kasl & Mahl, 1965), and, by some estimates, a couple of speech errors (e.g., substitution, addition, or deletion of a phoneme or word) occur approximately every thousand words for a person speaking their first language and over ten times as often for a person speaking in a second language (Garnham et al., 1981; Poulisse, 2000). Comprehenders must be able to arrive at the speaker's intended meaning most of the time despite the presence of this noise.

Consider the following example from a television reporter's interview with a veterinarianfoot_0 : "Can you demonstrate for us what it's like to brush our teeth's pet, just a little bit?" Most readers will likely agree that this is not what the reporter intended to say. We can infer that she probably intended to say "pet's teeth" but produced an exchange error resulting in a sentence that is syntactically well-formed but has a highly implausible literal meaning. Notice that the veterinarian also had no difficulty inferring the reporter's intended meaning, replying "Certainly" despite the nonsensical literal meaning of what was said. Figure 1 provides an illustration of how this probabilistic inference unfolds. This is a remarkable feature of human language: most of the time, when we encounter an utterance with an improbable meaning, we can infer the likely intended meaning, sidestepping a potential miscommunication.

In other cases, communication breaks down. The same inferences which often allow us to compensate for noise and errors in the input may lead us to infer something different than what the speaker intended. For instance, if the reporter had mistakenly said "Can you demonstrate for us what it's like to brush our pet's fur?," substituting "fur" for "teeth," it's unlikely that the veterinarian could recover the intended meaning because the meaning most consistent with the input is as likely as, if not more likely than, the meaning the reporter intended. Even in the absence of any errors by the producer, inferences may lead us to misinterpret the intended meaning. For instance, if a new patient came in asking for help with his ailing "hog", the veterinarian may reasonably assume that he had said "dog" -a more common pet which sounds quite similar.

A major result of Shannon's work is that transmission of information across noisy channels can theoretically be both robust and efficient: robust in the sense of reducing the probability of error to an arbitrarily low level, and efficient in the sense that information transmission can still achieve some non-zero maximum rate, namely what is known as the channel capacity.

2.2 A noisy-channel theory for human language processing

Language provides humans with a system for mapping from meanings to forms. Those forms-spoken, signed, or written-can be transmitted to other humans; if the intended meaning is correctly recovered from the form, then the form has been used to communicate successfully. However, the transmission process is error-prone. In natural language engineering applications, the need to correct these transmission errors has motivated successful noisy-channel modeling approaches ranging from optical character recognition (Raviv, 1967) to speech recognition (Jelinek et al., 1975) to spelling correction (Kernighan et al., 1990). The main thesis of the present paper is that the mechanisms of human language understanding are organized in a form well captured by the Noisy Channel framework provided by information theory. In this review we provide a detailed overview of this framework and, in particular, distinguish three types of noise, or distortion processes: (1) speaker-internal noise in language production mechanisms; (2) noise outside both speaker and addressee in the communicative environment; and (3) addressee-internal noise in perceptual and memory systems.

2.2.1 Components of the Noisy Channel model of linguistic communication

According to the noisy-channel theory of human language comprehension, the comprehender's internal model of the linguistic communication process can be analyzed as follows (Figure 2). (Throughout this discussion, we will use the term speaker to refer to the producer of spoken, signed, or written linguistic signal, and comprehender to refer to a recipient of the signal who is proficient enough in the language to have some hope of understanding it.) A communicative act begins with a speaker's intended meaning, or message in noisy-channel terminology, which we denote as m. The speaker uses her knowledge of language to generate an intended signal-a linguistic form-s that encodes the intended message m. Through mechanisms of language production, the speaker produces an utterance u to physically express s in an acoustic or visual format that can be transmitted over space and time. Speakers can and do make errors, so the conversion of intended signal s to utterance u is the first stage of noisy-channel language production in the theory. The second stage is the transmission of u through the physical environment to the comprehender; this introduces additional noise, such as other sounds in the environment being overlaid on the acoustic form of a spoken utterance. The resulting signal that reaches the comprehender we call the input i. Crucially, however, there is a third source of noise, internal to the comprehender. Perceptual systems have limited fidelity; furthermore, language understanding involves integrating inputs over time and thus relies on memory, which itself is limited and error-prone. The mechanisms of perception and memory that language comprehension relies upon transform a raw input signal into a received input representation r that the brain can operate on to apprehend linguistic meaning, but that form may not be a perfect representation of the input i.

The proposal is that language comprehension then involves inferences on the basis of the received input representation r that approximate normative probability theory. 2.2.2 Representations in the various stages of the Noisy Channel model

On the Noisy Channel theory proposed here, the noisy-channel model in the mind of the comprehender is tightly adapted to actual features of the communicative environment. It follows that the model's representations and dependencies between the representations reflect that environment. As is always the case in cognitive science, theory development has to proceed in the face of some uncertainty about the nature of the representations involved. Below, we elaborate on the representations in the Noisy Channel theory, starting with those that we can be most certain about because we can more easily observe or otherwise study them, and then proceeding to those that are necessarily part of the theory but whose structure we are more uncertain about.

Scientifically, we can be most certain about the representations of the produced utterance u and the input i.

An utterance is a physical signal: a temporally varying acoustic waveform, or visual pattern of manual articulations, or arrangement of graphemes into writing on a page, screen, or other surface. Likewise, in spoken language recognition, i will be a temporally varying acoustic waveform as it reaches the listener's ear; in signed language understanding or reading, i will be visual inputs on the retina. The relationship between the utterance u and input i is straightforward: u may degrade in its physical transmission, and is overlaid with other environmentally-generated signals, such as ambient sound; i is the resulting "noisy" version of u.

Environmental noise is causally independent of u, leading to the hypothesis that for the comprehender, i is conditionally independent of m and s given u, so that the conditional probability P (i|u) is a basic component of the comprehender's noisy-channel model.

Within the theory, an utterance is generated by language production mechanisms to express an intended signal s. We assume that the representation of s includes at least a partial sequence of words and potentially accompanying information, such as prosodic emphasis, for physically realizing those words in language production. It may also be that s further includes additional information, such as the word string's syntactic structure, that is not directly physically expressed in u. The intended signal s realizes an intended message m, whose representation we intentionally leave somewhat vague. In practice, we will generally assume that m includes considerable semantic information such as predicate-argument structure, but in some cases it could be a less structured communicative intent. The mapping from m to s is probabilistic and incremental -it is usually the case that there are multiple signals s to choose from to express a given m -and expressible as P (s|m). However, we assume that the intended signal s faithfully realizes m (e.g., that s has a parse that matches m's predicate-argument structure)-that is, that the mapping from m to s is not itself a source of noise. 3The received input representation r is the result of perceptual and memory systems' processing of the input i, and is the basis for incremental comprehender inferences. We cannot be certain about the precise representation of r, because it is internal to the brain and not accessible to direct measurement. Fortunately, however, the role of r in comprehender inferences can be entirely captured by its noisy-channel likelihood given the upstream representations that may be the targets of comprehender inference.

Limits on hearing and visual acuity as well as memory and attention affect the level of noise in r. The inferred intended message m ′ has an analogous, and currently under-specified, representation to m.

In some cases, the comprehender may be aware that some noise corruption occurred upstream of r and explicitly consider the noise generating process as part of their inference (e.g., when the comprehender notices a speech error but interprets the speaker's meaning according to what they think she meant to say). In other cases, the comprehender may seemingly not be aware that they have inferred a message that is inconsistent with the input (e.g., When there is a typographical error in a sentence but the reader doesn't notice it and interprets the sentence according to the error-free meaning). The mapping from i to r may introduce changes that "fix" corruptions that occurred upstream (e.g., during typed production). (Perception and memory can themselves be viewed as rational inference processes; we return to this in Section 3.2.) In both cases, the comprehender has engaged in noisy-channel inference and arrived at a posterior over candidate intended messages given the received input, P (m ′ |r). The precise definition and role of awareness are beyond the scope of this review, but one speculation is that representations of the received input r are accessible to conscious awareness while those of the input i are not.

2.2.3 Key commitments and predictions of the Noisy Channel theory

The key architecture described above implies a number of key commitments and resulting predictions, which we summarize here and discuss in greater detail in later parts of the paper: 1.

Effects of prior probability and likelihood. According to the theory, comprehenders use Bayesian inference to recover what the speaker said (u), intended to say (s), or meant (m). Bayesian inference involves balancing the prior probability of the hypothesized intended signal, P (s) (or P (u) or P (m)), against the likelihood that the received input r would have given that signal, P (r|s). Comprehenders should therefore favor interpretations that have high prior probability-that is, are likely things for the speaker to have said, intended to say, or meant-while relatively closely matching the received input.

2. Different types of noise have different distributions, but there are underlying commonalities as well. Figure 2 distinguishes three stages at which "noise," broadly understood, can affect the message generated by speakers: language production mechanisms by which the intended signal s is realized as the produced utterance u, environmental noise overlaid on the produced utterance u to produce the input i to the comprehender, and noise in the perceptual and memory systems that convert i to the received input representation r. These three types of noise have different characteristics: for example, the distribution of language production errors is sensitive to linguistic structure (Fromkin, 1973; Dell & Reich, 1981a), whereas environmental noise is not. However, there are also plausible commonalities among the different types of noise. In particular, while errors are collectively frequent, any particular error is rare. As a result we expect the noise model in the mind of the comprehender to have a parsimony bias across all noise types: hypotheses that require positing fewer errors should generally have a higher likelihood than hypotheses that require positing more errors. Within our framework, these general characteristics structure the "noise likelihoods" P (u|s), P (i|u), and P (r|i).

3. Comprehenders' internal noise models are calibrated to the statistics of the linguistic environment. In noisy-channel inference, as in other cases of probabilistic inference, performance is optimal if the true distributions are used. Together with the assumption of rationality in language comprehension, this implies that comprehenders' patterns of interpretations will reflect priors and noise likelihoods that match the statistics of the linguistic environment, at least insofar as the comprehenders' experience and resources make this possible. Qian & Levy (2023) call this the environmentally-calibrated noisy-channel hypothesis.

This hypothesis also implies that comprehenders' interpretation patterns may depend on the communicative medium (e.g., spoken versus written language), because both the prior and the noise likelihoods may differ across medium.

4. The target of inference may vary by context and communicative goal.

Inferring what a speaker said or meant, where the target of inference is s or m, is different than closely transcribing their error-prone speech, where the target of inference is u. Within the noisy-channel framework, we would expect to see different inferential patterns depending on the target of inference, because optimal inference would not correct errors that are generated by noise processes upstream of it. The comprehender may not be certain about what noise process gave rise to a particular observed error, but the nature of the error may provide clues about this: for example, a word exchange is an unlikely product of environmental noise in spoken language understanding, whereas missing a short phoneme is not unlikely at all.

3 Evidence in support of the Noisy Channel theory

The results of many empirical studies are consistent with predictions of the Noisy Channel theory. Below we provide a comprehensive review of these studies. For clarity, we organize the discussion around predictions regarding the effects of the prior (Section 3.1) and the noise likelihood (Section 3.2), though many of these studies implicate both. Some of the empirical findings reviewed below were originally reported in support of alternative accounts. 3.1 Evidence for effects of the prior on language processing

The formal framework that we outline in Section 2.2 suggests different ways that prior knowledge and preferences impact noisy-channel inferences. In this generative modeling framework, people may bring in probabilistic expectations about the message m, the intended signal s, and the produced utterance u. These expectations can arise from various sources, including experiences with the linguistic environment and general conceptual knowledge about the world. Depending on the goal of the inference, the prior may marginalize over different distributions (e.g. see the differences in the prior terms in Equation (1) and Equation ( 2)). 1. Interpretations that are more likely a priori are also more likely to be favored in the posterior, when holding the likelihood term (nearly) constant. Research across multiple domains of psycholinguistics has provided evidence consistent with this prediction. In the domain of speech perception, an acoustic input that is ambiguous between two phonemes is perceived as the phoneme that has higher prior probability given surrounding context. Using synthesized recordings of words, Ganong (1980) varied the voice onset time (VOT) of the first phoneme to create a continuum between voiceless (long VOT, e.g. /t/) and voiced (short VOT, e.g. /d/) stop consonants. For some of the resulting sound sequences, the long VOT variant formed an English word (e.g. task) but the short VOT variant did not (e.g. dask), whereas in others, the short VOT variant formed an English word (e.g. dash) but the long VOT variant did not (e.g. tash). Participants heard these sound sequences and were asked to report whether they heard a voiceless consonant or a voiced consonant. Given the same intermediate length VOT (equally ambiguous between voiceless and voiced as an isolated sound), participants were more likely to perceive it as voiceless if the voiceless phoneme formed a word (e.g., ? ask) and as voiced if the voiced phoneme formed a word (e.g., ? ash) together with the rest of the sound sequence. This is consistent with the idea that a strong prior expectation of the speaker producing a known word over a non-word guides the listener to favor the word interpretation in the posterior and to perceive the same acoustic input differently under different phonetic contexts. Massaro (1979) provides evidence for the same phenomenon in visual perception.

Similarly, context outside of the word can affect what is most likely in the prior and, consequently, the posterior. In a study by Yurovsky et al. (2017), both adults and preschool children heard recorded speech convolved with Brownian noise. Given a speech input, such as "I had carrots and [bi:z] for dinner," participants were asked to select from two pictures the one that best matched the heard speech. Among the two pictures, one depicted carrots and peas on a plate and the other depicted carrots and bees on a plate. Both adults and children were more likely to interpret the noisy sound sequence ([bi:z]) as depicting the plausible event (carrots and peas on a plate) than the implausible event (carrots and bees on a plate). In the reading domain, Rueckl & Oden (1986) showed that sentence context could bias readers to endorse one interpretation or the other for a stimulus that was visually ambiguous between two words (e.g., bears-beans).

Analogous effects of the prior have been observed in sentence processing experiments with noise that is more likely attributable to production errors than acoustic or visual noise. For example, Qian & Levy (2023) investigated error correction preferences in cases of subject-verb number mismatch, such as "The gift for the kids are under the bed". The observed sentence consists of a singular head noun as the subject and a plural verb. Minimally, there are two salient alternatives that the speaker may have intended to communicate: either that the subject is singular and the verb should have been "is", or the subject should have been the plural "gifts" and the observed verb is inflected correctly. Qian & Levy (2023) found that participants' preferences with respect to changing the subject or changing the verb depend on the prior expectations about the number of the subject phrase. As the preference of the observed subject phrase increases in the prior relative to other candidates, the proportion of participants choosing to edit the verb also increases.

For example, people are more likely to change the verb instead of the subject given the sentence "The rug under the chairs are made of wool" compared to "The gift for the kids are...," as a single rug under multiple chairs is strongly preferred a priori relative to other logically possible combinations (e.g., multiple rugs under multiple chairs), while a single gift for multiple kids is less likely a priori than multiple gifts for multiple kids.

2. A message or signal that is more probable in the prior is more robust to the corruption of noise in communication. Consider a signal x i that is corrupted into x p1 , x p2 , x p3 , etc., by different kinds of noise. If P (x i ) is sufficiently high, then the posterior for x i , P (x i |x pk ) ∝ P (x i ) P (x i → x pk ) (k = 1, 2, 3...), is higher than for other signals, P (x l |x pk ), such that a rational comprehender would successfully reconstruct the intended message from the perceived signals. In contrast, consider another signal x j being corrupted into x q1 , x q2 , x q3 , etc. If P (x j ) is sufficiently low, then at least some of the posteriors P (x j |x qk ) will be lower than for other signals P (x l |x qk ), meaning that a rational comprehender would recover a message different from the original.

There is long-standing evidence for this effect in speech perception in noise. In Miller et al. (1951), participants listened to recordings of nonsense syllables, words, and digits and were asked to report what they heard. The recordings were corrupted by different levels of white noise, resulting in different signal-tonoise ratios. Miller et al. (1951) found that given the same signal-to-noise ratio, listeners were more accurate in reporting back the heard digits and words that were more likely in the prior, compared to nonsense syllables.

Similarly, high-frequency words are more resistant to noise than low-frequency words. A classic study from Howes (1957) documented an inverse relationship between signal-to-noise ratio and log word frequency.

For a low frequency word to be intelligible, the critical signal-to-noise ratio must be higher, compared to a higher frequency word. Moreover, in noisy conditions, a low frequency target word may often be misidentified as a higher frequency word that is phonetically similar to the target word but the reverse misidentification pattern is unlikely (e.g., Savin, 1963; P. A. Luce & Pisoni, 1998). In other words, high frequency words, i.e. words that more likely to be heard a priori, can tolerate more corruption in the communication channel and still be correctly recognized. Higher frequency words are also more robust to (visual) noise in visual word recognition (Solomon & Postman, 1952). A signal's robustness to corruption can also depend on contextually-formed prior expectations. For example, Kalikow et al. (1977) measured the effect of the predictability of a word given the preceding sentence context on recognition accuracy in noise. Words that were more expected given the preceding context were on average more accurately recognized in signals corrupted by babble-type noise. Similarly, eye-tracking studies show that participants are less likely to notice letter substitutions (such as the intended word "shark" being presented as "sharp") when the preceding text strongly motivates the word "shark" (i.e. a description of swimming sharks during a marine cruise) compared with when the preceding text does not strongly motivate the word (i.e. a description of a couple visiting a zoo) (Ehrlich & Rayner, 1981). And misspelled words (e.g., "dack") are more likely to be recalled as their correctly-spelled alternatives (e.g., "duck") when the context is biased toward the alternative meaning ("The child fed the dack at the pond."), compared to when it is neutral ("The visitors noticed the dack by the house.") (Potter et al., 1993).

3. An a priori improbable interpretation that faithfully adheres to the form of an utterance may be dispreferred relative to a more probable interpretation, even if the latter requires positing noise corruptions to the form. In other words, a strong prior preference can override a preference for non-corrupted inputs in the likelihood. Comprehenders do, in fact, sometimes interpret sentences by assuming that an error occurred (even when the input did not contain any errors) in order to extract a more a priori plausible meaning. For example, Ferreira (2003) and Bader & Meng (2018) showed that a considerable number of participants interpreted a sentence like "The dog was bitten by the man." not according to the well-formed surface syntactic structure but as communicating an event in which the dog was the agent. Similarly, in Gibson, Bergen, & Piantadosi (2013) and Poppels & Levy (2016), participants were presented with sentences like "The mother gave the candle the daughter." or "The package fell from the floor to the table.". These sentences are well-formed. However, if they are taken at face value, they describe very unlikely events: in general, a daughter is more likely to be a recipient of an action than a candle, and things fall from a higher place to a lower place. Participants in these studies often responded to comprehension questions with an interpretation that was not faithful to the linguistic input, instead preferring a more plausible interpretation that required positing a noise corruption (e.g., word insertions, deletions, or exchanges that transformed a more plausible intended sentence into what they received). This prediction of Noisy Channel provides a new explanation for the long-standing puzzle of linguistic "illusions"-sentences that are consistently perceived to be well-formed even though they are not. For example, the sentence "More people have been to Russia than I have," an instance of the comparative illusion (Montalbetti, 1984), is often reported to be coherent (e.g., via acceptability ratings). Several explanations for this phenomenon have been proposed, all of them positing that the incoherent utterance is processed in relation to some similar, coherent form (Wellwood et al., 2018; E. O'Connor et al., 2013; Townsend & Bever, 2001). Consistent with these ideas, the noisy-channel explanation for the comparative illusion posits that participants interpret comparative illusion sentences according to a similar but more plausible intended message that they rationally infer (e.g., "People have been to Russia more than I have") (Zhang et al., 2025).

Unlike previous accounts which tended to be illusion-specific, Noisy Channel provides an explanation that generalizes to other phenomena including "Depth-charge" (Wason & Reich, 1979; Paape et al., 2020; Zhang et al., 2023), negative polarity (Vasishth et al., 2008; Xiang et al., 2009; Orth et al., 2021; Zhang & Gibson, 2024), and missing VP illusions (Gibson & Thomas, 1999; Hahn et al., 2022).

The prior can overwhelm the likelihood during real-time processing as well. For example, Slattery (2009) found that a word (e.g. brunch) with a higher-frequency neighbor (e.g. branch) induced longer reading times when embedded in a neutral context such as "Due to the freezing rain, the brunch was postponed a week." relative to a control word without a higher-frequency neighbor (e.g., buffet). Both neighbors (branch and brunch) fit with the preceding context, but the non-presented alternative (branch) is more probable.

The eye-tracking data suggest that readers may initially mis-perceive the target word brunch as branch, consistent with the notion that, even in the earliest moments of word processing, readers may infer that the identity of a word corresponds to a more probable alternative that has been corrupted by noise rather than infer a word identity that is faithful to the input but less probable a priori.

Similar effects hold across languages and types of noise. For example, Keshev & Meltzer-Asscher (2021) leveraged the morpho-syntactic properties of Hebrew to create sentences which have plausible meanings but have rare syntactic structures (object relative clauses). Reading time and sentence completion data showed that native speakers of Hebrew were willing to posit a mismatch in number (and gender) agreement in the sentence and interpret the stimuli according to a more commonly occurring syntactic structure rather than analyze the sentence according to a well-formed but infrequent structure. Analogous results have also been found in Russian (Poliak et al., 2023, reviewed in more detail below). Consistent with the noisy-channel prediction, these findings suggest that even when a sentence is well-formed on the surface, comprehenders are willing to posit that an error occurred and endorse an interpretation that is not faithful to the linguistic input when it is strongly preferred in the prior.

3.1.2 Different sources of prior expectation

Research in language processing has accumulated abundant evidence for language comprehenders' sensitivity to prior expectations that arise from different sources of information and levels of linguistic representation.

In the preceding section, we highlighted a few of these findings to provide empirical support for three specific predictions of the noisy-channel framework. Next, we provide a more comprehensive review of this evidence organized according to different sources of prior expectation.

Message plausibility & sentence context.

A message describing an implausible event has a lower prior probability P (m) than one describing a plausible event. As we have discussed previously, the prior over event plausibility often leads people to interpret a sentence that literally describes an implausible event as the noisy realization of a more plausible event (e.g., interpreting "The dog was bitten by the man" as communicating "the dog" as the "do-er", Bader & Meng, 2018; Ferreira, 2003). Such interpretations that are not faithful to the input string are also observed across a variety of linguistic structures and languages (Gibson, Bergen, & Piantadosi, 2013; Poppels & Levy, 2016; Poliak et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2023; Zhan et al., 2023; Mao et al., 2025; Poliak et al., 2025; Bader & Meng, 2025). Notably, this occurs in the absence of any memory burden. In the reading studies reviewed here, the sentences are available to participants while they answer the comprehension questions. Listeners are also sensitive to the plausibility of messages and engage with non-veridical interpretations of received speech (Gibson et al., 2017; Yurovsky et al., 2017).

Message plausibility contributes to the prior during incremental processing of linguistic input as well.

As a sentence unfolds, listeners continually update their expectation of what may come next, and they are surprised when the continuation is implausible given world knowledge (e.g., He planted beans in his car; Kutas & Hillyard, 1980; Hagoort et al., 2004; Kuperberg & Jaeger, 2016; Ryskin & Nieuwland, 2023, for review). Given a supportive context that constrains the possible semantic content of the upcoming word, readers tend to skip the next word when it is highly predictable (Rayner et al., 2011). Participants are also less likely to notice a target word being substituted with a close neighbor (e.g., sharp instead of shark ) when it is highly predictable (Ehrlich & Rayner, 1981; Slattery & Yates, 2018).

Discourse and broader context considerations. Prior expectations about message plausibility generally come from people's commonsense understanding of the world. But these expectations can be overridden by information in the local discourse or consideration of the broader context of the message (e.g., conversational partner, text genre, register). For instance, typically a word that is semantically implausible given the preceding context (e.g. "The girl comforted the clock") elicits a larger negative amplitude in the N400 window of the event-related potential relative to a plausible (high probability) word (Kutas & Hillyard, 1980).

But if the sentence is embedded in a cartoon-like discourse context about a girl interacting with a clock with a human face that looks sad, the N400 effect is reduced (Nieuwland & Van Berkum, 2006). Conversely, a word that is generally plausible in context (e.g.,"Every evening I drink some wine before I go to sleep.") can elicit an increased N400 when it has low probability given the particular speaker producing the sentence (e.g., a young child; Van Berkum et al., 2008). Similarly, when listening to child-produced speech, adult word recognition indicates that they adapt their prior expectations to reflect the kinds of messages that children are likely to discuss (Meylan et al., 2023).

These effects of the discourse context on the prior are directly reflected in the noisy-channel inferences that readers make. Chen et al. (2023) showed that participants were more likely to make a non-literal inference when reading an implausible sentence (e.g., inferring that the daughter received something after reading "The mother gave the candle the daughter") when it was preceded by a short discourse context which was supportive of the more plausible alternative meaning (e.g., "When the power outage happened, the daughter asked the mother for a candle. The mother found a candle in the kitchen cabinet.") compared to when the preceding sentences were unrelated or absent.

In addition to updating their priors based on discourse information, comprehenders appear to update their priors based on more abstract statistical properties of the broader context. For instance, when participants are presented with a high ratio of implausible to plausible sentences, N400 effects decrease (Delaney-Busch et al., 2019) (but see Nieuwland, 2021) and participants make fewer noisy-channel inferences (Gibson, Bergen, & Piantadosi, 2013; Yurovsky et al., 2017). For instance, Yurovsky et al. (2017) changed participants' prior over the semantic plausibility of the intended message through exposure trials, during which the speaker either talked about highly expected events like "My cat has three little kittens" (Plausible condition) or rarely expected events "My cat has three little hammers" (Implausible condition). After listening to the implausible speaker in the pre-test trials, participants were less likely to interpret the noisy sound sequence ("carrots and [bi:z]") as depicting the plausible event (carrots and peas on a plate) than the implausible event (carrots and bees on a plate). Further, in Gibson, Bergen, & Piantadosi (2013), for one group of participants the proportion of sentences with implausible literal meanings was higher than for the other. The participants who read a higher proportion of sentences with implausible semantic content were more likely to adopt a literal interpretation of the target sentences, such as interpreting "The mother gave the candle the daughter" as communicating an event where the candle was the recipient and the daughter was given to the candle. This suggests that, as the local linguistic environment changed and implausible meanings became more probable, readers' prior expectations changed and, consequently, those implausible interpretations received higher posterior probabilities (see also Chen et al., 2025).

Lexical and structural frequency. Humans are sensitive to the statistics of the linguistic environment.

The frequency of occurrence of a linguistic unit-lexical item, multi-word unit, abstract structure, phoneme sequence, etc.-has an important impact on its probability in the prior. At the coarsest level of distinction, non-words have lower prior probability than words. When the input is a non-word (or ambiguous between a non-word and word), listeners and readers are more likely to infer a signal (and corresponding message) that is consistent with a word. For instance, when readers see words in which a few letters are out of place (e.g., aviaiton), they readily recover the correctly-spelled sequence, which has higher prior probability (Bruner & O'Dowd, 1958; Chambers, 1979; Norris & Kinoshita, 2012). Letters are also more readily identified when they are part of a word than non-word sequence ("word superiority" effect; Reicher, 1969; Wheeler, 1970; Massaro, 1979). Similarly, in the auditory domain, Ganong (1980) (reviewed above) showed that listeners' perception of an ambiguous stop consonant (e.g., ambiguous between /d/ and /t/) is affected by whether the voiced or the voiceless consonant would result in a recognizable word in the vocabulary; They are more likely to identify the same ambiguous sound as /d/ in ? ash and as /t/ in ? ask. As reviewed above, frequent words are more likely to be retrieved or identified, particularly in the presence of noise (P. A. Luce & Pisoni, 1998; Norris & McQueen, 2008).

Similarly, an input with an infrequent structure has a lower prior probability, and comprehenders might be attracted to other messages/signals with relatively more frequent structures and thus with relatively higher prior probabilities. Using the properties of word order and morphological agreement in Russian, Poliak et al. (2023) demonstrated that noisy-channel inferences can be guided by the frequency/prior probability of the syntactic structure even when the literal and non-literal interpretations are equally semantically plausible (see also Keshev & Meltzer-Asscher, 2021). For instance, in the sentence Рейчел увидел Чарли (Rachel saw-MASC Charlie), the verb увидел is unmarked and, therefore, masculine. The likely subject is Charlie (typically a masculine name). 4 However, that parse would mean that the subject came after the verb-an allowable but less frequent word order in Russian. When asked who saw somebody, readers often ignored the morphological cues and interpreted the sentence non-literally according to a more frequent word order (subject-verb-object) (i.e., they chose the first noun, Rachel, as the subject close to 50% of the time in some conditions). In noisy-channel terms, readers inferred that the intended sentence/message had been Рейчел увидела Чарли (Rachel saw-FEM Charlie) and the morphological ending indicating that the subject of the verb was feminine had been corrupted (i.e., the -а had been deleted). When the word order was more frequent (Рейчел увидела Чарли; Rachel saw-FEM Charlie), the sentences were almost never interpreted non-literally (see also Poliak et al., 2025). Other findings also suggest that, in the case of implausible sentences, comprehenders more often endorse non-veridical interpretations when the structure is less frequent/non-canonical (e.g. passive) than when it has a canonical structure (e.g. active) (e.g. Bader & Meng, 2018; Ferreira, 2003; Gibson, Bergen, & Piantadosi, 2013; Poppels & Levy, 2016).

The effects of lexical and structural frequency on the prior are incremental (see Norris & McQueen, 2008).

For instance, as the auditory signal unfolds, listeners look more to candidate referents with higher frequencies (e.g., more looks to the higher-frequency bed than to the bell when they hear "be...") (Dahan et al., 2001).

Similarly, readers appear to interpret an unfolding sentence with multiple possible parses according to the more frequent candidate structure (e.g., "warned" in "The experienced soldiers warned..." is interpreted as the main verb rather than the start of a relative clause), resulting in a slow-down when they reach a word that renders that more probable parse inconsistent with the input (e.g., "The experienced soldiers warned about the dangers conducted the midnight raid.") (MacDonald et al., 1992; Roland et al., 2007).

Perceptual expectations. Lower-level perceptual expectations play a role in the prior as well. For example, when participants listen to sentences or words in which an acoustic distortion is introduced (e.g., a cough replaces the /s/ in 'legi? latures'), they often fail to notice the corruption (e.g., they perceive the cough to have occurred after the word) and recover the much more likely signal (legislatures) (R. M. Warren, 1970; Samuel, 1981). Given the surrounding context (e.g., the other phonemes in legislatures), the /s/ has very high prior probability.

Comprehenders also expect that speech is produced at a relatively constant speed and infer a message that has this property if the input is realized with a less probable prosody (e.g., part of it is faster than the rest). In a study by Dilley & Pitt (2010), participants heard speed-altered versions of recordings for sentences like "Deena doesn't have any leisure or time..." In the Normal Rate condition, the sentence was presented at its original spoken rate. In the Slowed Context condition, the target region "-sure or t-" was kept unchanged but the rest of the speech was slowed down. In the Speeded Target condition, the target region was sped up but the rest of the speech was kept unchanged. In the Speed Target and Context condition, both the context and the target region were sped up. Participants were less likely to report hearing the function word "or" when the context was slowed down or the target region was sped up, compared to the conditions where the context and the target region were presented at the normal spoken rate or both were sped up. Similarly, when presented with speed-altered versions of recordings for the sentence "Deena doesn't have any leisure time. . . ", participants were more likely to report hearing the function word "or", which was not there in the original production of the speech, when the context was sped up or the target region ("sure t-") was slowed down, compared to the cases where the recording were presented at the normal rate or slowed down altogether. This work shows that people are able to estimate speech rate from a minimal exposure to the signal, and that the prior expectation of a relatively stable speaking rate guides people' inferences about the intended linguistic signal from noisy perceptual input.

Following the rational analysis approach (Anderson, 1991), we assume that comprehenders model different kinds of noise in ways that are broadly consistent with the noise distributions in the world. As stated in Section 2.2.3, a rational comprehender will make optimal inferences if their noise model and prior perfectly match the true distributions in the world. The true distributions are unlikely to be accessible to the typical human language comprehender, but their internal noise model (and prior) may approximate these true distributions (with greater accuracy as their exposure to language use increases). The implication is that the comprehender's noise model is calibrated to the distributions of each noise type (e.g., production vs. environment) and source (e.g., speech production errors vs. typed production errors) (see 4).

As illustrated in Figures 2 and 4, noise may take multiple forms. Speakers/writers may introduce production errors, the utterance u may be distorted by other sounds in the environment, and the limitations of the comprehender's perceptual and memory mechanisms may result in a received input representation, r, that is not entirely faithful to the input, i. These three types of noise are assumed to have different independent distributions. For instance, speakers can corrupt a word by mispronouncing the first phoneme as a different, similar phoneme, whereas environmental noise would not corrupt a word in the same way. Despite type-and source-specific idiosyncrasies (e.g., speaking vs. writing), there are underlying commonalities across noise distributions as well. Simpler corruptions that are more easily generated are more likely (e.g., a speaker is more likely to produce an /s/ instead of an /S/ than a /p/ instead of an /S/ because the former two phonemes involve more similar motor plans; a reader is more likely to miss a short word than a long word because the latter takes up more space and the probability that at least one eye fixation falls within its boundaries is higher). And noise of each type can be composed of multiple independent sources. For example, poor hearing and failures of attention-both examples of comprehender-internal noise-generate independently distributed corruptions and can jointly affect the same input. Moreover, any combination of noise types may affect a given message (e.g., a given signal can be corrupted by both a speech error from the producer and a loud overlapping noise from the environment).

3.2.1 Production noise P (u|s).

During communication, the signal intended by the producer, s, is incrementally converted into an utterance, u. This process has the potential to introduce noise at many levels, from typographical errors which cause one letter to be replaced by another to higher-level planning errors which cause words to be improperly sequenced. Production noise distributions depend on the source of the noise (Figure 3A). We organize the discussion of production noise around three commonly-studied sources of noise: spoken production, writing/typing, and higher-level production processes (though, in practice, the distinctions between them may not always be clear-cut).

Spoken production. Speech production depends on the articulatory system. As with any motor system, movements are variable: in the same way that a dart lands in different locations on consecutive throws even when the same thrower has only one target (the bullseye), each production of a phoneme falls in a slightly different place in acoustic space (Hillenbrand et al., 1995). For a fluent speaker, most phoneme tokens will land close to the center of the distribution and be easily recognized as belonging to their phoneme category, but some peripheral tokens may be closer to a different phoneme which is nearby in acoustic space. Further, the space of probable speech errors for a given word is constrained by properties of the word itself, as well as the lexicon as a whole. For instance, speech errors tend to obey the phonotactics of the speaker's language and are more likely to result in another word than a non-word (Dell, 1995; Dell et al., 2021; Goldrick, 2004).

Several findings indicate that listeners are sensitive to the probabilities of different kinds of corruptions in spoken production (see Norris & McQueen, 2008). For instance, listeners often interpret the meaning of a corrupted spoken input according to what was intended without even noticing that there was an error, especially when the corruption is a commonplace one. Cole (1973) introduced mispronunciation errors on consonants in multi-syllabic words in a recording of a passage from "Through the Looking Glass." Consonants could deviate from the standard by only one phoneme but differing numbers of articulatory features (e.g., voicing, voicing and nasality, etc.). Mispronunciations, typically errors in execution by the speechmotor system, are more likely to correspond to small deviations in articulatory feature space than larger ones. Typed production. Written, typed, and signed production processes have their own modality-specific constraints and corresponding noise distributions, though these are less well documented than the properties of errors and variability in speech. Some of the constraints on speech errors have also been found in typing (e.g., repeated phoneme/letter effect; Pinet & Nozari, 2018). But some constraints differ, likely due to differences in the motor processes that are involved. For instance, in typing, the physical layout of the keyboard also affects what errors are likely, because a stray key press may be more likely to land on an adjacent key (and the distance on the keyboard doesn't reflect the acoustic similarity of letters). 6Readers are sensitive to the modality-specific probabilities of different kinds of corruptions in typed production. As with errors in speaking, typing errors typically involve a small number of changes to the intended word (mostly character deletions, insertions, substitutions, or transpositions), and readers were more likely to identify an intended word (e.g., The storyteller could tell an amusing anecdote) from a corrupted version (e.g., ...antidote) when the number of corruptions was smaller (Ryskin, Stearns, et al., 2021). Further, not all human typographical errors are equally common (e.g., Kernighan et al., 1990).

Deletions are more common than insertions among character-level typing errors. Substitutions can be viewed as one deletion and one insertion (Levenshtein et al., 1966) and are less common than insertions or deletions.

Comprehenders appear to be calibrated to these relative probabilities. As reviewed in Section 3.1, in Poliak et al. (2023), Russian speakers read semantically plausible, typed sentences, either in the more frequent SVO word order (e.g., "Рейчел увидела Чарли"; Rachel saw-FEM Charlie) or less frequent OVS word order (e.g., "Рейчел увидел Чарли"; Rachel saw-MASC Charlie). The single-character morpheme on the verb indicates which agent is the subject and which one is the object: if the verb ends in -a, the subject is feminine (e.g., Rachel) and if the verb is unmarked, the subject is masculine (e.g., Charlie). Word order provided another (probabilistic) cue. Readers often interpreted the sentences with an OVS word order non-literally, assuming that the more frequent SVO version of the sentence had been intended but an error (e.g., deletion of -a) transformed the sentence into the non-canonical form. Crucially, they were more likely to interpret the OVS sentences non-literally when a character deletion could be posited, as compared to a character insertion, as in "Чарли увидел Рейчел" (Charlie saw-MASC Rachel; SVO) →"Чарли увидела Рейчел" (Charlie saw-FEM Rachel; OVS). Readers were least likely to infer a non-literal meaning when this required positing a substitution of one character for another, as in "Рейчел увиделa Чарли и Кейт" (Rachel saw-FEM Charlie and Kate; SVO) →"Рейчел увидели Чарли и Кейт" (Rachel saw-PL Charlie and Kate; OVS). Inferences about the intended message were calibrated to the probability of single-character noise corruptions in typed production (see also Norris & Kinoshita, 2012).

Higher-level production processes Upstream aspects of production (e.g., planning, grammatical encoding, lexical selection), which are thought to be shared between spoken and written production, are also prone to errors. Producers may omit, insert, substitute, and even exchange whole words in a sentence, though the relative probabilities of these errors are not well-described because of the challenges associated with creating and annotating a valid corpus of whole-word errors (without knowing what the intended sentence was, it is often challenging to know how it may have been corrupted). Gibson, Bergen, & Piantadosi (2013) proposed a noise model, analogous to the probabilities of characterlevel corruptions in typing, in which deletions of words are more probable than equivalent insertions; and exchanges, which can be viewed as two insertions and two deletions, are the least likely. Assuming this noise model broadly aligns with the true distribution in the world (it is almost certainly an incomplete model), comprehenders appear to be sensitive to the probabilities of corruptions that may occur during higher-level stages of production. As reviewed above (Section 3.1), when faced with a semantically implausible sentence such as "The mother gave the candle the daughter", readers often respond 'yes' to the question "Did the daughter receive something?" suggesting that they infer that a more plausible meaning was intended (e.g., "The mother gave the candle to the daughter") but that it was corrupted (e.g., by the deletion of "to") during transmission (Gibson, Bergen, & Piantadosi, 2013; Poppels & Levy, 2016). Indeed, readers spend more time fixating/re-reading the region of the sentence where the "to" could have been (between "candle" and "daughter") than another comparable region of the sentence, indicating that they are considering it as a likely locus of deletion noise (Cutter et al., 2024). Crucially, readers make more inferences when the implausible received sentence requires positing a deletion rather than an insertion as in "The mother gave the daughter to the candle" (Gibson, Bergen, & Piantadosi, 2013; Poppels & Levy, 2016). And implausible sentences which required positing word exchanges, such as "The ball kicked the girl" elicited fewer inferences than sentences which could have resulted from more probable corruptions (e.g., one insertion or deletion). Further, when whole words are exchanged in production, it is typically with other words of the same category (e.g., nouns with nouns, prepositions with prepositions, etc.; Dell & Reich, 1981b). Similarly, readers often fail to notice word transpositions, especially when these consist of two short words of the same class (Huang & Staub, 2021), plausibly because they make rapid, real-time noisy-channel inferences about the intended message.

Further, comprehenders adapt their noise models to the distribution of corruptions in the environment (or their estimate of that distribution). Readers are more likely to make non-literal inferences about the intended meaning of a sentence when some proportion of surrounding filler sentences contain errors (Gibson, Bergen, & Piantadosi, 2013). Moreover, readers learn what kinds of errors are likely versus unlikely. In Ryskin et al. (2018), participants were asked to read semantically implausible sentences and correct them if they noticed any errors. For some participants, a third of filler sentences contained an error. These were either deletions of a word, insertions of a word, exchanges of two words, or a mix of all three types.

Participants' corrections reflected the predominant error type that they encountered. For instance, they were more likely to correct the sentence "The mother gave the candle the daughter." to "The mother gave the candle to the daughter if they were exposed to many deletion errors than if they were exposed to many exchange errors (in the latter case they were more likely to correct the sentence by exchanging words, as in "The mother gave the daughter the candle."). Moreover, the comprehender's estimate of the production noise distribution appears to depend on top-down attributions (e.g., assumptions that a given speaker is error-prone) as well as learning from bottom-up evidence (e.g., hearing the speaker make errors). Listeners were more likely to make non-literal inferences about a speaker's intended meaning when the speaker had an accent suggesting they may not be a native speaker of English (Gibson et al., 2017). Even when an accented speaker makes no errors, listeners appear to assume that they are more likely to produce errors relative to a native-sounding speaker.

Higher-level production processes can generate many other kinds of errors (beyond word deletions, insertions, etc.). Some errors may be dependent on properties of the signal itself. For instance, while subject-verb agreement errors are rare for adult, native speakers of English, when completing a sentence with a complex noun phrase such as "The gift for the kids...," speakers and writers are more likely to produce a subject and verb that don't agree in number. They may say "The gift for the kids are under the bed" rather than "The gift for the kids is under the bed" (Bock & Miller, 1991). These errors likely arise for multiple reasons including uncertainty in the conceptualization of the objects being described (Brehm & Bock, 2013) and local frequency of co-occurrence ("kids are" is more common than "kids is").

Comprehenders are sensitive to the probabilities of corruptions that may occur at these earlier stages of production. Visual world paradigm eye-tracking shows that, soon after hearing the agreement error on the verb (e.g., 'The gift for the kids are..."), listeners look to images in the display which correspond to more appropriate subjects (e.g., multiple gifts) but are inconsistent with the preceding input (Brehm et al., 2021). This gaze pattern suggests that listeners may be considering the probability that the plural subject ('gifts') was intended but the '-s' was accidentally omitted by the speaker (among other possible alternatives). Further, in the context of these complex agreement structures, a mis-inflection of the verb (is → are) is a more likely production error than an incorrect number marking on the subject (gift → gifts) (Kandel & Phillips, 2022). In an error correction task, comprehenders were more likely to attribute the locus of an agreement error to the verb than the subject, indicating that they are finely calibrated to the probabilities of production errors in ways that are context-specific (e.g., in the context of these complex structures, fewer/simpler corruptions may no longer be the most likely, Qian & Levy, 2023).

3.2.2 Environmental noise P (i|u).

The environment-everything that is outside of the production system of the speaker and the comprehension system of the listener-contains infinitely many sources of noise. A cough from a bystander may cover a portion of the acoustic stream, a smudge on a page may turn one letter into another (or into something visually ambiguous between two letters), or an interrupted internet connection may lead to erasure of a portion of an audiovisual representation of u (Figure 3B). As with production noise, corruptions which are more easily generated by their source should be those that, in general, have higher probability in the comprehender's noise model. For instance, the noise coming from a poor connection during a video conference is very likely to cause erasures but insertions of unrelated words would be highly unlikely.

Comprehenders' inferences are sensitive to the probability of environmental noise. For instance, as reviewed in Section 3.1, when participants listen to sentences or words in which an acoustic distortion is introduced (e.g., a cough replaces the /s/ in 'legi? latures'), they recover the much more likely signal (legislatures) (R. M. Warren, 1970; Samuel, 1981). A cough masking the sound of one phoneme is a probable corruption, while a speaker omitting an /s/ in the middle of a word is less likely. Similarly, given an acoustically ambiguous speech input such as, "I had carrots and [bi:z] for dinner", both adults and children were more likely to interpret the ambiguous sound ([bi:z]) as depicting the plausible event (carrots and peas vs. carrots and bees), particularly when the speech was convolved with Brownian noise (Yurovsky et al., 2017).

Moreover, comprehenders' interpretations are guided by their estimate of the probability of environmental noise regardless of the presence of added (e.g., experimenter-manipulated) noise. In a visual world paradigm study, McQueen & Huettig (2012) manipulated the probability of radio burst noise corrupting the input by splicing noise into non-critical portions of a stimulus sentence. When participants heard a temporarily ambiguous word onset ("bea..." for "beaker") they looked at images corresponding to a word beginning with the same onset ("beetle") more so than words that do not share an onset ("speaker", "candle"). As the word continued to unfold, the images of words which share an ending ("speaker") received more attention relative to words that shared no sounds (e.g., " candle"), replicating prior findings (Allopenna et al., 1998) and indicating that listeners maintain uncertainty about the previous input. Crucially, the latter (so called "rhyme") effect was greater when the preamble contained a burst of radio noise relative to when no radio noise was added, suggesting that listeners estimated a higher probability of noise while processing the target word ("beaker") and, as a result, the posterior probability of the intended signal being "speaker" given that the input was "beaker" was greater, despite the fact that the target acoustic input was unchanged.

3.2.3 Comprehender-internal noise P (r|i).

Finally, noise can be introduced by comprehender-internal processes (see examples in Figure 4C). Unlike production noise and environmental noise, comprehender-internal noise cannot be directly experimentally manipulated and is difficult to measure. The processes that are thought to generate it can be coarsely classified as belonging to sensory-perceptual systems (e.g., hearing, vision), attention, or memory.

Sensory-perceptual.

In the same way that we previously noted that speech errors are most likely to result in tokens that are near-neighbors of the intended phoneme in acoustic space, noise in the auditory system is more likely to distort a phoneme (e.g., /s/) to a different phoneme which is nearby in acoustic space (/sh/) than one that is further away (/d/). Similarly, visual noise is more likely to distort a grapheme (e.g., o) to a different grapheme that is closer in visual feature space (p) than one that is less similar (x). Indeed, listeners are more likely to mistake a phoneme or grapheme for a neighbor in visual or acoustic space (e.g., Geyer, 1977; Klatt, 1968; P. Luce, 1986; Miller & Nicely, 1955), and non-words are more likely to be processed as words when they are acoustically and/or visually similar to an existing word (P. A. Luce & Pisoni, 1998; Marcet & Perea, 2017).

Perception is often characterized as a process of inference from uncertain sensory input (Knill et al., 1996; Sims, 2016; Cusimano et al., 2024). In particular, Bayesian models have been fruitfully applied to explain many aspects of the perception of linguistically-meaningful inputs (e.g., phonemes, graphemes, auditorilypresented words, visually-presented words, Clayards et al., 2008; Feldman et al., 2009; Kleinschmidt & Jaeger, 2015; Norris & McQueen, 2008; Norris & Kinoshita, 2012; Bicknell & Levy, 2010). For instance, the Bayesian model from Norris & McQueen (2008) captures multiple behaviors related to auditory word recognition. In their model, the identity of the word is inferred from the perceptual input by integrating the prior probability of the word (e.g., its frequency) and the likelihood of noise corrupting the intended phonemes to the perceived phonemes. This noise model is estimated using perceptual confusion data from a gating task with all possible (Dutch) diphones and is assumed to capture both the probability of production errors and the probability of misperception. 7 7 The comprehender's noise model is likely to be calibrated both to possible production errors and possible perceptual confusions, though they may receive different weighting depending on the circumstances. For instance, auditory and visual acuity tend to decrease with age. Consistent with increased probability of sensory noise in the comprehender's noise model, 8 older adults' spoken word recognition accuracy is decreased particularly for words that are acoustically similar to many high-frequency words compared to words that are acoustically similar to few low-frequency words (Sommers, 1996). Further, older adults who have lower-fidelity hearing or vision rely more on context than younger adults during tasks like word recognition (Wingfield et al., 1991). When words have higher prior probability (e.g., because they are preceded by a sentence context that makes them predictable), they are more likely to be correctly recognized by older adults than if they have low prior probability. This effect of word probability was reduced for younger adults. And older readers are more likely to interpret well-formed but semantically implausible sentences, such as "The mother gave the candle the daughter," non-literally than younger readers (Cutter et al., 2024), plausibly due to increased probability, in their noise model, of having misperceived the sentence. (Older adults may also have decreased attentional and memory capacity, which may contribute to this increased rate of noisy-channel inferences.) Attention. Attention is not uniformly allocated to the input during language processing. 4 Open Questions and Future Directions

We have proposed that the Noisy Channel theory provides a unifying computational-level account for understanding human language comprehension and reviewed a large body of evidence in support of its predictions.

In what follows, we discuss remaining open questions that we view as important avenues for further theory development. We also outline fruitful emerging lines of inquiry that stem from taking a noisy-channel perspective on language comprehension. 13 As with perceptual noise or attentional lapses, it is often difficult to disentangle whether the comprehender is more likely to attribute a particular error to the producer or to memory noise. For instance, some high-frequency words (e.g., function words) are likely to be corrupted by production noise (e.g., deletions, exchanges) as well as by memory noise. In contrast, recency effects should pertain primarily to memory noise and not to production errors, suggesting potential avenues for isolating the contributions of these different noise sources to the comprehender's noisy channel inference. Similarly, the weighting of noise sources likely depends on the context. When a comprehender is reading sentences without time pressure or any kind of memory burden, the memory noise may be down-weighted in favor of production noise, but when the input is ephemeral (e.g., auditory presentation, RSVP), memory noise may play a more important role.

4.1 Cross-linguistic generality

A consensus in cognitive science is that a theory of human cognition must generalize across populations and languages (e.g. Henrich et al., 2010; Blasi et al., 2022). While evidence for Noisy Channel theory is mostly drawn from experiments with native English (usually monolingual) speakers, located in the United States, some studies have used other languages: Hebrew (Keshev & Meltzer-Asscher, 2021), Mandarin (Liu et al., 2020; Zhan et al., 2023; Mao et al., 2025), German (Bader & Meng, 2018 , 2025), Russian (Poliak et al., 2023 (Poliak et al., , 2025)), Hindi (Poliak et al., 2025), and Standard Arabic (Dodd et al., 2024). While the results of these experiments support Noisy Channel, these languages are only a small sample of the world's languages and only ones that are spoken in industrialized societies. Thus, more cross-linguistic work remains to be done in order to test the generality of Noisy Channel.

4.2 Variability across populations and stages of acquisition

In addition to capturing principles that are general across cultures and languages, the Noisy Channel theory should explain variation in language comprehension between individuals. While most studies reviewed herein have focused on the typical convenience sample of college students and online participants, some populations, such as individuals with language disorders, older adults, second-language learners, and children, may differ in terms of their prior expectations or their model of the noise. Noisy Channel predicts that they should also differ in terms of their language comprehension behavior in systematic ways. Individuals with aphasia (IWAs), in particular those termed "agrammatic," appear to over-rely on semantic plausibility rather than interpret implausible sentences literally, according to the syntax. For example, when hearing "The ball kicked the girl," they would be likely to say that this sentence matches a picture of a ball being kicked by a girl-a more plausible event (Caramazza & Zurif, 1976; M. F. Schwartz et al., 1980). This was originally argued to constitute evidence of a specific syntactic deficit. More recently, this behavior has been interpreted as consistent with noisy-channel processing under an assumption of increased noise (Gibson et al., 2016; T. Warren et al., 2017; Ryskin et al., 2025). Similarly, older adults appear to make more inferences than young adults (but fewer than IWA) consistent with an assumption of increased comprehender-internal noise relative to young adults (Cutter et al., 2024) (see Section 3.2).

Noisy Channel also makes predictions about how comprehension changes over the course of language acquisition. Yurovsky et al. (2017) showed that children as young as 4-5 years old make rational inferences and rely on prior information (semantic plausibility) when input is noisy (audio overlaid with Brown noise).

Further, children rely on plausibility more than adults, likely because their sensory processing skills are not yet fully developed and the received inputs are therefore noisier (Neuman & Hochberg, 1983). However, children also have less experience with the language than adult native speakers, meaning that their prior expectations about what they are likely to hear, as well as their estimates of what corruptions might be introduced, are less well calibrated. As a result, children may make different inferences than adults in some cases. For example, adults hearing "She wrote the letter A to her friend." might infer that "She wrote the letter to her friend." was intended and an "A" had been inserted, whereas children might infer that no corruption had occurred, and the letter in question is not a friendly missive but the character 'A' (discussions of specific letters of the alphabet are likely higher in the prior for children than for adults). Similarly, Futrell & Gibson (2017) argued that second language (L2) learners may appear to rely more on world knowledge and discourse cues than native speakers because they either have less accurate knowledge of their second language or they assume a higher probability of noise. To our knowledge, the implications of a Noisy Channel account of second language acquisition have yet to be directly tested.

4.3 An algorithmic-level account

Developing an algorithmic account of noisy-channel inference represents a substantial computational challenge but it is an important next step for theory development in psycholinguistics. In order to perform exact Bayesian inference according to Equations ( 1) to ( 3), the algorithm would have to enumerate the full-possibly infinite-set of candidate intended sentences and the noise corruptions that could have altered them. Approximate inference algorithms may provide more tractable solutions that are asymptotically correct, thereby providing a resource-rational algorithm for noisy-channel inferences. Below we propose some desiderata for such an algorithm and discuss early forays into its development.

4.3.1 Desiderata of an algorithmic-level account

Functional description of prior and noise. An algorithmic-level account of noisy-channel processing should provide an explicit description of the prior, as a function that assigns probabilities to representations of the intended message, and of the noise, as a function that transforms an intended message using noise sampled from some probability distribution. Explicitly encoding the prior and noise in functional forms allows for assigning values to relevant probabilities in the Noisy Channel framework, such as P (r | m).

An algorithmic-level account should provide a mechanism for computing these probabilities for general inputs. For example, a candidate functional form for the prior is a neural network language model which samples linguistic strings, while a simple candidate noise function might introduce word substitution errors in proportion to their edit distance from the intended word.

Incrementality. An algorithmic-level account of noisy-channel processing should be incremental, in the sense of processing one unit (e.g. word, morpheme, or other unit) at a time in the linear sequence of linguistic input. An ideal algorithmic account of noisy-channel inference would allow for modeling the dynamic nature of inference as linguistic input is processed incrementally, as well as the global inferences made upon seeing all of the input (Levy, 2008b). In contrast, algorithms that take in an entire sentence at once may not be able to model the fine-grained time-course of noisy-channel inferences.

Reanalysis. Evidence from reading time studies suggests that readers, when reaching a point of difficulty in a sentence, re-read previous material to re-interpret the sentence (Frazier & Rayner, 1982). Thus an algorithmic account of noisy-channel language processing should not only capture incremental processing of one new linguistic unit at a time but also support the reanalysis of previously encountered linguistic material, reflecting the comprehender's uncertainty about whether previously observed units faithfully reflect a speaker's intended message or whether they were products of noise. Such reanalysis could be either a basic component of the algorithm or triggered by specific conditions in the course of algorithmic execution, such as the Error Identification Signal proposed by Levy (2008b).

Computational constraints. While the tradition of rational analysis (Anderson, 1991) involves making minimal assumptions on the computational constraints available for a computational model of cognition, these minimal assumptions can still guide model-building. One such minimal assumption is that of algorithmic complexity. For example, we know that human processing time for sentences is roughly linear in the length of the sentence. Therefore, algorithms with O(N ) complexity-where execution time and/or memory requirements grow linearly with input size (N )-can be considered more cognitively plausible than algorithms with O(N 2 ) complexity.

Relation to human behavioral and/or neural measures. An algorithmic-level account should provide intermediate and/or final outputs that can be used as concrete predictors for behavioral measures of human processing. An example of this is language model surprisal, where the final output layer of a neural network language model can be interpreted as a probability distribution over the vocabulary, which can be used as a specific, numerical predictor for human psychometric or neural data such as reading times or ERPs.

4.3.2 Sequential Monte Carlo inference as a candidate algorithmic account

Sequential Monte Carlo (SMC) is an inference algorithm for inferring distributions over latent variables given observations that are sequential in nature. SMC naturally defines a bound on computational resources in the form of the number of particles, each of which corresponds to a hypothesis about the variables in the model.

In previous work, particle filters have been used to model human processing of garden path sentences and digging-in effects (Levy et al., 2008). More recently, an implemented Noisy Channel model that combines an LLM prior, a symbolic error model, and SMC inference was shown to capture patterns of noisy-channel inferences from the literature (Clark et al., 2025). For example, the model shows lower surprisal compared to baseline models at near-neighbor errors such as "The medics cleaned the wound to prevent an inflection" from Ryskin, Stearns, et al. (2021), and produced error corrections for ambiguous agreement errors that correlated with human judgments from Qian & Levy (2023).

4.4 Neural mechanisms

In the current review, we have primarily marshalled behavioral evidence in support of a Noisy Channel theory of language comprehension. However, findings from studies using neuroscientific measurements are consistent with the Noisy Channel account as well, and they suggest avenues for exploring the underlying neural mechanisms. For instance, magnetoencephalography (MEG) data reveal that higher auditory processing regions in anterior superior temporal gyrus (STG) represent phonemic information (e.g., that /b/ or /p/ are equally likely given the acoustic input) within approximately half a second of stimulus onset but lower-level auditory regions continue to maintain subphonemic information, allowing listeners to maintain uncertainty about prior input and revisit the categorization of a phoneme in light of later input (/p/ is more likely than /b/ given "? arakeet " Gwilliams et al., 2018). Further, recordings of electrical activity directly from the cortical surface reveal that, during phonemic restoration of a noise-substituted phoneme (e.g.,legi#lature), activity in STG reflects the phoneme that listeners perceive (Leonard et al., 2016). Moreover, the tobe-perceived phoneme was represented in left frontal cortex before the onset of noise, indicating that the perception of the phoneme was driven by top-down prediction (e.g., lexical priors).

Outside of speech comprehension, Noisy Channel has been linked to two key event-related potentials (ERPs) in language processing, the N400 and P600 (Ryskin, Stearns, et al., 2021; Li & Ettinger, 2023; Li & Futrell, 2023). A large swath of ERP studies has shown that a word that has low prior probability typically elicits greater negativity in the N400 time window (e.g., Frank et al., 2015; Szewczyk & Federmeier, 2022) and that the prior probability of the word depends on many aspects of the input, such as plausibility given world-knowledge, lexical frequency, and discourse context (see Kutas & Federmeier, 2011; Federmeier, 2022, for reviews). Words that appear to be the result of a syntactic or other (e.g., typographical) error elicit positive responses in the P600 time window (Osterhout & Holcomb, 1992; Hagoort et al., 1993; Laszlo & Federmeier, 2009; Ito et al., 2016; Ryskin, Stearns, et al., 2021; Yacovone et al., 2025). For example, when readers encounter a word that has low prior probability in context (e.g., "Before lunch he has to deposit his paycheck at the bark") but is orthographically close to an a priori more probable word (e.g., "bank"), their N400 response is smaller and their P600 response is larger, relative to a word that has low probability and no high-probability orthographic neighbors (Laszlo & Federmeier, 2009; Ito et al., 2016; Ryskin, Stearns, et al., 2021; Yacovone et al., 2025). Further, the smaller the Levenshtein distance between the critical word and its more plausible alternative, the smaller the N400 and the greater the P600 (Ryskin, Stearns, et al., 2021; Li & Ettinger, 2023). While many aspects of the patterns of N400 and P600 effects reported in the literature seem to be consistent with a Noisy Channel account, direct tests of noisy-channel predictions have been limited thus far. Further, the precise nature of the linking function remains debated (Ryskin, Stearns, et al., 2021; Li & Ettinger, 2023; Li & Futrell, 2023).

4.5 Implications for language production

4.5.1 Effects of noisy memory on real-time production

Our discussion of the noisy-channel language processing framework has largely focused on comprehension.

We reviewed empirical evidence that suggests that comprehenders deploy richly structured knowledge in noisy-channel inferences, and this structure is learned from the environment, including the statistics of language production (see also MacDonald, 2013). One open question is how this knowledge is deployed during production itself. During production, speakers plan utterances incrementally (Brown-Schmidt & Konopka, 2015) and monitor their own output (e.g., Nozari et al., 2011). In other words, even within a sentence, what is produced early on serves as context for what comes later. The representation of this preceding context may itself be noisy. We can speculate that the producer likely relies on the same noisychannel inferences as the comprehender to reconstruct the context and produce a continuation that has high probability given this noisily represented context (see Hahn et al., 2022). Some work using sentence completion tasks provides evidence consistent with this view. For instance, speakers sometimes produce completions that are ungrammatical (e.g., missing a main verb, having improper subject-verb agreement).

These errors are more likely when the ungrammatical completion would be correct following an alternative preamble that is close to the preamble they observed (e.g., one morpheme deletion away) and has higher prior probability (Bergen et al., 2012; Ryskin, Bergen, & Gibson, 2021). It remains to be seen whether error patterns in naturalistic production (as opposed to sentence completion) similarly reflect a noisy representation of preceding context (see also Goldberg & Ferreira, 2022).

4.5.2 The design of utterances for a noisy-channel audience

Another open question is to what extent speakers model a rational, noisy-channel comprehender and make choices to optimize for successful communication with such a comprehender. At the acoustic level, people involuntarily raise the amplitude of their voice when speaking against a noisy background, also known as the Lombard effect (Lombard, 1911; Zollinger & Brumm, 2011). Further, this acoustic adjustment is sensitive to the frequency profile of the background noise: people do not show the Lombard effect when the background noise does not interfere with the frequency range of human speech (Stowe & Golob, 2013), suggesting that it has a communicative function. Similarly, speakers engage in phonetic reduction (producing a phoneme with shorter duration, less intensity, etc.; Lieberman, 1963) when a word is predictable in context (Gahl, 2008; Aylett & Turk, 2006; Bell et al., 2009; Tily et al., 2009; Hunnicutt, 1985; Fowler & Housum, 1987), suggesting that they are adjusting their articulatory effort to optimize intelligibility for the listener. Speakers have also been shown to engage in syntactic reduction-they are more likely to omit an optional complementizer (that) when the presence of a relative clause is highly predictable (Levy & Jaeger, 2007; Jaeger, 2010). And they choose longer forms (e.g., "information" vs. "info") when the word is less predictable from context (Mahowald et al., 2013). However, the extent to which these effects reflect the speaker modeling a rational comprehender performing noisy-channel inference as opposed to production-side pressures remains an open question (see e.g., Gahl et al., 2012).

4.6 Implications for language evolution & change

We have argued that all communication can be thought of as occurring across a noisy channel. Consequently, the language itself may be shaped by the noisy-channel inferences of comprehenders. For instance, new words or collocations may be introduced as a result of comprehenders making rational inferences about speaker utterances (e.g., inferring that speakers must have meant the more plausible-sounding collocation "intensive purposes" rather than "intents and purposes"; Reddy, 2009). On a longer timescale, the structure of the language may have become optimized for efficient communication over a noisy channel. On the phonetic level, it has been suggested that vowels in different languages are maximally dissimilar (e.g. Jakobson, 1968; Liljencrants & Lindblom, 1972; Flemming, 1997; J.-L. Schwartz et al., 1997) in order to make sure one vowel is unlikely to result in another vowel due to noise. Languages may also avoid using certain types of sounds if the sounds are highly susceptible to noise in the environment. For instance, it has been suggested that languages spoken in densely vegetated places are less likely to use obstruents (e.g., fricatives), since in these environments, the high-frequency components of a sound are likely to be absorbed by plants, resulting in confusion for the listener (Maddieson & Coupé, 2015). On the morphosyntactic level, it has been suggested that although subject-object-verb (SOV) is the most common word order in human language and is the preferred word order when developing a communication system (Givón, 1979; Goldin-Meadow et al., 2008; Gibson, Piantadosi, et al., 2013 , but cf. Struhl et al. (2017)), languages adopt the subject-verb-object (SVO) word order to ensure the information about agent and patient is still recoverable even if part of the sentence is deleted due to noise, particularly when the roles can be reversed (Gibson, Piantadosi, et al., 2013). Further, Shcherbakova et al. (2024) suggested that SOV languages achieve the same robustness to noise as SVO languages by developing case marking to encode the agent and the patient in a sentence. In addition, across word orders, languages tend to minimize the lengths of dependencies between words in a sentence (Ferrer i Cancho et al., 2004; Futrell et al., 2015), plausibly in order to minimize the probability that relevant context for processing a word is lost due to noisy memory (Futrell et al., 2020). In addition to work using Noisy Channel to explain language evolution in retrospect, artificial language learning and transmission studies provide experimental evidence that languages may evolve to maximize robustness to noise in communication (e.g., Stevens & Roberts, 2019).

4.7 Understanding the behaviors of Large Language Models

Large Language Models (LLMs) are artificial neural networks with many parameters that are trained on large quantities of text, typically using the training objective of next-word prediction. They have emerged as powerful tools for generating human-like language. Predictability estimates from LLMs are known to correlate with human reading times (e.g., Shain et al., 2024; Wilcox et al., 2023), while the internal activations of LLMs while processing a given sentence can predict the neural activity of humans processing the same sentence (e.g., Caucheteux et al., 2023; Goldstein et al., 2022; Schrimpf et al., 2021).

Given the success of LLMs as models of human language, it is natural to ask how robust LLMs are to the types of noise observed in human language, and whether LLMs exhibit signs of rational inference in a similar way to human comprehenders. LLMs are robust to certain word-level transformations, such as word order perturbations or single-word deletions (Sinha et al., 2021; J. O'Connor & Andreas, 2021). For instance, models pre-trained on sentences with randomly shuffled word order still achieved high accuracy on many tasks, illustrating how simply knowing the identity of the words in a sentence (and not their order) is often sufficient for predicting the relations of the words to each other (Sinha et al., 2021). This parallels how human comprehenders make non-literal interpretations that favor plausible meanings when the syntax has low prior probability (Liu et al., 2020; Bader & Meng, 2018; Meng & Bader, 2021; Mahowald et al., 2022; Poliak et al., 2023), as well as how the language network in the brain responds equally strongly to sentences with word order perturbations as it does to sentences without perturbations (up to a point) (Mollica et al., 2020). Similarly, the responses of LLMs to comprehension questions about sentences from Gibson, Bergen, & Piantadosi (2013) such as 'The mother gave the candle the daughter." mirror those of human comprehenders (Cai et al., 2024). LLMs are more likely to interpret a sentence non-literally when it expresses an implausible meaning, with a higher rate of non-literal interpretations when the observed utterances are consistent with a plausible meaning that has undergone a deletion error (as opposed to an insertion error). Moreover, Hu et al. (2026) have shown that the string probability assigned by an LLM depends on the likelihood of its underlying meaning and its distance from the closest well-formed string (e.g., in terms of character edits).

Thus, LLM probability can be understood as reflecting the output of production over a noisy channel.

The fact that (some) LLM behaviors reflect rational noisy-channel inference has far-reaching implications.

Like humans, LLMs are biased toward meanings and forms that have higher prior probability-sometimes with undesirable consequences (McCoy et al., 2023). For instance, LLMs are more likely to output correct answers to questions when the question has high a priori probability. The apparent tendency of LLMs to over-rely on the prior can lead models to very non-human-like performance, especially with adversarial test data, and can be seen as a limitation of these models and their training objective. Yet, as is the case with humans, in many (non-adversarial) situations, consideration of the prior may lead to appropriate inferences that allow the LLM to provide an answer that addresses the question that the user intended even if they expressed it incorrectly. One speculative possibility is that current LLMs over-rely on the prior because they do not have appropriate noise models. The work by Cai et al. (2024) suggests that some aspects of the noise model are qualitatively similar between LLMs and humans (e.g., that deletions are more likely than insertions), but describing the noise model in LLMs is an important avenue for future work.

Additionally, under which conditions (e.g., properties of training data, architecture, training objective) noisy-channel-like behavior emerges in LLMs is an open question. Studying when and how noisy-channel inference manifests in LLMs can thus shed light on the mechanisms that support it in humans.

5 Conclusion

We have argued that the remarkable successes, as well as the failures, of human language comprehension and processing can be understood in terms of rational (Bayesian) inference over input that is subject to noise.

Many seminal and recent findings lend support to this Noisy Channel account, and it has the potential to provide a unifying framework capturing disparate phenomena that, to date, have been explained by a fractionated landscape of psycholinguistic theories. Further, Noisy Channel makes novel, testable predictions.

Developing the Noisy Channel theory further and connecting it to neuroscience, evolutionary, and artificial intelligence perspectives, provides a productive avenue for advancing our understanding of human language comprehension and processing.

A Appendix: Situating the Noisy Channel theory in the landscape of psycholinguistic accounts

Some of the empirical findings reviewed above, in support of the Noisy Channel account, were originally reported in support of alternative accounts. Further, several psycholinguistic frameworks aim to explain similar phenomena to those which are the target of explanation for Noisy Channel theory. In what follows, we attempt to situate the Noisy Channel account within the space of existing psycholinguistic accounts, focusing on those which may overlap in scope with a Noisy Channel account.

For each alternative proposal that we consider, we first briefly review its primary commitments and empirical basis. We then aim to establish its position in terms of Marr's levels of analysis (Marr, 1982) and, when possible, sketch the conditions under which this proposal makes different predictions from the Noisy Channel proposal, suggesting potential avenues for adjudicating between the proposals or bridging across them (e.g., in cases where they represent distinct levels of analysis).

In many cases, the comparison is speculative. Many accounts have evolved over time and multiple variants exist in the literature with subtly different commitments. In such cases, we have focused on summarizing the current and agreed-upon aspects of the proposal, to the best of our knowledge. Some variants of a given account may not be adequately captured by our brief review. Similarly, the majority of accounts reviewed here, including the Noisy Channel account, have some flexibility, such that certain justifiable modifications could potentially change the predictions made by the account. Thus, reasonable readers may disagree about the circumstances under which we view a proposal as agreeing or disagreeing with the Noisy Channel account. This flexibility will likely diminish as these proposals are formalized and implemented. The goal of this comparison is not to argue that one or another account is right, but rather to spur further investigation and refinement of psycholinguistic theories.

A.1 The Dependency Locality Theory (DLT)

Processing a continuous stream of language input requires integrating new information with representations of past input to form meanings, thereby placing a load on a language comprehender's working memory, which has constraints on the capacity and duration of information storage. A.2 Cue-based Memory Retrieval accounts

Similar to the DLT, Cue-based Memory Retrieval accounts explain why language processing is easier for some structures and harder for others by appealing to the limitations of human memory (Lewis et al., 2006; Lewis & Vasishth, 2005; Vasishth et al., 2019). In brief, as a sentence unfolds, each word or phrase is encoded as a bundle of features (e.g., syntactic category, grammatical number, semantic features). In order to integrate an incoming word with the preceding parse, past bundles are retrieved from memory based on the overlap between their features and the cues generated by the incoming word (Figure 5B). Integration is typically more difficult when multiple possible bundles in memory share features with the retrieval cue (interference) or when the encoded representations in memory have decayed. Cue-based retrieval has been used to explain a number of processing phenomena including the difficulty of recursive structures, subjectverb agreement errors, and the interpretation of anaphora (Jäger et al., 2017 (Jäger et al., , 2020;; Lago et al., 2015; Lewis et al., 2006; Lewis & Vasishth, 2005; Van Dyke & McElree, 2006 , 2011; Wagers et al., 2009). For example, in a sentence like "The report that the doctor annoyed the patient was surprising," readers slow down at the verb "was" (Hahn et al., 2022). According to a memory retrieval interference explanation, "was" triggers the retrieval of candidate subjects (see Figure 5B). Multiple items in memory, like "doctor" and "patient," share features with the correct subject, "report," and may interfere with its retrieval causing processing delays.

In contrast, in a sentence like "The report that the doctor cured the patient was surprising," interference is reduced because "doctor" is retrieved as the subject of "cured" (due to its syntactic and semantic features) and no longer interferes with the retrieval of "report."

Noisy Channel, as instantiated by models of expectation-based real-time comprehension with lossy context representations (Futrell & Levy, 2017; Futrell et al., 2020; Hahn et al., 2022), can account for some of the same real-time processing phenomena. For example, "The report that the doctor annoyed the patient..." is one edit away from "The report by the doctor annoyed the patient..." which is a plausible and well-formed beginning of a sentence (Figure 5D). The comprehender with noisy memory may infer that they read the latter. "Was surprising" has low probability following this alternative context leading to processing delays when it is read. However, given a verb like cured, the alternative context with by is less probable ("The report by the doctor cured the patient..."). The reader is then more likely to infer that the context is the former (veridical) version leading to a higher probability for "was" and reduced processing difficulty.

The predictions of Noisy Channel and Cue-based Memory Retrieval accounts have not been exhaustively compared. The example above suggests that they will often make similar predictions, despite their fundamental differences. While Noisy Channel is a computational-level account concerned with the goal of the language processing system-to infer intended meanings in the presence of noise, Cue-based Memory Retrieval is an algorithmic-level account of how words are integrated with preceding structures in real time.

It may be possible to reconcile the two accounts by treating Cue-based Memory Retrieval as an algorithmic implementation of noisy-channel processing. However, in some cases, the accounts appear to make distinct predictions. Continuing the example above, the Noisy Channel account explains how processing difficulty is also dependent on the first noun. In a sentence that starts with "The fact that the doctor annoyed the patient ..., " the non-veridical version of the context (with "by" instead of "that") has lower probability ("The fact" is often followed by an embedded clause starting with "that"). As a result,"was" is more expected in context, leading to reduced processing difficulty relative to the version with "report." Neither cue-based memory retrieval nor DLT can account for this pattern, which is observed in reading times (Hahn et al., 2022), without modification.

A.3 Expectation-based approaches

The same linguistic string can often be parsed in multiple (syntactically licit) ways, and several psycholinguistic accounts have been interested in explaining how comprehenders resolve these ambiguities in real time.

According to multiple constraint satisfaction models (e.g., MacDonald et al., 1994; Tanenhaus et al., 1995), comprehenders consider all possible parses of the unfolding input in parallel and bring to bear all manner of multimodal information (e.g., syntactic, semantic, pragmatic, visual) to rank the parses in terms of which are most likely. Processing difficulty is incurred when multiple parses compete for selection or when the most expected parse becomes incompatible with the input and a reranking of parses takes place (e.g., in a garden path sentence).

Computational implementations of the expectation-based approach have relied on a probabilistic ranking of candidate parses (e.g., Jurafsky, 1996; Spivey & Tanenhaus, 1998). In particular, according to Surprisal theory (Hale, 2001; Levy, 2008a), as a sentence unfolds, each word elicits an update of the probabilities associated with candidate parses, which requires some reallocation of resources. The greater the change in the probability distribution, the greater the reallocation of resources. Processing difficulty for a given word results from the reallocation it elicits and is captured by the negative log probability of the incoming word, w i given the preceding context (Figure 5C), which includes the preceding words and any extra-sentential context, C (Eq. 6). Words that are higher in surprisal (i.e., less predictable) require a greater change to the probability distribution over candidate parses and are read more slowly, even when accounting for factors such as word length and frequency (Levy, 2008a; N. J. Smith & Levy, 2013; Shain et al., 2024).

In its original form, Surprisal theory does not address how the human language processing system deals with noise in the input. For example, it does not explain the successful recovery of intended meanings from ill-formed utterances or the non-veridical interpretation of well-formed utterances that nevertheless recovers a speaker's intended meaning. The theory assumes, as a simplification, that comprehenders have a perfect representation of both the language statistics that they have experienced over their lifetime and of the recent context. However, several extensions of Surprisal theory integrate it with Noisy Channel by assuming that the representation of the context is subject to noise (Levy, 2011; Futrell & Levy, 2017; Futrell et al., 2020; Hahn et al., 2022). Resource-rational lossy context surprisal captures reading behaviors that "clean-input" surprisal does not. For instance, Surprisal theory does not predict greater difficulty at "was" for "The report that the doctor annoyed the patient was..." than for "The fact that the doctor annoyed the patient was...," because a verb like "was" is an expected continuation following an accurately remembered "that"-clause, whereas according to lossy-context surprisal, the former is more susceptible to corruption (e.g., substitution of "that"; see Section A.2 for detailed explanation). Recent extensions of Surprisal theory take noise in the input into account by adjusting surprisal values based on the graded similarity of possible intended messages (Meister et al., 2024) or formalizing comprehension effort as the cost of inferring the latent intended message from the perceived input (rather than assuming a deterministic relationship between them) (Vigly et al., 2025).

A.4 Self-Organizing accounts

Self-Organizing accounts focus on how language comprehension deals with local coherences-syntactic parses that are grammatical given a subpart of the input, but not given the entire input. According to Self-Organizing accounts, incremental language processing proceeds in a bottom-up manner, with separate syntactic tree fragments or "treelets" attaching to each other to form an integrated parse of a sentence. Under the SOPARSE model of Tabor et al. (2004), for instance, each word in the input stream anchors a tree fragment with attachment sites that can be linked to other tree fragments. The strength of a link between two nodes is dependent on its current strength, the activation levels of its supporting treelets and a featurebased similarity match between the two nodes. In this model, reading times are modeled as the time it takes for fragments to stabilize under the above feedback mechanisms. In addition to the prediction that greater activation of incorrect treelets increases reading time, it is also predicted to increase the probability that a sentence is (mis)interpreted according to the activated incorrect treelet. Consider sentences such as 7, from Tabor et al. (2004): tossed takes longer to read in 7a than 7b, and 7a results in more interpretations that are not consistent with a grammatical understanding of the sentence (e.g., responding "yes" to the question "Did the player toss something?" ; Tabor et al. (2004)). In this pair of sentences, there is only one globally grammatical syntactic parse: player is both the patient of the matrix verb smiled and of the verb tossed from the reduced object relative clause. However, in 7a, a locally-coherent interpretation is also available: the substring "the player tossed the frisbee" may be parsed as an active sentence where player is the agent.

According to Self-Organizing approaches, the local coherence that is present in 7a but not in 7b causes comprehenders to read it slower and misintepret it more frequently. Evidence for local coherence effect have also been found using the visual world paradigm (Kamide & Kukona, 2018), graded number agreement (G. Smith et al., 2021), and lexical ambiguity resolution (Kukona et al., 2014).

(7) a. The coach smiled at the player tossed a frisbee. b. The coach smiled at the player who was tossed a frisbee.

The Noisy Channel theory predicts similar behavior, but provides a different, computational-level explanation. As described in earlier sections, when comprehenders read 7a and reach the word tossed, they face a low-probability construction (reduced object relative clause). This prompts them to consider potential corruptions to the input that they perceived that would make for a more probable construction (e.g. active transitive sentence). For example, if the perceived at is a corruption of and or as, that would mean that the intended message was a coordinated sentence like "The coach smiled as/and the player tossed the frisbee," which is more probable structurally than a reduced object relative clause (Levy et al., 2009). However, in other situations, Self-Organizing accounts may make different predictions from Noisy Channel. In its current version, it is not clear how generally the Self-Organizing account should be applied; whereas it successfully explains examples such as 7, it would also predict local coherence effects in cases where they are not observed.

For example, in the subject relative clause example in Figure 5a, the substring "the editor hoped for a story" forms a locally coherent structure (an active sentence with editor as subject), even though it is not licensed by the full sentence, where editor is the goal of the embedded verb sent. Yet, we are not familiar with work that finds that participants understand editor to be the agent of hoped, although a Self-Organizing account may predict that. In contrast, Noisy Channel would not predict non-literal interpretations, because there is no neighboring sentence where editor would be the agent of hoped. Given that Noisy Channel is described at the computational level, and Self-Organizing approaches are algorithmic, bottom-up theories, it may be possible, in principle, to view some form of a Self-organizing account as an algorithmic implementation of Noisy Channel, though this has not yet been explored.

A.5 The Competition model

The Competition Model was proposed as a mechanistic, functionalist account of language acquisition and processing (Bates & MacWhinney, 1982; Bates et al., 1989; MacWhinney et al., 1984; MacWhinney, 2022).

The model proposes that language processing is based on an online competition (or cooperation) between communicative functions or cues. A cue is defined as an information source present in the surface structure of utterances that allows the language user to link linguistic form with meaning or function, such as morphological, syntactic, prosodic, semantic, and pragmatic information. Cue availability is defined as the proportion of times the cue is available over the times a task needs to be performed (e.g., assigning thematic roles); Cue reliability is defined as the proportion of times the cue points to the correct interpretation over the total number of occurrences of the cue, in language usage as measured in corpora. Sometimes cues cooperate or converge by pointing to the same interpretation or production. Bates et al. (1989) identify three such cues: preverbal positioning, subject-verb agreement, and animacy. With these cues in mind, consider 8a, where there are two possible candidates for subjecthood/agenthood: the nouns boys and ball. Animacy, word order, and verbal inflection all suggest that the subject of the sentence is boys, and therefore the comprehender is predicted to consistently assign subjecthood to boys. However, sometimes, cues compete by pointing to conflicting interpretations. For example, in 8b, word order suggests that the subject is ball, whereas animacy and verbal inflection suggest that the subject is boys. In this case, the Competition Model predicts that comprehenders may assign subjecthood sometimes to ball and sometimes to boys, depending on the strength of the involved cues.

(8) a. The boys are chasing the ball.

b. The ball are chasing the boys.

Noisy Channel makes a similar prediction here: 8a will be interpreted literally (i.e., with boys as subject) nearly all the time, whereas 8b will sometimes be interpreted literally (i.e., with ball as subject) and sometimes non-literally (i.e., with boys as subject). Non-literal interpretations are likely because 8b is ungrammatical (and thus improbable a priori ), but a grammatical, plausible utterance is available assuming a single exchange error between boys and ball. Despite making similar predictions in the example above, the theories diverge in why they make these predictions. Whereas Noisy Channel assumes a generative production process that results in a probability distribution over possible strings reflecting the speaker's grammatical knowledge and what they are likely to talk about, as well as a generative noise process in which minimal corruptions tend to have higher probability, the competition model makes no direct appeal to grammar or errors and instead relies on probabilistic cues that vary in their strength. The theories also span different levels of analysis (Marr, 1982), where Noisy Channel, as described here, is a computational-level theory, and the Competition Model is relatively more algorithmic, proposing a process by which a comprehender may settle on an interpretation through cooperation and conflict between specific cues. The scope of the predictions of the Competition Model is narrower than that of Noisy Channel: it requires an established set of cues before it can be applied. This is a challenge for the Competition Model, as it is not currently worked out what should be considered a cue. In contrast, noisy-channel processing operates on strings and their probabilities, thus being applicable to any string given a generative model of the language and a noise model.

A.6 "Good Enough" Comprehension

The Good Enough theory posits that the goal of sentence processing is to achieve a correct understanding of the sentence quickly and efficiently (Ferreira et al., 2002; Ferreira & Patson, 2007; Karimi & Ferreira, 2016; Christianson et al., 2001; Ferreira et al., 2001). Inspired by dual-process models of decision-making (Kahneman et al., 1982), Good Enough proposes that sentence processing unfolds in two parallel streams: a heuristic stream and an algorithmic stream. The heuristic stream provides a fast analysis that is partially based on the grammar and relies on "quick-and-dirty" heuristics about the language (e.g., the first noun of a sentence in English is often the subject/agent; Ferreira, 2003) and the world (e.g., "a dog" is a plausible subject for the verb "bites"; Ferreira et al., 2002). The algorithmic stream interprets sentences slowly and systematically, according to the grammar of the language. When the heuristic stream arrives at an interpretation, processing may terminate before the algorithmic stream completes its analysis, which may cause the comprehender to adopt an interpretation of the sentence that is not licensed by the grammar (Ferreira et al., 2002; Dempsey et al., 2023). Consider Example 9 from Ferreira (2003), where a transitive sentence is varied to be active or passive (which is at odds with the agent-first heuristic), and plausible or implausible (which is at odds with the world-knowledge heuristic). Ferreira (2003) found that, whereas participants identified thematic roles correctly in sentences like 9a nearly all the time, they were more likely to make mistakes (relative to what is licensed by the grammar) in sentences that were either passive (9b) or implausible (9c), and even more likely to make mistakes in sentences that were both passive and implausible (9d). The explanation of this pattern, according to Good Enough, is that the more heuristics point to thematic role assignment that is not supported by the grammar, the more likely the heuristic stream is to end up with an interpretation that is not consistent with the grammar. In turn, comprehenders whose algorithmic stream did not complete processing may interpret the sentence according to the heuristic stream.

(9) a. The dog bit the man. b. The man was bitten by the dog.

While Example 9 is borrowed directly from Ferreira (2003), as reviewed above, very similar experiments were conducted within the Noisy Channel framework, using a variety of constructions and languages, finding similar results (Gibson, Bergen, & Piantadosi, 2013; Chen et al., 2023; Poppels & Levy, 2016; Poliak et al., 2025). However, the two theories take different perspectives on the same behavior: what Good Enough Processing sees as a failure to interpret the sentence according to the grammar, Noisy Channel sees as rational, non-literal interpretation that maximizes the probability of correctly interpreting the intention of the speaker. (While some have taken this to mean that Good Enough Comprehension must happen outside of conscious awareness and Noisy Channel requires conscious awareness, e.g., Paape (2023), Noisy Channel makes no commitments regarding the level of conscious awareness that it involves). Moreover, although the two theories share key predictions, not all predictions likely overlap. Noisy Channel predicts differences in the rates of non-literal interpretations based on the noise model, such that, for example, non-literal interpretations are more probable when the assumed corruption involves a deletion rather than an insertion (e.g., Gibson, Bergen, & Piantadosi, 2013). Good Enough, in its current form, does not appear to have a straightforward explanation for these effects. More broadly, for a thorough comparison of Noisy Channel and Good Enough, it will be important to clarify the full set of heuristics that contribute to Good Enough processing, in order to make predictions about how any arbitrary sentence may be interpreted.

In a recent attempt to unite Noisy Channel and Good Enough, Li & Futrell (2024) propose that the comprehender attempts to rationally infer the intended message using information that they continuously extract from the input. Early on in processing, few bits of information are extracted, leading to a "shallow" representation and an interpretation that appears to be heuristic-based. As processing continues and more bits are extracted, the representation becomes deeper, leading to interpretations that appear algorithm-based.

According to this model, the same noisy-channel inferences performed on different amounts of information, depending on whether processing was brief or more temporally extended, may give the impression of a dualprocess mechanism. As both theoretical accounts (Noisy Channel and Good Enough) are developed further, and their various assumptions and commitments are made more explicit, more precise predictions against which to test empirical data will help to clarify the relationship between these accounts.

A.7 Summary

Noisy Channel theory has a broad scope-aiming to explain all aspects of human language comprehension and processing. It overlaps with many existing proposals in terms of the phenomena it speaks to. Some accounts, such as the DLT, cue-based memory retrieval, Surprisal, and SOPARSE, are concerned primarily with explaining real-time processing difficulty. Via resource-rational lossy context surprisal, Noisy Channel appears to subsume DLT and Surprisal, providing a more accurate fit to human behavior than either theory independently. The predictions of resource-rational lossy context surprisal have not been directly pitted against those of Cue-based memory retrieval or Self-organizing accounts. Future work is needed to determine whether they are incompatible or whether one of these may offer an algorithmic-level account of the computations described by resource-rational lossy context surprisal.

The Competition model and Good Enough theory primarily aim to explain how comprehenders arrive at a final interpretation (though some versions have implications for real-time processing as well). In many cases, they appear to make the same predictions as Noisy Channel, making them difficult to tease apart.

One possibility, which bears further investigation, is that Noisy Channel captures the intuitions of both of these accounts and grounds them in probability theory, providing a more unified and generalizable account of language comprehension.
