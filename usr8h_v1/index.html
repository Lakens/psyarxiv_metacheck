description RATIONALE
Category learning, the process of identifying commonalities among experiences and grouping these experiences into equivalence classes, is a critical aspect of cognition. This ability allows for the split-second decision-making and information inference. Research suggests that category learning can lead to increased distance between the perceptual representations of stimuli in different categories and a respective decrease in distance within categories, qualities known respectively as acquired distinctiveness and acquired equivalence (Goldstone, 1994). These qualities have been identified in humans for a wide range of stimuli, including visual (Folstein et al., 2013), auditory (Ley et al., 2012), olfactory (Qu et al., 2016), and multisensory (Viganòa, Borghesani, & Piazza, 2021) stimuli.

This review will qualitatively analyze research on the changes to the neural representations of stimuli after category learning. Previous reviews have focused on visual stimuli (Ashby & Maddox, 2011; Seger & Miller, 2010; Zeithamova et al., 2019). This review distinguishes itself from these previous reviews by discussing stimuli of all modalities, allowing for greater generalization of findings. This review will limit its scope to studies analyzing the changes in the neural representations of stimuli that follow category learning. This will allow for direct answers to the research questions and for clear recommendations to be made for similar research in the future. To provide an understanding of the representational changes that occur following category learning, this review aims to provide an overview of evidence for such changes in a wide range of studies.

OBJECTIVES
The primary objective of this review is to assess the effects of category learning on the neural correlates of stimulus perception in neurotypical adults. Additionally, we aim to qualitatively compare these effects among different stimulus modalities.

This review seeks to answer the following questions:
1. How does category learning impact the neural representations of stimuli?
2. How does this impact compare for stimuli in different sensory modalities?

ELIGIBILITY CRITERIA
We will include studies using experimental or quasi-experimental designs comparing neural activity related to learned categories to neural activity unrelated to learned categories in neurotypical young or middle-aged adults. We will include studies that feature participants of different age ranges or health statuses if the outcomes for our population of interest are reported separately. Any task in which subjects learn to sort stimuli into pre-defined groups will be considered a category learning task. The category learning task in question must use a novel category structure. A category structure will be considered novel if the categories are not related to any real-world categories, such that participants are unlikely to have prior experience sorting stimuli into similar categories. The neural activity in question must be recorded during a task unrelated to categorization, such as passive viewing or discrimination. Our outcome of interest is neural activity related to stimulus perception and unrelated to active categorization.

Literature search results will be screened and managed using Covidence. Study screeners will use a checklist to verify inclusion requirements for each study. Data will be extracted from included studies using a data extraction form. We will pilot this form on a small subset of studies and test its reliability, adjusting until we have a data extraction form with high reliability. Extraction for each study will be completed independently by all reviewers. Any disagreements will be resolved via group discussion. If there are any missing data, we will attempt to contact study authors to obtain these data.

OUTCOMES AND PRIORITIZATION
Our main outcome of interest is neural activity related to stimulus category membership. Where possible, we will record accuracy and effect size measures. These values will be described and analyzed across subgroups.

Our secondary outcome of interest is the effectiveness of the category learning task, which we will measure by participants’ accuracy during a generalization test and during the final block of the learning task. These data will help us to determine if learning effectiveness is related to our main outcome of interest. These values will be described and analyzed across subgroups.

RISK OF BIAS IN INDIVIDUAL STUDIES
The Downs-Black tool assesses risk of bias in individual studies, assigning each study a value between 0 and 31 with higher values indicating higher quality (Downs & Black, 1998). This tool is helpful in the evaluation of healthcare interventions, but lacks sufficient generality for use in this review. We have thus modified some items in the Downs-Black tool for use in this review. This modified Downs-Black tool will be piloted and, if shown to be sufficiently reliable, will be used to assess risk of bias for all included studies. Should this tool lack sufficient reliability, the Joanna Briggs Institute critical appraisal checklist for quasi-experimental studies will instead be used to assess quality (Tufanaru et al., 2020).

In evaluating risk of bias, we will focus on results related to our main outcome of interest at the study level. Studies will be evaluated independently by all reviewers and disagreements will be discussed until consensus is reached. This outcome will be reported descriptively for all the studies included in this review as well as for each subgroup. Quantitative comparison between subgroups will be conducted where possible.

DATA SYNTHESIS
We will conduct a narrative synthesis. We will begin by summarizing the data gathered from different subgroups separately while exploring potential sources of heterogeneity among them. We will then outline theories to explain how category learning may shape the neural representations of stimuli. Then, we will develop a preliminary synthesis of the findings of included studies. We will look for relationships in the data within and between studies, examining variability in outcome measures, population characteristics, study designs, and other data items. We will end the narrative by assessing the robustness of the synthesis. We will describe the amount and quality of the evidence included and methods used to synthesize the evidence. We will not perform any meta-analysis due to the expected variability in study designs.

META-BIASES
Publication bias is known to be present in the neuroimaging literature (Jennings & Van Horn, 2012). We will acknowledge this as a limitation of the present review alongside any further limitations that arise during the review process.

CONFIDENCE IN CUMULATIVE EVIDENCE
Confidence in cumulative evidence will be determined by an assessment of the overall risk of bias for included studies. Other factors that might impact our confidence in cumulative evidence, such as inconsistency and effect size, will not be assessed due to the expected variability in study designs.

How Ethics Approval Check works

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). Abstract

Category learning is a fundamental mechanism underlying concept formation, yet the extent to which it induces stable neural representational changes across sensory modalities remains unclear. This preregistered systematic review assesses functional neuroimaging studies that examined whether experience with a novel category structure alters the brain's responses to the newly categorized stimuli, specifically outside of active categorization. Across 26 experiments meeting inclusion criteria, 81% reported learning-related changes in neural representations of stimuli following category acquisition. These effects were observed using fMRI, EEG, and MEG in a variety of paradigms. Eighteen visual and seven auditory studies tended to find these changes in higher-order cortical regions associated with object and speech processing. Other kinds of evidence were sparse. The single olfactory study found altered patterns of connectivity following learning. A minority of studies (5/26) found no evidence of taskindependent representational changes, with task-dependent or null results being reported. The current state of the literature suggests that category learning can produce durable changes in the neural representations of stimuli in the brain, with the loci and nature of these changes varying across modalities. Limitations of the existing literature and suggestions for future work are highlighted.

Neural representational changes after category learning across sensory modalities: A systematic review

Categorization is a fundamental cognitive process wherein distinct stimuli are sorted into functional equivalence classes (Kéri, 2003). It is through learning these functional equivalence classes that conceptual knowledge emerges (Sloutsky & Deng, 2019). For example, one might categorize the items in Figure 1 as Cups, an equivalence class for objects that can be used for the function of drinking water. In using functional equivalence classes, differences between items within a category are minimized or ignored, as all members can be used for similar purposes. After gaining extensive experience with a given category, category members that vary along category-irrelevant features may become more difficult to discriminate (Goldstone, 1994). This decreased sensitivity to task-irrelevant features is, in many cases, flexible.

We are capable of ignoring their differences and identifying both objects in Figure 1 as Cups, but we are not blind to those differences. However, some changes in sensitivity to different stimulus features may remain stable, persisting in tasks not only requiring active categorization. In this review, we discuss empirical research on the topic of stable representational changes of concepts and how they emerge through category learning.

Whereas many reviews of category learning focus on visual domains, our review will carefully consider various stimulus modalities. Our results will highlight how extensive category learning experience can lead to stable representational changes of learned concepts in the brain and identify areas for further investigation.

In examining how people group items together to form concepts, many cognitive models assume that concepts are represented in multi-dimensional psychological space. Concepts closer in psychological space tend to be more similar in terms of their features or dimensions, and functional equivalence classes are formed by partitioning this space (See Figure 1). When categorizing objects, some dimensions may be more relevant than others. For example, to differentiate between cups and mugs, you might look at the height of the vessel (mugs tend to be shorter) or, more importantly, the vessel's ability to tolerate heat. Cognitive models assume that relevant dimensions are given heavier consideration in categorization decisions, and that the multi-dimensional psychological space is stretched along these dimensions such that objects differing along relevant dimensions appear less similar to one another (Kruschke, 1992; Nosofsky, 1984 Nosofsky, , 1986)). In a similar vein, objects differing along irrelevant dimensions appear more similar to one another. These distortions have been referred to as acquired equivalence and acquired distinctiveness (Goldstone, 1994) or as dimensional modulation (Folstein et al., 2015). This quality has been observed in a wide range of stimuli including visual (Folstein et al., 2013), auditory (Ley et al., 2012), olfactory (Qu et al., 2016), and multisensory (Viganò et al., 2021) stimuli. The learning of functional equivalence classes gives rise to concept formation by distorting perceived distances among items in psychological space.

Dimensional modulation can be flexible or stable (Folstein et al., 2012; Gauthier & Palmeri, 2002; Goldstone, 1998; Palmeri & Gauthier, 2004; Richler & Palmeri, 2014).

Flexible dimensional modulation occurs when the importance of a relevant dimension shifts depending on task demands. Stable dimensional modulation occurs when the perceptual discriminability of category-(ir)relevant dimensions is modified long-term.

More specifically, stable dimensional modulation maintains similarity changes between concepts regardless of the current task. Faces are one class of objects that are learned early on in life and may be perceived categorically, resulting in lasting effects on an individual's representation of faces. Facial features have numerous dimensions that can be used to classify and distinguish one face from another. For example, facial expressions exist on a natural continuum, where expressions defined by clear categorical boundaries (e.g., a happy face versus a sad face) are easier to distinguish from one another than those within the same category, which may vary in intensity but share similar characteristics.

Another important example of stable representational changes can be seen in the perception of phonemes, the smallest units of sound in a language that can distinguish one word from another. For example, the sounds /p/ and /b/ in "pat" and "bat" are phonemes. Two distinct sounds might be close enough in perceptual space to be classified as the same phoneme and therefore treated as functionally equivalent.

Phoneme categories vary among cultures and languages, suggesting that they must be learned through experience. Categorical perception of phonemes occurs even when the listener is not currently engaged in speech comprehension or categorization. In other words, speech categories are perceived categorically after being learned, even in the absence of category-relevant task demands. The persistence of categorical perception of speech sounds, even when they are irrelevant to task demands, suggests a form of representational stability that endures across contexts.

With these representational changes being so well-established behaviourally, it is natural for researchers to ask how, if at all, these representational changes are manifested in the brain. Neural representations of concepts have been evaluated using functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI), electroencephalography (EEG), and magnetoencephalography (MEG). fMRI is a method of neuroimaging that detects changes in blood-oxygen-level-dependent (BOLD) signal (oxygenation concentration), a proxy for measuring activity or neural populations, and can be used to understand how the brain responds to or processes stimuli. Both EEG and MEG measure neuronal activity via electric fields at the scalp surface and can be used as a direct measure of the brain's neural activity in real-time. One common method of designing and examining EEG data is the event-related potential (ERP) technique. ERPs are waveforms produced following some event, such as a stimulus presentation. Waveforms produced within approximately the first 100ms post-event are generally associated with sensory processing while deeper information processing is associated with later waveforms (Sur & Sinha, 2009; Woodman, 2010).

Another method of designing EEG experiments is frequency tagging, wherein stimuli are presented at repetitive temporal frequencies, evoking steady state visually evoked potentials at those frequencies; their harmonics; and their intermodulation (IM) frequencies, the sums or differences of integer multiples of the tagged frequencies (Norcia et al., 2015; Vialatte et al., 2010). IM responses are thought to arise from neurons that nonlinearly combine the fundamental frequencies observed. IM responses serve as a marker of holistic integration of visual features that may result from learning.

More complex, higher-order IMs are involved in later levels of the visual processing hierarchy. In the present review, we consider fMRI and M/EEG studies to assess learning-related changes to the neural representation of concepts. As both fMRI and M/EEG allow researchers to record and analyze data in a variety of ways, Table 1 outlines some commonly used designs and analytic methods that studies included in the present review used to assess changes in neural representation.

The representational stability of phonemes and faces -natural categories that humans learn through unstructured, lifelong experiences -as a result of learning is wellestablished. Whether similar learning-induced changes occur for novel, artificially constructed categories remains less clear. There exists a large body of work which demonstrates flexible concept use. For example, Gauthier et al.'s (1998) Greeble study highlights how extensive training on a novel category of items can induce task-related changes in how the brain responds to these stimuli. One goal of this review is to understand how category learning impacts the neural representations of stimuli outside of active categorization demands, for example in tasks involving passive viewing, samedifferent discrimination, or oddball detection. Understanding how, if at all, flexible representations stabilize over time is essential to understanding how humans acquire and represent novel concept knowledge later in life, especially in intentional settings.

In this review, the main question we concern ourselves with, is whether stable dimension modulation can emerge in a laboratory setting in a set of artificially learned concepts. Further, where many reviews of the categorization literature focus on visual stimuli (e.g., Ashby & Maddox, 2011; Seger & Miller, 2010; Zeithamova et al., 2019), here we include stimuli from all sensory modalities, comparing and contrasting learning related neural changes across modalities. A review of this nature has not yet been conducted. We hope to synthesize current knowledge in this area, addressing questions of how category learning impacts the neural representations of stimuli, as well as how this impact compares for stimuli in different sensory modalities.

Methods

The protocol for this review was pre-registered at https://osf.io/5dmau. Deviations from this protocol were minimal and described throughout the methods.

Eligibility Criteria

Inclusion and exclusion criteria for this review were developed using the Population, Intervention, Comparison, Outcome, Study Design (PICOS) framework (Amir-Behghadami & Janati, 2020). This review includes studies using experimental or quasi-experimental designs comparing neural activity related to learned categories to neural activity unrelated to learned categories in neurotypical young or middle-aged adults. We included studies that featured participants of different age ranges or health statuses if the outcomes for our population of interest were reported separately. Any task in which subjects learned to sort stimuli into pre-defined groups, with more than one item per group, is considered a category learning task (e.g., this review does not consider single-item categories; see Nosofsky, 1986). In comparing learned vs.

unlearned categories, several study designs were considered. Within-participant pretest-posttest designs which measured and compare neural responses before and after learning were considered, as were within-participant designs wherein participants studied only one of many novel category sets. Between-participant designs could also fit the inclusion criteria by comparing representations between trained and untrained participants.

Studies were only included if the learning task involved a novel category structure. A category structure was considered novel if the categories did not map directly onto any real-world categories with which the participants would have experience, ensuring that participants learned throughout the study and did not rely on past experience to learn the category structures. Neural activity must have been recorded when participants were presented with stimuli during a task unrelated to categorization, such as passive viewing or same-different discrimination. Our outcome of interest is neural activity related to stimulus perception and unrelated to active categorization. For synthesis, the studies were grouped according to neuroimaging techniques and stimulus sensory modalities. Our pre-registered protocol included a grouping related to experimental designs, but this grouping was abandoned after achieving the final review sample, when the heterogeneity of methods became apparent.

Outcome Measures

Our outcome of interest in this review is functional neuroimaging data gathering during exposure to stimuli outside of an active categorization task. This includes functional neuroimaging methods such as EEG, MEG, fMRI, and fNIRS. Measurements for this outcome may be global or local activity levels, temporal activity patterns, or spatial activity patterns. Techniques like fMRI adaptation and event-related potentials were eligible.

Search Strategy

PsychINFO (Ovid), Embase (Ovid), Scopus, Web of Science, OSF Preprints, and Dissertations and Theses (ProQuest). PsycINFO, Embase, and Scopus were all initially searched on 14th February 2021. The search was conducted again on these and the remaining sources on 13th May 2021 and one final time on 8th March 2024. We also conducted backward citation tracking on several relevant reviews (Ashby & Maddox, 2011; Goldstone & Hendrickson, 2010; Grill-Spector & Weiner, 2014; Seger & Miller, 2010; Zeithamova et al., 2019). 2. Given the high methodological heterogeneity in the study of category learning, search terms were chosen to emphasize sensitivity over precision (Kumar et al., 2025).

Selection Process

The study selection process was managed using Covidence, a web-based platform that facilitates collaboration in the production of systematic reviews (Veritas Health Innovation, 2026). During title and abstract screening, two independent reviewers made decisions on whether a given study met review criteria. Conflicts between reviewers were resolved by discussion between the disagreeing reviewers in a group setting. During title and abstract screening, reviewers had access to a detailed checklist of inclusion criteria and were instructed to advance a paper to full-text review if there were no obvious violations of the inclusion criteria. These inclusion criteria included the population of interest, a category learning task being conducted, the relevant comparison groups being included, functional neuroimaging, and an experimental or quasi-experimental design as described above. During full-text review, two independent reviewers screened each study using the same inclusion criteria as the title and abstract screening, as well as one additional criterion: the reporting of a measure of representational change in the brain. We were interested in neural activity differences between learned and unlearned categories -any study that did not include this comparison was excluded. See the Appendix for the PRISMA flowchart.

Data Collection and Management

Data were extracted from included studies using a data extraction spreadsheet that was piloted on a subset of the included studies. Data extraction was completed independently by the first two authors, and mismatched data items were resolved via discussion. Data items from the preregistered protocol were collected, including: publication year, journal, initial sample size, final sample size, participant ages, gender distribution of sample, number of participants lost, reasons for lost participants, country of data collection, descriptions of the stimuli, descriptions of the category structure, details of the learning task (including duration of learning), task performed during neuroimaging, study design, and outcome.

Risk of Bias Assessment

Our pre-registered protocol indicated a risk of bias assessment plan that was not executed due to the heterogeneity of the included studies. Publication bias is known to exist in the neuroimaging literature (Jennings & Van Horn, 2012). We acknowledge the lack of risk of bias assessment or meta-bias assessment as a limitation of this review.

Synthesis Measures

Outcome measures were synthesized narratively. The experimental designs, analytic methods, and outcomes for each study were reported. Trends were highlighted as appropriate. Studies were grouped according to their neuroimaging methods and stimulus modalities, and differences and similarities between groups were described. Sources of heterogeneity that may have impacted results, including variability in outcome measures, population characteristics, study designs, and other data items, will also be reported. We will then connect existing results to theories which might explain how category learning shapes the neural representations of stimuli. We did not perform any meta-analysis due to the variability in study designs and outcome measures.

Results

In this review, we identified and evaluated empirical publications that address whether the neural representations of newly learned artificial categories change reliably even during tasks that did not require explicit categorization, including passive viewing, same-different discrimination, and target detection. We consider learning-related changes in neural representation that correspond to measures associated with comparing the representations pre-and post-test (i.e., measure and compare neural responses before and after learning), as well as to trained versus untrained stimuli (i.e., stimuli used during category learning versus entirely new stimulus categories). Across the 26 identified experiments meeting our inclusion criteria, 21 (81%) reported that category learning induced stable changes in the way that the brain responded to nowcategorized stimuli. These representational changes took the form of alterations to functional connectivity, patterns of activation, activation magnitude, and release from adaptation. As a reminder, Table 1 contains brief descriptions of the methodologies included in this review. New areas may have been recruited in the processing of stimuli, connectivity may have shifted, or neural indices of attention may have been adjusted.

We summarize our core findings below.

Generally, the experiments included in this review reported evidence of successful category learning in participants. This provides a behavioural basis of the ability to learn and form conceptual knowledge of these artificial categories. Often, these increases in categorization accuracy over time were observed for both trained and untrained exemplars from the trained categories, suggesting that once conceptual knowledge is acquired, people are readily able to generalize their knowledge to perceptually similar stimuli. However, they are less able to do so for untrained categories, as they have no knowledge of the conceptual structure. Additionally, few studies showed behavioural evidence of flexible dimensional modulation.

Another key question of this review is whether the nature of representational changes differs across sensory modalities. Of the included studies, 18/26 (69%) used visual stimuli, 7/26 (27%) used auditory stimuli, 1/26 (4%) used olfactory stimuli, and 1/26 (4%) used haptic stimuli. One of these studies used stimuli that had both haptic and visual features. More detailed findings are described below. Briefly, visual studies tended to identify changes in higher-order visual cortex associated with object processing. Auditory studies tended to produce changes in the auditory cortex and related frontal regions, often implicated in speech processing. Of the studies that found no representational changes, two were auditory and three were visual. Of those studies that identified changes, it seems that changes tended to concentrate in higher-level processing regions, rather than regions involved in low-level perceptual processes.

Changes following haptic and olfactory category learning are described below.

Electrophysiological Studies

Six (23%) of the 26 included experiments used electrophysiological imaging methods (EEG, MEG), and each of these studies found a stable change in representation due to learning. Four (67%) of these 6 studies examined event-related potentials (ERPs; Folstein & Monfared, 2019; Folstein et al., 2017; Kaan et al., 2008 Kaan et al., , 2013)). One study examined areal mean signals (AMS) using MEG (Hultén et al., 2009) and one used a frequency tagging paradigm to examine intermodulation frequency (IM) responses (Vergeer et al., 2018). All electrophysiological studies included only examined a single stimulus modality, four visual (67%; Folstein & Monfared, 2019; Folstein et al., 2017; Hultén et al., 2009; Vergeer et al., 2018) and two auditory (Kaan et al., 2008 (Kaan et al., , 2013)). (2017) examined whether extended category training would elicit changes in the N250 and the Selection Negativity (SN). These visual ERP components are posterolateral negativities between 200 and 400ms post-stimulus onset, thought to reflect the formation of new concept representations and attentional modulation to features of categorized objects respectively. Their key question was whether category learning alters attentional modulation of learned visual stimuli, leads to the creation of new representations, or both. Visual stimuli were split into trained and untrained sets, e.g., horses vs. bugs, which were visually distinct from each other. Participants engaged in feedback-based category training on the trained set, learning to sort the stimuli into one of two categories. Then, participants completed a target detection task for trained and untrained stimuli while connected to the EEG. N250 components were more pronounced for trained stimuli, suggesting that new representations for the categorized stimuli were formed after training. Similarly, they found more pronounced SN when target features were presented, indicating attentional modulation. In summary, this study suggests that attentional modulation and representational change both occurred as a result of learning outside of explicit categorization demands. In a direct follow-up to Folstein et al. (2017), Folstein and Monfared (2019) examined how extensive feedbackbased category training would impact neural correlates of selective attention and, independently, the neural representations of visually presented items. This withinparticipants study found that for trained stimulus sets, participants had earlier onset and longer duration for their SN, indicating earlier and more sustained attention directed at relevant stimulus features. Visual ERP evidence indicates that category learning results in lasting changes to brain representations including the formation of new representations for newly categorized stimuli as well as earlier, more sustained, and more prominent attention modulation.

Kaan and colleagues ran an experiment wherein native Thai speakers, Mandarin Chinese speakers (non-Thai speakers with tonal language experience), and English speakers (non-Thai speakers without tonal language experience) were trained on Thai tone categories (Kaan et al., 2008 (Kaan et al., , 2013)). None of the English speakers had experience with any tonal languages and none of the Mandarin Chinese speakers had any experience with tonal languages aside from their native language. Despite the Thai tone categories being real-world categories, they were completely novel to the English-and Chinese-speaking participants. Before and after training, participants watched a movie during an EEG oddball paradigm. In their analyses published in 2008, the Chinese and English speakers showed a left-lateralized late negativity for high-rising deviants, indicating that these speakers processed the high-rising contours differently from native Thai speakers. As lateralization may shift from left to right when listeners become proficient in detecting the contours, the categorization training in this experiment was not sufficient to make the non-Thai speakers process the high-rising contours in a similar manner to Thai speakers. However, for low-falling tones, the mismatch negativity (MMN) was higher in English speakers than Chinese speakers before learning, indicating that the English speakers had higher sensitivity to the differences among the tones; these differences in pre-training MMN corresponded to behavioural performance.

There were no group differences in MMN for low-falling tones post-training, suggesting the English speakers became more like native tone-language speakers post-training.

Although this study found similar training-induced changes in ERP measures in all three groups of participants, the English group became less distinct from the other groups post-training. In 2013, the authors examined representational changes to neural networks associated with learning Thai lexical tones. They found that after category training, native-English speakers showed an increase in gamma-band power and stronger alpha-band synchrony across EEG channels after training compared to speakers of languages with tonal distinctions. Increases in gamma-band power have been associated with learning and formation of new neural representations of stimuli in memory. Additionally, alpha and gamma-band activity were negatively correlated in the native-English speakers, further reflecting the formation of new lexical tone categories.

In summary, auditory ERP evidence indicates that category learning results in lasting changes to brain representations including the formation of new representations for newly categorized stimuli in English speakers. completed a picture naming task in which they were shown pictures of unfamiliar, real objects along with their name and function. In hierarchical theories of vision, the structural units or basic features of visual input are combined at lower levels to create and inform holistic shape representations at higher levels of visual processing. One experiment by Vergeer et al. (2018) used frequency tagging to assess learning-induced changes to holistic shape representations. Before and after training, stimuli were presented one at a time onscreen while paying attention for any colour changes on the fixation cross. Vergeer et al. Lack of Significant Representational Change

Four experiments finding no representational change used an event-related fMRI rapid adaptation technique (fMRI-RA). The degree of adaptation depends on the similarity between two stimuli. Thus, a change in adaptation between pre-and postcategory learning would signify a change in perceived similarity between the items. This makes it possible to assess whether learning induced changes resulted in dimensional modulation, such as between-category expansion (increased sensitivity to relevant dimensions) and within-category compression (a loss in sensitivity to dimensions not relevant to categorization). Jiang et al. (2007) did exactly this using visual car morph stimuli, they did not find any category-related changes in adaptation between pre-and post-training in the lateral occipital cortex (LOC), typically associated with object recognition.

In an experiment by Wu et al. (2020), participants were trained to classify distortions of visual prototypes into one of two categories. Prototype model-based representational similarity analysis was conducted before and after training based on fMRI data captured during passive viewing, pitch discrimination, and active categorization of the stimuli. The prototype model accounted well for post-learning activation during the task involving categorization, but not during the non-categorization tasks, suggesting that the enhancement of category-specific information was taskdependent. This experiment also did not find the expansion in between-category distances that should be expected of dimensional modulation theory. As noted by the authors, these results are not inconsistent with this theory but are instead inconclusive.

Two similar experiments using auditory stimuli yielded similar results in the left superior temporal gyrus, a region associated with auditory processing (Jiang et al., 2018). Participants were scanned in an event-related fMRI-RA paradigm, in which they were presented with pairs of novel morphed auditory monkey call exemplars varying in acoustic similarity. Using this method, the authors compared pairs of exemplars within the same category and pairs of exemplars from different categories before and after training to see if stimuli from different categories would evoke a larger neural response.

They found that category training induced sharper neural tunings in response to specific stimuli (stimulus identity), but no category-selectivity. Multi-voxel pattern analysis was also used to examine category-selectivity in the auditory cortex after training. However, the training-related representational changes found in the auditory cortex were task dependent. Only in tasks explicitly involving categorization were representational changes found. Overall, this subset of experiments provides evidence that stable dimensional modulation does not necessarily occur following the learning of novel categories.

Nineteen out of the 20 BOLD studies (95%) included in this review conducted experiments in unisensory stimulus modalities. Thirteen experiments (65%) were visual, five were auditory (25%), and one was olfactory (5%). Example stimulus sets from studies included in this review can be found in Figure 2.

Regions of interest in visual concept learning studies included regions of high-level visual cortex such as LOC, ventral temporal cortex, and ventral occipital temporal cortex. The ventral visual pathway has been found to play a role in object processing. Brants et al. (2016) explored whether learning visual categories was associated with changes in selectivity for behaviourally relevant stimulus features. In their task, participants were trained on two out of three sets of objects in either a categorization task or a differentiation task. Neural selectivity was assessed before and after learning. During scanning, participants completed a colour change detection task that did not relate to the learning paradigms or the shape properties that were relevant during learning. They found increased selectivity in LOC for trained items compared to untrained items after learning, suggesting that neural selectivity was enhanced for relevant stimulus features after training within a task that was unrelated to categorization. No training effects were found in other cortical regions such as fusiform face area or early visual areas, suggesting these effects were specific to higher-order areas known to play a role in object processing and recognition.

Other studies that found increased neural selectivity in LOC for visually trained compared to untrained objects include Gillebert et al. (2009) and van der Linden et al. (2008). Gillebert et al. (2009) found evidence of changes in the neural representations of artificial stimuli that persisted in a task unrelated to categorization in the LOC, but not in early visual areas or left inferior frontal cortex. This further suggests that the persisting neural selectivity was localized to regions involved in object and shape recognition. Additionally, Gillebert et al. (2009) found no evidence of learning-related neural changes to the relevant or irrelevant category dimensions. Despite changes to neural selectivity, there was no evidence of dimensional modulation in LOC or other areas, suggesting that dimensional modulation is not necessary for increased selectivity. Other research has found representational changes in the form of reorganization of activity patterns to stimuli in relation to learned social values associated with face stimuli (Familiar, 2021). After learning, faces associated with similar social values evoked more similar patterns of activity in the left ventro-medial anterior temporal lobe (vmATL) as revealed by multi-voxel pattern analysis. Importantly, this shift persisted in a task outside of explicit categorization.

In the visual domain, evidence for acquired distinctiveness of perceptual dimensions (e.g., increased perceptual discriminability of relevant features) has been demonstrated by few researchers (Brants et al., 2016; Folstein et al., 2013; van der Linden et al., 2008). However, using similar methods, other studies found no evidence of acquired distinctiveness for the relevant dimension after learning in a task not requiring categorization (Gillebert et al., 2009; Jiang et al., 2007; van der Linden et al., 2010). None of the studies examining visual perceptual changes showed evidence of acquired equivalence in regions of visual cortex (Folstein et al., 2013; Gillebert et al., 2009; van der Linden et al., 2008 van der Linden et al., , 2010)), however many of them also did not directly test for it (Brants et al., 2016; Clarke et al., 2016; Familiar, 2021; Luo et al., 2024; Op de Beeck et al., 2006; Wong et al., 2009; Wu et al., 2020).

Of the five experiments examining auditory categorization using fMRI, three (60%) found evidence of representational change occurring after learning (Kaan et al., 2013; Keidel, 2007; Ley et al., 2012; Luthra et al., 2019) and two (40%) did not (Jiang et al., 2018 experiments 1 & 2). Regions of interest in these studies included regions of auditory cortex such as superior temporal gyrus and superior temporal sulcus, which have been associated with processing and discriminating fine acoustic differences between complex sounds. Additionally, frontal regions were investigated including the inferior, middle, and superior frontal gyri, which have all been shown to be sensitive to acoustic or phonetic category boundaries and active during the acquisition of new speech categories.

Evidence of representational changes were observed through decreased activation in frontal ROIs after learning (Luthra et al., 2019), increased activation when listening to stimuli from different categories compared to stimuli from the same category (Luthra et al., 2019) and successful decoding of distinct neural responses to categories during passive listening (Ley et al., 2012). Two studies found evidence for representational changes relating to acquired distinctiveness of category dimensions in auditory cortex (Ley et al., 2012; Luthra et al., 2019). Ley et al. (2012) trained participants to discriminate novel auditory stimuli from two different categories. Stimuli could be categorized according to pitch; one category of sounds belonged to the "low pitch" category and the other to "high pitch". Participants were scanned before and after training during a passive listening task. Using a classifier to define prototypical responses to low pitch sounds compared to high pitch sounds, they compared the pretraining and post-training neural responses during passive listening. They found that before learning, the classifier was unable to distinguish between the two sound categories in auditory cortex, but after category learning, neural response patterns for sound classes could be discriminated along the trained dimension. Their results provide evidence for specific representational changes in auditory cortex after learning in a task unrelated to categorization of those stimuli.

Only one (4%) study examined changes in neural representations after learning olfactory categories. Howard et al. (2016) found that learning rewards associated with odours alters sensory-reward networks without altering local cortex representations. In their olfactory paradigm, participants were classically conditioned with a set of four odour stimuli (two minty and two citrus). One minty odour and one citrus odour were selected to be paired with a monetary reward, while the other two odours were paired with no reward. Participants were tested on their ability to acquire new information (e.g., distinguish which odour from the category was paired with the reward), while also maintaining their original perceptual category representations (e.g., minty versus citrus).

Unlike the results of the other unisensory stimulus modality experiments included in this review, the representational changes due to learning were not found in PC and OFC, regions typically found to play a role in odour processing and category and identity recognition. Instead, changes in the connectivity between ROIs, such as the vmPFC and OFC were observed after participants learned to group the stimuli into their functional reward categories (e.g., rewarded vs. unrewarded). While natural perceptual odour category, odour identity, and reward value were coded in PC, OFC, and vmPFC respectively, learning-related changes were observed through increased connectivity between OFC and vmPFC. Compared to other stimulus modalities in which learning resulted in reorganization of category representations within a given ROI, no learningrelated changes were found within the PC or OFC. Instead, representational changes appeared through increased functional connectivity between OFC (identity-coding) and vmPFC (value-coding), which predicted subjective increase in odour reward value.

Increased connectivity between these regions may reflect a mechanism by which reward value categories can be updated, while keeping stimulus-specific identity intact.

However, olfactory category learning has seldom been studies and thus no strong conclusions can be made.

Significant Representational Change: Multisensory

Only one of the reviewed studies (5%) assessed changes in the neural representations of concepts spanning multi-sensory stimulus modalities. Weisberg et al. (2007) assessed visual and haptic modalities. Viewing an object does not only activate visual properties about that object, but also information about how an object can be used or manipulated which is stored in the sensory and motor systems (Barsalou, 1999; Martin, 1998; Weisberg et al., 2007). To assess representational change, participants performed a visual object-matching task before and after learning the functions of novel objects. Following training, neural activity increased in left-lateralized regions that have been shown to be associated with motion and manipulation, including the posterior middle temporal gyrus, intraparietal sulcus, and premotor cortex. Within these structures, the anterior sub-regions showed heightened neural tunings to trained compared to untrained objects. Whereas posterior lateral regions and the ventral temporal cortex displayed a generalized effect, in which changes to neural tunings were equivalent for both trained and untrained items. These findings echo previous animal studies showing that the ventral premotor cortex and anterior regions of the intraparietal sulcus respond when monkeys grasp or view objects they have had experience manipulating (Jeannerod et al., 1995). Weisberg et al. (2007) demonstrate that when objects are learned through multi-sensory associations, the neural representations of those objects can be reorganized across modality-specific and higher-order cortical regions. Learning motoric information recruits parietal and premotor regions typically involved in object manipulation, indicating that knowledge of an object's functional use can become ingrained in its neural representation. In other words, experiential learning can flexibly reshape concept representations to incorporate newly acquired information spanning multiple stimulus modalities and these changes can be observed in a task that is unrelated to categorizing the objects themselves. However, only one of the 20 BOLD studies examined representational changes for concepts spanning multisensory stimulus modalities, and thus more research is needed to understand whether learningrelated changes extend to other multi-sensory modalities.

Limitations of Existing Work

This review was designed with comparisons among stimulus modalities in mind.

Visual stimuli tended to dominate the identified experiments, appearing in the majority (18/26). Auditory stimuli were used in a smaller portion of experiments (7/26). Olfaction (1) and touch (1) were each the subject of one experiment, and no gustatory experiments were identified. It thus remains unclear if and how stable representational changes emerge following extensive learning experience in different sensory modalities.

Additionally, all but one of the experiments included in this review involved learning in a unisensory domain. As humans learn naturalistic concepts, information from varying sensory domains is integrated (Fetsch & Noppeney, 2023). The relative lack of multisensory research on this topic leaves open the question of how applicable these findings are to real-world concept acquisition. Despite this being a central concern to the review, this review is unable to draw strong conclusions about how category learning in different sensory modalities affects the brain due to the high homogeneity of sensory domains currently represented in the literature.

Discussion

Category learning is a cognitive process that is essential to concept formation, a fundamental cognitive skill that underlies how people gather meaning from the world (Sloutsky & Deng, 2019). Eleanor Rosch argued that people learn categories and concepts for the sake of cognitive economy, the efficient use of cognitive resources wherein items are not differentiated from each other if differentiation is "irrelevant for the purposes at hand" (Rosch, 1975, p. 384). Research since then in cognitive psychology and cognitive neuroscience has proposed different theories and models for how the mind carries out this process of learning and representing categories and concepts (Ashby & Maddox, 2011; Barrett & Miller, 2026; Kruschke, 1992; Minda et al., 2024).

We wanted to know whether category learning results in stable changes in how the brain responds to stimuli, and if this differs across sensory modalities. Our systematic review of the literature suggests that extensive experience with grouping items into categories can lead to stable neural changes that extend beyond active categorization, similar to what is seen in real-world examples like the categorical perception of phonemes and faces. Electrophysiological studies suggest that category learning experience results in sustained and focused attention on category-relevant features, holistic integration of stimulus features, and deeper processing of stimuli. Support was found for the emergence of novel representations for newly categorized items in both the visual and auditory domains. BOLD studies reveal changes in neural tunings in high-level cortex involved in representing concepts and in connectivity between regions.

In the visual and auditory domains, the trends indicated that the stable changes emerged in higher-order brain regions rather than primary sensory regions, similar to conceptual or semantic information. Together, electrophysiological and fMRI results suggest that category learning leads to durable changes to representational systems in the brain, biasing processing toward category-relevant information even beyond explicit categorization demands. These lasting changes may shape the way stimuli are processed and attended to. Our findings are consistent with the idea that these lasting changes may be the building blocks of concept formation and categorical perception.

The kinds of lasting changes that we have explored in this review explain many phenomena observed when people learn sets of novel categories, but how do these findings explain the complexities and intricacies seen in real-world concepts? For example, many real-world concepts and categories overlap and seem to nest within hierarchical structures. When seeing a bird, one might classify it according to its species (e.g. "belted kingfisher"), as simply a "bird", or as an "animal." The classification choice might depend on one's level of expertise or momentary interest in that particular bird (Tanaka & Taylor, 1991). For hierarchical categories, it is reasonable to expect that representational neural changes would exist at the observer's most commonly used level of classification, but this possibility has not yet been empirically examined.

There are also many examples of cross classifications in which items occupy multiple unrelated categories, which may be taxonomic or script based (Ross & Murphy, 1999). A domestic cat fits very neatly into the Feline category but also the House Pet category. A bagel is simultaneously a member of the Breakfast Food category-a script category-and the Breads category-a taxonomic category. In these circumstances, we believe it is possible that flexible representations are deployed, as the same features that distinguish cats and dogs must be underemphasized when looking at the House Pet category. There is behavioural evidence of the simultaneous use of multiple categories (Murphy & Ross, 2010), as well as children's flexible use of multiple category structures (García Coni et al., 2019). The ability to flexibly deploy conceptual knowledge has been related to the development of executive control in children (Blaye et al., 2006), so all conceptual knowledge may not be stable. Based on this current review of the literature, however, a conclusion cannot be drawn about the presence of stable representational changes to reflect these kinds of overlapping category structures.

Given the importance of the flexible deployment of category knowledge, this area warrants further investigation.

Limitations of this Review

We undertook the current review to help answer a general question: does category learning induce lasting changes in the brain? We looked specifically at preand post-learning dimensional modulation and changes in how stimuli are processed and perceived. The work we reviewed allowed us to speculate that category learning can result in stable representational changes at the neural level, but we are still limited to what conclusions we can draw about a representation. The term "representation" remains somewhat ill-defined in cognitive science and is used widely without consensus in the field, an issue that becomes further problematic when one is considering representational change. deCharms and Zador (2000) define a neural representation as a message that uses rules to carry information to perform a function. Another way to think of neural representations is that they are characterized by two properties: content and function. Content is the information carried in a representation, such as sensory input, and function is the subsequent resulting cognitive process or behaviour (Shea, 2018 (Shea, /2020)). These descriptions of neural representations are quite broad. Some may disagree that some of the analyses included in this review truly constitute assessments of "representation." We believe that our broad definition ensures thorough coverage of the existing literature on this topic, as changes to patterns of connectivity or neural indices of attention modulation are important to document, regardless of whether the reader believes that these constitute "representational" changes. Our chosen definition of representation is broad and appropriate for our research questions, but we acknowledge that our way of defining representation may not be widely agreed upon.

We encourage further work examining what may or may not constitute a neural representation.

The experiments included in the present review had a high amount of heterogeneity in terms of methods and experimental paradigms. This is beneficial in the sense that representational change has been examined using a variety of training methods and neuroimaging techniques, but the minimal amount of similarities between experiments makes it difficult to draw strong conclusions about which factors might make lasting representational change more or less likely, or which might change the nature of that representational change.

Generally, category learning is a very paradigm-driven area of research, with the methodology across studies being highly comparable and with emergent design being easily observable across experiments. However, experiments included in this review were very heterogeneous with respect to the stimuli used, the amount of training during the categorization task, the auxiliary tasks measuring representational changes outside of categorization, as well as the experimental paradigms and analytical methods themselves. While the reviewed studies add interesting findings to this body of literature, the amount of heterogeneity between studies should be taken into consideration when interpreting results and conclusions.

Conclusion

The present review examined whether category learning induces stable representational changes beyond tasks involving explicit categorization, and whether these changes occurred across stimulus modalities. We found evidence from several experiments that it is possible for category learning to induce stable representational changes in the brain. The nature of these changes seems to differ depending on the stimulus modality examined.
