Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. Abstract

Mother-infant affective synchrony, the moment-to-moment matching of mothers' and infants' emotions, is usually studied within the dyad when alone, yet everyday caregiving unfolds across varied social contexts. This study examined how momentary close company (partner, family member, or friend) relates to maternal affect and synchrony. Between September 2022 and September 2025, 253 mothers of infants aged 3-24 months (M = 9.4 months; 52.6% female; 56.7% Hispanic or Latinx) in Los Angeles, USA, rated their own and their infant's affect up to six times daily for up to 14 days. Maternal and infant affect covaried at the same moment (negative β = 0.24; positive β = 0.28). Mothers reported higher positive and lower negative affect with a close companion or infant than alone or with distant company (acquaintances, coworkers, strangers). Negative synchrony was weaker with a close companion present (β = -0.05), although close company was unrelated to infant distress. Close company was also linked to higher infant happiness (β = 0.05) but not positive synchrony. Supplementary analyses found no lagged negative-affect effects and no partner-versus-family difference in the moderation. Maternal distress symptoms predicted higher chronic negative affect and stronger negative-affect inertia but not within-dyad dynamics. Early coregulation may extend beyond the dyad to the wider caregiving network.

Social Context Moderates Mother-Infant Affective Synchrony in Everyday Life: An Ecological Momentary Assessment Study

The mother-infant dyad is central to theories of early emotional development, such as the mutual regulation model (Tronick, 1989) and microanalytic accounts of attachment (Beebe et al., 2010), because the emotional exchange between mother and infant is among the most frequent and developmentally consequential interactions of early life. Behavioral, physiological, and neuroimaging work has shown that mothers and infants coordinate their affect, gaze, vocalization, and autonomic arousal in real time, producing the moment-to-moment matching of behavioral and physiological signals, a process called biobehavioral synchrony (Feldman, 2007 (Feldman, , 2012)). This coordination supports the infant's emerging capacity to self-regulate (Kopp, 1989).

When caregivers read and respond to infant signals, infants come to experience their internal states as coherent and manageable (Beebe et al., 2010; Tronick, 1989). Synchrony of positive affect, such as shared smiles and mutual gaze, reflects the rewarding basis of secure attachment and predicts better self-regulation later in development (Atzil & Gendron, 2017; Feldman, 2017; Feldman et al., 1999). Synchrony of negative affect is more ambiguous: it can reflect responsiveness to distress in well-functioning dyads, but in the context of maternal psychopathology, it more often marks a vulnerability pathway through which maternal low mood and infant distress become coupled (Field et al., 1990; Granat et al., 2017; Smith et al., 2022).

Such coupling is typically bidirectional, as parents both match and influence their infants' arousal from moment to moment (Wass et al., 2019).

Because real-time coordination of affect and arousal between mother and infant is central to early emotional development, factors that affect it have important developmental implications (Lan et al., 2024), and maternal distress is a particularly consequential one (Stein et al., 2014).

Maternal distress, conceptualized here as symptoms of depression, anxiety, and chronic stress, perturbs not only mothers' average mood but also the contingency, warmth, and timing of their interactions with infants, yielding withdrawn or intrusive interactional styles and disrupted synchrony (Field, 2010; Granat et al., 2017). At the population level, these perturbations translate into increases in offspring risk for behavioral and emotional problems (Stein et al., 2014). For example, maternal depression is associated with higher internalizing, externalizing, and general psychopathology and with lower positive affect in children (Goodman et al., 2011). This pattern is consistent with an integrative developmental model in which maternal depression confers risk through multiple, partially independent mechanisms, including the child's own heightened negative affectivity (Goodman & Gotlib, 1999; Xu et al., 2026). These associations leave substantial variance to be explained by moderators of when, how, and for whom maternal distress carries forward into the moment-to-moment interactions between mother and infant, a central question in models of the intergenerational transmission of risk (Goodman & Gotlib, 1999).

Maternal distress may also appear in how mothers' own affect changes over time, not only in the dyad. Beyond mean levels, moment-to-moment affect dynamics contain important information about psychological functioning (Houben et al., 2015; Kuppens et al., 2010). One such dynamic is emotional inertia, the within-person carry-over of an affective state from one moment to the next, which is elevated among individuals with low self-esteem and depressive symptoms and prospectively predicts the onset of major depression (Kuppens et al., 2010 (Kuppens et al., , 2012)).

Meta-analytic findings link higher inertia to lower psychological well-being, with stronger effects for negative than for positive emotions (Houben et al., 2015). Maternal distress may therefore be visible both in mothers' own affect dynamics and in the dyad.

Effects of Social Context on Mother-Infant Synchrony and Maternal Affect

Mothers rarely care for their infants entirely on their own, so dyadic synchrony and maternal affect may also depend on who else is present. Across cultures and evolutionary history, mothers of young infants have shared care with allomothers, that is, individuals other than the mother who help care for the child and who help mothers co-regulate their stress (Hrdy, 2009), and such help is likely to matter most when it comes from close companions, whose familiarity and ongoing investment make it most dependable. Family systems theory makes a parallel point, in that individual relationships are nested within family-level processes, such that dyadic functioning is shaped by the surrounding subsystems (McHale & Lindahl, 2011). Within this ecology, the immediate social context of caregiving, whether a mother is alone, with only her infant, or with a close companion, is a candidate moderator of the affective processes described above. Close companion refers to a romantic partner, family member, or friend, that is, a familiar person with an ongoing relationship to the mother, as distinct from distant company such as acquaintances, coworkers, and strangers. The presence and quality of close companions are active determinants of maternal well-being and the caregiving environment (Hrdy, 2009; McHale, 2007). Emotional closeness and global support have been identified as partner factors that protect against both perinatal depression and anxiety, with communication, conflict reduction, and relationship satisfaction showing the strongest evidence for depression specifically (Pilkington et al., 2015). Supportive coparenting predicts maternal adjustment and infant outcomes over and above the romantic relationship itself (Feinberg, 2003; McHale & Lindahl, 2011; Teubert & Pinquart, 2010). Most momentary evidence for these benefits comes from outside the parenting literature. Among young adults, being with close companions is associated with lower negative affect and higher positive affect, especially among individuals high in anxiety and depressive symptoms (Hur et al., 2020; Shackman et al., 2018). Whether the same holds for mothers of infants, for whom the postpartum period is a time of heightened vulnerability to depression and anxiety (Stein et al., 2014), is less clear.

The mechanism most often proposed for these benefits of close company is social buffering, the dampening of stress-system reactivity in the presence of a familiar, supportive companion (Hostinar et al., 2014). Across animal models and human studies from infancy through adulthood, the proximity of a trusted figure attenuates activation of the hypothalamicpituitary-adrenal axis in response to challenge (e.g., separation, pain, or social-evaluative stress; Gunnar, 2017; Hostinar et al., 2014). This dampening is thought to operate through oxytocin release and prefrontal regulation of threat appraisal, which together reduce the stress-system activation that a challenge would otherwise trigger (Hostinar et al., 2014). If the presence of close companions buffers maternal stress, this presence may also buffer the real-time emotional processes through which maternal distress would otherwise carry forward to the infant. Whether such buffering is visible at the level of moment-to-moment mother-infant affective synchrony, and whether it acts symmetrically on positive and negative synchrony or selectively on the negative pathway, remains largely unexamined.

Mother-Infant Synchrony Beyond the Laboratory

Testing these questions requires methods that capture the dyad in the social settings where caregiving takes place, which existing approaches rarely do. Most research on motherinfant synchrony relies on brief, structured laboratory episodes (e.g., the Still-Face Paradigm, free play, and teaching tasks) that, by design, exclude third parties and the daily-life context (Wilcox et al., 2025). Observational work notes that laboratory settings may alter the interaction they are meant to measure and limit generalization to natural home conditions (Kidby et al., 2023; Tamis-LeMonda et al., 2017). In addition, coparenting research typically uses global or trait-level ratings of partner support and examines between-person effects on maternal mood rather than within-person modulation of dyadic interaction (Feinberg, 2003; Pilkington et al., 2015). Triadic laboratory paradigms show that mother-father-infant interactions reflect familylevel dynamics beyond the mother-infant dyad and predict later child adjustment, but these paradigms are also limited to brief, instructed episodes (Fivaz-Depeursinge & Corboz-Warnery, 1999; McHale & Lindahl, 2011). Naturalistic studies have begun to capture real-time affective and autonomic coupling between parents and infants outside the laboratory (Smith et al., 2022; Wass et al., 2019). These studies show that, during ordinary daily activity, parents' arousal rises in response to peaks in their infant's arousal, that greater parental responsiveness at these moments is followed by faster infant recovery (Wass et al., 2019), and that higher parental anxiety is accompanied by tighter physiological coupling across the day (Smith et al., 2022).

They did not, however, examine whether the presence of a close companion reshapes the realtime affective synchrony between mother and infant, and momentary, daily-life evidence on this question remains scarce.

Ecological momentary assessment (EMA), paired with multilevel modeling, is well suited to fill this gap. EMA samples affect, behavior, and social context many times per day in everyday settings, typically through brief smartphone surveys prompted by text messages or app notifications, minimizing recall bias and capturing fluctuations that laboratory tasks cannot (Bolger & Laurenceau, 2013). It has been used to show that everyday social context is linked to momentary affect, with emotional experience differing when people are alone, with close companions such as partners, family, and friends, or with more distant acquaintances (Chung et al., 2025; Hur et al., 2020; Shackman et al., 2018). We adopt this close-versus-distant distinction to characterize the company present at each moment. A recent EMA study of 110 mother-infant dyads found that maternal and infant positive emotions, as well as maternal and infant negative emotions, covaried from moment to moment across the day, although maternal emotion regulation did not moderate that covariation (Hibel et al., 2025). Everyday dyadic affective covariation is therefore detectable outside the laboratory, but whether the momentary social context surrounding the dyad shapes it remains an open question. Repeated sampling as used in EMA separates what happens within a dyad from differences between dyads. It can show whether a mother's affect and her infant's affect covary from one moment to the next, whether that synchrony changes with whoever else is present, and whether dyads differ in these dynamics according to the mother's overall level of distress.

The Current Study

Our study applies an EMA approach and dynamic structural equation modeling to a community sample of 253 mothers of infants aged 3 to 24 months. Using repeated daily-life reports of maternal and infant affect and of the company present at each moment, together with a between-person index of maternal distress, we addressed two aims. Aim 1 asks how momentary social context relates to mothers' positive and negative affect and how between-person distress predicts mothers' chronic affect level and emotional inertia. We hypothesized that being alone or with distant company would be associated with less favorable momentary affect for mothers, that is, higher negative affect and lower positive affect, than being with the infant or a close companion, and that greater between-person distress would predict higher chronic negative affect and greater emotional inertia. Aim 2, our primary aim, is confined to occasions when the infant was present and asks whether maternal affect covaries with infant affect at the same moment and whether the presence of a close companion moderates this synchrony. We hypothesized that the presence of a close companion would be associated with attenuated mother-infant negative-affect synchrony relative to moments with only the infant present, because the negative-affect pathway is the one most consistent with the intergenerational transmission of risk.

Method Participants and Study Design

Data came from a larger study on the development of infant emotion and attention (approved by the University of Southern California Institutional Review Board, UP-21-01082); EMA data for the present sample were collected between September 2022 and September 2025.

We did not set a target sample size in advance, and we enrolled every eligible dyad during the study window. A mother-infant dyad was eligible if the infant was 3 to 24 months old, the infant was born within three weeks of their expected due date, and the mother spoke English or Spanish. We used flyers, Facebook advertisements, in-person community events, and BuildClinical, a third-party recruitment service, to recruit in the greater Los Angeles area of California, USA. All study materials were available in English and Spanish, and every caregiver gave informed consent before participating. Of the 297 caregivers who consented, we excluded 7 fathers who were primary caregivers, to focus the analyses on mothers. We excluded another 37 mothers who completed fewer than 20% of their scheduled surveys (84, or 42 for mothers enrolled for 7 days; M = 6.9 surveys completed). Most of these mothers (31, 84%) completed fewer than 10 surveys. The final sample was 253 mother-infant dyads (infants: 52.6% female; 3.1 to 23.9 months; mean age = 9.4 months). Table 1 reports the sample's demographic information. Recruitment was open to any eligible dyad in the greater Los Angeles area rather than targeted to a particular sociodemographic group, and no group was oversampled or screened out on the basis of race, ethnicity, language, income, or family structure. At enrollment, mothers reported their race, ethnicity, primary language, education, household income, and marital status, as well as their infant's sex (female or male). Infant gender identity was not assessed given the infants' ages, and the larger study did not collect data on sexual orientation or religion.

As part of the larger study, mothers were invited to complete an EMA protocol. For up to 14 consecutive days, each mother received a text message with a survey link six times a day, sent randomly between 7:30 a.m. and 8:30 p.m. with at least 120 minutes between texts. This yielded up to 84 scheduled surveys per mother, or 42 for mothers who took part for 7 days. Texts were sent via SurveySignal (Hofmann & Patel, 2015), and mothers completed the surveys in Qualtrics (Qualtrics, Provo, UT). Each survey took about five minutes to complete, and mothers had one hour to respond, with reminders sent at 15 and 50 minutes if they had not yet responded. We asked them not to respond when it was unsafe to do so, such as while driving. Mothers could stop after the seventh day or continue for the remaining seven days, and 211 of the 253 mothers (83%) completed the full 14 days. Mothers received $5 for each day of the protocol they completed (up to $70) and could earn additional bonuses for reaching 70% ($10) and 85% ($20) survey completion, for up to $100 in all. They were paid in cash or by an electronic Amazon gift card, according to their preference.

Measures

Momentary Assessments

Maternal affect. In each survey, mothers rated how they currently felt on 18 affect labels adapted from the Positive and Negative Affect Schedule (Watson et al., 1988), using its 5-point scale from "very slightly or not at all" to "extremely." We averaged the negative labels (i.e., sad, worried, nervous, afraid, hopeless, annoyed, angry, lonely) into a momentary negative affect score. We averaged the positive labels (i.e., calm, cheerful, content, enthusiastic, joyful, relaxed, accepted, connected) into a momentary positive affect score. The remaining two labels, sluggish and tired, index fatigue rather than negative or positive affect and were not included in either score. Each mother therefore had one negative and one positive score at every completed assessment. Both momentary composites showed good multilevel reliability, estimated with multilevel omega at each level (Geldhof et al., 2014; negative affect: within-person ω = .78, between-person ω = .96; positive affect: within-person ω = .87, between-person ω = .97).

Social context.

In each survey, mothers reported whether they were actively interacting with their infant (yes or no) and who they were with. The companion checklist options included the baby, a romantic partner, family, close friends, friends, acquaintances, coworkers, strangers, and nobody, and mothers could select more than one. We counted an assessment as infant present only when the mother selected the baby on this checklist. We treated a romantic partner, family members, close friends, or friends as close companions (Hur et al., 2020; Shackman et al., 2018), and acquaintances, coworkers, or strangers as distant others. Each assessment was then assigned to one of five mutually exclusive categories: alone (nobody), infant only, close company (at least one close companion and no distant others, with or without the infant), distant company (distant others only), and mixed company (distant others together with the infant, a close companion, or both). Within infant-present assessments, this allowed us to compare moments when the mother and infant were joined by a close companion with moments when the mother was with the infant only.

Infant affect. The survey displayed the infant affect items when the mother selected the baby as a companion, and she then rated how she thought her infant currently felt. She rated three labels, calm, happy, and distressed, on the same 5-point scale used for maternal affect. We used the happy rating for infant happiness and the distressed rating for infant distress; the calm rating was not analyzed. Only ratings from infant-present assessments, as defined above, were used for the Aim 2 models.

Questionnaires

Maternal distress. Separate from the daily surveys, mothers completed a set of questionnaires assessing their emotions and well-being. We administered the questionnaires through REDCap (Harris et al., 2019) and emailed each mother a personalized link to complete at her convenience before her two-week participation period. We treated a questionnaire as missing when a mother answered fewer than 80% of its items. The questionnaires also included demographic and infant temperament measures, from which we drew the covariates described below, and other measures not analyzed here.

We measured maternal distress with four scales: the Beck Anxiety Inventory (Beck et al., 1988), the Beck Depression Inventory (Beck et al., 1961) or Beck Depression Inventory-II (Beck et al., 1996), a 9-item version of the Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale that omits the selfharm item (Chen et al., 2023; Cox et al., 1987), and the 10-item Perceived Stress Scale (Cohen et al., 1983; Cohen & Williamson, 1988). Each scale showed good internal consistency in this sample (Beck Anxiety Inventory α = .88; Beck Depression Inventory-II α = .87; Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale α = .85; Perceived Stress Scale α = .88; for the original Beck Depression Inventory, α = .82). A small number of early participants (n = 13) completed the original Beck Depression Inventory, whereas later participants completed the Beck Depression Inventory-II, and each mother contributed the version she completed. Anxiety, depression, and stress symptoms overlap and often co-occur, reflecting a shared general-distress dimension (Clark & Watson, 1991; Henry & Crawford, 2005). Following that literature, we converted each scale total to a z score and averaged the z scores into a single distress composite. The composite showed good reliability in this sample (α = .86).

Covariates. From the same questionnaires, we drew four covariates for the betweenperson models: infant age (in months) and maternal education (in years); maternal ethnicity coded as Hispanic versus non-Hispanic; and infant temperamental negative affectivity, indexed by the negative-affectivity scale of the Infant Behavior Questionnaire-Revised (Gartstein & Rothbart, 2003).

Data Analysis

Design and Analysis Transparency

The hypotheses and analyses were not preregistered. The EMA protocol was designed for a larger longitudinal study, and the present questions were formulated after data collection began, so no sample-size target or analysis plan was fixed in advance. The directional hypotheses for both aims were specified before estimation, and the four primary models described below were the only models fit to test them. The primary analyses therefore fall toward the exploratory end of the confirmatory-to-exploratory continuum, and the five supplementary analyses are exploratory. The Mplus input files for the primary and supplementary models will be made publicly available on the Open Science Framework (OSF).

Main Analyses

We analyzed the momentary data with dynamic structural equation modeling (Asparouhov et al., 2018; McNeish & Hamaker, 2020). Figure 1 presents the conceptual model for both aims. This approach fits these data well, because it separates within-person from between-person variation, models how affect carries over time, and lets associations differ across mothers (McNeish & Hamaker, 2020). All models ran in Mplus Version 8.3 (Muthén & Muthén, 1998 -2017) with Bayesian estimation and a two-level random-effects structure. Estimation used Gibbs sampling with two Markov chain Monte Carlo chains, a minimum of 10,000 iterations, and a maximum of 50,000. Estimation continued until the convergence criterion was met. We fixed a random seed of 20260415 for reproducibility and kept the default diffuse priors.

Convergence was assessed with the Gelman-Rubin potential scale reduction factor, using a strict convergence criterion of .005. Across models, the potential scale reduction factor settled below this criterion and stayed stable over the final iterations. Posterior histograms were smooth, and trace plots showed acceptable mixing. Point estimates are reported as posterior medians, and uncertainty as 95% credible intervals. An effect is counted as significant when its 95% credible interval excludes zero. We report fully standardized coefficients throughout, so each estimate is on a correlation-like metric and can be read as an effect size.

Maternal affect served as the outcome, and Mplus separated it into within-and between-person components. The within-person level included a first-order autoregressive slope, which indexes emotional inertia, that is, how strongly affect at one assessment carried over to the next. This slope was free to vary across mothers. To keep the autoregression in real time, we used the TINTERVAL option with a scaled time variable to account for longer lags such as overnight. A random slope captured the effect of being with a close companion. Fixed effects for being alone, being with a more distant companion, and the prompt's position within the day adjusted these estimates for the remaining social contexts and for time-of-day differences in affect. We allowed only the close-company effect to vary randomly because it was the focus of the analysis and because models that additionally freed the alone and distant-companion slopes did not converge. All social-context indicators were entered uncentered (1 or 0) and coded against a reference of being with the infant only. Infant presence is therefore captured by the contrast between the infant-absent (alone) category and the infant-only reference. At the between-person level, each mother's proportion of assessments spent with a close companion and her grand-mean-centered distress predicted the random intercept and both random slopes, which were free to covary. Her proportions of assessments spent alone and with distant company also predicted the random intercept. With all three proportions in the model, the within-person context effects reflect momentary differences within each mother rather than differences between mothers in how often they were in each context. The proportions were grand-mean centered, so the random intercept reflects a mother's affect when she was with her infant only, at average proportions of the three contexts. Across mothers, mean affect during infant-only assessments correlated r = .95 (negative affect) and r = .96 (positive affect) with mean affect across all assessments, so we refer to the random intercept as a mother's average, or chronic, affect. Each between-person model additionally included infant age, infant temperamental negative affectivity, maternal education, and maternal ethnicity as predictors of the random intercept, to adjust for infant and family characteristics that could otherwise account for differences in affect. Continuous covariates were grand-mean centered, and ethnicity was entered as a single indicator. Covariates were modeled as endogenous variables with estimated means and variances, so all available cases contributed to estimation under a missing-at-random assumption; missing covariate values, which occurred mainly for infant temperament, were treated as unknown parameters and drawn at each iteration of the Bayesian estimation, which serves the same role that full-information maximum likelihood serves in frequentist estimation.

Aim 2 asked whether a mother's momentary affect was synchronized with her infant's affect at the same moment (mother-infant affective synchrony), and whether close company moderated that synchrony. These models used infant-present assessments only (10,920 assessments from 253 mothers). Separate models were used to examine negative and positive affect. Infant affect served as the outcome and was separated by Mplus into within-and betweenperson components, while the matching maternal components were precomputed in R and entered as separate predictors. The within-person level included a random synchrony slope from the mother's momentary affect to her infant's affect, a random slope for the product of that affect and the close-companion indicator (capturing whether close company moderates mother-infant affective synchrony), and a fixed direct effect of close company on infant affect. Each random slope comprises a fixed component, the average association across mothers, and a personspecific deviation from it; the synchrony and moderation coefficients reported below are these fixed components. We kept the direct effect of close company fixed rather than random because our hypotheses concerned variation in the synchrony rather than in the direct effect, and because freeing this slope as well did not converge. We entered the close indicator in raw form, so it contrasted close-company assessments (coded 1) with all other infant-present assessments (coded 0). These were almost entirely occasions with the infant only (46.4% of infant-present assessments); the remaining 2.4% involved mixed company, that is, distant others such as acquaintances or strangers present together with a close companion (1.5%) or with the infant only (0.9%), and these were also coded 0. We used this binary contrast, rather than a separate category for mixed company, because those occasions were too infrequent to estimate reliably.

At the between-person level, the mother's chronic affect (her average momentary affect across momentary assessments), her proportion of assessments spent with a close companion, and her distress symptoms (the questionnaire-based composite of anxiety, depression, and stress) predicted the random intercept and both slopes, which again were free to covary. These models included the same four covariates as predictors of the random intercept, and infant age additionally predicted both random slopes, testing whether mother-infant synchrony and its moderation by close company varied with infant age across the studied age range. Covariates were centered and handled as in Aim 1.

Supplementary Analyses

We ran five sets of supplementary analyses. First, we re-estimated the Aim 2 models with close context centered within person rather than in raw form, to confirm that the moderation did not hinge on how we coded social context or on individual differences in social context. Second, we estimated a bivariate cross-lagged model to probe the direction and timing of the synchrony, including maternal and infant autoregression, a random maternal-to-infant cross-lag, a fixed infant-to-maternal cross-lag, the same-moment residual covariance, and the between-person association of the two intercepts. Third, we re-estimated this cross-lagged model, building lags only from consecutive same-day infant-present assessments, a more conservative test that does not lean on the model's handling of missing infant data to account for gaps; infant autoregression was fixed there because of convergence issues. Fourth, we exchanged the outcome and predictor of the Aim 2 models, regressing maternal affect on concurrent infant affect, to examine directionality and whether results were specific to predicting infant affect from maternal affect.

Fifth, to test whether the close-company associations were specific to a companion type, we replaced the close-company indicator with two indicators, one for the presence of a romantic partner and one for the presence of other family members. Both indicators equaled 1 when a partner and other family members were present together (7.0% of infant-present assessments), so each coefficient reflects the association with one companion type while holding the other constant. Friends were not modeled as a separate category because they were present at too few infant-present assessments (3.7%) to support a stable estimate; assessments at which friends were the only close companions (1.3% of infant-present assessments) were excluded from these models, so that the reference category consisted of infant-only assessments and the few mixedcompany assessments, as in the primary models. Every supplementary model kept the estimator, sample definitions, and reference logic of the primary models, except where noted. In each supplementary model, the four covariates predicted the random intercepts, and infant age also predicted the random slopes linking maternal and infant affect, as in the primary Aim 2 models.

At the within-person level, maternal positive affect also showed inertia (β = 0.35, CI [0.33, 0.36]), and it varied with social context in the same broad direction. As with negative affect, being alone (β = -0.12, CI [-0.13, -0.10]) or with more distant company (β = -0.04, CI [-0.06, -0.03]) was associated with lower positive affect, relative to being with only the infant.

Supplementary Analyses

We conducted five sensitivity analyses. First, the moderation results were robust regardless of how close company was operationalized. With close company centered within person rather than coded in the original social-context categories, the buffering of negative synchrony remained (β = -0.04, CI [-0.07, -0.01]), as did the null positive moderation and the direct positive effect (β = 0.05, CI [0.03, 0.07]). For the second and third analyses, two crosslagged specifications addressed timing and direction. The same-moment association persisted with both the mother's and the infant's autoregression and the cross-lagged paths estimated in the same model (β = 0.17 for negative affect, β = 0.27 for positive), supporting the concurrent coupling as a process in its own right, even with temporal dynamics controlled. For negative affect, no lagged path emerged in either direction, whether in the full-sample model (mother to infant β = 0.02, CI [-0.01, 0.05]; infant to mother β = 0.01, CI [-0.01, 0.03]) or the more conservative valid-lag model restricted to consecutive same-day infant-present pairs (β = 0.01, CI [-0.02, 0.04]; β = 0.01, CI [-0.01, 0.03]). For positive affect, a forward mother-to-infant lagged path was significant in both specifications (full sample β = 0.06, CI [0.03, 0.08]; valid lag β = 0.04, CI [0.01, 0.06]), and a smaller infant-to-mother lag appeared in both (full sample β = 0.02, CI [0.002, 0.042]; valid lag β = 0.02, CI [0.001, 0.045]). For the main results, this means that the negative-affect synchrony is momentary rather than carried forward in time: neither partner's negative affect predicted the other's at the next prompt, so the concurrent synchrony reflects shared moment-to-moment context rather than time-lagged transmission. For positive affect, small lagged paths persisted in both directions, the mother-to-infant path being the larger, suggesting a modest forward influence alongside the concurrent synchrony.

Fourth, we further asked whether the moderation was specific to predicting infant affect from maternal affect. In models with the roles reversed, so that maternal affect was regressed on concurrent infant affect, concurrent coupling appeared in both valences (negative β = 0.16, CI [0.13, 0.20]; positive β = 0.24, CI [0.21, 0.27]), consistent with the symmetry expected when two series co-fluctuate at the same moment. The moderation, however, was not symmetric: close company attenuated the negative synchrony when infant distress was predicted from maternal affect but not when the roles were reversed (interaction β = -0.01, CI [-0.05, 0.03]), and the positive synchrony was stronger with a close companion present (interaction β = 0.04, CI [0.01, 0.07]). More distressed mothers showed stronger synchrony in the model predicting maternal from infant distress (β = 0.23, CI [0.02, 0.43]). For the main results, this means that the closecompany buffering of negative synchrony is specific to predicting infant distress from maternal affect, rather than a symmetric pattern that would be expected if it merely reflected a sharedmethod artifact.

Fifth, in a final sensitivity analysis to further understand the effect of social context in the presence of close companions, we decomposed close company into the presence of a romantic partner and the presence of other family members. The buffering of negative synchrony was not specific to either companion type. Neither a partner (interaction β = -0.02, CI [-0.05, 0.02]) nor a family member (interaction β = -0.03, CI [-0.06, 0.02]) moderated the synchrony on its own, and a direct test of the difference between the two unstandardized interaction coefficients was not significant (Δb = -0.26, CI [-0.76, 0.27]), so we treat them as a single close-companion effect rather than as two distinct effects. The direct association with infant happiness, by contrast, held for both: the presence of a partner (β = 0.03, CI [0.01, 0.05]) and of other family (β = 0.04, CI [0.02, 0.07]) were each associated with higher infant happiness, and these two associations did not differ from each other (Δb = -0.02, CI [-0.09, 0.04]). Together, these results suggest that the associations reflect the presence of close companions in general rather than of a romantic partner specifically.

Discussion

Our study investigated how the immediate social context of caregiving relates to mothers' own affect and to the moment-to-moment affective synchrony between mothers and infants, using up to two weeks of EMA data in a community sample of mothers. There are four notable findings. First, being alone or only with more distant company was associated with less favorable maternal affect than being with only the infant or with a close companion. Second, higher maternal distress symptoms predicted both higher chronic maternal negative affect and stronger negative-affect inertia. Third, we observed synchrony between mothers' and infants' momentary affect in both positive and negative valences, and the presence of a close companion was associated with weaker negative synchrony but did not modulate positive synchrony. Fourth, the association of close company with infant affect differed by valence: close company was associated with weaker synchrony between maternal negative affect and infant distress, whereas it was directly associated with higher infant happiness. Together, these results show that social context is associated with the real-time emotional exchanges of the mother-infant dyad and suggest that these associations differ by type of company and by valence.

Social Context and Distress Symptoms Are Associated With Momentary Maternal Affect

At the level of mothers' own affect, momentary social context tracked emotional state as expected. Being alone or with more distant company was associated with higher negative and lower positive affect, relative to being with the infant only, consistent with the view that the caregiving environment is shaped by who is present (Hrdy, 2009; McHale, 2007). Prior momentary work has shown that the presence of close companions is associated with more favorable momentary affect in adults (Hur et al., 2020; Shackman et al., 2018); here, being with the infant alone was as favorable as being with a close companion for negative affect, suggesting that the infant's presence itself, not only that of a supportive adult, may buffer maternal negative affect. Although being alone was associated with higher negative affect in the moment, mothers who spent more time alone or with a close companion, rather than with their infant only, reported lower average negative affect. This pattern fits the view that parents' stress depends on the balance between the demands of caregiving and the resources available to meet them, such as support from a coparent or family and time for leisure (Mikolajczak & Roskam, 2018). Regular time away from the infant and regular close company may add to these resources and make caregiving less taxing. Because this is a between-mother association, it may reflect the circumstances that supply these resources, for example help from family or childcare, rather than an effect of company itself.

Between mothers, greater distress predicted not only higher average negative affect but also stronger negative-affect inertia, the within-person tendency for an affective state to carry over from one moment to the next (Koval et al., 2012; Kuppens et al., 2010). This suggests that maternal distress is visible not only in average mood but also in how persistently negative affect carries over across the day. This pattern also has developmental significance. Elevated inertia reflects difficulty recovering from negative states and prospectively predicts the onset of depression (Kuppens et al., 2012), so a mother whose negative affect is both higher and more persistent may offer fewer moments of repair for her infant and may gradually shape the infant's own emerging regulation. Momentary inertia may therefore be a modifiable marker of risk that could be addressed before it consolidates into disorder or is transmitted across generations.

Social Context Is Associated With the Affective Dynamics of the Mother-Infant Dyad

Within the dyad, mothers' and infants' affect levels were coupled within the moment in both valences: when a mother's affect rose above her own typical level, her infant's affect was also higher at the same moment. This concurrent synchrony is consistent with biobehavioral models in which mothers and infants coordinate affect in real time (Feldman, 2007 (Feldman, , 2012)), and it appeared at both the within-and between-person levels. For negative affect, this synchrony was weaker when a close companion was present, and close company was associated with the coupling itself rather than with the infant's level of distress. This is what a social-buffering account predicts when buffering acts on reactivity: the proximity of a familiar, supportive companion dampens stress responses (Gunnar, 2017; Hostinar et al., 2014), and closeness is a resource that lowers perceived threat and reduces the effort of self-regulation (Coan & Sbarra, 2015). Negative synchrony therefore behaves like a channel that a third party can interrupt, loosening the moment-to-moment coupling between maternal and infant distress. From a dynamic-systems view, coregulation can either dampen or amplify arousal. In well-regulated dyads, each partner's responses bring the other back toward a calmer state, whereas in dysregulated dyads negative arousal can escalate in a self-sustaining loop (Wass et al., 2024). An additional person may interrupt such a loop, for example by taking over part of the caregiving or by helping the mother regulate her own arousal, which would weaken the moment-to-moment contingency between her distress and her infant's. This interpretation also fits a broader view of coregulation as a family-level rather than a purely dyadic process, in which family members' emotion regulation is interdependent and the presence of additional people reshapes how a dyad coordinates emotion (Paley & Hajal, 2022).

Positive affect followed a different route. Close company was not associated with the strength of positive synchrony, but was directly associated with higher positive affect in both mother and infant. Positive affect therefore behaves less like a channel and more like a resource that a third party can add to, raising the shared level of positive affect without altering its coupling. This is consistent with developmental views in which early positive emotions build resources and strengthen social bonds (Fredrickson, 2001; Seehagen et al., 2025). The contrast between the two valences fits recent frameworks that treat interpersonal emotion regulation as including both affect-improving and affect-worsening processes that operate on the relationship rather than on a single emotion (Arican-Dinc & Gable, 2026). It also converges with work showing that positive and negative parent-infant synchrony respond differently to context, with positive synchrony increasing during shared engagement while negative synchrony does not (Kidby et al., 2023).

Several supplementary analyses further examined these interpretations and demonstrated that our results were robust to several specifications. In the cross-lagged models, the negative synchrony was concurrent, and neither partner's negative affect predicted the other's at the next prompt. For positive affect, small lagged paths appeared in both directions, and the path from mother to infant was the larger of the two. The spacing of our prompts helps explain this pattern.

Studies that recorded mothers' and infants' physiology continuously found that the two partners' arousal covaried over the course of a brief laboratory reunion (Waters et al., 2014 (Waters et al., , 2017) ) and that, at home, parents' arousal rose within minutes of peaks in their infant's arousal (Smith et al., 2022; Wass et al., 2019). Our prompts were typically about two and a half hours apart, so influence on that time scale falls within a single prompt and appears as concurrent synchrony.

Mothers' positive affect was also more persistent than their negative affect (inertia of 0.35 vs. 0.27). Because mothers' and infants' affect were coupled at each prompt, a positive mood that lasted until the next prompt would also be reflected in the infant's affect at that prompt, which may account for the small path from mothers' earlier positive affect to their infants' later happiness.

We also re-estimated the models with the roles of outcome and predictor exchanged, so that maternal affect was predicted from concurrent infant affect. Concurrent coupling appeared in both directions, as expected when two series co-fluctuate, but the buffering of negative synchrony was specific to predicting infant distress from maternal affect and was not symmetric. This is informative because the main concern with single-informant data is that a mother's general response style could inflate the apparent coupling between her own and her infant's affect. Such an artifact should distort the association equally in both directions, yet the closecompany buffering appeared in only one. The asymmetry is therefore difficult to explain as an artifact and is more consistent with a genuine feature of the dyad's negative-affect dynamics.

Together, these supplementary checks support reading the buffering as a real effect rather than a byproduct of the modeling choices.

When close company was decomposed into the presence of a romantic partner versus other family members, neither buffered the negative synchrony on its own, though both pointed in the same direction, and the direct association with infant happiness held for both. The associations therefore did not localize to the romantic partner but generalized across close companions. This is more consistent with a broad allomothering account, in which any sufficiently close and familiar caregiver can support the mother-infant system (Hrdy, 2009), than with a partner-specific coparenting account, where partner support is treated as uniquely important (Feinberg, 2003). It also fits evidence that support from extended kin contributes to maternal postpartum well-being beyond the partner alone (Riem et al., 2023), and field observations that responsive care for infants is routinely distributed across several close caregivers rather than provided by one (Chaudhary et al., 2024). The literature on distributed care further suggests that involvement concentrates in a small set of close, familiar figures rather than diffusing across a wide network (Chaudhary et al., 2024; Riem et al., 2023). Clinically, this suggests that support need not target the mother-infant dyad in isolation: mobilizing a mother's close relationships, including partners and other family, may ease the moment-to-moment transmission of distress and support infant regulation. This is consistent with process models of parenting, in which support from partners and wider social networks buffers caregiving against stress (Belsky, 1984).

Maternal distress symptoms were associated with the dyad at the between-person level but did not moderate mother-infant synchrony. More distressed mothers had higher chronic negative affect, stronger inertia, and infants with higher average distress, yet in the primary models distress was not associated with the strength of the moment-to-moment synchrony in either valence or with the extent of the close-company buffering. One interpretation is that, in a community sample, maternal distress raises the baseline level of negative affect in both mother and infant without changing the strength of the moment-to-moment link between them. Whether distress would be associated with the dynamics themselves in a clinical sample remains open.

Prior work asking related questions has examined depression specifically rather than a broad distress composite: in a community sample, mother-infant physiological synchrony moderated the link between maternal depressive symptoms and children's later internalizing problems, such that higher symptoms predicted worse outcomes only when synchrony was low (Lan et al., 2024), maternal depression has been tied more broadly to altered synchrony (Granat et al., 2017), and parental anxiety has been tied to heightened physiological synchrony in naturalistic settings (Smith et al., 2022). A comprehensive assessment would recruit across the full range of distress, separate its components, and ask whether each interacts with the within-person coupling and the social buffering effect observed here.

The synchrony and its buffering were stable across the infant age range studied. Although mothers rated older infants as lower in distress and higher in happiness on average, consistent with normative change in the expression and regulation of affect over the first two years, the strength of the moment-to-moment coupling and the degree to which close company attenuated negative coupling did not vary with age. Because fewer infants were in their second year (30.4% were 12 months or older; see Table 1), these tests had less information about older infants and should be read as an absence of evidence for age differences rather than as evidence of equivalence. These patterns also held after adjustment for infant temperamental negative affectivity, maternal education, and maternal ethnicity, making it less likely that the coupling or its buffering reflects stable infant or family characteristics.

Generalizability

The sample was a non-clinical community sample recruited between 2022 and 2025 in the greater Los Angeles area, USA. About half of the mothers identified as Hispanic or Latinx and about one in ten reported Spanish as their primary language, so the findings speak to a population underrepresented in the literature on mother-infant synchrony. The sample was also, on average, well educated and drawn from a single metropolitan region, and only mothers serving as primary caregivers were retained. Because sociocultural generalizability cannot be inferred from a single sample, whether close-companion presence moderates negative synchrony in the same way in other regions, in other caregiving arrangements, or at clinical levels of distress requires direct comparison across demographically distinct samples. The moderation did not vary with infant age within the 3-to 24-month range studied, but this stability cannot be assumed to extend into early childhood. As children begin to talk, move independently, and form their own relationships with other caregivers and peers, a third person may come to act on the child directly, for example as an alternative source of comfort, rather than mainly through the mother's regulation, which could change how that person's presence relates to mother-child affective synchrony. Testing the moderation across these transitions would show whether the pattern documented here is specific to infancy.

Limitations and Future Directions

There are several limitations in this study. Both mothers' and infants' affect were reported by the mother, so the synchrony reflects a single informant's perception, and sharedmethod variance cannot be excluded; a mother's own reporting tendencies could contribute to the coupling between her and her infant's affect. The supplementary analysis on directionspecific buffering makes this explanation less likely but does not eliminate it. A general reporting tendency, such as rating everything more negatively when in a low mood, should inflate the association between maternal and infant ratings equally in both directions, yet closecompany buffering appeared only when infant distress was predicted from maternal affect. A reporting bias that itself depends on context could still produce an asymmetric pattern, however.

For example, if mothers rated their own feelings less reliably when others were present, the added measurement error would weaken the association only in models in which maternal affect is the predictor. Each infant affect score was also based on a single item. Pairing maternal reports with observational or physiological infant measures, as recent momentary studies have begun to do, would address both limitations by estimating the coupling from two independent sources and from richer indices of infant affect (de Barbaro et al., 2023).

The associations are also concurrent and correlational. The presence of a close companion was observed, not manipulated, so we cannot rule out that it co-occurs with other features of those moments, such as the ongoing activity (e.g., mealtimes, outings, or bedtime routines), practical help with caregiving, or the infant's state. The cross-lagged analyses address only the timing of the coupling, not whether close company causes it. Social context was reduced to a single contrast, the presence of a close companion versus all other infant-present moments, because mixed-company moments were too rare to model separately. In addition, companions were classified by relationship category, which is only a proxy for how close a mother feels to them; some relatives may not be close to her, and some coworkers may be. The same applies to friends, whom we counted as close companions following prior work (Hur et al., 2020; Shackman et al., 2018), even though the checklist distinguished close friends from other friends.

Friends were the only close companions at 1.3% of infant-present assessments, which limits how much this choice could affect the close-company contrast.

Several directions follow from these findings. First, the momentary surveys also asked mothers how close or connected they felt to each person present, which would allow future analyses to test whether felt closeness, rather than relationship category, accounts for the buffering, and whether the quality of the mother's relationship with her partner or other relatives moderates it, because a conflictual relationship may not provide the support that a warm one does. Second, similar analyses could be conducted in clinical samples and with multi-item or observer-based indices of infant affect. Third, programs that strengthen mothers' support networks, such as coparenting interventions or efforts to involve extended family in infant care, could be evaluated for their associations with the moment-to-moment dynamics of the dyad, not only with maternal symptoms (Feinberg, 2003). For parents and practitioners, these results suggest that time spent with trusted others while caring for an infant is a context in which infant distress is less tightly linked to the mother's own negative feelings. Conclusion a close companion was associated with weaker synchrony between maternal negative affect and infant distress and with higher infant happiness, in addition to higher maternal positive affect.

Studies of mother-infant synchrony have mostly observed the dyad on its own, and these findings indicate that partners and other family members are part of the context in which that synchrony occurs. Our results suggest that supporting the people around a mother may be one way to support her infant. More broadly, these findings point to a modifiable link in early risk transmission: if the same-moment link between maternal distress and infant distress is weaker when a close companion is present, then the caregiving network is not only a source of support for the mother but a plausible target to support the infant's developing emotion regulation.
