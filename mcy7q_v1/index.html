Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Theory and research indicate that daily events-frequently occurring, time-discrete experiences that disrupt everyday life-are important predictors of momentary subjective wellbeing (SWB). However, findings about the associations between daily events and SWB vary substantially across studies, potentially reflecting differences in samples, event characteristics, or assessment methods. Moreover, it remains unclear whether these associations emerge at both the within-and between-person level and whether daily events prospectively predict SWB. To address these limitations, we conducted a preregistered systematic review and meta-analysis of within-and between-person associations between daily events and SWB across 217 intensive longitudinal studies (Ntotal = 48,311). Consistent with theoretical accounts, findings showed small-to-medium positive associations between daily uplifts and SWB, within (r = .16) and between persons (r = .21), and medium-to-large negative associations between daily hassles and SWB, within (r = -.18) and between persons (r = -.28). Moderator analyses further showed that these associations varied systematically depending on the type of daily event, its operationalization, and the examined SWB dimension. Furthermore, whereas daily events were associated with SWB when assessed concurrently, there was little evidence that these associations persisted into subsequent moments or days. Overall, the present meta-analytic review provides a comprehensive account of the associations between daily events and SWB. It indicates that daily uplifts and hassles are important correlates of SWB at both the within-and between-person level, but that their associations with SWB are relatively short-lived within individuals.

How Are Daily Events Related to Subjective Well-Being? A Meta-Analytic Review of Intensive Longitudinal Studies

Subjective well-being (SWB) refers to how individuals evaluate and experience their lives (Diener, 1984). It is a multifaceted construct comprising both affective and cognitive dimensions (Diener, 1984; Diener et al., 1999). Affective SWB includes positive affect, such as feeling joyful, excited, or enthusiastic, and negative affect, such as feeling distressed, upset, or afraid. Cognitive SWB includes life satisfaction, which reflects evaluations of one's life as a whole, and domain satisfaction, reflecting evaluations of specific areas of one's life.

Higher SWB is associated with a range of important outcomes, including better health, more stable social relationships, and better work performance (e.g., Diener et al., 2017; Moore et al., 2018; Tenney et al., 2016). SWB is also increasingly considered an important policy indicator that complements traditional economic metrics of societal progress and prosperity (e.g., Diener et al., 2015; Frey & Stutzer, 2012; Oishi & Diener, 2014). Thus, understanding the causes and correlates of SWB is of critical relevance.

Existing meta-analyses have identified a wide range of SWB correlates, including relatively stable individual characteristics, such as genetic factors and personality traits (e.g., Anglim et al., 2020; Pluess & Pluess, 2015; Steel et al., 2008), and broader life circumstances, such as social relationships, socioeconomic status, and major life events (e.g., Bühler et al., 2024; Simonsmeier et al., 2026; Tan et al., 2020). However, these metaanalyses have mainly focused on between-person differences in SWB. In other words, they have examined why some individuals report higher or lower SWB than others on average. Yet, SWB also fluctuates substantially within individuals over time, from moment to moment and from day to day (e.g., Eid & Diener, 1999; Luhmann et al., 2021). This distinction raises an important complementary question: Which factors are associated with fluctuations in momentary SWB within individuals? Daily events-frequently occurring, time-discrete experiences of everyday life-are theorized to be an important source of these within-person fluctuations in momentary SWB (e.g., Almeida, 2005; Haehner et al., 2024; Kanner et al., 1981). Numerous intensive longitudinal studies have found that negative daily events, also called daily hassles, are associated with lower momentary SWB (e.g., Mey et al., 2025; Toma et al., 2025; Vize et al., 2024), whereas positive daily events, also referred to as daily uplifts, are associated with higher momentary SWB (e.g., Hill et al., 2022; Klaiber et al., 2024; Zheng et al., 2023).

Intensive longitudinal studies are particularly well suited to examine these associations because they repeatedly assess daily events and SWB within individuals over several days or weeks, reducing recall bias and improving ecological validity (e.g., Almeida, 2005; Bolger et al., 2003; Raphael et al., 1991). Moreover, they allow distinguishing within-person associations-whether individuals report higher or lower SWB when experiencing more or fewer daily events than usual-from between-person associations-whether individuals who typically experience more or fewer events than others report correspondingly higher or lower SWB (Almeida, 2005; Reis & Gable, 2000).

Despite the extensive body of intensive longitudinal research on daily events and SWB, no quantitative synthesis of these associations is available. Such a synthesis is important for several reasons. First, it can establish the strength and robustness of the associations between daily events and SWB while distinguishing within-and between-person associations. Second, a quantitative synthesis can help contextualize the importance of daily events for SWB relative to other established correlates, such as personality traits or socioeconomic status (Anglim et al., 2020; Tan et al., 2020). Third, it can quantify heterogeneity in daily event-SWB associations and systematically examine study, sample, and assessment characteristics that may account for this heterogeneity, helping to clarify the conditions under which daily events are more or less strongly related to SWB.

With the present meta-analysis, we aimed to provide a comprehensive synthesis of the associations between daily events and SWB in intensive longitudinal studies. We had three research aims: (1) to quantify the within-person associations between daily events and momentary SWB; (2) to quantify the between-person associations between daily events and SWB; and (3) to identify study, sample, and assessment characteristics that moderate these associations.

Theoretical Perspectives on Daily Events and SWB

Several theoretical frameworks from different areas of psychology converge on the idea that daily events contribute to fluctuations in momentary SWB. In the following, we summarize four key frameworks and their propositions regarding the associations between daily events and SWB. Furthermore, we derive testable predictions about when these associations are expected to be more or less pronounced (see Table 1 for an overview).

The Transactional Model of Stress and Coping (e.g., Lazarus, 1980, 1991; Lazarus & Folkman, 1984) proposes that the effect of daily hassles on momentary SWB depends not only on whether a hassle occurs but, more importantly, on how it is appraised. This proposition also reflects a widely shared intuition: the same everyday event can have very different consequences for how people feel depending on how it is interpreted (Haehner et al., 2024; Rauthmann et al., 2014). For example, a conflict at work may have little effect when it is perceived as a manageable disagreement that can be resolved through constructive communication. However, when the same conflict is perceived as threatening to one's job and difficult to cope with, it likely results in a larger decrease in momentary SWB. Thus, according to the Transactional Model of Stress and Coping, subjective perceptions are central to understanding how daily hassles are associated with fluctuations in momentary SWB. This emphasis is also reflected in how daily events are typically measured. Specifically, participants are often asked to not only report whether a daily event occurred or to describe it but also to rate their subjective perception of the event, such as perceived severity or importance (e.g., Doorley & Kashdan, 2021; Longua et al., 2009; Toma et al., 2025).

According to the Transactional Model of Stress and Coping, measures that assess subjective perceptions of events should be more strongly associated with momentary SWB than measures of mere daily event occurrence.

The Daily Stress Process Model (Almeida, 2005) focuses on the exposure and the reaction to daily hassles. It suggests that sociodemographic characteristics, psychosocial resources, and health conditions influence both individuals' exposure to daily hassles and their reactions to them. For example, individual differences in age, gender, and education are theorized to explain differences in daily hassle exposure and people's reaction to these hassles. Sociodemographic characteristics might thus moderate the associations between daily hassles and momentary SWB. Furthermore, the Daily Stress Process Model proposes that characteristics of daily hassles (e.g., their frequency and content) and how people appraise them (e.g., their perceived relevance and severity) influence people's reactions to these hassles. Associations between daily events and SWB may therefore also vary across types of hassles because of differences in their objective characteristics. For example, work-, social-, and health-related hassles may be differently associated with SWB as they may differ in frequency, content, and objective severity. Moreover, measures capturing individuals' subjective appraisals of daily hassles should be more strongly associated with SWB than measures of their mere occurrence, consistent with implications of the Transactional Model of Stress and Coping.

More recently, the Daily Stress Process Model has been extended to propose that the effects of daily hassles on SWB can persist beyond the moment or day on which they occurred (Almeida et al., 2026). Such carry-over effects may reflect a prolonged response to daily hassles that extends into subsequent measurement occasions. This raises the question of whether daily hassles are associated with SWB only concurrently or also at later moments or days.

The Hedonic Adaptation Prevention Model (Sheldon et al., 2013) complements the described hassle-focused perspectives by including predictions regarding the effects of daily uplifts. Specifically, the Hedonic Adaptation Prevention Model posits that positive major life events can increase individuals' exposure to recurring daily uplifts, which, in turn, can enhance momentary and long-term SWB. For example, starting a new job may increase individuals' exposure to work-related daily uplifts, such as successfully completing a difficult tasks or enjoying coffee breaks with teammates. These recurring uplifts may increase momentary SWB and, over time, contribute to higher long-term SWB. However, the longterm implications of recurring uplifts are theorized to depend on their perceived variety and novelty, such that greater variety and novelty are expected to result in more enduring effects on SWB. Thus, similar to the Transactional Model of Stress and Coping and the Daily Stress Process Model, the Hedonic Adaptation Prevention Model proposes that the impact of daily uplifts depends on the interplay between both characteristics of the uplift itself and how individuals perceive it.

Finally, Affective Events Theory by Weiss and Cropanzano (1996) provides a rationale for further distinguishing between the affective and cognitive dimensions of SWB when examining the effects of daily events. Affective Events Theory proposes that daily events can elicit changes in momentary affective SWB, and that these changes can subsequently influence cognitive dimensions of SWB. For example, a conflict at work is expected to increase negative affect, which may subsequently reduce one's job satisfaction.

Affective Events Theory thus implies that daily events may be more strongly associated with affective than with cognitive SWB because changes in affective SWB are theorized to represent a more proximal response to daily events.

Empirical Research on Daily Events and SWB

Building on these theoretical frameworks, an extensive body of research has examined whether and how daily events are associated with SWB (e.g., Kanner et al., 1981; Nezlek, 2005; Toma et al., 2025). Early work primarily relied on cross-sectional designs and retrospective reports of daily events that occurred over the past days or weeks (e.g., DeLongis et al., 1988; Kanner et al., 1981; McCullough et al., 2000). More recently, researchers have increasingly adopted intensive longitudinal designs, in which participants repeatedly report the occurrence of daily events and their momentary SWB once or multiple times per day over periods of several days or weeks (e.g., Mey et al., 2023; Sin et al., 2017; Toma et al., 2025).

In these intensive longitudinal studies, daily events are commonly assessed using either event checklists or open-response questions, in which participants are asked to describe, for example, the most important daily hassle or uplift that occurred that day (e.g., Kiang & Buchanan, 2014; Klaiber et al., 2024; Scheibe, 2021). In some cases, participants are also asked to rate objective or subjective characteristics of the reported daily events, such as their severity or subjective importance (e.g., Dunkley et al., 2014; Mey et al., 2023; Toma et al., 2025).

Intensive longitudinal methods, including daily diary and experience sampling methods, offer several advantages for investigating the associations between daily events and SWB. First, asking participants about daily events and SWB in the preceding hours or day reduces recall bias and enhances ecological validity compared to measures focusing on longer time intervals (Almeida et al., 2011; Bolger et al., 2003; Raphael et al., 1991). Second, repeated assessments of daily events and SWB within the same individuals allow distinguishing within-from between-person associations. Within-person associations capture how experiencing more or fewer daily events than usual is associated with momentary SWB fluctuations within the same individual, whereas between-person associations capture how individuals who differ in their typical exposure to daily events also differ in their typical level of SWB (Almeida, 2005; Reis & Gable, 2000). This distinction is important because theoretical frameworks on how daily events contribute to fluctuations in momentary SWB primarily make claims about within-person processes rather than between-person differences (e.g., Almeida, 2005; Lazarus & Folkman, 1984; Sheldon et al., 2013; Weiss & Cropanzano, 1996). Third, repeated measurement in intensive longitudinal studies allows examining the temporal ordering of daily events and SWB, bringing research closer to establishing causality. Specifically, rather than only observing that daily events and SWB co-occur, prospective associations can be tested, such as whether experiencing a daily event at a particular moment or on a particular day is linked to higher or lower SWB at subsequent moments or the following day (Stawski et al., 2023; Witzel et al., 2023; Witzel & Stawski, 2021).

Building on these advantages, several intensive longitudinal studies have examined the associations between daily events and SWB. These studies converge on a relatively intuitive pattern: at both the within-and the between-person level, daily uplifts are positively associated with SWB, whereas daily hassles are negatively associated with SWB (e.g., Demerouti & Cropanzano, 2017; Doorley & Kashdan, 2021; Klaiber et al., 2024; Longua et al., 2009; Mosteo et al., 2023; Wirth et al., 2025). However, despite this consistency in the general direction of the associations, their strength varies considerably across studies. Thus, although daily events appear to be relevant for SWB, key questions remain about the magnitude of these associations, the conditions under which they are more or less pronounced, and the level of analysis at which daily events are most predictive of SWB.

The growing number of intensive longitudinal studies provides a strong empirical basis for a quantitative synthesis of the associations between daily events and SWB. Such a synthesis can advance the literature in several important ways. First, a meta-analysis provides robust estimates of the strength of the associations between daily events and SWB. This can help to clarify whether daily hassles or uplifts are more strongly associated with SWB, and whether these associations primarily unfold within or between individuals, or both. Second, a comprehensive meta-analysis can inform about the relevance of daily events in the broader context of other established predictors of SWB such as personality traits (e.g., Anglim et al., 2020), thereby helping to evaluate their relative contribution to explain differences in SWB.

Third, a meta-analysis can provide a strong test of theoretical predictions derived from existing frameworks concerning daily events and SWB (see Table 1 for an overview). Fourth, a meta-analysis would enable a systematic examination of moderators that may explain heterogeneity in daily event-SWB associations within and across studies.

Potential Moderators

Several study, sample, and assessment characteristics may explain why the associations between daily events and SWB differ within and across studies. Here, we examined 12 potential moderators derived from theory and empirical research on daily events and SWB (e.g., Almeida, 2005; Almeida et al., 2023; Lazarus, 1991; Sin & Almeida, 2018).

Publication Year.

The methods to study daily events and SWB have evolved substantially over the past decades. For example, early intensive longitudinal studies relied on diary-booklets or telephone interviews (e.g., Bolger et al., 1989; Emmons, 1991; Peeters et al., 1995a), whereas more recent studies increasingly used mobile devices to capture daily events and momentary SWB (e.g., Forchuk et al., 2026; Lawhead et al., 2026; Toma et al., 2025). Beyond the mode of assessment, other methodological aspects such as the frequency and the timing of assessments may have changed over time. Such changes could influence the characteristics and quality of the resulting data, including participant burden, compliance, and socially desirable reporting (Bolger et al., 2003; Stone et al., 2002). Consequently, daily event-SWB associations may differ across intensive longitudinal studies conducted in different periods. To test this possibility, we examined publication year as a potential moderator of the associations between daily events and SWB.

Sample Characteristics.

Consistent with theoretical predictions of the Daily Stress Process Model (Almeida, 2005), we examined whether the associations between daily events and SWB varied as a function of four sample characteristics: sample type, gender composition, mean age, and region of data collection. First, convenience and college samples represent distinct participant populations that may differ in characteristics relevant to the exposure and reaction to daily events, including educational background and everyday life contexts (Almeida, 2005; Almeida et al., 2023; Grzywacz et al., 2004). We thus investigated whether daily event-SWB associations differed between convenience and college samples to assess the extent to which these associations generalize across different populations.

Second, women tend to report more daily hassles and uplifts than men and may also show greater SWB reactivity to daily events (Almeida et al., 2023; Almeida & Kessler, 1998; Sin & Almeida, 2018; Witzel et al., 2023). We therefore examined whether the percentage of females in a sample moderated the associations between daily events and SWB.

Third, the frequency of daily hassles tends to decrease with age, whereas the frequency of daily uplifts tends to increase (Almeida et al., 2023; Mroczek & Almeida, 2004; Sin & Almeida, 2018). In addition, older adults are proposed to show lower emotional reactivity to daily events (e.g., Bleidorn & Hopwood, 2024; Carstensen, 1992; Carstensen et al., 1999). Accordingly, we tested whether daily event-SWB associations varied depending on sample mean age.

Finally, because cultural or contextual factors are expected to shape both the experience and appraisal of daily events and SWB itself, associations between daily events and SWB may also differ across regions of data collection (e.g., Hochschild, 1979; Hofstede, 1997; Kitayama et al., 2000; Lazarus, 1991). For example, Oishi et al. (2007) suggested that daily uplifts are more strongly associated with life satisfaction in Asian than in Western cultures because average life satisfaction is lower in Asia and people with lower life satisfaction may benefit more from individual uplifts. However, this has rarely been tested directly, underscoring the need for a meta-analytic synthesis.

Event Characteristics. We also considered three characteristics of daily events as potential moderators of daily event-SWB associations. First, the Daily Stress Process Model and prior research suggest that associations between daily events and SWB may depend on the type of events experienced (e.g., Almeida, 2005; Davis, 2011; Nezlek, 2005; Nezlek et al., 2008). However, evidence regarding which types of events show stronger associations is inconclusive. Whereas some studies stress the importance of social hassles for SWB relative to work-or achievement-related hassles (e.g., Almeida et al., 2002; Bolger et al., 1989; McIntyre et al., 2008; Pollock et al., 2016; Udayar et al., 2023), a few others point to the opposite pattern (Davis, 2011; Santiago et al., 2017). We therefore examined whether the strength of the associations varied across different types of daily events.

Second, differences in the assessment method of daily events may influence the strength of their associations with momentary SWB. For example, predefined event checklists are a common and efficient approach to assess whether daily events occurred but may overlook idiosyncratic details and contextual nuance (e.g., Harkness & Monroe, 2016; Monroe, 2008; Santee et al., 2023). In contrast, open-response questions assess richer contextual information but require additional coding and may be limited to particularly salient or impactful events. Finally, interview methods allow researchers to comprehensively assess subjective and objective characteristics of daily events (Almeida et al., 2002), but are more resource-intensive and may be less feasible for intensive longitudinal studies. These differences in how daily events are assessed may influence their associations with SWB. We therefore examined whether daily event-SWB associations differed across checklists or questionnaires, open-response measures, and interview methods.

Third, the associations between daily events and momentary SWB may also depend on how daily events are operationalized, that is, how daily events are quantified. This may involve binary indicators (i.e.,0 = no event occurred,1 = at least one event occurred), numeric counts of events, or scale scores that combine event occurrence with subjective perception of events. Numeric counts may show stronger daily event-SWB associations than dichotomous measures because they capture more variation in the number of events that occurred. Furthermore, drawing on theoretical frameworks such as the Transactional Model of Stress and Coping (e.g., Lazarus, 1991) or the Daily Stress Process Model (Almeida, 2005), combined scale scores may show stronger associations with SWB than dichotomous or numeric count measures because they capture additional variation in how individuals perceive daily events (e.g., perceived severity or importance). We therefore investigated whether the associations between daily events and SWB differed depending on whether daily events were operationalized as dichotomous, numeric count, or scale score.

SWB Characteristics.

Previous research found that SWB dimensions differ in their associations with daily events (e.g., Mosteo et al., 2023; Newman et al., 2020; Zautra et al., 2005). Specifically, daily hassles seem to be particularly strongly related to negative affect (e.g., Lin & Lachman, 2021; Monroy et al., 2021; Mosteo et al., 2023; Zautra et al., 2005), whereas daily uplifts showed particularly strong relations with positive affect (e.g., Gallaty & Zimmer-Gembeck, 2008; Mosteo et al., 2023; Wirth et al., 2025). Furthermore, Affective Events Theory implies that the associations of daily events with affective dimensions of SWB may exceed those with cognitive dimensions of SWB (Weiss & Cropanzano, 1996). Drawing on this, we examined whether the strength of daily event-SWB associations differed across positive affect, negative affect, life satisfaction, domain satisfaction, and global well-being.

Moreover, measurement error may attenuate daily-event-SWB associations (e.g., Wiernik & Dahlke, 2020). Therefore, we further investigated whether the strength of the associations between daily events and SWB varied as a function of the reliability of the SWB measure.

Study Design.

We examined two study design characteristics as potential moderators of the associations between daily events and momentary SWB. First, we examined differences between studies using daily diary versus experience sampling methods.

Specifically, daily event-SWB associations could be stronger in experience sampling studies, because their more frequent assessments may enable these studies to better capture momentary associations between daily events and SWB, whereas daily diary studies may be more susceptible to recall biases (Bolger et al., 2003). At the same time, aggregate end-of-day measures as typically used in daily diary studies may yield stronger associations because they can capture the cumulative impact of multiple hassles or uplifts over the course of the day and may be disproportionately influenced by particularly salient events.

Second, we examined whether correlation lag moderated the within-person associations between daily events and momentary SWB. Although theoretical frameworks have traditionally conceptualized associations between daily events and SWB as relatively immediate (e.g., Almeida, 2005), more recent extensions of the Daily Stress Process Model suggest that these associations may persist beyond the moment or day on which they occur (Almeida et al., 2026). Consistent with this possibility, some empirical studies have reported prospective associations between daily events assessed at a specific moment or on a specific day and SWB at subsequent moments or the following day (e.g., Stawski et al., 2022; Witzel et al., 2023; Witzel & Stawski, 2021). We therefore compared the magnitude of concurrent and prospective within-person associations to examine whether associations between daily events and SWB are primarily immediate or persist to the subsequent measurement occasion.

The Present Study

In the present meta-analysis, we synthesized evidence from intensive longitudinal studies on the associations between daily events and SWB. We had three goals: (1) to quantify the within-person associations between daily events and momentary SWB; (2) to quantify the between-person associations between daily events and SWB; and (3) to identify study, sample, and assessment characteristics that moderate these associations.

Building on previous research and theoretical frameworks (e.g., Almeida, 2005; Sheldon et al., 2013; Sin et al., 2020), we tested two broad confirmatory hypotheses. First, we hypothesized that daily uplifts would be positively associated with SWB at both the withinand between-person level (Hypothesis 1). Second, we hypothesized that daily hassles would be negatively related to SWB at both the within-and between-person level (Hypothesis 2).

Inclusion Criteria

We included studies in the present meta-analysis if they fulfilled the following nine inclusion criteria. First, the study used an empirical, quantitative research design (i.e., no review, comment, or qualitative study). Second, the study focused on human subjects. Third, the study was written in English or German (i.e., languages the authors are familiar with).

Fourth, the study used a daily diary or experience sampling design. Fifth, daily events were assessed via self-report at least once per day. Sixth, SWB was assessed via self-report at least once per day. Seventh, the study did not focus on a clinical sample and was not an intervention study. Eighth, the study provided sufficient information to compute effect sizes (i.e., correlation and corresponding sample size). When a study met all eligibility criteria but did not report sufficient information to compute effect sizes, we contacted the corresponding author to request the required information. 1 Ninth, the study included a unique sample that was not already represented in the meta-analytic dataset. In cases where multiple studies used the same or overlapping datasets, we retained the publication that reported the largest sample size and the greatest number of relevant effect sizes.

Coding

Coding was conducted in a three-step procedure by four independent coders (three doctoral students, one postdoctoral researcher). In a first step, we screened titles and abstracts to identify studies eligible for full-text assessment. In a second step, we screened the full texts of the identified studies to determine eligibility for inclusion. In both of these steps, 100 studies were double coded to examine interrater agreement. Interrater agreement was 89% during title and abstract screening and 86% during full-text screening. Disagreements were resolved through discussion. In a third step, we extracted information on the study, study design, assessment, and effect size characteristics from eligible studies. Regarding the sample, we coded the year of data collection, the name of the dataset, whether the sample was a convenience, college, or representative sample, the percentage of female participants, the mean age, the country of data collection, and the ethnic composition of the sample. For the daily event assessment, we coded the valence and type of the assessed daily events, the measure name, type, and reliability, the operationalization of daily events, and the number of assessments per day and across the entire study period. For the SWB assessment, we coded the assessed SWB dimension, the measure name and reliability, whether the measure was reverse-coded, and the number of SWB assessments per day and across the entire study period. Regarding the effect size, we coded the sample size, the number of observations (for within-person effect sizes only), the compliance rate, whether the effect size was estimated at the within-or between-person level, the effect size estimate, and whether it represented a concurrent or prospective association. Finally, for the study design information, we coded whether the study used a daily diary or experience sampling design and whether it consisted of a single assessment period or multiple bursts. Data Analysis

Data analyses for the present meta-analysis were conducted in R (Version 4.5.2) using the metafor package (Viechtbauer, 2010).

Calculation of Effect Sizes

Effect sizes of interest were within-person and between-person correlation coefficients. We extracted Pearson's product moment correlations when daily events were operationalized as continuous variables (e.g., scale score reflecting daily event occurrence and severity or numeric count reflecting event frequency) and point-biserial correlations when daily events were operationalized as dichotomous variables (e.g., whether a daily event occurred or not). We transformed correlation coefficients to Fisher's z prior to meta-analytic aggregation using the escalc()-function from metafor. After the meta-analytic aggregation, we back-transformed Fisher's z effect sizes to correlation coefficients to facilitate interpretation.

Meta-Analytic Procedure

We conducted four separate random-effects meta-analytic models. Specifically, we estimated within-person and between-person associations with SWB separately for daily uplifts and daily hassles. Individual studies often contributed multiple effect sizes corresponding to different combinations of daily event types and SWB dimensions. To account for the resulting dependency among these effect sizes, we applied a three-step procedure recommended by Viechtbauer (2010) and Pustejovsky and Tipton (2022). In a first step, we estimated the approximate variance-covariance matrix of the dependent effect sizes using the vcalc()-function from metafor, based on literature-informed working assumptions on the dependency of different daily event types and SWB dimensions from existing studies 2.

Specifically, within each well-being dimension, we assumed a correlation of ρ1 = .30 for different types of daily hassles and a correlation of ρ1 = .35 for different types of daily uplifts (e.g., Newman et al., 2023; Newman & Nezlek, 2022; Nezlek et al., 2018). Across SWB dimensions, we assumed a correlation of ρ2 = .40 for both daily hassles and daily uplifts (e.g., Anglim & Grant, 2016; Busseri, 2025; Lucas et al., 1996). In addition, because several studies also reported both concurrent and prospective associations, we specified a temporal autocorrelation of φ = .55 for SWB (e.g., Newman et al., 2023; Newman & Nezlek, 2022; Nezlek et al., 2018). Second, we estimated multilevel random-effects meta-analytic models based on this approximate variance-covariance matrix using the rma.mv()-function from metafor, with effect sizes nested in studies. In all models, Fisher's z transformed effect sizes were weighted by the inverse of their sampling variance so that coefficients estimated with a higher precision received more weight. Third, we estimated cluster-robust standard errors as implemented in the R package clubSandwich (Pustejovsky, 2025). Importantly, the working assumptions specified in the first step affect the weighting of effect sizes and, consequently, the efficiency of the meta-analytic estimates. More accurate assumptions about the correlations can therefore yield more precise estimates. However, the cluster-robust standard errors estimated in the third step provide valid confidence intervals and significance tests even if these correlations are misspecified (Pustejovsky & Tipton, 2022).

Publication Bias

Because a large amount of studies included in this meta-analysis were not designed to primarily test associations between daily events and SWB, we did not expect strong effects of publication bias. Nevertheless, we evaluated potential publication bias using three methods.

First, we visually inspected funnel plots for patterns that may indicate small-study effects. Funnel plots display the relationship between the effect sizes and their corresponding standard errors. Asymmetry in funnel plots indicates that smaller, less precise studies tend to report different effect sizes than larger, more precise studies. Importantly, such asymmetry is not specific to publication bias but may also reflect genuine between-study heterogeneity, systematic differences between smaller and larger studies, or sampling variability (Sterne et al., 2011). Thus, we interpreted funnel-plot asymmetry as potentially indicating small-study effects that may reflect publication bias.

Second, we performed Egger's Regression Tests (Egger et al., 1997) by including the standard error of the effect sizes into the meta-analytic models as a moderator. A statistically significant regression coefficient for the standard error indicates that studies with less precise estimates tend to report larger effect sizes, being an indication for small-study effects or publication bias.

Third, we used the PET-PEESE approach outlined by Stanley and Doucouliagos (2014) to estimate a bias-corrected effect size. PET-PEESE combines the precision-effect test (PET) and the precision-effect estimate with standard error (PEESE), which perform differently depending on the size of the true effect. PET is more accurate when the true effect is zero, whereas PEESE performs better when the true effect is nonzero. PET-PEESE resolves this limitation by selecting the intercept from either the PET or PEESE model as a bias-corrected estimate, depending on whether the PET intercept is statistically significantly different from zero or not.

Descriptive Statistics

Overall, our meta-analytic dataset included 217 intensive longitudinal studies, comprising 260 samples and a total of 48,311 participants. These studies contributed a total of 836 within-person and 897 between-person correlations between daily events and SWB.

Most of the included studies used a daily diary design (79%). On average, studies assessed daily events 1.51 times (SD = 1.41) per day and 20.61 times (SD = 23.30) across the study period. The included studies were published between 1991 and 2026 and the majority of them were journal articles (91%). The mean participant age across samples was 31.70 years (SD = 15.65). Samples included slightly more female participants on average (M = 62%, SD = 25%) and were mostly non-representative, convenience samples (70%). Samples were recruited across North America (65%), Northern Europe (17%), Asia (12%), Southern Europe (3%), and Australia (3%).

For daily uplifts, we included 50 studies and 305 effect sizes for the within-person association with SWB, and 74 studies and 270 effect sizes for the corresponding betweenperson association. For daily hassles, we included 119 studies and 531 effect sizes for the within-person association and 191 studies and 627 effect sizes for the corresponding between-person associations. Moderation Analyses

We further examined whether study, sample, or assessment characteristics moderated the associations between daily events and SWB. Detailed results of these moderation analyses are provided in Table 4 and Section 4 of the http://pasa.psycharchives.org/reviewonly/33a8afedfcb9a86ba04d0f2bc883b48d76d0c15705a07969bd0245d510577af0. Figures 3 and 4 illustrate predicted correlations based on the estimated moderator coefficients. In the following, we summarize the most important findings, focusing primarily on those that consistently emerged at both the within-and between-person level.

First, the region of data collection emerged as a significant moderator of the associations between daily hassles and SWB. Specifically, predicted correlations were significantly stronger in Northern Europe (within-person: -.24; between-person: r = -.36) than in Northern America (within-person: r = -.16; between-person: r = -.26) and Asia (within-person: r = -.15; between-person: r = -.25), suggesting that cultural or contextual factors may shape how strongly daily hassles are linked to SWB.

Second, event type significantly moderated the strength of the within-person associations between daily uplifts and SWB and both the within-and between-person associations between daily hassles and SWB. Predicted correlations were significantly stronger for social uplifts (within-person: r = .21) and general uplifts (within-person: r = .19) than for work-/achievement-related uplifts (within-person: r = .10). In contrast, predicted correlations were significantly stronger for general hassles (within-person: r = -.22; betweenperson: r = -.31), than for work-/achievement-related hassles (within-person: r = -.17; between-person: r = -.26), social hassles (within-person: r = -.13; between-person: r = -.25), and health-related hassles (within-person: r = -.14; between-person: r = -.20).

Together, these findings indicate that not all types of daily events are equally strongly linked to SWB and that the relevance of event type may depend on event valence, with social events showing the strongest associations with SWB when positive and work-/achievement-related events showing strongest associations with SWB when negative.

Third, the associations between daily uplifts and SWB were significantly moderated by how daily uplifts were measured and operationalized. Predicted correlations were significantly stronger when daily uplifts were assessed using checklists/questionnaires (within-person: r = .16; between-person: r = .22) compared with interviews (within-person: r = .07; between-person: r = .01). Furthermore, scale-based operationalizations (within-person: r = .15; between-person: r = .22) and numeric counts (within-person: r = .15; between-person: r = .25) of daily uplifts showed significantly stronger predicted correlations with SWB than dichotomous indicators (within-person: r = .08; between-person: r = .13). For daily hassles, the pattern was similar, although the difference did not reach statistical significance between scale-based operationalizations and numeric counts and at the betweenperson level. Specifically, scale-based operationalizations (within-person: r = -.20) of daily hassles showed significantly stronger predicted correlations with SWB than dichotomous indicators (within-person: r = -.15). The consistently stronger associations observed for scale-based operationalizations are consistent with theoretical frameworks emphasizing the importance of subjective perception and appraisal for the effects of daily events on SWB (Almeida, 2005; Lazarus, 1991; Lazarus & Folkman, 1984), as scale-based operationalizations can capture individuals' subjective perceptions of events, whereas dichotomous indicators capture only their occurrence.

Fourth, the dimension of SWB emerged as one of the most important moderators of all associations between daily events and SWB and explained a considerable amount of within-study heterogeneity (ΔI 2 Within = 5.80-32.10). Predicted correlations between daily uplifts and SWB were significantly smaller for negative affect (within-person: r = .10; between-person: r = .03, reverse coded) than for positive affect (within-person: r = .22; between-person: r = .35). In contrast, predicted correlations between daily hassles and SWB were significantly stronger for negative affect (within-person: r = -.24; between-person: r = -.41, reverse coded) than for life satisfaction (within-person: r = -.16; between-person: r = -.15) and positive affect (within-person: r = -.11; between-person: r = -.13). Daily hassles thus seem to be more closely tied to individuals' negative than positive emotional experiences, whereas the opposite pattern emerges for daily uplifts.

Fifth, the reliability of the SWB measure was as a significant moderator of all daily event-SWB associations-although partly in unexpected directions. Specifically, for daily hassles, estimated correlations with SWB were stronger for more reliable measures (withinperson: r = .10 at -1 SD of reliability vs. r = .18 at +1 SD; between-person: r = .06 at -1 SD of reliability vs. .30 at +1 SD), whereas, for daily hassles, estimated correlations were weaker for more reliable measures (within-person: r = -.20 at -1 SD of reliability vs. -.17 at +1 SD; between-person: r = -.33 at -1 SD of reliability vs. .23 at +1 SD).

Based on classical measurement theory, one would expect that more reliable measures show stronger correlations. One possible explanation for the unexpected finding for daily hassles could be that differences in reliability are confounded with differences in the examined SWB dimension, as negative affect measures were slightly less reliable on average than positive affect measures in the studies included in the present meta-analysis.

Finally, correlation lag emerged as the strongest moderator of the within-person associations between daily events and SWB and explained a substantial amount of withinstudy heterogeneity (daily uplifts: ΔI 2 Within = 9.25; daily hassles: ΔI 2 Within = 32.91). In contrast to concurrent correlations (daily uplifts: r = .21; daily hassles: r = -.22), prospective correlations (i.e., between daily events at one time point and SWB at the subsequent time point) were close to zero (daily uplifts: r = .04; daily hassles: r = -.03). As most of the prospective correlations (85%) stemmed from daily diary studies, this pattern implies that associations between daily events and SWB may be largely limited to the same day, with little evidence that these associations persist into the following day. To further examine the extent to which daily event-SWB associations persist, we conducted two exploratory analyses: one restricted to daily diary studies reporting prospective correlations between daily events and SWB one day later, and one restricted to experience sampling studies reporting prospective correlations between daily events and SWB assessed later the same day (see Section 4 of the http://pasa.psycharchives.org/reviewonly/33a8afedfcb9a86ba04d0f2bc883b48d76d0c15705a07969bd0245d510577af0). Prospective correlations in the daily diary studies were comparable to those observed in the main analysis, further suggesting that daily event-SWB associations are primarily contemporaneous, with limited evidence of persistence into the following day. However, prospective correlations in the experience sampling studies were also broadly similar to those in the main analysis, despite SWB being assessed only a few hours after the daily event. Thus, these findings suggest that daily events are most strongly associated with SWB concurrently, with only limited evidence that these associations persist when SWB is assessed several hours later or on the following day.

In addition to testing each moderator separately, we also estimated meta-analytic models including all moderators simultaneously. Detailed results of these multivariate moderation analyses are provided in Section 4 of the http://pasa.psycharchives.org/reviewonly/33a8afedfcb9a86ba04d0f2bc883b48d76d0c15705a07969bd0245d510577af0. Although several moderators were no longer statistically significant after including others, three moderators remained robust. First, daily event type remained a significant moderator of the associations between daily hassles and SWB. Second, the dimension of SWB emerged as a significant moderator of all associations between both daily uplifts and daily hassles and SWB. Third, correlation lag remained the strongest moderator of the within-person associations of daily uplifts and daily hassles with SWB. Notably, the moderation by SWB reliability was no longer significant for any daily event-SWB associations after adjustment, which is consistent with the possibility that the initial moderation findings primarily reflected differences in the SWB dimension assessed. Publication Bias

We used three methods to evaluate whether findings of the present meta-analysis were influenced by publication bias (see Section 5 in the http://pasa.psycharchives.org/reviewonly/33a8afedfcb9a86ba04d0f2bc883b48d76d0c15705a07969bd0245d510577af0 for funnel plots and further details). First, funnel plots indicated no marked asymmetry indicative of smallstudy effects or potential publication bias. Second, across all four meta-analytic models, Egger's regression tests (Egger et al., 1997) suggested that the standard error was not a significant moderator of the effect sizes, providing limited evidence for small-study effects or publication bias. Third, we estimated bias-corrected effect sizes using the PET-PEESE approach (Stanley & Doucouliagos, 2014). Because the PET intercept was significantly different from zero for all four meta-analytic models, we used the corresponding PEESE intercepts as bias-corrected estimates. These differed only minimally from the original metaanalytic correlations (Δr ≤ .02), providing limited evidence that small-study effects or potential publication bias materially affected the estimated effect sizes and the overall conclusions.

Discussion

Psychological theories and common intuition converge on the notion that daily events matter for how individuals feel. But how much do they actually matter and under what circumstances? The present meta-analysis provides the first comprehensive synthesis of the associations between daily events and subjective well-being (SWB). Integrating data from 217 intensive longitudinal studies, we found robust evidence that daily uplifts are positively associated with higher SWB, both within (r = .16) and between persons (r = .21). Likewise, we found that daily hassles are negatively associated with lower SWB, both within (r = -.18) and between persons (r = -.28). We further identified several methodological and contextual factors that moderated these associations, such as the type of event and the SWB dimension examined. Finally, we found that the effects of daily events were largely immediate, with little carry-over to subsequent moments or days. In the following, we discuss the implications of these findings for theoretical accounts of daily events and SWB, the role of methodological and contextual factors in shaping daily event-SWB associations, and directions for future research.

How Important Are Daily Events for Within-and Between-Person Differences in SWB?

The present meta-analysis found robust associations between daily events and SWB both within and between persons. At the within-person level, daily uplifts showed small-tomedium positive association with momentary SWB, whereas daily hassles showed small-tomedium negative associations with momentary SWB. Thus, at moments or on days when individuals experience more daily uplifts than usual, they tend to report higher SWB than is typical for them; conversely, at moments or on days when they experience more daily hassles than usual, they tend to report lower SWB than is typical for them. These within-person associations provide strong empirical support for theories highlighting the importance of daily hassles and uplifts for fluctuations in momentary SWB (e.g., Almeida, 2005; Lazarus, 1991; Weiss & Cropanzano, 1996). Furthermore, they suggest that daily uplifts and daily hassles are similarly relevant to within-person fluctuations in momentary SWB. Specifically, experiencing more daily uplifts or hassles than usual was associated with comparable differences in momentary SWB. This highlights the need to consider both daily uplifts and daily hassles in research on momentary SWB, which has predominantly focused on hassles (e.g., Sin & Almeida, 2018; Zautra et al., 2005). At the between-person level, daily uplifts showed a medium positive association with SWB, whereas daily hassles showed a mediumto-large negative association with SWB. Thus, individuals who generally experience more daily uplifts also tend to report higher SWB on average, whereas those who generally experience more daily hassles tend to report lower SWB on average.

The present findings allow us to compare the relevance of daily events to other correlates of SWB. Table 5 provides an overview of between-person associations for established correlates of SWB, including personality traits, socio-economic status, and social context, as reported in existing meta-analytic research (e.g., Anglim et al., 2020; Krämer et al., 2026; Tan et al., 2020). Between-person associations between daily events and SWB were of a similar magnitude as those reported for leisure engagement and subjective financial status and larger than those reported for openness and time spent in solitude (Anglim et al., 2020; Krämer et al., 2026; Kuykendall et al., 2015; Tan et al., 2020). Individual differences in the experience of daily uplifts and hassles are thus meaningfully associated with SWB, with associations comparable in magnitude to those of several established SWB correlates. For within-person associations between daily events and SWB, few meta-analytic benchmarks are available, as the present study is among the first to synthesize evidence on within-person predictors of momentary SWB. One directly comparable meta-analysis reported within-person associations of r = .13 between physical activity and SWB and r = -.19 between sedentary time and SWB (Bourke, Judge-Mears, et al., 2026). Thus, the observed within-person associations between daily events and SWB were slightly larger than that for physical activity and comparable to that for sedentary time.

When directly comparing between-person and within-person associations between daily events and SWB, our findings suggest that between-person differences in the typical experience of daily uplifts and hassles are more strongly associated with average SWB than within-person deviations from these typical experiences are associated with momentary fluctuations in SWB. A possible explanation for this pattern is that between-person associations partly reflect stable characteristics that influence both daily event experiences and SWB. Specifically, personality traits or broader life circumstances may shape how often people experience daily uplifts and hassles while also contributing to their average levels of SWB, thereby strengthening the associations between daily events and SWB at the betweenperson level. For example, neuroticism has been associated with greater exposure to daily hassles, greater reporting of daily hassles, and lower SWB (e.g., Bolger & Schilling, 1991; Haehner et al., 2026; Monroe, 2008; Suls & Martin, 2005; Wenzel et al., 2026). Similarly, life circumstances such as stressful jobs and strained romantic relationships may increase exposure to daily hassles (Almeida, 2005; Almeida et al., 2023) and are associated with lower SWB on average (Charles et al., 2021). Thus, between-person associations may overstate the extent to which daily events themselves are related to SWB because part of the observed associations may be due to other factors that influence both daily event experiences and SWB.

Even though within-person associations tended to be smaller, they may be of higher practical and theoretical relevance. Within-person associations are less susceptible to confounding by stable between-person differences. Furthermore, they align more closely with theoretical perspectives that emphasize how daily events contribute to fluctuations in SWB and thus provide a more direct test of these perspectives (e.g., Almeida, 2005; Lazarus, 1991; Sheldon et al., 2013). Nevertheless, concurrent within-person associations cannot establish the direction of effects. Daily events may influence SWB, SWB may influence exposure to or experiences of daily events, or both processes may operate simultaneously. Prospective within-person associations can help disentangle these possibilities by examining whether daily events predict subsequent SWB.

Do Associations Between Daily Events and SWB Persist Over Time?

To clarify the direction and temporal persistence of daily event-SWB associations, we examined prospective within-person associations between daily events and subsequent SWB.

These prospective associations were significantly weaker than the concurrent associations and close to zero. This pattern held for both experience sampling studies, which typically used lags of several hours, and daily diary studies, which typically used a 1-day lag. Thus, although daily events are meaningfully associated with momentary SWB when assessed concurrently, our findings suggest that their associations with subsequent SWB are substantially weaker. This contradicts extensions of the Daily Stress Process Model (Almeida, 2005; Almeida et al., 2026), which propose that associations between daily events and SWB can carry over to subsequent days. Furthermore, these findings raise important questions for future research and theory about how daily event-SWB associations unfold over time and what accounts for these temporal dynamics.

One possibility is that adaptive emotion regulation processes lead to quick recovery from daily hassles or adaptation to daily uplifts, such that SWB returns toward its usual level relatively quickly (e.g., Kuppens & Verduyn, 2015; Sheldon et al., 2013; Suls et al., 1998).

For example, experiencing a conflict at work may temporarily lower SWB, but effective emotion regulation may help individuals recover quickly, such that their SWB returns toward its usual level within a few hours (Kaurin et al., 2026). Thus, the small prospective associations observed do not necessarily imply little impact of daily events on SWB. Instead, they may reflect an inappropriate time span for capturing daily event effects. Another possibility is that prospective associations partly reflect other factors that influence subsequent SWB. For example, other daily events, social interactions, physical activity, or sleep may introduce additional variability in momentary SWB, thereby weakening associations with an earlier daily event (Bourke, Harrison, et al., 2026; Bourke, Judge-Mears, et al., 2026; Liu et al., 2019). Taken together, the small prospective associations do not necessarily imply that daily events have little enduring relevance for SWB. Furthermore, even if associations between daily events and SWB dissipate relatively quickly, frequent exposure to such events may accumulate over time and contribute to longer-term differences in SWB (e.g., Almeida, 2005; Kanner et al., 1981; Monroe, 1983).

When Are Daily Events Most Strongly Associated With SWB?

Findings from the present meta-analysis indicate that daily events are robustly associated with both momentary fluctuations and average levels of SWB, with the strength of these associations varying by the level of analysis and the time lag between assessments. But do these associations also differ across study, sample, and assessment characteristics? Our findings suggest that daily event-SWB associations are largely consistent across samples but vary by daily event type, event operationalization, and SWB dimension. In the following, we discuss these findings in more detail and consider their implications for predictions derived from theoretical frameworks on daily events and SWB.

Who Is Most Sensitive to Daily Events?

Prior research and theoretical frameworks, including the Daily Stress Process Model, suggest that sociodemographic characteristics such as age, gender, and educational attainment shape how individuals' SWB responds to daily events (Almeida, 2005; Almeida et al., 2023; Carstensen, 1992). For example, younger adults may experience larger declines in SWB following daily hassles than older adults because of age-related differences in motivational priorities and emotion regulation strategies (e.g., Bleidorn & Hopwood, 2024; Carstensen, 1992). The present findings, however, provide little evidence that sociodemographic characteristics moderate the associations between daily events and SWB. Associations were similar across convenience and college samples, as well as across samples differing in gender composition and mean age. This may reflect limitations in how sociodemographic characteristics were examined in the present meta-analysis. Specifically, the distinction between convenience and college samples provides only a coarse proxy for everyday life contexts. Moreover, age and gender were examined at the sample rather than the individual level, potentially obscuring meaningful individual differences in daily event-SWB associations.

Among the sample characteristics, region of data collection was the only significant moderator: associations for daily hassles were stronger in Northern European than in North American and Asian samples. This may reflect broader cultural processes or norms that shape how daily hassles are experienced and evaluated. For example, cultures differ in the extent to which hassles are attended to, interpreted in relation to the self and others, and translated into judgments of SWB (e.g., Hofstede, 1997; Kitayama et al., 2000; Lazarus, 1991). Such differences could affect how strongly similar hassles are associated with SWB across cultural contexts. Interestingly, no significant regional differences emerged for associations between daily uplifts and SWB. This contrasts with previous research suggesting that associations between daily uplifts and SWB are stronger in Asian cultures due to the lower average SWB leaving more room for improving SWB (Oishi et al., 2007). Given the small number of studies from some regions and the considerable heterogeneity within regions, however, this null finding should be interpreted cautiously.

Which Daily Events Matter Most for SWB?

Beyond individual characteristics, the nature of daily events may shape how strongly they are associated with SWB (e.g., Almeida, 2005; Almeida et al., 2002; McIntyre et al., 2008). Daily events can take many forms, including events at work, events involving social relationships, and events related to health. The Daily Stress Process Model (Almeida, 2005) implies that different types of events may differ in their associations with momentary SWB.

Consistent with this idea, several studies have suggested that social events may be particularly strongly associated with SWB (e.g., Bolger et al., 1989; McIntyre et al., 2008; Udayar et al., 2023). The present findings support theoretical predictions of the Daily Stress Process Model, as associations with SWB varied across different types of daily events.

However, no single event type (e.g., social events) was consistently most strongly associated with momentary SWB. Within individuals, daily uplifts showed stronger associations with SWB when they involved social experiences than when they involved work-or achievementrelated experiences. In contrast, daily hassles showed stronger associations with SWB when they involved work-or achievement-related experiences than when they involved social experiences. Thus, the relevance of different event types appears to depend on their valence: Social events may be particularly relevant when positive, whereas work-or achievementrelated events may be more important when negative. Existing theoretical accounts that emphasize the importance of event type may therefore be extended by considering event type and valence jointly.

How daily events were operationalized also mattered for their associations with SWB.

Overall, scale-based operationalizations of daily events showed stronger associations with SWB than dichotomous indicators, perhaps because they tend to capture both the occurrence and the subjective perception of events. This interpretation is consistent with the Transactional Model of Stress and Coping (e.g., Lazarus, 1991) and the Daily Stress Process Model (Almeida, 2005), which propose that the impact of daily hassles on SWB depends largely on how individuals appraise and perceive them. Thus, measures that capture subjective perceptions of daily events may be more sensitive to their implications for SWB than measures that simply indicate whether an event occurred. However, it should be noted that scale-based operationalizations that assess perceptions of daily events may partly overlap with SWB, which could inflate the observed associations between daily events and SWB (e.g., A. G. C. Wright et al., 2020). Interestingly, we further found that numeric counts of daily uplifts showed associations with SWB that were comparable to those observed for scale-based operationalizations. Thus, the frequency of daily uplifts may also matter for SWB, alongside individuals' subjective perceptions of them. For example, a particularly impactful uplift may be associated with SWB to a similar degree as several smaller uplifts.

Which Dimensions of SWB Are Most Strongly Associated with Daily Events?

SWB itself is a multidimensional construct (e.g., Diener, 1984; Diener et al., 1999; Lucas et al., 1996), and different dimensions of SWB may be differently associated with daily uplifts and hassles. Consistent with previous research (e.g., Kennedy-Moore et al., 1992; Klaiber et al., 2024; Mosteo et al., 2023), we found that daily uplifts were most strongly associated with positive affect with little effects on negative affect, whereas daily hassles were most strongly associated with negative affect with little effects on positive affect. Associations for life satisfaction and domain satisfaction fell in-between the effects observed for positive and negative affect.

This pattern suggests that daily uplifts contribute to positive emotional experiences without necessarily diminishing negative emotional experiences, just as experiencing daily hassles may add to negative emotional experiences without necessarily diminishing positive emotional experiences. Thus, our findings may be particularly relevant for understanding affective SWB as a multidimensional construct, with positive and negative affect not simply being opposite ends of a single continuum (e.g., Schimmack, 2007; Schimmack & Grob, 2000).

Furthermore, our findings are consistent with predictions from Affective Events Theory (Weiss & Cropanzano, 1996), as the strongest associations emerged for affective rather than cognitive dimensions of SWB. However, future research needs to test whether changes in affective SWB really mediate changes in cognitive dimensions of SWB.

Directions for Future Research

Beyond synthesizing the magnitude and examining the moderators of daily event-SWB associations, the present meta-analysis provides a unique perspective on the strengths and gaps of the existing literature on daily events as well as priorities for future research.

First, future research should pay closer attention to the timescales over which daily events are associated with SWB. Although our findings suggest that daily events are most strongly associated with concurrent SWB and less strongly with SWB several hours later or on the following day, little is known about when daily event-SWB associations peak or how quickly they decline. Most existing intensive longitudinal studies have focused on events occurring over the past few hours or the previous day, and theoretical frameworks have not yet specified the time interval over which these associations should persist (e.g., Almeida, 2005; Almeida et al., 2026). Future research should therefore examine the temporal trajectory of daily event-SWB associations in greater detail. One approach would be to use finer-grained designs with repeated SWB assessments with a high temporal resolution following daily events (e.g., Kaurin et al., 2026; Philippi et al., 2026) Second, despite the extensive body of intensive longitudinal research on daily events, there is little consensus on how to best conceptualize and measure daily events (Haehner et al., 2024; A. G. C. Wright et al., 2020). A related challenge is that what constitutes a daily uplift or hassle may depend on the person and the context. For example, experiencing a daily event such as meeting a friend for dinner may be perceived as an uplift in some instances but as neutral in others. Future research should work toward greater consensus on how daily events are defined and measured. At the same time, measures should clearly distinguish between objective characteristics of daily events and individuals' subjective perceptions of them to avoid confounding event characteristics and SWB (Almeida et al., 2002; Haehner et al., 2024; A. G. C. Wright et al., 2020).

Third, most previous research has examined daily events as broad categories and has rarely differentiated between more specific types of events. Although the present findings provide some initial insights by distinguishing between work-related, social, and healthrelated events, evidence on other types of events remains limited. Future research could use more differentiated taxonomies of daily events (e.g., Haehner et al., 2024) and examine how event type intersects with other event characteristics, particularly valence. Such research would clarify which daily events are most strongly associated with SWB.

Fourth, the majority of studies included in the present meta-analysis relied on relatively small convenience or college-student samples (Naverage = 175). Future research should investigate the associations between daily events and SWB in larger, more diverse, and population-representative samples to establish the generalizability of the present findings.

Moreover, 88% of the included studies were conducted in Western cultural contexts, leaving it unclear to what extent the observed daily event-SWB associations generalize to other cultures (e.g., Henrich et al., 2022; van de Vijver, 2013). Future research on daily events and SWB in non-Western cultures is required to better understand which findings are culturally universal and which are specific to Western countries.

Limitations

At least five limitations should be considered when interpreting the present findings.

First, although the present meta-analysis included 217 studies, more than 1,700 effect sizes, and over 48,000 participants, it was limited by the reporting of existing research. Several intensive longitudinal studies assessed daily events and dimensions of SWB but reported neither within-nor between-person correlations. Although we contacted the corresponding authors to request the relevant information, we were unable to include some potentially relevant studies due to missing information.

Second, the effect sizes examined in the present meta-analysis were correlations derived from intensive longitudinal studies using observational designs. Thus, the observed associations cannot establish whether daily events causally influence SWB, whether SWB influences the occurrence or perception of daily events, or whether both are influenced by individual differences in characteristics such as personality traits or life circumstances. To address this, future research should complement intensive longitudinal studies with experimental or quasi-experimental designs that allow for stronger inferences regarding causal direction of the associations between daily events and SWB.

Third, we developed a detailed working definition of what constitutes a daily event (see http://pasa.psycharchives.org/reviewonly/33a8afedfcb9a86ba04d0f2bc883b48d76d0c15705a07969bd0245d510577af0 for more details) to guide coding and data extraction, but some conceptual heterogeneity remains within our classification, which may have contributed to the observed heterogeneity in the meta-analytic estimates. Across studies, daily events were defined, measured, and operationalized in different ways, and it was not always clear whether a reported variable referred to an event itself or reflected a broader experience or situation. As in any meta-analysis, our classification thus involved a trade-off between inclusiveness and conceptual similarity across studies.

Fourth, we only examined sociodemographic characteristics, including age and gender, at the sample level and not at the individual level, limiting our moderation analyses.

Individual participant data (IPD) meta-analysis could provide an important next step in addressing this limitation (Riley et al., 2021). IPD meta-analysis would enable direct tests of individual-level moderation and thus provide a stronger test of theoretical predictions.

Fifth, some moderator categories were represented by only a small number of studies, which may have limited the precision of their estimates. For example, the region moderator included only a few studies based on samples from Australia, Southern Europe, and Asia.

Furthermore, the present meta-analysis could not examine several potentially important moderators identified in theoretical frameworks and previous research. For example, the associations between daily events and SWB may vary depending on psychosocial factors such as personality traits, specific characteristics of the daily events, and individuals' subjective appraisals (Almeida, 2005; Weiss & Cropanzano, 1996). However, the available literature did not provide sufficient and consistent information to examine these moderators systematically.

Conclusion

The present meta-analysis provides a comprehensive synthesis of the associations between daily events and SWB by integrating findings across existing intensive longitudinal research. Daily uplifts were robustly associated with higher SWB and daily hassles with lower SWB at both the within-and between-person level, with associations broadly comparable to those of established correlates of SWB. Importantly, the strength of these associations was not uniform but depended on several factors, including the type of daily events and the dimension of SWB assessed. Daily events also showed substantially stronger concurrent than prospective associations with SWB, suggesting that their links with SWB are relatively immediate and short-lived, with little carry-over to subsequent hours or days.

Together, these findings establish daily events as meaningful correlates of SWB and highlight the importance of when, what, and how daily events are assessed. Future research is needed to clarify the temporal dynamics of SWB-daily event associations, the generalizability across samples, and the causal relationships between daily events and SWB.
