Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. Full text

Abstract

Externalizing psychopathology is shaped by environmental and contextual factors, particularly those tied to disadvantaged backgrounds. There is insufficient work examining how broader contextual variables affect measurement models of latent externalizing. The current research examined the degree to which indicators traditionally used to model externalizing are similarly indicative of the latent construct across racial and contextual factors (i.e., metric and scalar invariance), using data from two studies of system-involved adults (Study 1: M age =36.5, 67% male; Study 2: M age =32.1, 62% male). In both studies, metric invariance generally held but not scalar invariance as a function of race/ethnicity and community-level racialized contexts, particularly for drug use, antisocial behavior and criminal justice-related indicators. Results suggest that contextual factors influence not only externalizing level but affect the measurement of the construct itself.

Context in System-Involved Adults

Externalizing behaviors like substance use, aggression, and antisocial behavior, are related to negative costs for individuals, communities, and society including exposure to violence, unemployment, family stress, and crime (Lahey et al., 2021). Psychology researchers have conducted work to understand propensities toward these behaviors, identifying a general liability towards impulsive, disruptive, and disinhibited behaviors that is broadly referred to as the externalizing (EXT) construct (Krueger et al., 2021). In understanding causal factors in EXT, the psychology field has particularly focused on individual-level explanations (e.g., traits, genes, etc. Moore, 2011), although pathways shaped by sociocultural influences are also acknowledged.

That is, important work highlights the roles of immediate environments (families, peers, neighborhoods) in the onset and manifestation of externalizing behaviors (Beyers et al., 2003; Dishion & Owen, 2002) and have shown that these environments intersect with individual-level propensities to influence EXT outcomes (Hicks et al., 2009).

At the same time, work examining how broader contextual variables affect the measurement of latent EXT itself is lacking. In a review of multiple literatures, Verona and Fox (2025) highlight that the psychological literatures on EXT psychopathology and research focusing on systemic (e.g., criminal justice) disparities often operate in parallel without integration, potentially leading to measurement models that risk misattributing context-driven behaviors to individual pathology. For example, criminal behavior is commonly used as an indicator of EXT (Krueger et al., 2007; Venables et al., 2012); however, police contact or arrests may be driven by criminal justice system policies, regardless of individual-level traits (Kochel et al., 2011; Lytle, 2014). Failing to attend to potential measurement bias risks overestimating individual trait propensities and underestimating community and systemic processes. Across two studies, we extend Verona and Fox's (2025) framework to evaluate the extent to which racial and contextual variables may alter the interpretation of the measurement of latent EXT.

Externalizing Background

Latent EXT references deficits in impulse control and general tendencies to "act out", modeled with overt problem behaviors as observable indicators of the latent construct (e.g., violence, inattention, aggression; Krueger et al., 2021). Traditional measurement models of psychopathology have included an EXT factor alongside internalizing (e.g., anxiety and depression) in correlated factor models to represent distinct types of psychopathology (Cicchetti & Toth, 2014). More recently, the Hierarchical Taxonomy of Psychopathology (HiTOP; Kotov et al., 2017) incorporates the EXT spectrum among other dimensions, including internalizing (e.g., major depressive disorder, obsessive compulsive disorder), thought (e.g., schizophrenia), and somatoform disorders. EXT on its own is generally modeled as either a single factor or by separate disinhibition/substance use (e.g., impulsivity, substance use, low self-control) and antagonism/antisocial (e.g., hostility, aggression, low regard for others) dimensions in correlated or higher order factor models (see Krueger et al., 2021 for review).

Current conceptualizations of psychopathology have been criticized for a hyper-focus on dispositional or biological factors and limited consideration of cultural context (see Rodriguez-Seijas et al., 2023 for review). This body of research has informed conceptualizations of personality and psychopathology in ways that may not adequately represent the experiences of individuals from diverse backgrounds. As data show, sociological contexts are important to consider alongside individual propensities (e.g., Beyers et al., 2003). As such, there is a need to further integrate evidence for the role of systemic factors in the measurement and study of EXT.

Racial and Systemic Factors

Systemic factors are defined here as those partly reflecting the influence of social and government systems or processes extending beyond immediate environmental and individuallevel influences. In Bronfenbrenner's (2005) ecological system model (which asserts that human behavior is shaped by individual interaction with varied ecological systems), systemic factors are referred to as the exosystem (local government, social services, healthcare access) and macrosystem (economic and political systems, majority cultural norms, systemic injustice).

Applying this framework to measurement, we can assess the extent to which indicators of EXT may reflect systemic factors so that scores on these measures are potentially inflated (or deflated) relative to trait propensities.

One form of systemic influence involves historical and extant policies around race and ethnicity. Race is often used as an individual characteristic or identity in psychology, when considered through the perspective of individual differences (Skinner-Dorkeeno et al., 2023). At the same time, theories of systemic racism assert that issues of race are embedded in and throughout various social systems (economics, politics) that maintain unfair treatment toward people of color (Bonilla-Silva, 1997), which partly explain pathways to justice system involvement (Fagan et al. 2010; Kochel et al., 2011; Lytle, 2014). Accordingly, race/ethnicity is a concept that cannot be easily separated from systemic practices and policies in the United States. Disparities in healthcare and law enforcement practices in Black and Hispanic communities in the U.S. (Kochel et al., 2011; Lytle, 2014; Xu et al., 2024) highlight how race relates to systemic inequity. As such, the influence of race-based policies can differentially affect the functioning of measures of EXT behaviors across different racial and ethnic groups in the United States.

Socioeconomic disadvantage reflects a separate but related set of systemic factors that can influence EXT indicators. Underfunded schools and lack of access to healthcare in various communities in the U.S. disproportionately affect poor individuals regardless of race (Braveman et al., 2010). Historical practices like redlining and discriminatory housing policies, which denied resources to neighborhoods predominantly housed by Black or immigrant residents, have led to modern-day concentrated poverty in these communities (Simes, 2018). These geographical areas are even now associated with inhibited social mobility and high levels of police surveillance and, in turn, incarceration rates (Holder et al., 2022; Simes, 2018). These conditions raise the likelihood of detecting criminal behaviors, thereby inflating the link between poverty and criminalization. Importantly, low-income people of color face compounded risk for detection of criminal behavior due to overlapping racial and economic marginalization (Felson & Krajewski, 2020; Holder et al., 2022; Simes, 2018). In turn, feelings of injustice have been linked to increased violence and substance use (Scheuerman, 2013), as potential responses to the unfair practices they observe. Thus, some of the externalizing behaviors observed in low-income communities may partially reflect adaptation to disadvantaged and low resource contexts.

In sum, individuals living in marginalized communities may become embedded in the criminal justice system for reasons that go beyond individual-level traits. In turn, justice system involvement affects trajectories toward future antisocial behavior, crime, and violence (Cauffman et al., 2021). This research suggests that we should examine more closely how we operationalize indices of EXT and potential bias inherent in these measurements. This is important when we consider the extent to which some of our measures of EXT may be used to make legal or other decisions (e.g., sentencing, dangerousness), such that their use can disproportionately disadvantage those coming from diverse backgrounds.

Measurement Invariance in EXT

Measurement invariance testing provides a formal method for evaluating whether latent constructs operate equivalently across groups or contexts. In this research, it is used to determine whether the EXT construct and its indicators function similarly across racial and systemic contexts. Few studies have tested invariance in adult EXT models and even fewer have examined invariance as a function of broader contextual variables, including community-level indices of disadvantage. Several studies that examined invariance of a two-factor internalizingexternalizing correlated model have found full invariance across racial/ethnic groups in adults (Eaton et al., 2013; Hopwood & Moser, 2011) and youth (He & Li, 2021). In contrast, Feaster et al. (2010) found partial scalar invariance of a single EXT factor model, such that Black and Hispanic youth were rated higher by observers on conduct disorder (CD) symptoms, a more severe disorder, yet lower on oppositional defiant disorder (ODD) symptoms, a less severe disorder, compared to White youth. These results align with previous research suggesting that minoritized individuals are more likely to have their externalizing behaviors interpreted as problematic compared with White individuals, even when the level of observed behavior is similar (Okonofua & Eberhardt, 2015; Owens, 2022).

Measures of psychopathy, closely related to EXT (Smith & Newman, 1990), often show differential item functioning across race, particularly on arrest history items in youth samples (Tsang et al., 2014; Ray, 2023; Bontemps et al., 2022). For example, Tsang et al. (2014) found that items related to legal involvement (i.e., revocation of conditional release, criminal versatility) on the Psychopathy Checklist: Youth Version (Forth et al., 2003) had lower discrimination values (mathematically similar to metric noninvariance or differences in factor loadings) for Black versus White justice-involved youth. This study additionally found differences in difficulty parameters (similar to scalar noninvariance), such that items representing grandiosity, lacking regret, promiscuous behavior, failure to take responsibility, and criminal behavior were rated higher in Black than White youth at the same level of latent psychopathy.

A recent study conducted by Lee & Chmielewski (2026) used moderated nonlinear factor analysis (MNLFA; an analysis also used in the present paper) to evaluate measurement invariance of HiTOP dimensions across several sociodemographic moderators in a large nationally representative sample of adults in the United States. In their EXT latent model, with indicators that were categorical diagnoses of EXT disorders, results indicated weaker loadings for the alcohol use disorder (AUD) indicator at lower levels of income, suggesting that AUD symptoms are less robust indicators of latent EXT with more economic disadvantage. At the intercept level, lower income was associated with higher intercepts for AUD and history of conduct disorder (CD). As for race, Black participants had higher intercepts for CD and attention deficit hyperactivity disorder, whereas the AUD intercept was instead higher in White than Black participants, at mean levels of latent EXT. These results are similar to another recent study by our team in system-involved youth (Smith & Verona, 2026) that showed evidence of metric and scalar noninvariance as a function of race and systemic factors (e.g., neighborhood disadvantage, perceptions of procedural injustice). Consistently, juvenile justice involvement and school discipline showed higher intercepts among Black youth and at lower perceptions of procedural justice, suggesting that institutional indicators may be particularly influenced by contextual processes.

Overall, research available for invariance testing of EXT-related latent models is minimal, especially in adults. However, it tends to suggest that indicators of EXT do not always reflect the latent construct in the same way across groups racial/ethnic groups. Results evidencing differential intercepts suggest the overestimation of EXT risk for individuals coming from minoritized and disadvantaged communities when using certain indicators. To broaden this research, the current study considers moderators beyond race and income, such as communitylevel indicators of social disadvantage and structural racism, that may further reveal noninvariance in the measurement of EXT.

Current Research

Although the literature suggests that racial and systemic contexts may be relevant to biases in the measurement of EXT (e.g., Kochel et al., 2011; Lytle, 2014), research has historically interpreted the construct and its indicators as reflecting stable dispositions (Moore, 2011) Pre-registered hypotheses indicated that minority race and contextual factors reflecting systemic disadvantage would weaken indicator loadings (metric noninvariance), suggesting that EXT indicators may be less reflective of the latent EXT construct in marginalized groups. Given the minimal research on invariance as a function of broader contextual variables, we did not preregister hypotheses as to which indicator loadings would be most affected by disadvantaged contexts. We also explored potential differences in indicator intercepts (scalar noninvariance), reflecting whether observed behaviors vary by context even when latent EXT levels are held constant. Hypotheses of intercepts were not pre-registered.

Deviations from the preregistration are noted on OSF. These include that the larger study originally outlined two related but distinct research questions which are written up separately-one focused on externalizing (preregistration hypothesis 1, focus of the current paper) and the second on psychopathy (preregistration hypothesis 2). The preregistration initially included three samples, but data for one is not included in the present analyses due to continued collection. Study 2 also used the Structural Racism Effect Index and census tract-level variables, including the percentage of Black residents, instead of individual census indicators matched by ZIP code to better capture structural disadvantage.

Interviewers were thoroughly trained graduate and undergraduate students supervised by a Ph.D.-level licensed psychologist. Compensation ($20-$45) was provided upon completion of the study. See Sadeh et al., (2011) and Verona et al., (2016) for further details on procedures. Indicators of Externalizing. Interview and self-report measures of psychopathic personality, substance use, mental health diagnoses, and aggression were used to operationalize several indicators of EXT in Study 2. First, drug and alcohol use disorder symptoms were assessed using the alcohol and drug modules of the Structured Clinical Interview for Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fourth Edition, Text Revision (DSM-IV-TR;

American Psychiatric Association, 2000), Axis I Disorders (SCID; First et al., 1996). Each symptom of the former diagnoses of alcohol and drug dependence, the most severe of the substance use diagnoses in the DSM-IV, was rated on a three-point scale:1=absent/false,2=subthreshold, and3=threshold/true. In line with dimensional models of EXT psychopathology (Markon & Krueger, 2005), symptom counts of alcohol and drug use disorder symptoms were calculated separately by summing the number of threshold-level symptoms endorsed for each participant. Secondary ratings of symptoms were available for 173 (alcohol dependence, 21.8%) and 174 (drug dependence 21.9%) participants, showing good interrater reliability (ICCs=.91 for both, ps<.05).

Second, drug use severity was measured through the SCID, which assessed frequency and chronicity of use across eight drug categories. For each category, a score of 3 indicated threshold-level use, assigned when a street drug was used more than ten times in one month, or for prescribed drugs, used more than prescribed or dependent on them. These scores were then summed across drug categories to compute a composite index reflecting the number of drugs used at clinically significant levels.

Third, adult antisocial behavior and child conduct problems were both assessed using an interview that corresponding to DSM-IV-TR symptoms of antisocial personality disorder (ASPD) and childhood history of conduct disorder (CD). In both cases, dimensional scores were derived by summing the number of symptoms endorsed at threshold. Secondary ratings of symptoms were available for 294 (ASPD, 37.1%) and 293 (CD 36.9%) participants, showing acceptable interrater reliability (ICCs=.84 and .89 respectively, ps< .05).

Fourth, criminal-legal involvement was operationalized slightly differently in Study 2, given that we did not calculate number of total arrests due to Study 2 lacking data on arrest counts. Instead, we calculated the number of different types of charges participants received in their adult life, obtained from self-report and public records using similar procedures as in Study 1. Trained undergraduate and graduate students coded whether participants received a charge across 16 different types of crimes (e.g., theft, murder, sexual offense, robbery). We summed the number of yeses to obtain a total number of different charge types (i.e., 0-16). Thus, criminallegal involvement in Study 2 captures criminal charge variety and allows us to examine legal problems in a distinct way.

Fifth, irresponsible-reckless traits were measured using the lifestyle facet score from the Psychopathy Checklist: Screening Version (PCL:SV; Hart et al., 1995). The lifestyle facet score was calculated by summing the impulsivity, lacks goals, and irresponsibility items, each rated on a 3-point scale (0=no,1=maybe,2=yes), with higher scores indicating greater irresponsible-reckless traits. Secondary ratings were available for 111 (14%) participants, showing acceptable interrater reliability (ICC=.63, p< .05).

Race and Contextual Factors.

We used similar moderators as Study 1, with refinements in their measurement and included new racialized contextual variables that expanded on Study 1.

First, racial/ethnic identity focused on non-Hispanic White vs. Black comparisons, given that we had wide representation of these two groups in the sample (n=403 Black; n=290 White). Second, we improved geographic precision for the Census-derived indices of the SDI by primarily using census tract data. When full addresses were unavailable, zip code data were used (n=268).

We also explored additional community-level moderators in Study 2 to specifically expand on racial context. The Structural Racism Effect Index (SREI; Dyer et al., 2023) was added as a moderator to capture broader, systemic disparities that disproportionately affect racially and economically marginalized groups. The SREI uses Census variables representing built environment, criminal justice, education, employment, housing, income and poverty, social cohesion, transportation, and wealth. See the following for information on the calculation and validation of the SREI: https://www.sreindex.com/. The SREI has demonstrated strong associations with life expectancy, physical health, historically redlined neighborhoods, and mental health outcomes (Dyer et al., 2023). While the SREI moderator was not preregistered, our pre-registration included several exploratory community level moderators (e.g., employment, housing, poverty), including many of the ones that make up the SREI composite. Another exploratory contextual moderator that was included in the preregistration, the percent composition of Black residents living in participants' respective Census tracts, was also analyzed in Study 2 to broaden examination of racialized contexts.

We followed the same approaches as Study 1. Continuous moderators in the MNLFA were examined separately, which is consistent with prior research utilizing this framework (Bauer, 2017). We similarly applied the B-H correction to race/ethnicity and SDI metric invariance analyses but did not for any scalar invariance analyses nor metric invariance analyses for the SREI and racial composition in participant communities given these were exploratory.

Factor Model Specification

The original, proposed single factor model, described in the pre-registration, fit the data poorly (χ²(14)=266.152, CFI=.822, TLI=.733, RMSEA=.151, SRMR=.078). We ran several exploratory factor analyses to evaluate sources of misfit (see Supplemental Materials) and based on these results, a two-factor model was specified with Substance Use and Antisocial correlated factors (see Figure 2). This final model demonstrated adequate fit, χ²(13)=79.196, CFI=.951, TLI=.921, RMSEA=.080, SRMR=.052, is consistent with prior research (Krueger et al., 2021; Verona et al., 2011) and was retained for all subsequent analyses in Study 2.

General Discussion

The current work examined whether racial and contextual factors, namely race/ethnicity, location-based socioeconomic deprivation, and/or structural racism indicators moderated the psychometric measurement properties of latent EXT across two large samples of systeminvolved adults. As found in prior studies on measurement invariance of EXT by race/ethnicity (Eaton et al., 2013; Hopwood & Moser, 2011; Lee & Chmielewski 2026; Smith & Verona, 2026), results showed configural and at least partial metric invariance (loadings) across racial groups in both studies. Moderation by Race/Ethnicity

Across both studies, race/ethnicity emerged as an important source of measurement noninvariance and demonstrated fairly consistent effects. Although metric invariance was largely achieved in the first study and only partially in the second study, scalar invariance by race was not achieved in either study. Consistent with previous studies in youth (Feaster et al., 2010; Smith & Verona, 2026), observed behavioral indicators of latent EXT and related processes can be partly explained by racial/ethnic group-level processes that are separate from individual trait propensities. The applied implication of this finding is that reasoning about latent levels of an EXT trait based on observed levels of a given indicator is going to cement racial/ethnic bias into the inference.

Several intercepts were found to be higher in non-White or Black vs. White groups. In Study 1 (a jail sample), the "street code adherence" indicator produced the largest effect, with non-White and Hispanic participants having higher intercepts than non-Hispanic White participants at equivalent mean levels of EXT. Street code adherence captures the use of aggression to protect oneself -norms that Anderson (1999) originally theorized as adaptive responses to conditions of systemic neglect, violence, and institutional distrust in economically disadvantaged Black communities. Elevated endorsement at mean levels of EXT among non-White and Hispanic participants may therefore be explained by a contextual adaptation to perceived injustice rather than greater underlying pathology, a finding consistent with prior work (Holmes & Smith, 2018).

While we did not observe different intercepts across White and non-White individuals in Study 1 for arrest history, we did in Study 2 (a varied justice-involved sample), where we focused more narrowly on Black-White comparisons. The scalar noninvariance we observed for criminal charges in Study 2 suggests using these manifest scores can inflate inferred EXT levels for Black individuals, in particular. This is consistent with past literature on decision-making in correctional settings, highlighting that Black defendants are disproportionately misclassified as high-risk relative to their actual recidivism rates (Angwin et al., 2022). Indeed, in high-stakes assessment settings (e.g., correctional or legal contexts), individual observed indicators rather than latent mean levels of EXT are used as predictors of outcomes (e.g., recidivism, likelihood of response to treatment, etc.). These scalar noninvariance findings can plausibly reflect the influence of systemic disparities and structural disadvantage that affect criminal outcomes in Black individuals, consistent with previously well-documented disparities in the treatment of Black vs. White individuals in the justice system (Kochel et al., 2011).

Not all indicator intercepts were elevated in Black or minoritized participants. Across both studies, substance use indicator intercepts were higher in White than Black participants, although drug use severity showed a weaker loading in Black participants. Together, these results suggest that drug use is a less reliable indicator of latent EXT in Black than White participants, but that intercepts for substance use overestimate latent EXT risk in White participants. These results could be spurious, but they are also consistent with previous work showing higher AUD intercepts in a nationally representative sample of adults (Lee & Chmielewski, 2026) and higher substance use in a justice-involved youth sample (Smith & Verona, 2026) as a function of Black racial identity. It is potentially counterintuitive that intercepts for substance use severity and symptoms in Studies 1 and 2 were higher for White participants compared to Black participants, at equivalent levels of latent EXT. These findings are inferentially divergent from formulations that characterize Black individuals as having more of these problems given structural disadvantages relative to White individuals (McNulty & Bellair, 2003). However, in justice-involved samples, this pattern could be consistent with our broader thesis when considered alongside the likely presence of collider bias in this sample (Novak et al., 2023). Because participants in the current studies are system-involved adults, the sample is conditioned on criminal contact, and substance use related charges are a common route to justice system involvement (Gill et al., 2022). If patterns of overpolicing and disproportionate surveillance for Black individuals result in more criminalization of substance use (Kochel et al., 2011; Fagan et al., 2010; Lytle, 2014), then the degree of observed substance use required to be system-involved, and therefore likely present in this dataset, is lower. Nonetheless, because at least one other study that included a nationally representative sample found a similar result (Lee & Chmielewski, 2026), future work should explore how patterns of substance use (e.g., opioid misuse in rural/suburban areas; Palombi et al., 2018) that differentiate White from non-White participants may intersect with structural factors to impact substance use indicators in EXT models. The Impact of Continuous Location-Based Indices Across both samples, the Social Deprivation Index (SDI; Butler et al., 2013) was used to examine the impact of location-based socioeconomic positioning on EXT measurement. We observed conflicting results across studies, calling for further examination of this moderator in future work. In Study 1, we did not observe significant moderation of loadings or intercepts by SDI levels. The limited findings for SDI in Study 1 could have been due to lower geographical precision, where only zip code data could be used to define community-level resources. Prior MEASUREMENT INVARIANCE OF EXTERNALIZING BY RACIAL CONTEXT work documents that lesser geographic resolution attenuates the estimated effect sizes of neighborhood-level health effects, with key differences between zip code-level and Census tractlevel indices due to aggregation bias (Krieger et al., 2002).

In Study 2, in contrast, higher SDI predicted a weaker loading and lower intercept for drug use severity, both similar to results for race in this sample and in a previous study with youth (Smith & Verona, 2026). Another study of community adults also reported that lower income predicted a weaker loading for the substance use indicator (in this case AUD) in their EXT model (Lee & Chmielewski, 2026).

Thus, several studies indicate that substance use may be a less reliable indicator of EXT, and potentially underestimate EXT, among individuals in marginalized or low-resourced communities. Along with increased geographic resolution of the SDI than we had in Study 1, Study 2 allowed for broader measurement of substance use indicators (SCID interview-derived symptoms of alcohol and drug use separately, drug use chronicity vs. symptoms) compared to Study 1 (MAYSI self-reported substance use scale), which likely allowed us to model the two correlated factors of EXT, substance use and antisocial, and more sensitively reveal patterns of noninvariance. At the scalar level in Study 2, higher intercepts for ASPD symptoms and criminal charges were observed at higher SDI, results fairly consistent with findings for race/ethnicity, where we observed elevated intercepts for aggression/antisocial attitudes (adherence to street code) in non-White participants (Study 1) and higher criminal history intercepts in Black participants (Study 2). Beyond SDI, the use of the SREI (Dyer et al., 2023) and area-level racial composition, expanded on our ability to uncover the effects of the U.S. racial context more directly on EXT measurement in Study 2. Paralleling results of race/ethnicity and community-level SDI (the two preregistered moderators), the intercepts for criminal charges (elevated) and drug use severity (suppressed) were significantly moderated as a function of higher structural racism (SREI) and area-level Black racial composition (both exploratory moderators). At the same level of latent EXT, living in a location with structural disadvantage or with a greater representation of Black individuals is predictive of greater observed variety in criminal charges. This finding is particularly striking and notable; even after accounting for latent EXT, criminal charges significantly track structural racism exposure and community racialization in one's local neighborhood. At the metric level, we found that criminal charges loaded more strongly at higher levels of SREI. That is, in more structurally racist environments, observed criminal charge history carries a stronger signal of EXT.

While this effect was small and not found for other moderators, one possible explanation may be that individuals residing in environments with more structural racism may have fewer informal or discretionary alternatives to a criminal charge (e.g., less diversion, less benefit of the doubt), so when EXT-related behavior does occur, it is more likely to manifest as a criminal charge than in other communities.

As with the race/ethnicity results, the observed effect of location-based indices of disadvantage on the intercept for ASPD symptoms and criminal charges in Study 2 aligns with past work demonstrating the effects of environmental adaptations that may manifest as EXT and of higher levels of policing in areas of concentrated poverty, which could lead to both more diagnoses of antisocial personality (Zayas, 2023) and system involvement in individuals residing in those communities (Brownlow et al., 2024; Holder et al., 2022). Across our findings, we can relate the legacy of historical segregation and concentrated poverty to biases in EXT measurement models. Although the location-based moderators capture overlapping dimensions of broader structural disadvantage (rs = .58-.69), the congruence between individual identity and structural context pertaining specifically to race provides compelling converging evidence that risk assessment and criminal sentencing may systematically alter the trait propensity scores of racially minoritized individuals or those from higher deprivation communities because the indicators of EXT propensity carry excess variability attributable to identified structural exposures. Risk assessment instruments used in criminal justice contexts rely heavily on aggressivity or prior criminal records as proxies for future risk, and such instruments are widely used to inform pretrial detention decisions, sentencing, and parole determinations (Skeem & Lowenkamp, 2016). Given the observed elevated intercepts for aggression (in Study 1), and antisocial personality symptoms, and criminal history (in Study 2) as a function of disadvantaged context, this would be especially important to pay attention to. Research also suggests that aggression and antisocial behavior may occur in response to perceived injustice (Scheuerman et al., 2015), meaning that measurement bias may extend beyond institutional indicators like criminal history to individual-level behaviors and diagnoses that are often treated as trait-like.

In terms of criminal history, several scholars have warned that reliance on such indicators may function to encode and perpetuate existing racial disparities in the criminal justice system, as individuals from over-policed and structurally disadvantaged communities accumulate more extensive criminal histories independent of their underlying behavioral propensities (Harcourt, 2015; Skeem & Lowenkamp, 2016). Similarly, the Psychopathy Checklist-Revised (PCL-R; Hare, 2003) is regularly used to make criminal-legal decisions (Argueta-Cevallos, 2021) and scoring of several of the items relies quite a bit on correctional files and criminal records, including number and types of arrest. More work is needed on the correlates and meanings of these scores for individuals most impacted by the justice system, especially given the heavy use of the PCL-R for making sentencing, correctional housing, and even death penalty decisions (Argueta-Cevallos, 2021). The present findings provide measurement-level evidence that would warrant caution in how such instruments are used, such that the problem is not merely one of differential base rates, but of differences in the actual measurement properties as a function of contextual disadvantage.

Nevertheless, it is important to note that the direction of effects of the moderators on indicators varied. Whereas antisocial and criminal history intercepts were elevated as a function of Black/minority race or structural disadvantage, several substance use-related intercepts were lower as a function of those same moderators. When considering this in the context of assessment and related decision making, the effects operating in opposite directions may partially or fully cancel one another out when interpreting the latent score, even when meaningful item-level effects are present (Chalmers et al., 2016). It would therefore be important to give attention to specific indicators when interpreting assessment results. Future research should examine whether and under what conditions this cancellation occurs in practice.

Limitations, Constraints on Generalizability, and Conclusions

The current studies have several limitations, particularly since they involved secondary analyses of already-collected data. First, a notable limitation was the coarse categorization of race/ethnicity in Study 1, using non-Hispanic White vs. Non-White/Hispanic as a shorthand. 3Future work should leverage larger datasets that have enough individual minoritized racial categories to replicate and extend the current results. Ideally, future work would also be able to identify and isolate the relevant causal factors (e.g., systemic discrimination, racialized stress) to measure instead of the underspecified "race" variable, and this would produce maximally impactful examinations of the measurement of EXT. A productive avenue for future investigation concerns intersectional analyses that may be important (e.g., race x gender interactions, individual factors crossed with location-based structural indices, etc.).

Given the nature of the work presented here already involving many analytic comparisons, it was beyond the scope of the current paper to examine all possible crossings of these factors. Second, the EXT latent variable models were quite different across studies due to the available indicators in each dataset, with Study 1 reflecting a more general EXT factor, whereas Study 2 was able to differentiate the antisocial and substance use manifestations of EXT. Future studies would do well to harmonize datasets with shared indicators in ways that allow consistent structural modeling of EXT to evaluate direct rather than conceptual replicability. However, despite differing measurement models, the results indicated a conciliatory picture of the impact of racerelated variables on EXT scalar noninvariance. Notwithstanding these limitations, the current work is unique in showing evidence for the presence of contextually driven measurement noninvariance in EXT indicators across racial/ethnic groups and location-based indicators of socioeconomic deprivation and structural racism. Accurate modeling of psychopathology dimensions in diverse populations requires that measurement validity be taken seriously and treated as a substantive scientific question that is interrogated carefully and empirically.
