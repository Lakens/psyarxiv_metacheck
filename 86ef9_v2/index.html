Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Objective: Farmers, their families, and employees face daily stressors that can adversely affect their mental well-being. To better understand stress and mental well-being in farming populations, this study examines this relationship in the United Kingdom (UK) and Canada.

Method: Using an online cross-sectional survey, 569 adults from farming communities (37% male, 63% female; Mage = 47.57, SD = 12.55) completed measures of stress event load (EL), personal vulnerability to stress (PV), and subjective mental well-being. Data were analysed using hierarchical multiple regression, with simple-slopes analysis, and analysis of variance.

Results: Higher PV was associated with lower mental well-being, after accounting for EL (unstandardised B = -.40, 95% CI [-.45, -.35]). Although a significant interaction between EL and PV was observed (unstandardised B = .02, 95% CI [.01, .02]), after Bonferroni correction, higher EL was significantly associated with higher mental well-being only among participant with high PV. ANOVA also identified significant differences between stress-risk groups (ηp² = .31), with participants in high PV groups reporting the lowest well-being.

Conclusion: While studies have traditionally focused on farming-specific sources of stress, this research suggests that understanding mental well-being in farming communities requires considering not only the volume of stressors people face but also their perceived capacity to cope. Future research should examine individual coping capacities alongside the economic and structural sources of stress using longitudinal measures to clarify the associations between psychological resources, stress, and mental well-being.

1. Introduction

Stress is a daily feature of life for those living and working in farming communities, including not only farmers but also their families, farm workers, and employees in related industries.

Many stressors, such as weather and government policy, are beyond an individual farming community member's control [1]. Existing literature typically focuses on the perceived stress reported by farmers or identification of the farming-specific sources and frequencies of stress [2] [3] [4] [5] [6] [7]. However, individuals exposed to similar types and/or levels of stress do not always experience the same psychological effects [8]. Although some exposure to stress can be motivating, distress can occur when stress becomes chronic or when the burden exceeds available resources [9, 10]. While many studies concentrate on farm suicide, the stress iceberg recognises that many more people who live a farming way of life can suffer from the negative effects of stress [11]. Chronic stress can profoundly impact both mental and physical health and is directly linked to diminished well-being [12].

According to the Transactional Model of Stress and Coping, individuals primarily appraise whether the demands, external tasks or situational pressures they face in their environment are stressful [9]. Secondarily, individuals evaluate these stressors against their perceived adequacy to cope [9]. This secondary appraisal involves assessment of whether they have sufficient cognitive and emotional resources to manage the stress load. When resources are lost, depleted, compromised, or perceived as inadequate, individuals become more personally vulnerable to the adverse effects of stress, which can harm their mental well-being [13]. Stressors cannot be entirely eliminated from the farming environment, so it is essential to deepen our understanding of how members of farming communities appraise and are personally vulnerable to stress [14].

The use of this approach will enable the identification of the controllable and potentially malleable factors, in a community that are beset by many uncontrollable elements.

Personal vulnerability refers to the extent to which individuals subjectively perceive their internal resources as deficient or inadequate to cope with, or control their current demands [14].

Vulnerability may be shaped by individual differences in cognitive and affective processes, including beliefs, attitudes, and behaviours [15]. This includes psychological factors that reflect how someone feels about themselves, their abilities, and their circumstances. These include, for example, self-efficacy, self-esteem, and affectivity [16]. Self-efficacy reflects confidence in one's ability to control outcomes or achieve goals [17]. Those with higher self-efficacy have been found to exhibit lower emotional reactivity to stress and more adaptive coping responses [18]. In contrast, individuals with lower self-esteem, or self-worth, lack confidence in their ability to manage stressors, which is associated with increased stress severity, higher negative affect, and decreased positive affect [19, 20]. Positive affect, such as cheerfulness, can buffer against stress [21]. Meanwhile, negative affectivity, for example, feelings of anger, hopelessness, nervousness, and fear, is associated with more threat-focused appraisal of stressors and more emotionally maladaptive coping strategies [22, 23].

Psychological factors such as self-esteem, self-efficacy, and affectivity can be responsive to psychological interventions [24, 25]. Psychological interventions work with individuals to help them recognise their resources, expertise, and coping strategies to manage, adapt to, or meet their circumstances with positive coping. To inform such interventions, evidence is necessary; however, there is a lack of literature examining stress reactivity and personal vulnerability among farming populations, despite growing research into their mental well-being.

The World Health Organisation defines mental well-being as a positive state in which individuals can function successfully and cope with stress [26]. Existing studies in farming communities that consider mental health and well-being often employ scales which measure clinically focused outcomes [27] [28] [29] [30]. For example, the Generalized Anxiety Disorder Scale-7 [31] or the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale [32]. However, in farming communities, stress is a daily reality, and its effects may be significant even for those who do not endorse clinical symptoms. Given this, measures that more accurately reflect routine functioning, for instance, subjective mental well-being, might be beneficial in advancing the available literature and in designing psychological interventions for farming communities [33, 34]. Moreover, farming community members remain reluctant to discuss mental health and may be unwilling to honestly complete surveys that allude to mental ill-being or illness [35]. Assessing mental well-being using positively framed terms may promote engagement and reduce socially desirable responses [35, 36]. In the UK, several studies have utilised the Warwick-Edinburgh Mental Well-being Scale (WEMWBS) to capture mental well-being in farming populations [37] [38] [39]. WEMWBS is a widely validated questionnaire that measures the positive aspects of mental well-being, including optimal functioning, satisfying relationships, and positive affect [40]. Comparable data utilising this validated measure, however, remain scarce within farming communities worldwide, which prevents meaningful comparison.

Collecting data and comparing experiences across countries is important as it can enable assessment of whether individual reactions to stress are consistent across nations or whether cultural and contextual factors shape stress appraisal and responses [41]. Given the influence of geo-political and socio-economic factors in farming, this is particularly important. A crossnational approach has previously been used, for example, For example, researchers have previously measured adolescent health and wellbeing across multiple countries [42, 43].

Previous research has also analysed comparability in farming-specific stressors across countries, which presented both interesting and contrasting differences, including the interwoven nature and importance of financial stressors [44]. This type of comparison, if extended to psychological measures in members of farming communities, could enable the identification of both contextual differences and shared areas of concern that may not be apparent in single-country or temporally different studies.

The present study aims to investigate the relationship between stress and mental well-being among farming communities in the UK and Canada. In the UK, farmers are disproportionately affected by generalised anxiety disorder and depression [39, 45, 46]. Similarly, Canada reports higher rates of stress, anxiety, and depression among its farmers compared to general population norms [47, 48]. The burden of farming-specific stressors in the UK and Canada is well documented in previous research [4, 6, 39, 49]. The effects of these stressors now need to be contextualised in relation to their impact on personal vulnerability and mental well-being.

In this research, stress was measured using event load (EL) (the perceived burden of external demands) and personal vulnerability (PV) (the perceived insufficiency of internal resources to cope with such demands). We use the two constructs here to conceptualise the two processes in stress appraisal, namely, to measure a person's perception of the volume of stressors they face and whether they feel able to cope with its demands. Using their responses regarding PV to stress (high or low) and their EL of stressors (high or low), participants will be categorised into one of four stress-risk groups: High-Risk, Low-Risk, Fragile, or Challenged groups [50]. This categorisation will be fully detailed in the Method section.

This study aims to address the following research questions: 1.

Do EL and PV predict mental well-being, and is their interaction significantly associated with differences in mental well-being? 2. When categorised by EL and PV, does mental well-being differ between stress risk groups among members of the farming community?

3. What are the levels of mental well-being among farming community members in the UK and Canada, and do these levels differ significantly? This is an exploratory study; however, it was hypothesised: 1. Higher EL and higher PV will be significant independent negative predictors of mental well-being, and their interaction will be a significant predictor of variance in mental well-being.

2. Participants categorised into higher-risk stress groups (i.e. higher PV and EL) will report lower levels of mental well-being than those categorised into lower-risk stress groups (i.e. lower PV and EL). 3. Members of farming communities in both the UK and Canada will report low levels of mental well-being. No directional hypothesis is proposed regarding any difference in mental well-being between the UK and Canada. 2. Method 2.1 Design This cross-sectional study was conducted across farming communities in the UK and Canada between September 2023 and January 2024. Ethical approval was granted by the Northumbria University Human Research Ethics Board (Reference 4941). The study was pre-registered with the Open Science Framework prior to data analysis: https://osf.io/rxku3/overview?view_only=9d110545e68747879ac00d73c4fcdc11.

2.2 Participants

Participants were recruited using purposeful and snowball sampling [51]. A total of 655 individuals accessed an online survey. Inclusion criteria required participants to be over 18 years of age, reside in the UK or Canada, and have a self-defined connection to the farming community, either through work or family. This could include a farmer, family member, employee, or a connection through related occupations. No incentive for participation was offered. After excluding incomplete responses, the final sample comprised 569 participants; sample characteristics are fully described in the Results section.

2.3 Measures

Data were collected as part of a larger research project examining the sources of stress among members of farming communities in the UK and Canada. We report here findings from two scales used in this study. Additional data from the same participants have been reported elsewhere [44].

The survey also included demographic questions on sex and age. Participants were not asked whether they had ever been diagnosed with a mental health disorder.

Stress Overload Scale-Short

We assessed stress using the Stress Overload Scale-Short (SOS-S) [50]. Warwick Edinburgh Mental Well-Being Scale (Short) (SWEMWBS) We measured subjective mental well-being using the 7-item SWEMWBS [33] to capture psychological function and mental well-being. The shorter version of the WEMWBS was selected to reduce demands on participants' time. Participants described their experience over the previous two weeks on a 5-point Likert scale. Higher scores indicate higher well-being, after being converted from raw scores to a scale provided by the original authors. In this sample, the scale demonstrated good reliability, with α = 0.82 [95% CI: 0.80, 0.84] and ω = 0.82 [95% CI: 0.80, 0.84].

2.4 Procedure

An online questionnaire was hosted on SurveyMonkey. The survey link was widely shared in farming-related groups on social media platforms, including Instagram, X, and Facebook. We also emailed agricultural organisations in Canada, such as 'Farm Management Canada' and producer groups at national and provincial levels, to request they share the survey details.

The survey and all supporting documentation were provided in both English and Canadian French. For the SWEMWBS, the seven corresponding items from the previously validated fourteen-item French version of the WEMWBS were used [52]. For the SOS-S and all supporting documentation, a Canadian French-speaking individual translated the scale and all supporting documentation specifically for this study.

Participants were first provided with an information sheet detailing the study's aims. Tacit informed consent was used to simplify the research process, which was described to participants on the information sheet. At the end of the survey, participants were provided with a debrief which contained information about mental health support organisations relevant to their respective countries.

2.5 Treatment of Data and Statistical Analyses

Incomplete responses were first removed from the dataset (n = 68). Participants who did not provide their sex or age were excluded from analysis (n = 17), as were participants with two or more missing responses on either scale (n = 1). For participants with only one missing item per scale, missing values were imputed using the arithmetic mean across the remaining scale items [53]. Data were analysed using IBM SPSS v31. Descriptive statistics were calculated.

Statistical significance was set for all inferential analyses at p < .05.

A hierarchical multiple regression was conducted to explore the associations of continuous of PV and EL scores and their interaction with mental well-being. To probe the interaction between PV and EL, Process Model 1 was employed to compute simple-slopes analysis and Johnson-Neyman analysis [54]. Process Model 3 was also employed to examine whether country moderated the interaction PV and EL. For this, country was coded with UK = 0 and Canada = 1. PV and EL scores were mean-centred prior to the analyses. Regression coefficients are reported as unstandardised beta's (B) unless otherwise specified.

A 2-by-4 Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) was also conducted using general linear modelling to estimate differences in well-being scores among the four stress risk groups identified in the SOS-S (Low-Risk, Fragile, Challenged, High-Risk) and by country. Post-hoc test comparisons were adjusted using the Bonferroni correction, a statistical method that adjusts the significance level when conducting multiple tests to prevent false positive results [55].

Sensitivity power analyses were conducted in R [56] using the pwr package [57] to estimate the minimum detectable effect size for the pre-registered ANOVA. With the available sample size (N = 569) 80% power corresponded to an effect of η² of .025 or greater. Power to detect small country x stress group interactions may, however, have been limited by the unequal group sizes, particularly in the Fragile group.

3.1 Sample Characteristics

UK participants comprised 179 females, 111 males, and 1 participant who chose not to disclose their sex. The ages of UK participants ranged from 19 to 83 years (M = 47.43 years, SD = 12.57, Median = 48). In Canada, participants comprised 177 females, 100 males, and 1 participant who did not disclose their sex. Participants in Canada ranged from 26 to 82 years (M = 47.73 years, SD = 12.55, Median = 46). The Canadian sample included Canadian French participants (n =88). In the sample, there were no significant differences between the two countries regarding age (t(567) = 0.28, p = .778) or sex (χ²(2) = 0.29, p = .866). Many participants in the UK identified as farmers (62.9%), while Canadian participants were more likely to identify as a farm owner (68.7%) (χ²(12) = 88.08, p < .001; χ²(12) = 107.24, p < .001).

Other characteristics, including, for example, socioeconomic data, were not collected because they were not required for the analyses.

We also conducted exploratory analyses to assess whether the stress groups varied by sex, age, and country. Chi-square tests of independence showed significant associations between stress groups and sex (χ² (3) = 20.62, p < .001, Cramér's V = .19), with females more likely than males to be classified in the High-Risk group. When categorising by age group, participants in the two youngest categories were combined due to the low response rate among those aged 18-24. Older participants (65+) were more likely to be classified in the Low-Risk group than younger participants. Lastly, UK participants were more likely to be in the High-Risk and Fragile groups than those in Canada (χ² (3) = 16.62, p < .001, Cramér's V = .17).

3.2 Inferential Analyses

To assess relationships between variables, we conducted tests of distribution, normality, and association across the combined UK and Canadian samples. Pearson's bivariate correlation revealed a strong positive association between PV and EL, r(567) = .71, p <.001. This shared variance is consistent with their conceptual relationship as dimensions of stress overload within one scale. Descriptive statistics for the subscales are presented in Table 3, which demonstrate good internal consistency and no issues with normality. The first model included only EL as a predictor. This model was significant, F(1, 567) = 161.85, R² = .22, explaining 22% of the variance in SWEMWBS. EL was a significant negative predictor of SWEMWBS (B= -.29, t = -12.72, p < .001), indicating that greater event load was associated with lower mental well-being.

In model two, PV was added as a predictor variable. This model was also significant F(2, 566) = 220.81, p <.001, R² = .44, accounting for 44% of variance in SWEMWBS. The addition of PV showed significant improvement over model 1, ∆R² = .22, ∆F(1, 566) = 217.96, p <.001.

In model 2, PV remained statistically significant after accounting for EL (B = -.37, t = -14.76, p < .001) whilst EL was no longer significant (B = -.00, t = -.08, p = .939). Therefore, after accounting for personal vulnerability, event load was no longer significantly associated with mental well-being. This was contrary to our hypothesis.

The third model included the interaction between EL and PV. This model remained significant, F(3,565) = 159.36, p <.001, R² = .46, explaining 46% of the variance. The addition of the interaction was a significant improvement over model two: ∆R² = .02, ∆F(1, 565) = 20.91, p <.001. In this final model, PV remained a significant negative predictor (B = -.40, t = -15.68, p <.001), EL remained non-significant (B = .04, t = 1.49, p = .136), and the EL x PV interaction was significant, indicating that the association between event load and well-being was moderated by personal vulnerability (B = .02, t = 4.57, p <.001).

Using continuous, mean-centred PV and EL scores, a simple-slopes analysis was conducted to examine the association between EL and well-being at low (-1SD), mean, and high (+1SD) levels of PV (Figure 2). This revealed that the conditional association between EL and mental well-being was significantly negative when at mean-centred PV values below -6.20 and significantly positive at values above 1.00 (identified using unadjusted 95% confidence intervals). These results suggest there is value in further investigating members of farming communities' cognitive appraisals of their stress-coping capacity.

4. Discussion

The present study investigated the association between personal vulnerability, stress overload, and mental well-being among farming community members across the UK and Canada. In both countries, we found higher personal vulnerability was significantly associated with lower mental well-being across all levels of stress event load. Although event load was associated with well-being when considered alone, its predictive ability diminished when personal vulnerability was included. There has been considerable emphasis on resilience in farming communities [38, 47, [60] [61] [62] [63]. However, within the farming community, resilience has been associated with self-reliance and stoicism [64, 65]. Whilst such endurance in the face of uncontrollable factors can help people weather challenges, the promotion of positive coping can enable them to thrive, rather than merely survive. Our findings highlight the need to consider personal vulnerability in future research on tailored interventions to promote positive coping and strengthen individuals' psychological resources.

We found that members of farming communities with higher personal stress vulnerability reported significantly lower well-being than those with lower personal stress vulnerability. This pattern held across both high and low levels of event load and across both countries. Previous studies have identified low levels of well-being among farmers, along with high levels of perceived stress and various types of farming stressors [37, 66, 67]. However, according to the Transactional Model of Stress and Coping [9], an individual's response to stress is based on both their primary appraisal of the stressor and their secondary appraisal of whether they perceive they have sufficient resources to manage its demands. Personal vulnerability is conceptualised as the extent to which individuals subjectively perceive that their internal resources are insufficient to cope with their current demands [14]. This experience could be shaped by psychological resources which can alter individual perceptions of coping capacity during secondary appraisal [68, 69]. Our results extend the existing literature by providing empirical support that the burden of stress demands alone does not account for lower levels of mental well-being. When considered alongside personal vulnerability, event load was not an independent predictor of well-being. This suggests that measuring perceived coping capacity can provide more useful information about mental well-being in farming communities than measuring event load alone. Given this finding, we suggested further investigating the role of personal vulnerability and its associated psychological resources in determining suitability for potential interventions in farming communities.

The present research also found that the significant negative association between event load and mental well-being was attenuated following the inclusion of personal vulnerability in the regression model. Unexpectedly, among individuals high in personal vulnerability, higher event load was associated with slightly higher mental well-being although this effect was modest, and overall well-being remained lower than that of those with average or low vulnerability. This finding does not, of course, mean that higher stress is good for well-being.

However, there are plausible explanations as to why this unexpected result may have occurred.

Previous research among women in farming in the UK has found that providing instrumental support to others, which could be categorised as part of a person's event load, is associated with both increased perceived stress and higher levels of well-being [70]. Providing practical support to others, although associated with higher stress, may give people a sense of worth or value [71]. The significant association observed among individuals with higher levels of personal vulnerability could possibly reflect differences in self-efficacy, given previous research among farming populations which found that self-efficacy was negatively associated with stress [72]. As alternative explanations, including, for example, burnout, are also possible, this supposition requires further empirical investigation across farming populations of all genders. Further, given the substantial correlation between the sub-scales of personal vulnerability and event load, the results may reflect overlap or shared variance. These findings therefore reinforce the need for increased research on appraisal processes of individual differences in psychological constructs among farming populations and their association with well-being, beyond the relatively limited current literature [73] [74] [75] [76].

While individuals in Canadian farming communities reported significantly higher mental wellbeing scores than those in the UK, this difference was small. Worryingly, both countries reported poor well-being, which was below their respective population norms, although differences in sampling and methodology may limit direct comparisons. In the UK, well-being scores in our sample were indicative of low well-being [77]. At the time of data collection, farmers and their families in the UK were in transition regarding grant arrangements for the Sustainable Farming Incentive following Brexit [78]. Farmers in Canada were experiencing weather-related issues and labour shortages [79, 80]. The immediacy of these multiple issues may have affected the reporting of both stress and mental well-being among participants.

Nevertheless, the scores reported in this study are also consistent with the extant UK literature, which reports comparable low well-being scores [37] [38] [39]. Given these findings and the extant literature, we recommend action to improve the well-being of farming community members in both the UK and Canada through greater structural support and targeted psychological interventions. We recognise that in some regions, Canadian farmers benefit from provincial and federal Government-funded support programmes providing dedicated mental health support [81]. Our cross-sectional data cannot establish whether this could account for any of the difference in well-being scores; however, we note that, at the time of writing, Government funding for the provision of similar programmes is currently lacking in the UK.

4.1 Practical Implications

Although we recognise that the data are cross-sectional and therefore cannot establish causation, the findings identify personal vulnerability to stress as a relevant consideration for future research into designing psychological interventions to support farming communities.

Given that personal vulnerability remained significantly associated with well-being after accounting for event load, equipping individuals in farming populations with practical and achievable strategies to develop psychological skills that can improve the appraisal of coping resources may be beneficial [14, 73]. Developing psychological skills may equip individuals with tools to respond more effectively to the challenges they face. In farming terms, psychological interventions do not simply help farmers manage the inflow or outflow of their stress bucket; they instead increase the capacity of the bucket itself [82]. Our findings therefore suggest that research should investigate the effectiveness of interventions that reduce vulnerability to stress and promote positive coping by strengthening psychological resources.

Such interventions must be specific to farming, practical, and considerate of farmers' time pressures [82]. The ifarmwell programme in Australia has shown preliminary support for this approach, demonstrating that interventions which target modifiable psychological resources may help improve mental wellbeing in farming populations [83]. Of course, psychological interventions must complement, not replace, efforts to understand and reduce the economic and structural sources of farming stress.

4.2 Strengths, limitations and directions for future study

Although this research offers a novel contribution to the farming psychology literature, it is not without limitations. The absence of a sampling frame prevents calculation of a response rate, and as the data were primarily obtained from participants reached via social media, they may be subject to bias [84]. The sampling method also resulted in an unequal gender distribution, which limits the generalisability of the findings. As noted, the cross-sectional nature of the data precludes temporal inferences and may be influenced by broader events specific to farming communities in both nations at the time of data collection. To mitigate the effects of societal events on the data and to account for the implications of wider national and structural factors, multi-wave longitudinal data collection is needed to monitor changes and developments at both the individual and group levels. Longitudinal research could also assess the effectiveness of any interventions designed to reduce personal vulnerability and examine their associations with mental well-being over time. We believe this represents a useful direction for future research.

We also advocate for the broader reporting of detailed data to build a comprehensive picture of the mental well-being of farming communities to inform targeted support. For instance, publishing variance statistics and demographic breakdowns would facilitate better informed cross-national comparisons, and the assessment of whether well-being deficits are consistent across the heterogeneous farming community.

Despite these limitations, the use of validated scales provides a foundation for replication in other countries. Developing a global picture of well-being among farming populations using the same measure can allow for comparisons across countries and promote the sharing of best practices in interventions and support. That said, we acknowledge limitations in the translation method used for the Canadian French version of the SOS-S. Scales translated into other languages can follow a process that includes forward and back translation, followed by psychometric validation [85]. Given the scope and aim of this study, we did not undertake the full forward-and-back translation process for the SOS-S. Because cultural differences can affect wording and responses, findings from the Canadian French version should therefore be interpreted with caution. This study is the first to directly compare these constructs in farming communities across nations. We recommend that future research in these populations incorporate invariance testing to strengthen the validity of comparisons.

A well-being study that includes farming populations and non-farmers across multiple nations would allow for direct comparisons between the farming community and the general population [45, 86]. The expansion of such research would generate valuable normative data for cross-national comparisons, thereby highlighting the unique challenges faced by members of farming communities relative to the general population norm. Finally, the inclusion of biological measures such as salivary cortisol, alongside self-report assessments, may represent a valuable expansion of stress research in farming populations [87, 88]. Although physiological assessments have previously been constrained by practical and time pressures in the farming community, technological developments could facilitate a multi-modal approach in future research, potentially enhancing understanding of both perceived and physiological stress responses [89, 90].

5. Conclusion

The greatest asset on any farm is the farmer, their family, and their employees. However, studies have traditionally focused on farming-specific sources of stress rather than on the personal characteristics that shape an individual's capacity to cope. In this study, higher personal vulnerability to stress was significantly associated with lower mental well-being among members of farming communities in the UK and Canada. This demonstrates the importance of measuring perceived coping capacity alongside event load. Scores for mental well-being in both UK and Canadian farming communities remain worryingly low. As many stressors in the farming environment are largely uncontrollable, greater attention to understanding the psychological factors that influence how stressors are appraised and managed is needed. This study provides evidence for policymakers, healthcare practitioners, and public health and mental health service providers to inform future research into designing targeted programmes to improve well-being in farming communities. Longitudinal research is now required to inform and measure farming-specific interventions to improve mental wellbeing, not only in the UK and Canada but also in farming communities globally.
