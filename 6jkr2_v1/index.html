How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Generative AI chatbots have become part of 15-year-olds' schoolwork. However, it is less clear whether these students also evaluate what these systems produce. Using PISA 2025 data from more than 500,000 students across 59 countries, we examined how often students are asked in class to evaluate AI output, who is asked, and how closely the practice is associated with students' AI use. On average across countries, 57.5% of students used AI chatbots for schoolwork at least weekly, yet only 35.3% reported being asked often or very often to evaluate the quality of AI output in class, whereas 33.2% reported being asked never or almost never. Interestingly, students who used AI more often also reported being asked more often to evaluate AI output, in all countries (pooled r = 0.34). Yet, 25% of at-least-weekly users still reported no or almost no AI-output evaluation. Furthermore, students from more advantaged socioeconomic backgrounds reported being asked more often to evaluate AI output, independent of their level of AI use. Overall, students reported being asked to evaluate AI output less often than to evaluate online information. Yet, the association of AI use with evaluation was substantially stronger for AI-output than for online-information evaluation. In sum, many students who use AI regularly report few opportunities to evaluate its output in lessons, and these opportunities are socially stratified, extending an established pattern of digital inequality to generative AI.

Introduction

A central question for teaching with generative AI (GenAI) is no longer whether students use these tools, but whether they also learn to properly evaluate what the tools produce. On average across OECD countries, 86% of 15-year-olds reported in PISA 2025 that they use GenAI chatbots for school at least occasionally-most often to help them learn, to research topics, to summarize material, and to draft texts (OECD, 2026c). International policy frameworks have responded by calling for a critical and constructive use of GenAI in schools, in which students learn not only to use these tools but also to evaluate what they produce (OECD & European Commission, 2026; UNESCO, 2024).

Research on digital technology in schools has long drawn on international large-scale assessments to better understand students' access to and use of information and communication technology (ICT), as well as how these relate to achievement and ICT-related attitudes. This tradition typically focused primarily on how often students use ICT and how its use relates to outcomes such as academic achievement and ICT self-efficacy (e.g., Chen & Hu, 2020; Hu et al., 2018). For GenAI, such a focus on frequency falls short. Whether chatbot use supports or undermines learning depends less on how often students use it than on how they engage with its output-whether they accept it wholesale or examine it critically (Bastani et al., 2025). A central open question is therefore not only how often students use GenAI, but whether lessons also provide opportunities to critically evaluate what it produces. Evidence on this question has so far come from two separate strands of research: teacher surveys that document how teachers themselves use AI (OECD, 2025), and studies on students' GenAI literacy that documented gaps in their critical evaluation skills (Bråten et al., 2026; Park, 2025).

What is missing so far is cross-national evidence on whether students who use AI more often are also asked more often to evaluate its output, and on how such evaluation opportunities are distributed across socioeconomic backgrounds among students who use AI equally often (see OECD, 2026c, Box I.4.2, for descriptive evidence).

Here, we therefore examined, across 59 countries participating in PISA 2025, whether opportunities to evaluate AI output in lessons are associated with students' AI use, whether they are equitably distributed across socioeconomic backgrounds, and how they relate to the established practice of evaluating online information. In addition, we examined whether teachers' professional development on AI is specifically associated with these opportunities.

Evaluating AI Output as an Instructional Demand

Evaluation of AI output is necessary for at least two reasons. First, AI chatbots generate statistically plausible text, not verified knowledge, so their output can be fluent, confident, and wrong (Farquhar et al., 2024; Huang et al., 2025; Ji et al., 2023).

Second, learning requires cognitive effort, and experimental studies show that AI chatbot use can reduce that effort and the depth of students' reasoning (Fan et al., 2025; Stadler et al., 2024). Without safeguards, it can also impair performance once the tool is no longer available (Bastani et al., 2025; Kosmyna et al., 2025). Evaluating what an AI produces can address both of these shortcomings, as it reflects the moment at which a fluent but wrong answer can be identified, and it requires students to engage with the content effortfully rather than accept it wholesale.

However, accumulating evidence suggests that students can hardly do this on their own. A systematic review of 51 studies on students' GenAI literacy identified critical evaluation of AI output as a core competency with which students particularly struggle (Park, 2025). Furthermore, additional qualitative evidence indicates that even more experienced secondary school students hold systematic misconceptions about how chatbots generate text and where their information comes from (Bråten et al., 2026).

So, evaluating whether what an AI chatbot produced is correct or not requires knowing something about the topic-precisely the kind of knowledge students are still acquiring.

And school lessons are the setting in which this gap can be closed as a teacher can provide missing knowledge, model how to evaluate a claim, and give feedback on students' evaluations in a setting that reaches all students. Whether lessons actually fulfill this role, however, depends on whether they offer students the opportunity to practice evaluating AI output in the first place.

Opportunities to Learn and Students' AI Use

Opportunity-to-learn frameworks (Carroll, 1963; Schmidt et al., 2001; Seidel & Shavelson, 2007) direct attention to such opportunities, that is, to what teachers offer and how students use them. Following this tradition, we treat the frequency of AI-output evaluation in lessons as an instructional outcome in its own right, as it is a necessary condition for students to develop evaluative competence and therefore an appropriate dependent variable when the question is whether classrooms are offering those conditions.

Student reports of what happens in lessons are a common way to measure such opportunities, although they combine shared classroom experience with individual perception (Lüdtke et al., 2009; Sergiou et al., 2026). For the question of whether schools provide such opportunities, students' reports are an appropriate level of measurement-they capture what happens in classrooms, which teacher reports or curriculum documents do not.

Two properties of these opportunities matter for AI output. First, they should reach the students who use AI, since those students face the demands of evaluating AI output whenever they use AI chatbots. If evaluation opportunities are not more frequent among students who use AI more often, the students most exposed to AI output would not be the best prepared to evaluate it. However, there are reasons to expect that evaluation opportunities do increase with AI use. Instruction is not fixed but adapts to what students bring into the classroom (Corno, 2008). As such, it seems warranted to assume that where students use AI more often, teachers are more likely to encounter AI-generated work and to take it up as an object of instruction. Yet even if evaluation opportunities increase with AI use, this does not ensure that students who use AI equally often are also offered equal opportunities to evaluate AI output.

The Social Distribution of Evaluation Opportunities

The second property is therefore that evaluation opportunities should be distributed fairly across social backgrounds. Research on the so-called digital divide indicates that inequalities in digital participation increasingly lie not in access to technology but in how it is used as well as in the outcomes people derive from its use (Scheerder et al., 2017; van Deursen & van Dijk, 2014; Wang et al., 2026).

Opportunities to learn critical evaluation have followed this pattern before. Already in PISA 2018, students from more advantaged backgrounds were more likely than their more disadvantaged peers to report having been taught at school how to recognize biased information online (OECD, 2021). Recent work on GenAI suggests that unequal capacity to use these tools well may become a central mechanism through which digital inequality operates (Capraro et al., 2024). For students' performance on ICT literacy tests, these disparities are small but robust. A meta-analysis of large-scale assessment data has established an average positive association between socioeconomic status and ICT literacy across K-12 samples (Scherer & Siddiq, 2019). Part of this association runs through schools themselves. In large-scale assessment data from seven OECD countries, students from more advantaged backgrounds attended schools in which students used ICT in a wider range of lesson activities, and this difference statistically accounted for a meaningful share of the association between parental education and students' digital skills (Loh et al., 2025). In this context, AI use for schoolwork is common among students from all social backgrounds, but opportunities to learn to evaluate what AI produces may be less evenly distributed-and are least easily compensated outside school by less advantaged students, extending a well-documented inequality in digital education (OECD, 2021; Scheerder et al., 2017; Wang et al., 2026).

Continuity or Specificity

Before chatbots, critical evaluation of information mattered mainly when students judged online sources-a long-standing concern in media-education research (McGrew et al., 2018) and curriculum design (Vuorikari et al., 2022). By PISA 2018, about two-thirds of 15-year-olds in OECD countries reported having been taught at school how to decide whether to trust information from the internet (OECD, 2023). We ask whether teachers fold AI output into this practice or treat it as something new. We refer to the first possibility as the continuity view, in which AI output is one more kind of information to be evaluated, and to the second as the specificity view, in which it is qualitatively distinct. Classroom practice may well fall somewhere between the two. This is a question about instruction, not competence, but instruction and competence are linked. If evaluating AI output draws on the same skills, both tasks should tend to occur together in lessons. Prominent AI literacy frameworks list the evaluation of AI and its output as a competence (Allen & Kendeou, 2024; Long & Magerko, 2020; Ng et al., 2021; OECD & European Commission, 2026; UNESCO, 2024), but they do not specify whether it belongs to established information literacy or is a competence in its own right. The continuity view is grounded in the observation that content-evaluation strategies developed for websites also apply to chatbot answers (Wineburg & McGrew, 2019), and that digital competence frameworks have integrated AI into existing competence areas rather than defining a separate one (Vuorikari et al., 2022). The specificity view, on the other hand, is grounded in the observation that evaluating what a language model produces requires knowledge about how such models generate responses and where they get their information from (Bråten et al., 2026; Long & Magerko, 2020) and thus places distinct metacognitive demands on users (Tankelevitch et al., 2024). Three indicators show which view better describes classroom practice.

None of them is decisive on its own, so we interpret them jointly. First, whether AI-output evaluation occurs about as often in lessons as online-information evaluation.

Many students now use AI chatbots to research topics for school (OECD, 2026c), so if teachers treat AI output as one more source, they should ask students to check it about as often. A lower frequency, however, could also mean that the practice is simply newer and less established. Second, whether online-information evaluation is the strongest correlate of AI-output evaluation among the other digital lesson activities, which would indicate close overlap with established information-evaluation practice. If teachers fold AI output into existing evaluation tasks, students who are often asked to evaluate online information should also often be asked to evaluate AI output, more so than students who often do unrelated activities such as programming or creating videos.

Third, whether AI use predicts AI-output evaluation more strongly than it predicts online-information evaluation, which would indicate AI-specific coupling beyond general digital practice. Under the continuity view, more AI use simply means more information to check, which should raise both kinds of evaluation alike. A clearly stronger link with AI-output evaluation would instead suggest that teachers respond to AI use with a practice of its own.

Teachers' Professional Development

Whether opportunities to evaluate AI output are provided largely depends on teachers, and teachers' readiness to engage pedagogically with GenAI is inconsistent. In the Teaching and Learning International Survey 2024, the share of lower-secondary teachers who had used AI in their work ranged from 14% in France to 75% in Singapore and the United Arab Emirates, with an OECD average of 36%. Most teachers who did not use AI reported lacking the knowledge and skills to do so, and educational systems in which more teachers used AI were also those in which more teachers had received professional learning on it (OECD, 2025). At the same time, research on AI in education has focused on applications in teaching rather than on teachers' professional development needs (Tan et al., 2025).

Evidence from international assessments shows that participation in ICT-related professional development is associated with more teaching with technology, but not uniformly. In Germany and the Czech Republic, teachers who participated in such professional development used computers in class more often and placed more emphasis on developing students' ICT skills than teachers who rarely participated (Drossel & Eickelmann, 2017). Across eight countries in the International Computer and Information Literacy Study (ICILS) 2018, teachers' participation was positively associated with their technology-related teaching practices in the pooled sample, but in only four to five countries depending on the outcome, whereas teachers' ICT self-efficacy and collaboration were associated with these practices almost consistently (Konstantinidou & Scherer, 2022). One possible reason is that participation measures capture little of what makes professional development instructionally consequential.

Research on teacher learning has established that professional development changes classroom practice mainly when it is content-focused, involves active learning, is coherent with teachers' knowledge and school policies, is sustained over time, and involves collective participation (Desimone, 2009). Whether participation in professional development on AI shows an AI-specific association with instructional practice across education systems is therefore an open question. PISA 2025 allows a first look at this question, as principals reported how many of their teachers had taken part in professional development on dealing with students' access to AI chatbots.

The Present Study

The present study aims to provide, to our knowledge, the first cross-national analysis of how students' opportunities to evaluate AI output in lessons relate to their AI use and socioeconomic background. To this end, we drew on PISA 2025, the first PISA cycle to measure students' AI chatbot use alongside classroom opportunities to evaluate AI output (OECD, 2026a), and analyzed data from more than 500,000 15-year-olds across 59 countries.

In doing so, the present study contributes to research on teaching with digital technology in three ways: (i) it examines whether opportunities to evaluate AI output are associated with students' GenAI use; (ii) it tests whether these opportunities are equitably distributed across socioeconomic backgrounds; and (iii) it looks into whether teachers treat AI output as a new instructional object or as an extension of established online-information evaluation practice.

First, we described how common AI use for schoolwork and AI-output evaluation in lessons were, and how their prevalence compared. We then tested five hypotheses, preregistered before analysis of the international sample (see Methods): H1 More frequent AI use for schoolwork is associated with more frequent evaluation of AI output in lessons.

H2

Students from more advantaged socioeconomic backgrounds are asked more often to evaluate AI output, independent of their level of AI use.

H3a-i AI-output evaluation occurs at a frequency comparable to that of online-information evaluation.

H3a-ii Online-information evaluation is the strongest correlate of AI-output evaluation among 14 digital lesson activities.

H3b AI use predicts AI-output evaluation more strongly than it predicts online-information evaluation.

Hypotheses H3a-i, H3a-ii, and H3b jointly test which of the two views better describes classroom practice. As a complementary analysis, we examined the role of teacher professional development (PD) and investigated whether PD on AI chatbots is specifically associated with AI-output evaluation or whether its association with online-information evaluation is of comparable size.

Methods

Sample

We used data from the PISA 2025 public-use files (OECD, 2026b) for the 59 countries and economies that met our preregistered inclusion criteria. PISA is an international large-scale assessment designed to provide nationally representative samples of 15-year-old students in each participating country. Countries were included if at least 70% of weighted sampled students had valid responses on the AI-output evaluation item and on the index of economic, social and cultural status (ESCS), and when the AI-use items were administered in that country. Of the 90 countries and economies in the PISA 2025 public-use files, 6 did not administer the AI-use items, 4 had fewer than 70% valid responses on ESCS, and 21 had fewer than 70% valid responses on the AI-output evaluation item. The included countries are listed in Fig. 1, and the excluded countries, with the reason for exclusion, in Table S1. The analysis sample comprised 500,851 students, and in the 55 countries with school identifiers, students attended 16,031 schools. Country-level sample sizes ranged from 3,680 students in Malta to 34,639 students in the United Arab Emirates. We used the final student weight (W_FSTUWT) and the 80 balanced repeated replication (BRR) replicate weights with Fay's adjustment (k = 0.5) provided in the PISA 2025 data. The replicate weights allow standard errors to account for PISA's complex sampling design, in which students are sampled within schools (OECD, 2024; Rutkowski et al., 2010).

Measures

All student-level variables were drawn from the PISA 2025 student questionnaire; the teacher professional development variables were drawn from the school questionnaire.

AI use for schoolwork

Students reported how often they use "Artificial Intelligence chatbots (e.g. ChatGPT) for your school work" to summarize a text they had to read, to conduct preliminary research on a new topic, to draft texts for writing assignments, and to help them learn (1 = never or almost never;5 = every day or almost every day; items ST438Q01DA-Q04DA). Our main measure is the highest frequency reported across the four purposes, which preserves the five response categories, requires no threshold, and flags students as users at a given frequency as soon as any one purpose reaches that frequency. Where we describe students as weekly users, they used AI at least weekly for at least one purpose.

AI-output evaluation and online-information evaluation

In a battery of items on tasks carried out with digital resources in lessons this school year, students reported how often they had been asked to "assess the quality of information generated by Artificial Intelligence chatbots (e.g. ChatGPT, Google Bard)" (ST436Q16DA) and to "assess the quality of information you found online" (ST436Q02DA; both:1 = never or almost never;2 = sometimes;3 = often;4 = very often). Both measures describe tasks in lessons; we interpret them as indicators of opportunities to learn critical evaluation, not of students' own habits or competence.

For brevity, we refer to these student reports as AI-output evaluation and online-information evaluation in what follows. Student reports of frequency of instructional tasks are a standard operationalization of opportunity to learn in international large-scale assessments (Lüdtke et al., 2009; Schmidt et al., 2001; Sergiou et al., 2026).

The same battery includes 13 further activities, such as searching for information online, writing texts, creating presentations or videos, programming, and using virtual or augmented reality (ST436Q01DA, ST436Q03DA-Q14DA; full list in Table 1), each on the same four-point frequency scale.foot_0

Teachers' professional development

Principals reported how many teachers at their school had taken part, during the last academic year, in professional development on "dealing with students' access to Artificial Intelligence chatbots" (SC265Q13DA;1 = none or hardly any;4 = all or nearly all), and in an item on teaching media literacy (SC265Q10DA).

Socioeconomic status and covariates

Socioeconomic status was measured using PISA's index of economic, social and cultural status (ESCS), a composite of parental education, parental occupation, and home possessions.

Covariates were gender (male), immigrant background (second-generation and first-generation indicators relative to no immigrant background), and grade relative to the modal grade in the country.

Preregistration and open data

Hypotheses, analytical procedures, inclusion criteria, decision rules, sensitivity analyses, and the complementary analysis of teachers' professional development were preregistered on AsPredicted on October 15, 2026, prior to any analysis of the international data (AsPredicted #317607, https://aspredicted.org/8ee7ig.pdf). As disclosed in the preregistration, the German PISA 2025 sample (downloaded October 5, 2026) had been analyzed before preregistration, and the resulting estimates informed the specific hypotheses and the equivalence bounds for H3a-i. All hypotheses were therefore re-tested on the full international sample, and sensitivity analyses excluding Germany were conducted for every hypothesis to assess whether conclusions depended on the sample that initially informed them. Analysis code and supplementary materials are available at OSF (https://osf.io/n27km/overview?view_only=141d031f25d540908c7302fae234af2a).

Analytical approach

We used a two-stage integrative data analysis framework, also referred to as two-stage individual participant data meta-analysis (Brunner et al., 2023; Campos et al., 2025; Curran & Hussong, 2009; Riley et al., 2010). This approach allows features of international large-scale assessment data, including sampling weights and the clustering of students within schools, to be accommodated within each country before synthesizing the resulting country-specific estimates across countries.

In the first stage, all estimates were computed separately within each country using the final student weight and the 80 BRR replicate weights (Fay's adjustment k = 0.5) through the survey package in R (Lumley, 2004). As preregistered, each model used all students with complete data on its variables, without imputation, and contrasts within a hypothesis were estimated on the same students. The numbers of countries and students per analysis are reported in Tables S2 and S4 and in the text. In regression models, AI use, ESCS, and all 14 lesson activities (online-information evaluation and the 13 further activities described above) were z-standardized within country; gender, immigrant background, and grade were entered unstandardized.

Coefficients therefore represent the change in the outcome associated with a one-standard-deviation increase in the predictor within a country. Where the outcome was kept on its raw 1-4 scale (H2), a coefficient of 0.10 corresponds to an increase of 0.10 scale points; where the outcome was also z-standardized (H3a-ii, H3b), it corresponds to an increase of 0.10 standard deviations.

Each hypothesis was operationalized at the country level as follows. H1 was the weighted Pearson correlation between AI use for schoolwork and AI-output evaluation on their raw 1-5 and 1-4 scales. H2 was the coefficient of standardized ESCS in a regression of AI-output evaluation (1-4 scale) on standardized ESCS, standardized AI use, and covariates. To express this association as a probability, we additionally regressed a binary indicator of never or almost never being asked to evaluate AI output on the same predictors (linear probability model). H3a-i was the standardized paired mean difference (d av ) between AI-output and online-information evaluation (Lakens, 2013). H3a-ii compared the coefficient of standardized online-information evaluation with the coefficients of the 13 other digital lesson activities in a regression of standardized AI-output evaluation on all 14 activities, standardized AI use, standardized ESCS, and covariates. H3b compared the standardized coefficient of AI use predicting AI-output evaluation with the corresponding coefficient predicting online-information evaluation, estimated jointly in a stacked model with BRR replicate weights copied to both outcome rows to preserve within-student dependency. In an additional analysis that was not preregistered, we compared the coefficient of AI use predicting AI-output evaluation with that of searching for information online in lessons (ST436Q01DA) predicting online-information evaluation, using the same stacked model.

We also re-estimated H1 without students who reported never or almost never using AI for schoolwork. This analysis was not preregistered. We added it to check whether the correlation simply reflects that students who do not use AI have nothing to evaluate.

In the second stage, country-specific estimates were synthesized across countries using REML random-effects meta-analysis with Knapp-Hartung confidence intervals (Knapp & Hartung, 2003) and prediction intervals (metafor package; Viechtbauer, 2010). Correlations were pooled on the Fisher-z scale and share-type outcomes on the logit scale, then back-transformed to their original scales. For H3a-i, equivalence was tested with two one-sided tests (TOST; Lakens, 2017) against bounds of ±0.10 on the d av scale. These bounds correspond to half of a conventionally small effect (d = 0.20; Cohen, 1988), so that differences within them can be considered negligible in practical terms. For H3a-ii, the intersection-union test used the maximum of the 13 pooled contrast p-values. Holm's correction (Holm, 1979) was applied across the five preregistered hypothesis tests.

We report pooled coefficients (B) and 95% confidence intervals, together with between-country heterogeneity (τ² , I² , and 95% prediction intervals). Under the random-effects model, pooled coefficients represent the mean of a distribution of country-specific associations rather than assuming that the underlying association is identical across countries. The OECD reporting standard was followed throughout: country-level estimates based on fewer than 30 students or fewer than 5 schools were not reported.

Three preregistered sensitivity analyses were performed: (1) excluding Germany from all pooled estimates; (2) adjusting H2 for reading proficiency (ten plausible values combined with Rubin's rules (Rubin, 1987)); and (3) estimating H2 within schools (weighted school-mean centering). Full results of all sensitivity analyses are reported in Table S2 of the Supplementary Material.

Teachers' professional development

In a complementary analysis, we examined the association of principal-reported teacher professional development (PD) on AI chatbots with students' reports of AI-output and online-information evaluation in lessons. Outcomes were the student-reported evaluation items; the principal-reported PD measure was assigned to all students of the respective school. We estimated three nested regression models per country: (1) an unadjusted association; (2) adjusting for student ESCS, school-mean ESCS, gender, immigrant background, and grade level relative to the modal grade in the country; and (3) additionally adjusting for student AI use. A fourth model added PD on teaching media literacy as an additional predictor to model 3, to assess whether the association of PD on AI chatbots was specific to AI-related PD. In parallel to H3b, we also estimated, in a stacked model with BRR replicate weights copied to both outcome rows, the difference between the PD coefficient predicting AI-output evaluation and the PD coefficient predicting online-information evaluation. Standard errors were based on the BRR replicate weights, which account for the clustering of students within schools. Country-level estimates were pooled with REML random-effects meta-analysis as for the primary analyses. Analyses were restricted to countries with at least 10 schools with valid principal responses on the PD items. No formal hypothesis decision was made for this complementary analysis; we report pooled coefficients with 95% confidence intervals.

Exploratory analyses

Results

We examined associations between AI use for schoolwork and the evaluation of AI-output in lessons, together with socioeconomic and activity-specific correlates of that evaluation, across the 59 countries and economies in PISA 2025 that met our inclusion criteria. Each hypothesis was tested on the estimate pooled across countries.

Country-specific estimates were obtained with the final student weight and 80 BRR replicate weights, and pooled with REML random-effects meta-analysis using Knapp-Hartung confidence intervals (see Methods for details).

Prevalence of AI use and AI-output evaluation

Across participating countries, AI chatbot use for schoolwork was common among 15-year-olds, and both AI use and AI-output evaluation in lessons varied considerably across countries (see Fig. 1).

On average across countries, 57.5% of students (95% CI [55.0, 60.0]) reported using AI chatbots for schoolwork at least weekly, and about 90% reported any use (Fig. 2). However, only 35.3% (95% CI [33.0, 37.6]) reported being asked often or very often to evaluate the quality of AI-generated information in their lessons, and almost as many-33.2% (95% CI [30.6, 35.9])-reported being asked to do so never or almost never. Country-level use ranged from around 30% in Japan and Ireland to above 70% in Viet Nam, the United Arab Emirates, and Macao (China); country-level evaluation ranged from below 20% in Japan, B-S-J-Z (China), and the United Kingdom to above 50% in the United Arab Emirates, Albania, and Singapore.

Association between AI use and AI-output evaluation

The frequency with which students used AI for schoolwork was positively associated with how often they reported being asked to evaluate AI output in lessons (see Fig. 2). The pooled weighted correlation across the 59 countries was r = 0.34, 95% CI [0.32, 0.35], p(Holm) < .001. The association was present in every participating country: all 59 country-specific correlations were positive, and in all 59 the 95% confidence interval excluded zero. While the magnitude of the correlation varied across educational systems (95% prediction interval [0.20, 0.46]; I² = 96.4%, τ² = 0.005 on the Fisher-z scale; Fig. S1), its direction did not. One could argue that this correlation is partly built into the measures, since students who never use AI may have little to evaluate. We therefore repeated the analysis without these students. The correlation was somewhat smaller but still clearly positive (r = 0.27, 95% CI [0.25, 0.28], 95% PI [0.17, 0.36]; Table S2) and was significant in all 59 countries. Among students who used AI at all, those who used it more often were also asked more often to evaluate its output.

Yet frequent use did not imply frequent evaluation. Even among at-least-weekly AI users, 25.4% (95% CI [23.3, 27.6]) reported never or almost never being asked to evaluate AI output in lessons. Across countries, this percentage ranged from 13.6% in Albania to 52.3% in Japan (95% prediction interval [12.4, 44.9]).

Variance between schools (exploratory)

Intraclass correlations were estimated per country from unweighted random-intercept models without predictors in the 55 of the 59 countries whose public-use files included school identifiers (Iceland, Luxembourg, Macao [China], and Malta did not). Only a small share of the variance in AI-output evaluation lay between schools (median ICC = 0.04, interquartile range [0.03, 0.06]; range 0.01 in Greece to 0.12 in Indonesia; Table S3). The same held for online-information evaluation (median ICC = 0.05, range 0.00 to 0.17) and for AI use for schoolwork (median ICC = 0.04, range 0.01 to 0.18). Most of the variation in how often students reported being asked to evaluate AI output therefore occurred among students attending the same school.

Teachers' professional development

Of the 59 countries, 54 met the inclusion criterion of at least 10 schools with valid principal responses on the PD items (N = 336,606 students). Across these countries, a one-standard-deviation increase in principal-reported teacher PD on "dealing with students' access to AI chatbots" was positively associated with students' reports of AI-output evaluation in lessons (unadjusted B = 0.03, 95% CI [0.03, 0.04]).

The association persisted after adjustment for student ESCS, school-mean ESCS, gender, immigrant background, and grade (B = 0.02, 95% CI [0.02, 0.03]), and remained after additionally adjusting for student AI use (B = 0.02, 95% CI [0.01, 0.03]). The parallel coefficient predicting online-information evaluation was of the same size (B = 0.02, 95% CI [0.01, 0.03]), and the stacked model that preserved the within-student dependency, in parallel to H3b, confirmed that the two associations did not differ (∆B = -0.001, 95% CI [-0.006, 0.004]). Adding PD on teaching media literacy as a comparison predictor (N = 334,751) left the coefficient of PD on AI chatbots essentially unchanged (B = 0.02, 95% CI [0.01, 0.02]), while PD on media literacy was not independently associated with AI-output evaluation (B = 0.003, 95% CI [-0.001, 0.008]); for online-information evaluation, both PD measures retained small but detectable associations (PD on AI chatbots B = 0.02; PD on media literacy B = 0.01).

Between-country heterogeneity of the coefficients of PD on AI chatbots was moderate (I² = 45-64%), with 95% prediction intervals spanning zero in every specification.

Discussion

This study examined how often 15-year-olds across 59 countries reported being asked to evaluate the output of GenAI chatbots in lessons. We compared how common this practice is with students' own AI use for schoolwork, how closely the two are associated, how evaluation opportunities are distributed across socioeconomic backgrounds, and how AI-output evaluation relates to the evaluation of online information. In doing so, we address a gap in the literature on classroom integration of GenAI. While international data on students' AI use for schoolwork have accumulated rapidly, the OECD has so far reported only descriptively how often students are asked to evaluate AI output in lessons and that disadvantaged students report such tasks less often (OECD, 2026c, Box I.4.2). To our knowledge, it has not yet been examined how these opportunities relate to students' own AI use and whether the socioeconomic gap persists among students who use AI equally often.

Drawing on PISA 2025 data from more than 500,000 students, we tested five preregistered hypotheses derived from three complementary frameworks: i) opportunity-to-learn research on classroom instructional practice (Carroll, 1963; Schmidt et al., 2001; Seidel & Shavelson, 2007), ii) second-and third-level digital-divide research on the social distribution of digital competencies (Scheerder et al., 2017; van Deursen & van Dijk, 2014), and iii) the question of continuity versus specificity, that is, how AI-output evaluation relates to the established practice of evaluating online information (Long & Magerko, 2020; Wineburg & McGrew, 2019). GenAI use for schoolwork was common among 15-year-olds, while about a third of students reported never or almost never being asked to evaluate AI output in lessons. Students who used AI more often also reported more frequent AI-output evaluation in all countries, consistent with H1. Additionally, students from more advantaged socioeconomic backgrounds reported being asked more often to evaluate AI output independent of their level of AI use, which is consistent with H2. Contrary to H3a-i, however, AI-output evaluation and online-information evaluation did not occur with comparable frequency. On average, AI-output evaluation was less frequent and the difference exceeded the preregistered equivalence bounds, although its direction varied substantially across countries. Moreover, online-information evaluation was not the single strongest activity correlate of AI-output evaluation but was tied in magnitude with virtual-and augmented-reality use in lessons, which means H3a-ii was not supported. Finally, the association of AI use and AI-output evaluation was substantially stronger than the corresponding association with online-information evaluation in 58 of 59 countries, in line with H3b. In the following, we discuss how these findings relate to each of the three frameworks in turn.

AI use and classroom opportunities to evaluate AI output

The frequency of AI use for schoolwork was positively associated with the frequency of AI-output evaluation in all of the 59 participating countries. From an opportunity-to-learn perspective (Carroll, 1963; Schmidt et al., 2001), this pattern is what one would expect of an instructional system that responds, at least in aggregate, to the activities students bring into the classroom. Students who used AI more often for schoolwork were also more often asked to evaluate AI output in lessons. The association was moderate in magnitude and homogeneous in direction across educational systems, but its strength varied substantially. In some countries the association was considerably stronger than in others. Given the small intraclass correlations of both variables, differences between schools can, on average, account for only a small part of this association. Because PISA samples students across classes and does not identify them, our data cannot distinguish whether it reflects differences between classes and teachers, teachers responding to individual students, or differences in how students perceive the same lessons, nor whether being asked to evaluate AI output in turn encourages AI use.

The association, in other words, is universal in its sign but uneven in its reach.

The deeper issue, however, is that even where the coupling is present, it falls well short of universal provision. Among students who reported using AI chatbots for schoolwork at least weekly, roughly 25% nevertheless reported being asked never or almost never to evaluate AI output in lessons. This percentage ranged from 14% in Albania to 52% in Japan, so a substantial share of regular users lacked such opportunities in every country. These are students who meet the demands of evaluating AI output on a routine basis without being given opportunities to practice doing so in the one setting that reaches them systematically. GenAI places high metacognitive demands on its users (Tankelevitch et al., 2024), yet working with a chatbot can make students less likely to monitor and regulate their own learning (Fan et al., 2025).

Students who use AI often but rarely practice checking its output may therefore be more likely to trust it too much and to miss its mistakes. The implication is not that AI use should be reduced, but that opportunities to practice evaluation should reach the students who use it most.

The socioeconomic distribution of AI-output evaluation

The second important finding was that students from more advantaged socioeconomic backgrounds reported being asked more often to evaluate AI output during lessons, and this difference was independent of their level of AI use. The gradient was positive in every participating country and significant in a clear majority of them, robust in direction but variable in magnitude across educational systems.

Two aspects of this finding warrant attention. First, the gradient is not a gradient in AI use. Our analyses adjusted for the frequency of AI use for schoolwork.

Accordingly, the association between ESCS and evaluation reflects differences in the opportunities students are given, not in the technology they bring into the classroom.

Students who use AI at comparable frequencies are nevertheless offered unequal opportunities to practice judging what it produces. Nor does the gradient merely reflect the sorting of advantaged students into schools with more evaluation-oriented teaching: is still new.

In addition, the association between AI use and AI-output evaluation was substantially stronger than its association with online-information evaluation in 58 of 59 countries. Yet, this coupling was weaker than that between searching for and evaluating online information, so it does not appear to be unique to AI.

H3a-ii was not supported either: online-information evaluation was not the single strongest activity correlate of AI-output evaluation but was tied in magnitude with virtual-and augmented-reality use in lessons. This pattern of results allows for at least two readings our design cannot adjudicate between. One is that both practices are relatively uncommon, teacher-initiated activities that cluster in classrooms with more pedagogically ambitious instructional profiles. The other is that the shared variance operates at the school rather than the classroom level, with innovation-oriented schools adopting both practices in parallel. The intraclass correlations, however, are hard to reconcile with this second reading. On average, only about 4% of the variance in AI-output evaluation lay between schools (see Table S3), so most of the variation arises among students attending the same school, whether between classes or in how students perceive the same lessons. Distinguishing these explanations would require data linking students to specific teachers and classes.

Taken together, these observations do not clearly separate AI-output evaluation from established information-evaluation practice. The two are moderately correlated at the student level, and AI-output evaluation is tied to AI use in a similar way as online-information evaluation is tied to searching online, though less strongly. This suggests that evaluation tends to go along with the use of the respective medium, even though AI-output evaluation is still less common on average.

Teachers' professional development and the specificity question

The stronger coupling with AI-output evaluation we observed for students' AI use did not extend to teachers' professional development on AI. In our analysis, the share of teachers at a school who had participated in professional development on dealing with students' access to AI chatbots was only weakly, but positively associated with AI-output evaluation in lessons. Yet, the association was indistinguishable in magnitude from the parallel association with online-information evaluation. Where AI use for schoolwork was more strongly associated with AI-output than with online-information evaluation, teachers' AI-related professional development was not.

This pattern is not unique to AI-related professional development. In ICILS 2018, teachers' participation in ICT-related professional development was positively, but only inconsistently associated with their technology-related teaching practices. The association was positive in the pooled sample across eight countries, but statistically significant in only four to five of the eight countries depending on the outcome. Instead, teachers' ICT self-efficacy and collaboration with colleagues showed considerably more consistent associations with these practices (Konstantinidou & Scherer, 2022). The present result for AI-related professional development further extends this pattern. Its association with AI-output evaluation was small and positive on average, but not specific to AI. Additionally, its heterogeneity across countries leaves the association in any single country uncertain.

This pattern is consistent with the longer-standing finding that participation measures capture little of what makes professional development instructionally consequential. The literature on effective professional development identified content focus, active learning, coherence with other reforms, duration, and collective participation among teachers as the features that drive change in classroom practice (Desimone, 2009). A single-item principal report on the share of teachers who attended training on a given topic captures none of these features directly. Moreover, the item asked about training on dealing with students' access to AI chatbots. Such training may cover school rules as well as teaching, which makes it only a rough proxy for preparing teachers to address AI output in class. A second reason lies in the level of measurement.

Principals reported professional development for the whole school, but only a small share of the variance in either kind of evaluation lay between schools. A school-level measure can only relate to that share, so its associations were bound to be small, and there was little room for them to differ between the two outcomes. Accordingly, our null specificity result on this measure does not mean that professional development on AI is ineffective. It shows only that school-level participation rates, as measured in PISA, are not linked more closely to AI-output evaluation than to online-information evaluation.

Implications

Our findings arrive as international frameworks begin to specify what AI literacy should mean in schools. The OECD framework and UNESCO's AI competency framework for students both identify critical evaluation of AI and its output as a core competence (OECD & European Commission, 2026; UNESCO, 2024), and PISA will assess Media and Artificial Intelligence Literacy for the first time in 2029 (OECD, 2026a). The present cycle establishes a baseline against which that assessment can be read, and points to three issues its design will have to address.

The first concerns the object of policy. In educational-technology research, the focus has long been on access, with use and benefit treated as downstream consequences. Our results suggest that for GenAI this sequencing no longer holds. Use is widespread, while the instructional conditions under which that use could support learning are not. As such, any framework that measures only exposure or access will understate the relevant inequality.

The second addresses its distribution. The socioeconomic gradient in opportunities to evaluate AI output parallels earlier gradients in digital-skill opportunities (OECD, 2021; Scheerder et al., 2017; van Deursen & van Dijk, 2014; Wang et al., 2026) and emerges in the first cycle to measure these opportunities at scale. This parallel matters analytically as it situates AI-output evaluation within an established literature on second-and third-level digital divides rather than as a novel phenomenon-and practically, because it indicates where compensatory provision will need to be concentrated if the gradient is not to widen as AI use intensifies.

The third concerns the mechanism through which such provision would operate.

Teachers are the proximate agents of any classroom-level change, but our analysis suggests that the share of teachers participating in AI-related professional development is only weakly, and not specifically, associated with AI-output evaluation in lessons.

This finding is consistent with evidence that participation measures often do not capture specific features of professional development that drive instructional change (Desimone, 2009). The implication is not that professional development on AI is ineffective, but that its design and its measurement will have to move beyond participation if it is to inform the practices we have examined here.

Whether these conditions develop will determine how the next PISA cycle reads.

The present findings mark a point of departure and identify the dimensions along which movement can be assessed.

Limitations and Future Research

Some limitations qualify the inferences we draw in this study. The first addresses measurement. Our indicators of opportunity to learn rest on student reports of how often they were asked to carry out specific tasks in class. Such student reports of instructional practice are a standard operationalization in international large-scale assessments and have the advantage of capturing what reaches students. However, it needs to be noted that they confound shared classroom experience with individual perception (Lüdtke et al., 2009; Sergiou et al., 2026). Additionally, they do not speak to the depth, quality, or pedagogical framing of the opportunities being counted. For example, an in-depth, one-time teaching unit on evaluating AI output, followed by occasional brief follow-up impulses, could have a greater impact on students' related skills than frequent, superficial exposure. Furthermore, the frequency response scale used in the student survey-with options ranging from "never or almost never" to "very often"-allows for considerable subjective interpretation in students' responses. Both evaluation measures are also single items with unknown reliability. Measurement error could therefore weaken or, through shared response tendencies, strengthen the associations we report, and it could affect comparisons between the two items.

Moreover, part of the association between AI use and AI-output evaluation may be built into the measures, since evaluating AI output in class usually involves using AI.

Excluding students who never used AI weakened the correlation only slightly, but it cannot rule out such overlap among students who do use AI.

Another limitation is related to study design. It needs to be kept in mind that PISA data are cross-sectional, and our estimates describe associations at a single point in time. As such, we cannot establish whether more frequent AI use leads lessons to incorporate more AI-output evaluation, whether classrooms that already evaluate AI output encourage more frequent use, or whether both reflect characteristics of schools, teachers, or students that we have not measured. Nevertheless, the consistency of the observed associations across 59 educational systems constrains but does not resolve this ambiguity. Longitudinal data tracking the same students, or quasi-experimental variation in curricular introduction of AI-literacy content, would be needed to specify the direction of effects.

Finally, our findings are limited in their generalizability, both to other evaluative practices and to other countries. The AI-output evaluation item refers to a specific instructional task-assessing the quality of information generated by AI chatbots-and does not cover other evaluative practices that AI literacy frameworks include, such as reasoning about model limitations, identifying training-data provenance, or recognizing generated content outside instructional settings. The gradient we document in one such practice is unlikely to reflect the entirety of the AI-literacy gradient. As such, data from future cycles, including the PISA 2029 Media and AI Literacy assessment (OECD, 2026a), will be in a better position to establish how it extends to directly assessed competencies. Our findings also apply only to the countries we included. About a third of the countries and economies in PISA 2025 were excluded, most of them because too few students answered the AI-output evaluation item, and these systems may differ in how they approach AI in schools.

Conclusion

Across 59 countries, GenAI chatbots have become a routine part of 15-year-olds' schoolwork, yet many students who use them report few opportunities to evaluate their output in lessons. According to students' reports, opportunities to practice critical evaluation of AI output in lessons are missing for about 25% of at-least-weekly AI users, are unequally distributed by socioeconomic background, and are associated with students' AI use more closely than opportunities to evaluate online information are.

Moreover, teachers' participation in AI-related professional development, as currently measured, is only weakly associated with AI-output evaluation in lessons, and no more strongly than with the evaluation of online information. These findings establish, for the first PISA cycle in which these opportunities were measured at scale, where classroom integration of GenAI stands-and identify the dimensions along which progress towards the critical AI engagement that international frameworks call for will have to be made.
