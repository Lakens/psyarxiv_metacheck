Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). Statcheck might still miss one-sided tests that are described in another sentence, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. Some differences come from the ground truth: it lacks p-values with a correction name (“p Bonf = .061”), and counts partial eta squared written with its subscript after it (“η 2 p = .05”), “p = .05”, and some table notes and figure legends (“† p < .10, *p < .05”), which this module leaves out, as non-significant p-values. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

If you want to extend the package to be able to download files from additional data repositories reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Narrative therapy made narration the medium of its clinical work and the means through which it contests the truths of psychology; the status it grants the narrative shapes its accounts of knowledge, subject, problem, and change. This article reconstructs that status in White and Epston and in the poststructuralist radicalizations of Madigan and Guilfoyle. The tradition historicized psychopathological truths and the authority of expert knowledge and stabilized certain narrative properties (temporal sequence, biographical coherence, and attribution to a subject) as general conditions for understanding experience, an asymmetry of historicization. Taking Foucault's concept of dispositif as he formulated it, we shift the object of analysis from narratives to dispositifs of the narrative, configurations of discursive and nondiscursive practices, relations of force, and practices of the self within which narrating oneself becomes possible, required, and credible. Because the asymmetry concerns forms that periods and cultures made available and the clinic works with persons, the concept has a sociohistorical use, which follows them over time, and a clinical use, developed here, which describes their configuration in a particular life. This displacement makes the radicalization poststructuralist in lineage and postconstructionist in position, reorienting how narrative therapy conceives its object, listening, knowledge, agency, and change.

Introduction

Developed by Michael White and David Epston in the late 1980s, narrative therapy organized its clinical practice around a break with psychologism, the tendency to locate within the person what is produced through their relations with the knowledges, institutions, and norms that describe them (Madigan, 2011). The approach has since generated a growing body of research on therapeutic processes and outcomes (Hu et al., 2024; Moggia, 2019). Its accounts of knowledge, of the subject, of the problems brought to therapy, and of change depend on the status it grants the narrative, a term we use for the domain of narration as such, in the sense in which social theory speaks of the social or the political. Narration is at once the medium of its clinical work and the means through which it contests the truths of psychology, and that status is the question this article addresses as a contribution to psychological theory. White and Epston (1993) historicized psychopathological truths, the self that psychology presents as a substance, and the authority of expert knowledge; they also recognized the cultural conditions of their own analogies and a possible historical participation of writing in the linear organization of time. Stephen Madigan (2011) and Michael Guilfoyle (2014) carried these Foucauldian commitments toward the institutional authority of stories and toward a theory of the subject able to account for resistance, developments we refer to as the poststructuralist radicalizations of narrative therapy. In the reconstruction proposed here, these formulations stabilized certain properties of narrative organization, such as temporal sequence, biographical coherence, and attribution to a subject, as general conditions for understanding experience, and those properties received less historical examination than the stories, diagnoses, and identities they allowed the clinic to problematize. We call this an asymmetry of historicization.

If the asymmetry lies in a form the tradition used to understand experience without examining how that form became available, the narrative itself must undergo the historical analysis the tradition applied to psychopathological truths. Our thesis is that the Foucauldian concept of dispositif makes that analysis possible. The tradition already took from Foucault the inquiry into how knowledges, institutions, and relations of power produce the truths and subjects of psychology. Applied to narration, a step the tradition left untaken, the same tools describe dispositifs of the narrative: configurations of discursive and nondiscursive practices, relations of force, and practices of the self within which narrating oneself becomes possible, required, and credible. The displacement bears on the object of analysis and leaves Foucault's toolbox as he assembled it; the object comes to include the conditions under which certain practices are recognized, required, or authorized as narrative. It operates at two scales. The asymmetry concerns forms that cultures and periods made available, and it calls for a sociohistorical use of the concept that follows these dispositifs across time; narrative therapy works with persons, and it calls for a clinical use that describes how such dispositifs are configured in a particular life and how far that life can be narrated otherwise. This article develops the clinical use and situates the sociohistorical one. Both uses require an account of the capacity to act that places no subject prior to the relations that constitute it, since whoever narrates is produced within the same dispositifs.

The argument proceeds in four movements. The first reconstructs the status and function of the narrative in White and Epston. The second examines how Madigan and Guilfoyle modify that status at different levels. The third organizes the concept of dispositif into four analytical dimensions that take up the difficulties left open and distinguishes its two uses. The fourth names the position this displacement defines, a new poststructuralist radicalization with a postconstructionist orientation, and draws its consequences for how narrative therapy conceives its object of analysis, its listening, the knowledge it produces, agency, and therapeutic change.

The article thereby offers a critical reconstruction, a conceptual framework, and its clinical implications.

Narrative Therapy and the Status of the Narrative

The narrative names the meeting point of two trajectories that converged during the second half of the 1980s. The first unfolded in Adelaide, where White organized his clinical work around Gregory Bateson's (2012) epistemology, for which knowledge of the world proceeds through interpretations mediated by premises or maps. From it he derived negative explanation, according to which people adopt problematic courses of action because networks of presuppositions restrain other possible courses (White, 1986). The second unfolded in Auckland, where Epston worked within the anthropological debate over representation in ethnographic writing and oriented therapy through the metaphor of the rite of passage (Epston & White, 1992).

He also developed a clinical writing in which therapeutic letters turned lived experience into a coherent and credible account and returned to people the texts written about their lives (White & Epston, 1993).

The trajectories met when the temporal dimension of experience, scarcely elaborated by Batesonian cybernetics, led White (1992b), encouraged by Cheryl White and Epston, to replace the cybernetic analogy with the text analogy toward the end of the 1980s (Bubenzer et al., 1994).

The change accompanied the movement of family therapy away from the system metaphor that made the therapist an expert in the family's dysfunctions (Moreno, 2014), within an encounter with constructionism whose stakes remain under discussion (Travasaros, 2026; Ugazio, 2026).

Two lineages gave the narrative its theoretical foundations. The first, Jerome Bruner's (1998) cognitive psychology, incorporated around 1987 (Matheus & Bolze, 2022), made it a mode of thought that follows human intentions across time and evaluates accounts by their lifelikeness.

The second, the anthropology of experience, made it the dominant frame through which lived experience is organized and patterned (E. Bruner, 1986).

The reading of Michel Foucault (2002b) introduced a problem of another order: the historical constitution of objects, subjects, and truths within relations of knowledge and power, among which disciplinary power fabricates individuals through normalizing judgment and examination. White and Epston (1993) took up this problem to ask what place dominant stories occupy within those relations. In their reading, power operates constitutively in local sites such as clinics and families and recruits persons into evaluating and surveilling themselves under a normalizing gaze, and unitary knowledges with claims to truth displace the knowledges they subjugate. Stories thereby take part in the practices that produce subjectivity and acquire within them the authority to specify who a person is; a story becomes dominant when it holds that authority over a life. The same founding text gives writing a place of its own, attributing to it the preservation of knowledges, other ways of organizing information, and the ordering of events in temporal sequences, functions that ground the use of letters and other documents in therapy. Under a single name, the narrative thus gathers problems that stand at four different levels. The first two, form and constitution, are ways of conceptualizing narration: form through the narrative mode, which links intentions and events across time, and constitution through the text analogy, in which stories organize the experience of those who live them. The third, authority, concerns the relations in which certain stories acquire legitimacy over a life and others are subjugated. The fourth, inscription, names a material condition of the existence and circulation of stories, and writing thereby appears as an operation that the tradition mobilizes in excess of what its definitions of narration delimit. This heterogeneity raises a problem about the limits of the theoretical object, and it may explain why practitioners still answer the question of what narrative therapy is in disparate ways (Wallis et al., 2011). The problem can be followed in the approach's answers about knowledge, the subject, the problem, and change.

The narrative mode gave the tradition one component of its conception of knowledge. J. Bruner's (1998) distinction between paradigmatic and narrative modes concerns forms of intelligibility, and it helped legitimate the narrative intelligibility of particular experiences. The distinction between local and expert knowledge, in turn, concerns the relations in which knowledge is produced, legitimated, and authorized, given that a professional description can take narrative form and a person can produce paradigmatic knowledge about their own experience. Local knowledge, expressed in descriptions close to experience (Geertz, 1994), acquired its authority through the Foucauldian reading of subjugated knowledges, and practices such as consulting the consultants and co-research turned that authority into knowledge that is documented, consulted, and circulated (Epston, 1999 (Epston, , 2014;; Epston & White, 1992). The epistemology of the approach rests on the convergence of these two formulations, neither of which follows from the other, and in that convergence the narrative acquired a political function absent from its cognitive source. White (1992b) adopted the text analogy to account for the temporal dimension of experience, and in his formulations on subjectivity the analogy acquired a constitutive function.

If stories organize experience, the subject is social and multistoried, constituted in narratives received from a culture and realized in living them; a life is then the performance of texts. The temporal linking of events explains how a story organizes a life, and the availability of stories requires another explanation, because people access their experience through stories that a culture circulates and that others assign to them before they narrate themselves. White (1992a) therefore calls his position constitutionalist. In his account, lives are shaped by the meanings persons ascribe to their experience, by their place in social structures, and by the practices of language and relationship into which they are recruited, and agency lies in an indeterminacy within determinacy, in the gaps and contradictions that no plot fully resolves. Constitution thus came to depend on cultural and political relations that exceed the temporal organization of events.

The conception of the problem made those relations explicit. Problems acquire intelligibility and take shape within relations in which certain narratives receive authority over a life, and each culture and epoch attributes such narratives, always arbitrarily, to an internal or an external element (Epston, 1992). Externalization accordingly works as a counterpractice directed at their localization inside the person (White & Epston, 1993). The conception of change gathered all four levels. Re-authoring composes an alternative story from the unique outcomes that the dominant story left out and links them in landscapes of action and identity, a form taken from J. Bruner (White, 2016). The ceremonies, documents, and leagues that witness and circulate that story contest the authority of the dominant one and give the alternative a material permanence (Denborough, 2012).

This convergence gives the narrative a second, critical function, through which the tradition contested the expert's claim to establish the truth of problems as disorders and historicized psychopathological truths, the self that psychology presents as a substance, and the authority of expert knowledge (White, 1992a (White, , 1992b)). The narrative is therefore at once the medium through which the clinic knows a life and the means by which it contests the truths of psychology. This double place makes its own status a theoretical problem, and the founding text shows that its reexamination must start from resources the tradition already possesses. White and Epston (1993) open their founding text with the premise that persons who strive to make sense of their lives face the task of arranging their experience of events in sequences across time, connecting past, present, and anticipated events in a linear sequence so as to arrive at a coherent account of themselves and their world. The authors later acknowledge that the properties Jerome Bruner studied belong to literary texts and extend them to lives, holding that persons generally give meaning to their lives through stories and that most conversations share narrative properties. They also adopt his claim that narrative is the form able to describe lived time, which clock, calendrical, serial, and cyclical orders leave uncaptured (J. Bruner, 1987). The same text takes from Edward Bruner (1986) the recognition that some experiences may remain unstoried or lack the narrative resources to be expressed, with the result that lived experience exceeds the stories available. Narration thereby acquires the status of a general reference for understanding experience, formulated as a generality and qualified by a recognized excess. The link between problems and insufficient stories, presented as an assumption adopted for the discussion, keeps that reference on both sides of the clinical work: the problem becomes intelligible through one story, and its resolution through another.

The recognition of writing gives this generalization a historical index. White and Epston (1993) distinguish written from oral traditions, acknowledge ways of transmitting and organizing knowledge that do without writing, and note the cultural authority granted to visual evidence.

They also propose that the written tradition may be considered one of the important mechanisms in the introduction of a linear conception of time, given its capacity to record events and to make the transformations of a life perceptible. Three dimensions need to be distinguished here: first, the temporality of experience; second, the linear disposition of events; and third, the narrative intelligibility of a life. The tradition advances a historical and material thesis about the second, through writing, and a theoretical generalization about the third, through the premise and its Brunerian sources. The premise itself articulates the two, because it defines making sense of a life as connecting events in a linear sequence toward a coherent account. The tension lies in the difference of scope between the thesis and the generalization: the tradition recognizes the possible historical participation of writing in the linear organization of time and employs that same organization as a general reference for narrative intelligibility. The tradition's reflexivity about analogies reaches far, as it relates the preference for an analogy to ideological factors and prevailing cultural practices and examines the implications each analogy has for the lives it describes. The asymmetry of historicization lies in the scope of these recognitions. They reconstruct the provenance of the analogy and advance a thesis about the history of linear time, and they leave to be historicized the conditions under which what the analogy describes, a life organized as a story, came to be constituted as an object.

The founding texts formulate a generality of narrative intelligibility, qualified by quantifiers and by a recognized excess of experience. They draw the universality of narrative functioning from Jerome Bruner's (1998) cognitive psychology and take the recurrence of an articulation of past, present, and future across the ethnographies Edward Bruner (1986) analyzed as an indication on the cultural side. What the analysis identifies is the stabilization of the narrative properties the premise requires within an explanatory architecture that simultaneously recognizes historical conditions for some of them. The tradition recognizes that no story represents lived experience exhaustively and that some experiences lack available narrative resources. At the same time, its clinical elaboration of experiences that exceed a dominant story keeps a privileged orientation toward their articulation in another story, which leaves open the question of other ways of elaborating experience. This stabilization can be read as a naturalization insofar as narrative form is examined with less historical intensity than the stories, diagnoses, and identities it allows the clinic to problematize; a naturalizing tendency thus coexists in the tradition with critical resources able to question it. Referred to the mind, the stabilized properties take on the character of a universal cognitive capacity, a reading that Phipps and Vorster (2011) make explicit when they argue that the assumption of storied experience invokes an intrapsychic perspective and brings the approach close to personal construct psychology, script theory, and the self-concept. Referred to culture, they take on the character of a structure that persists across histories. Essentialization names this possible result of the naturalizing tendency, and in that measure the essentialism the tradition dismantled in diagnosis can be read as returning in the object that gives the approach its name.

What remains open is how narrating oneself became possible, required, and credible as a legitimate and expected way of relating to oneself within configurations of knowledges, practices, institutions, and relations of force, in such a way that narration takes part in historical forms of subjectivation. The maps through which White (2016) organized practice give this question clinical weight, because their landscapes of action and identity carry the premise into the structure of questioning. If the form is historical, a practice that presupposes it may read the problems now brought to therapy through categories formed under other conditions of intelligibility, a thesis whose clinical scope would require studying how the maps are used. The letters, documents, archives, leagues, and ceremonies on which the tradition's practice relies belong to those configurations. Their circulation, permanence, and disposition before others raise the possibility that they take part in constituting historically the very possibilities of narrating.

The Poststructuralist Radicalizations

The founding formulations brought Foucauldian analyses of power and subjectivity into an account of narrative intelligibility and left open whether their own narrative premises could undergo an equivalent historical inquiry. Madigan (2011) and Guilfoyle (2014) carried those analyses further: the first toward the institutional inscription of identities, the second toward an ontology of the subject able to account for resistance.

In Madigan's (2011) work, a story is first of all a discourse that has been internalized. His reading of externalization as the externalization of internalized discourse turns the problemsaturated story into a statement whose provenance can be traced to communities of discourse, that is, cultural formations in which norms are prescribed through exchanges structured by power relations. The everyday operation of such statements can be followed in conversational habits such as self-surveillance before an imagined audience and paralyzing guilt (Madigan, 1996).

Narration becomes political rhetoric, and its inspection takes the form of counterviewing, which examines the cultural provenance, normative assumptions, and effects of the descriptions a person holds of themselves and extends that examination to the therapist's own questions (Madigan, 2003).

The same inspection reaches the archive. Following Foucault's thesis that the file captured individuals and fixed them in time through writing, Madigan (2011) reads the psychiatric file as the site where expert archives translate a life into professional documents. He answers it in two ways: with the letter-writing campaign, a counterarchive composed by those who know the person within their relationships, and with communities of care that hold the person's counterstories until the person can reach them again (Madigan, 2012). Writing gives certain descriptions permanence, legitimacy, and circulation, and the file and the counterarchive dispute which descriptions acquire that efficacy.

What Madigan inspects in narration is its provenance and its authority; its form, at the same time, remains the one he inherited. He treats the therapeutic process as discourse understood as political rhetoric, a framework that allows him to retain the text analogy, the landscapes of action and identity, and J. Bruner's two modes (Madigan, 2011). His best-known question, who has the right to tell the story being told, contests the authorship of an account and presupposes that a life must become legible as a story; the file and the counterarchive thus compete for the same kind of object. The status of the subject shows a related tension. His work describes a self wholly determined and decentered by discourse and a person who can never be fixed and remains in a constant state of resistance (Madigan, 2003). A Foucauldian conception of power relations can hold both claims together provided it explains how resistance takes shape within the relations that constitute the person, an explanation that his apparatus, concentrated on the relations that produce dominant descriptions, leaves underdeveloped. His vocabulary of change, which speaks of reclaiming aspects of the self that the problem made the person forget, admits two readings: first, the recovery of subordinated experiences; second, the postulation of an identity prior to discourse. The first carries no commitment to the second. Guilfoyle (2012) takes up the question Madigan leaves open, who could resist the very discourses that constitute them, and his answer brings narration into the ontology of the subject.

His project arose from a clinical discomfort: White's conversation maps worked for him as roads someone else had signposted, leading the person regardless of the rhythm of the relationship (Guilfoyle, 2015). He argues that White's answers describe the conditions of resistance and leave its emergence unexplained, and he separates two problems: why someone refuses an assigned position and why someone seeks another (Guilfoyle, 2014). The first, negative resistance, rests on the bodily activity of a thin pre-subjected human being, because power over a body presupposes a body with power to act. The second, positive resistance, rests on a tendency to narrate oneself, which pushes the person back toward discourse because the void a refusal leaves is uninhabitable (Guilfoyle, 2016b); elsewhere he formulates this tendency as an agency prior to discourse and a permanent orientation toward it (Guilfoyle, 2016a). In the reconstruction proposed here, the tendency to narrate oneself thereby occupies an ontological function. The foundations implied that function in the privileged place they gave narration in the intelligibility of lived time, and Guilfoyle formulates it explicitly in relation to resistance and habitability.

The same theory gives narration its most precise delimitation in the tradition, an answer to the heterogeneity the founding conceptions left unresolved. Guilfoyle (2016a) distinguishes language, understood as meaningful words, from discourse, understood as a formation of knowledge and power, and reserves narrative for the inhabitable position that discourse offers.

The dominance of a narrative results from four operations through which power acts as the silent partner of knowledge: invisible coordination, the concealment of alternatives, individualization, and naturalization. Concealment no longer refers to a false consciousness, as alternative discourses remain available with so few supports in a person's history that they become uninhabitable (Guilfoyle, 2014). He thereby abandons the hidden truth of ideology and keeps its topology, in which what loses visibility is the possibility of inhabiting other positions, which therapy makes perceptible again (Guilfoyle, 2015). Madigan's counterviewing performs the same critical operation of making visible on another object. Narration becomes inspectable here as a position, and its absence becomes a clinical problem. Discursive famine names an unstoried experience that finds no resonance in the available stories and deprives the person of critical distance, temporal order, an orienting identity, and a place before others, a deprivation that can coexist with meaningful language. Guilfoyle (2018) recognizes that some experiences resist narrative articulation and that imposing coherent stories can distort them. His constitutive witnessing accordingly suspends the immediate demand for narrative coherence and sustains a client-led orientation toward the possible emergence of stories.

What Guilfoyle inspects in narration is its relation to habitability, and the properties examined in the previous section return in that relation. The deprivations that famine names (temporal order, an orienting identity, and a place before others) coincide with the properties whose stabilization the foundations left unexamined, and the theory makes them conditions of habitability. The first is the efficacy of practices irreducible to the content of stories. Raised with White and Epston, it reappears in Madigan's archives and in Guilfoyle's distributed agency, where institutions and assemblages are recognized and the concepts privilege the authority of descriptions and the individual capacity to resist. The second is the topology of the visible: both radicalizations privilege operations of making visible, which raises the question of whether an organization of the visible and the hidden suffices to understand how power relations are produced. The third is the historicity of narrative form, which gains weight because the counterarchive presupposes legibility as a story and discursive famine makes a narrative position a condition of habitability. The fourth, the conditions of emergence of resistance, becomes a demand of our own argument, given that setting aside a prediscursive orientation toward narration requires an understanding of agency that depends on no capacity external to the relations that constitute the subject. The Foucauldian concept of dispositif offers resources to work these four difficulties together.

Dispositifs of the Narrative

The four difficulties converge on one requirement: an analysis able to treat narration as produced, together with the practices, visibilities, forces, and forms of subjectivity that make it possible. We read Foucault's work as a historical ontology of the subject (Dreyfus & Rabinow, 2001), an inquiry into how power relations, knowledges, and institutions organize the conditions under which subjects are produced and governed and become able to refuse being governed in a particular way (Foucault, 1995), and the dispositif is the concept with which that inquiry describes heterogeneous configurations (Chignola, 2016). Applied to narration, it yields dispositifs of the narrative, the configurations through which narration is approached, known, incited, and made credible, and within which narrating oneself acquires consistency and a function. What the tradition calls the narrative, whether a cognitive capacity or a cultural structure, appears within them as a point of reference they produce, and the analysis accordingly attributes no conceptual unity to the narrative in advance.

The dispositif receives limited systematic treatment in Foucault's work (Chignola, 2016).

Its most concentrated formulation, in a 1977 interview, presents three aspects: a heterogeneous ensemble of elements, the relations among them, and a strategic function tied to an urgent need (Foucault, 1991). We order these aspects into four analytical dimensions: first, discursive and nondiscursive practices; second, relations of knowledge, power, and subjectivity; third, historicity, which traverses the three aspects; and fourth, urgency. This ordering keeps the elements of Foucault's formulation and arranges them for the object at hand, and each dimension takes up, in the same order, one of the difficulties the radicalizations left open. Foucault applied the concept to the configurations of societies and periods; its dimensions can also describe how a configuration composes a particular life, and the section closes by situating both scales.

Discursive and Nondiscursive Practices

The first dimension addresses a problem left open by Foucault's (2002a) archaeology. Its central concept was the episteme, the set of relations that unites, in a given period, the discursive practices from which sciences and other figures of knowledge arise, and archaeology described the rules through which discourse systematically forms the objects of which it speaks. Discourses remained bound to institutions, political events, and economic processes, which archaeology designated as nondiscursive domains without explaining their articulation with discourse. Foucault (1991) later presented the episteme as a specifically discursive dispositif, a particular case of the dispositif in its general form, which is both discursive and nondiscursive. The dispositif thereby complements the episteme in his analytic and makes heterogeneity constitutive, as it encompasses the said and the unsaid: discourses, institutions, architectural arrangements, regulatory decisions, scientific or moral statements, and the network established among them.

The distinction between the discursive and the nondiscursive cuts across the distinction between words and things. In Deleuze's (1987) reading, knowledge is composed of two heterogeneous forms, the articulable and the visible, each with its own historical conditions.

Statements are extracted from words, phrases, and propositions without coinciding with them, and visibilities are extracted from things and states of affairs as forms of luminosity that make some aspects appear and leave others in shadow. The nondiscursive therefore designates a register of its own, the visible, irreducible to the materiality of objects and to the words that name them. The two registers intersect without either deriving from the other (Deleuze, 1990), as when the prison makes the delinquent visible and penal law articulates delinquency (Foucault, 2002b).

Narrative therapy faces an analogous problem, and the distinction allows it to be stated at the level of the narrative itself. Its practice relies on letters, certificates, ceremonies, and leagues, and its theory explains their efficacy through the story they carry; what is seen in therapy is thus understood as what a story says. Within dispositifs of the narrative, narration designates a historically specific relation between the two registers, in which statements organized as plot, sequence, and attribution to a subject capture visibilities such as events, scenes, bodies, and gestures and give them the form of a life. Deleuze (1987) attributes primacy to the statement in (White, 2016). The thesis White and Epston (1993) advanced about writing and linear time acquires its full scope here. They attributed to writing the capacity to make the transformations of a life perceptible, and a linear order of events can be read as one historical form of the capture of the visible by the articulable. This irreducibility carries a methodological precaution. Guilfoyle (2014) calls anticipation the movement through which a therapist presupposes a hopeful path and leads the person along it. We extend the term to an additional type, the anticipation of the relation between discursive and nondiscursive elements, an anticipation that takes two frequent forms in clinical reading. The first assumes that the discursive determines the nondiscursive and reads a ceremony, a file, or the arrangement of a session as an expression of the story told in it. The second assumes that the nondiscursive determines the discursive and reads the story as a reflection of the institution in which it is produced. Each finds in a situation the events that confirm the relation it prescribed, and both leave unexamined which relation operates in the case at hand.

Relations of Knowledge, Power, and Subjectivity

The precaution leaves the relation between the two registers to be established in each case. Because neither derives from the other, the way statements and visibilities affect one another must be examined immanently, in the situation where they meet, and that mutual affection is where relations of power appear. In Deleuze's (1987) reading of Foucault, power is a relation of forces, and a force has no object other than other forces. It acts upon actions, inciting, inducing, facilitating, hindering, extending, or limiting them, with prohibition as one of its operations, and it is defined by its capacity to affect and to be affected (Foucault, 2005). Forces are informal and unstable, and they are actualized in the forms of knowledge, where they consolidate what can be said and what can be seen in a period. In a dispositif, understood as a skein of lines that never settle into equilibrium (Deleuze, 1990), a distribution of the sayable and the visible owes its relative stability to relations of force; the analysis accordingly asks which Subjectivity belongs to the same field. A line of subjectivation emerges when force folds back upon itself and affects itself, producing a relation to oneself that is a fold of the outside (Deleuze, 1987). The subject is therefore itself a force, a folding of the relations that traverse it, and its activity is positive: as a force, it acts upon other forces, incites them, and composes with them (Deleuze, 1990). Resistance names one effect of that capacity, the one in which a force diverts or refuses a conduct, and the fold also sustains the invention of ways of living, alliances, and practices that a configuration had not anticipated. The analysis therefore attends to the effects and actions through which a fold takes part in a composition and to the possibilities those actions open for others.

The distinction between power relations and states of domination specifies the scope of these folds. For Foucault (1999), power is a strategic game of freedoms among subjects capable of modifying the course of an action, and domination designates situations in which those possibilities become asymmetrically fixed and lose their reversibility. The self-surveillance that Madigan (2003) describes can be read as a fold whose reversibility depends on the relations that sustain it.

The same reading clarifies what we share with Guilfoyle (2012) and where we differ. To avoid positing a wholly captured subject, his work grounds negative resistance in the individual's embodied agentive capacities and states that refusal is itself no product of discourse or power.

We read this claim as sharing a premise with the failure-of-power account he attributes to White, insofar as both locate the conditions of emergence of resistance at a limit of what subjection can produce. Our divergence concerns where the capacity to act is situated. Understood as a relation of forces, power designates a field of possible actions in which indeterminacy belongs to its exercise. The subject, as a fold of those forces, acts within the same relations that subject it, and refusal becomes one effect of its capacity to act alongside invention and composition.

The subject thus appears as a fold produced within relations of force, and the same holds for narrating oneself, which the radicalizations referred to a cultural form or to a tendency of the subject. That produced character calls for a way of inquiring into how the production took place without referring it to a ground prior to the relations that compose it. This is the task of genealogy.

Historicity and Genealogy

The third dimension inquires historically into that production. It situates a dispositif's relations in time and shows that the diverse lines through which they were formed compose no coherent totality (Chignola, 2016). Deleuze (1990) Genealogy is Foucault's (1979c) mode of inquiry for this task, and its method rests on a distinction he reads in Nietzsche's vocabulary. Ursprung, the origin, names the search for an essence that precedes the accidents of history: a moment of perfection in which a thing coincided with its truth and of which its later forms would be expressions or degradations. Foucault sets that search aside and works with two other terms. The first, Herkunft, descent or provenance, follows the plurality of trajectories, accidents, and deviations that converge in a phenomenon without sharing a common root, and it fragments what appeared unified. The second, Entstehung, emergence, designates the threshold at which some of those trajectories meet within a particular state of forces and produce a configuration that did not previously exist, a scene of confrontation that belongs to no one and that no prior intention governs. The distinction separates genealogy from any history that reads the past as the realization of an essence, and what exists then appears as the contingent result of struggles. Applied to the narrative, the search for an origin would posit an original human need to organize experience in stories, the naturalization the first section identified; genealogy asks through which descents and at which thresholds narrating oneself became required. Its procedure is eventalization, which treats what presents itself as obvious as an event and asks how it came to function as a necessary reality (Foucault, 1995).

Eventalization extends to the whole configuration the historicization that the radicalizations applied to the movement from the institution into the voice through which someone judges themselves (Guilfoyle, 2016c; Madigan, 2003). Emergence includes the urgency to which a configuration came to respond, and giving form to that urgency is the task of the last dimension.

Urgency, Overdetermination, and Reflected Intractability

The fourth dimension gives form to that urgency. Foucault (1991) introduces urgent need to explain the strategic function a configuration acquires, and government, the conduct of conduct, names the rationality through which a configuration manages life and leads the governed to experience its objectives as their own (Foucault, 2006). Power relations pursue aims that no subject decides (Foucault, 2005), and a dispositif accordingly conducts lives without anyone directing it.

Such conduction might suggest that a dispositif governs without remainder. For Foucault (1991), a dispositif sustains itself through functional overdetermination, in which each effect, intended or otherwise, resonates or clashes with others and requires the heterogeneous elements to be readjusted, and it produces an excess that arises from its heterogeneity (Chignola, 2016).

The same interview describes a second process, strategic elaboration, through which the dispositif captures these overdeterminations and puts them to new uses (Foucault, 1991). Every excess thus helps condition the dispositif's renewal, and treating an overdetermination as the truth concealed behind it leaves that truth available for reincorporation and for hardening into the only possible way of inhabiting the dispositif. This position reframes the question of agency that runs through the tradition. White's (1992a) gaps, Madigan's (2003) permanent resistance, and Guilfoyle's (2012) embodied refusal retain the form of the debate over agency and structure, with a subject acting upon conditions.

Overdetermination supplies part of the excess with which folds can work, and by itself it contains no resistance, because its readjustments can transform a configuration or reproduce it strategically. A recomposition can be read as resistance according to four criteria: first, how it modifies the possibilities of action of those who take part in it; second, which relations of truth and authority it reorganizes; third, which new subjections it introduces; and fourth, how far it keeps open the conditions under which it can itself be questioned. These criteria are needed because a recomposition also redistributes visibilities and relations of force, and it can present itself as an expansion of autonomy as it intensifies the conduct of conduct.

The stance Foucault (1995) calls reflected intractability, indocilité réfléchie in the French text, gives these criteria their orientation. It questions being governed in this way, in the name of these principles, and at this price, and it acknowledges that all life unfolds within forms of government (Author, 2023). On the conduction a dispositif exercises over a life, the person, as a force that folds upon itself, can establish how far that conduction is accepted. This determination presupposes no interiority, because the fold is the outside folded upon itself (Deleuze, 1987).

Reflected intractability operates as a critical orientation within the relations that constitute the subject, and through it recompositions are examined, provisionally sustained, and reconsidered.

From this stance, overdeterminations serve as mobile platforms: useful as long as they sustain a different relation to the dispositif's lines, open to reappropriation by new forms of government, and relinquishable when that relation exacts more than one is willing to pay. Resistance thus emerges within the relations that constitute the subject without becoming a necessary consequence of their heterogeneity.

Two Uses of the Concept

The four dimensions thus sustain two uses of the concept, one at the scale at which Foucault worked, that of cultures and periods, and one at the scale at which narrative therapy works, that of a life. The first is outlined here, because following it requires historical archives and genealogical procedures that exceed this article; the second is developed, because the article's question concerns a clinical tradition. In its sociohistorical use, the analysis eventalizes the requirement to narrate oneself and follows its lines of descent. The previous sections suggest four of them: first, the techniques of writing that White and Epston (1993) associated with a linear conception of time; second, the file that fixed individuals in time and made their lives legible to institutions; third, the practices that made saying who one is a condition of being governed, which Foucault traced to the Christian pastorate and which spread into medical, pedagogical, psychological, and workplace practices (Ramírez & Yáñez-Urbina, 2021); and fourth, the psychological knowledges that located the truth of a person in an interiority to be told.

Their convergence suggests, as a thesis for research that presupposes no originary narrative capacity, a tendency in which the requirement to say who one is passed from the confession of a soul to the documentation of a case and then to the narration of a life, each configuration answering urgencies of its own, such as making suffering professionally recognizable or organizing responsibilities. Sedimentation excludes identity, since a configuration can leave lines that another rearticulates, and the narrative forms reaching the clinic today may carry lines whose current articulation differs from the one the founding texts described.

In its clinical use, the four dimensions become moments of an analysis in which the person takes part. The first invites the person to position the elements that compose the dispositif of their narration, the statements through which they are told and tell themselves and what of their life becomes visible, where, and before whom, without presupposing the relation between them. The second evaluates the effects of those relations: which forces sustain what can be said and seen of that life, which conducts they incite or hinder, and what the person's own actions produce on other forces. White's (2016) statement of position map already asks people to evaluate the effects of a problem, and the evaluation here bears on the dispositif. The third follows the provenance and emergence of the dispositif in that life and eventalizes it, an opening the tradition knew in the subjunctive mode it took from J. Bruner (1998) and that here operates beyond the text, without any search for the origin of a problem or of a self. The fourth identifies the urgency the dispositif answered in that life, how it was configured around it, and toward where it conducts that life.

The person analyzes the dispositif from within it. What changes are the relations the person holds with themselves inside the dispositif, and with them the networks of relations it composes, and the analysis follows where those relations lead without fixing a destination in advance. This absence of teleology marks our distance from Guilfoyle's prediscursive body and from a preferred narrative held as the destination of change, a destination that cannot be determined beforehand and that a dispositif can fill strategically. A therapeutic session is accordingly one site where a dispositif is actualized in its singularity, and the clinical use recognizes historical lines in a life without reading that life as an instance of its epoch.

Conclusions

Every radicalization contracts a debt with what it radicalizes. The one proposed here retains, from the founding formulations, the decision to locate the problem outside the person and a practice of letters, archives, ceremonies, and leagues more generous than its theory; from the radicalizations, it retains the extension of critique toward institutions and the conversion of the question of who resists into a clinical question. It changes the object of interrogation, because what these developments used as explanation becomes what must be explained, and it situates that object at two scales, the history of a culture and the trajectory of a life, and develops the second. Its displacements can be followed through four questions: knowledge, the subject, the problem, and change.

In relation to knowledge, narration becomes part of what requires inquiry, since what varies, historically and from one life to another, is the way certain narrative properties are articulated, acquire authority, and are mobilized within the dispositifs of a period or of a particular life. The distinction between dominant and local knowledges then involves three kinds of conditions that a dispositif can articulate, none of which is reducible to the others: first, conditions of intelligibility, under which an experience can be formulated; second, conditions of veridiction, under which certain descriptions count as true or false, certain subjects are entitled to make them, and their descriptions acquire consequences (Foucault, 2002a (Foucault, , 2007)); and third, conditions of habitability, under which a subject position can be occupied.

In relation to the subject, the tradition and its radicalizations organize the relation between social constitution and agency in different ways: White (1992b) locates subjectivity in culturally available narratives; Madigan (1996) locates it in the dialogue of communities of discourse; and Guilfoyle (2012) adds a prediscursive bodily activity to explain refusal. The immanence of subject and dispositif leads to the premise that the subject is a fold of forces, composed together with discourses, visibilities, bodies, spaces, and techniques and taking part with them in constructing the social. Foucault (1979b) opened this possibility when he read humanism as an economy of discourses that offers sovereignty in exchange for subjection. The multistoried subject then names the multiplicity of articulations in which a particular life is composed, and the thin prediscursive subject becomes dispensable.

In relation to the problem, the tradition and its radicalizations begin from a narrative's dominance, understood as saturation (White & Epston, 1993), as rhetoric sustained by communities of discourse (Madigan, 1996), or as a position that fixes the person (Guilfoyle, 2015). Dominance itself requires explanation, and Foucault's notion of fiction offers a resource for it. In a 1977 interview, Foucault (1979a) held that fiction can work within truth, produce effects of truth, and take part in fabricating realities that do not yet exist (Author, 2023); fiction thus names a productive dimension of configurations. In the clinical use, a problem is a configuration in a particular life, the way a dispositif of the narrative composes elements, conducts that life, and makes one way of narrating it required, and it arises when a narrative hardens into the truth of that life. Guilfoyle's (2018) discursive famine names a distinct difficulty, the absence of a habitable narrative position.

Change can then draw on three operations that collaborate and remain distinct. The first, genealogy, interrogates the necessity attributed to a configuration. The second, fiction, makes it possible to examine how configurations produce truths and to compose realities that do not yet exist. The third, profanation, returns to other uses what a dispositif had separated from common use (Agamben, 2011). Because dispositifs also produce capacities and forms of existence that those who take part in them may value, profanation presupposes no practices awaiting liberation, and reflected intractability, which orients the three operations, examines each composition without deciding in advance what should be kept or abandoned.

These displacements return the argument to the trajectory from constructivism to constructionism that shaped the tradition. Constructivism located construction in the knower, and constructionism relocated it in social exchange, language, and historically situated discursive practices (Gergen, 1996). Some constructionist formulations made the social and language the ground of explanation, with the result that the problem, the self, or the story was explained by a social construction whose own composition received less examination. The four displacements reverse that relation, as in each of them the social and narration become what must be explained. Íñiguez-Rueda (2007) situated this reversal in social psychology, in the terrain where the reassessment of constructionism articulates its contributions with feminist epistemology, social studies of science and technology, and performativity.

We call this reorientation postconstructionism, the term Íñiguez-Rueda (2007) used for that terrain and that Lykke (2010) proposed in feminist studies as an umbrella for theories that transgress the dilemmas of constructionism and include its insights. Postconstructionism analyzes how the social is produced, and its defining move displaces the question of the social construction of a phenomenon toward the question of how that phenomenon takes part in constructing the social. The new radicalization performs the same displacement on narration. It analyzes how the narrative is produced, along with the narrative constructions this production makes possible, at the two scales of its uses. The sociohistorical use would ask how writing, the file, confession, or psychological knowledges took part in constructing the narrative form of a period. The clinical use moves from the question of the narrative construction of a problem, an identity, or a life toward the question of how a diagnosis, a document, a ceremony, a body, or an institution takes part in constructing the narrative of that life. Foucault's tools, taken in their full consequence, already lead to this position: the dispositif is a heterogeneous ensemble whose composition must be described in each case (Foucault, 1991), and power produces the knowledges, objects, and subjects upon which it is exercised (Foucault, 2002b). The new radicalization is therefore poststructuralist in its Foucauldian lineage and postconstructionist in its position, and the dispositifs of the narrative name the compositions in which the production of the narrative can be described, in a period or in a life.

For narrative therapy, the clinical use changes the theory that sustains the practice at three levels. First, the object of analysis becomes the configuration that, in a particular life, makes it possible and required to narrate oneself in certain ways, and the person takes part in its analysis. The argument is theoretical, and its limits follow from that status. Developing its clinical implications requires reformulating narrative practices and research designs able to observe compositions in particular lives as well as stories; the sociohistorical use requires historical studies of dispositifs of the narrative, left to future work. Three claims remain theses whose elaboration exceeds this article: first, that postconstructionist traditions develop possibilities implicit in Foucault's work; second, that the narrative forms mobilized in clinical practice have a historicity that only a genealogy of their lines of descent can establish; and third, that the critical orientation through which recompositions are examined takes shape within the relations it examines. A final task concerns our own vocabulary. Terms such as dispositif or veridiction can become a new criterion of legitimacy if therapy recognizes as valid only what can be said in their terms, and acknowledging the situated partiality of this knowledge requires subjecting them to the same reflected intractability.

Therapy then takes part in the redistribution of visibilities, and its own operations of making visible enter the same analysis.
