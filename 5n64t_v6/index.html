Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. Some differences come from the ground truth: it lacks p-values with a correction name (“p Bonf = .061”), and counts partial eta squared written with its subscript after it (“η 2 p = .05”), “p = .05”, and some table notes and figure legends (“† p < .10, *p < .05”), which this module leaves out, as non-significant p-values. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download files from additional data repositories reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Abstract

Introductory statistics courses and conventional methods in behavioral and cognitive science are typically restricted to Euclidean space without explicitly stating this, leaving many researchers unaware of how to handle data with circular or periodic properties (e.g., time of day or orientation on a screen). This tutorial provides an accessible yet rigorous introduction, designed to bridge the gap between statistical theory and practical application. After an overview of common applications, we discuss different ways of representing circular data mathematically and fundamental principles for working with them. We explain angular and vector representations and operations, including key considerations for implementing them in computer code. We then survey methods for visualizing circular data, including linear and polar plots and presentation of summary statistics. Next, we discuss circular measures of central tendency, dispersion, and shape: how they relate to their Euclidean counterparts and why they differ. Finally, we turn to more advanced topics of the kind needed to construct models of circular data. We introduce the most common circular distribution families, including circular analogs of the Gaussian distribution and their properties, as well as flexible distribution families that can describe skewed and heavy-tailed data. The tutorial takes a methodoriented approach, not tied to specific software, but highlighting factors relevant to experimental design, data analysis, and statistical modeling. A consistent theme is the need to rigorously account for the inherent periodicity of circular data. We also provide a consistent notation for circular statistics and parameters, addressing the lack of established conventions in the field.

Introduction

Mathematical training for biological and psychological scientists typically focuses on the analysis of data in Euclidean spaces, particularly measurements that can be described as points on the real number line (referred to as R 1 ; Figure 1A), or as coordinates in a plane (R² ) or in three-dimensional space (R 3 ). However, many biological and behavioral variables have a periodic or cyclical nature that requires different methods of analysis. Measurements in a circular space (denoted S 1 ) can be described by a single number (as on the real line), but moving far enough through the value range in either direction returns you to the point where you started (Figure 1B). Such a space is topologically equivalent to a closed loop or circle and fundamentally different from the real line (which stretches to infinity in either direction) or a bounded interval (a section of the real line with two ends).

A simple example of a circular space is the directions of a compass: If you describe directions in terms of angles (e.g., measured clockwise from North), then 0 • , 360 • and 720 • all refer to the same direction, and the distance between 0 • and 359 • is the same as the distance between 0 • and 1 • . The topic of circular statistics describes methods for analyzing random variables in circular spaces. It is a subset of directional statistics, which extends to variables that can be represented as points on a sphere (S 2 , which includes directions in threedimensional space), and analogs in higher dimensions. This tutorial focuses on methods for circular data, as they are widely applicable to behavioral research and also provide a foundation for learning about directional statistics more generally. For an introduction to higher-dimensional directional statistics, refer to Mardia and Jupp (2009) and Pewsey and García-Portugués (2021).

This tutorial targets researchers who are familiar with statistical measures and distributions in Euclidean space and want to understand the corresponding circular equivalents. It develops progressively, from the basic properties and operations needed to present and analyze circular data to more advanced concepts, including the foundations for formal generative models that output circular variables.

Conventions for mathematical notation in circular statistics are less standardized than in Euclidean statistics. In cases where there is a strong convention, we have followed it, but for cases where there is not, we have developed a notation (Table 1 and Appendix A) that aims to be clear and self-consistent, although it should not be regarded as canonical. The abbreviations used in this tutorial are defined in Appendix B. Our goal is to give a concise introduction to circular statistics that allows readers to get started with employing them in research involving circular data. For a thorough theoretical introduction to the topic including mathematical derivations and proofs, we recommend Mardia and Jupp (2009). Applications

Many areas of behavioral and cognitive research involve data that are either inherently circular or benefit from being conceptualized as circular in order to design experiments or analyze data. To illustrate its broad practical relevance, this section presents use cases of circular statistics, including for spatial, temporal, and neural data.

Spatial and Directional Data

Circular statistics can be a valuable tool for behavioral research using spatial data. Many aspects of spatial cognition and behavior naturally involve circular variables, while in other cases spatial features that are not inherently circular may be constrained to a circular subspace for purposes of experiment or analysis. In the following, we will look at examples of both.

As described in the introduction, compass direction is an inherently circular spatial feature, with animal navigation and migration as a classic use case. Factors supporting navigation include the relative direction of the sun (Giraldo et al., 2018; Lindecke et al., 2019), moonlight and stars (Foster et al., 2019; Freas et al., 2024), and, in some animals, the orientation of magnetic fields (Packmor et al., 2024; Panagopoulos et al., 2024). Circular statistics have been used to study movement directions in migration routes of birds (Bolón et al., 2025; Ghosh et al., 2024; Packmor et al., 2024), insects (Huang et al., 2024; Vijayan & Somanathan, 2024), and mammals (Thompson et al., 2024). Circular statistics have also been employed to study movement patterns on smaller scales, such as bee waggle dances. This complex form of non-verbal, symbolic communication allows forager bees to inform each other about food sources: the angle of the waggle run relative to gravity encodes the angle of the food source in relation to the sun. Circular statistics allow for an appropriate analysis of these complex dances (Deverell, 2003; Schürch & Couvillon, 2013; Vijayan & Somanathan, 2024).

Location in space is an example of a feature that, although not circular by nature, may be structured circularly to support experimental design. In human vision research, for instance, it is common to record responses of experimental participants to visual stimuli presented via a computer display. If stimuli were presented at random locations across the whole display, differences in their retinal eccentricity (distance of the image from the central foveal region of the retina) would influence how they are processed by the visual system (Olivers, 2025; Staugaard et al., 2016). Additionally, the stimuli's varying proximity to the borders of the screen could influence how they are perceived (Fortenbaugh et al., 2012; May & Zhaoping, 2009; Schulz-Hildebrandt, 2025). Confining stimulus locations to an invisible circle on the display, while instructing participants to fix their gaze on a point at its center (see Stimulus Presentation in Figure 2A; Eriksen and Colegate, 1971), is a common experimental technique that reduces variability by ensuring all stimuli are viewed at the same eccentricity and comparable distances from the screen edges. In this design, stimulus location can be incorporated into the behavioral analysis as a circular variable, equal to its angular position on the circle (Favila et al., 2022; Schneegans & Bays, 2017).

The psychophysical method of analog reproduction on a circle, developed by Prinzmetal et al. (1998), was subsequently extended by Wilken and Ma (2004) to study visual working memory -the cognitive resource that retains and manipulates information on the order of seconds. In contrast to previous methods that made a binary measurement of whether or not an object was remembered, their cued recall task (see Figure 2) allowed for assessing how precisely visual information can be recalled, inspiring new models of working memory capacity (Bays et al., 2009; Fougnie et al., 2012; Ma et al., 2014; van den Berg et al., 2012). A wide range of visual features that permit circular representations have been used in this task, including color hue (Wilken & Ma, 2004), motion direction (Ester et al., 2014), and even shape (Salmela et al., 2012).

Temporal Data

Human and animal behavior is often structured in recurring patterns, many of which follow approximately or exactly repeating cycles. Some of these cycles have a biological basis (e.g., hormonal rhythms), while others are shaped by environmental factors or, in humans, by cultural conventions (e.g., weekly schedules). Often, biological and cultural factors jointly determine how humans experience time and its influence on behavior (Patke et al., 2020).

A key feature for classifying and distinguishing periodic systems is the length of a single cycle. The most commonly studied chronobiological rhythm is the circadian rhythm (from Latin circa -approximately, dies -day), which has a period of about 24 hours. Numerous physiological and behavioral processes in humans and other animals vary systematically across the day. Among others, circadian rhythms influence sleep-wake cycles (Saper et al., 2005; Zisapel, 2018), hormone secretion (Nelson et al., 2025; Zisapel, 2018), body temperature (Refinetti, 2020), and activity patterns (Lautenschlager et al., 2024; Palmeirim et al., 2025). Neural Data

In the previous subsections, we discussed behavioral studies involving spatial and temporal features that are intrinsically circular, or constrained experimentally to be. In the following, we examine how circular statistics have been applied to investigate neural processes related to circular features or that have periodic properties themselves.

The discovery of the columnar organization of the primary visual cortex (V1) by Hubel and Wiesel (1962) was a major advance in sensory neuroscience, recognized by a Nobel Prize. Through electrophysiological recordings in V1 in cats and monkeys, they identified neurons that were selectively responsive to specific orientations of a visible bar or grating presented on a flat screen. Each cell had a "preferred" orientation that caused it to be most active (Figure 4A), and neurons with similar orientation preferences were grouped into vertical columns (Hubel & Wiesel, 1962 , 1968; Hubel et al., 1977). These orientation columns form orderly, repeating patterns across the cortical surface, often described as "pinwheels", because preferred orientations of neurons rotate continuously around a central point, covering the full range of orientations (Blasdel & Salama, 1986; Bonhoeffer & Grinvald, 1991; Bonhoeffer et al., 1995; Maldonado et al., 1997; Ts'o et al., 1990). While Hubel and Wiesel and early followup studies were primarily descriptive or employed Euclidean statistics, later work recognized the circular nature of orientation data and used circular statistical methods (Blasdel, 1992; Maldonado et al., 1997; Olson & Grossberg, 1998; Ringach et al., 2002). More recent visual-processing research has applied circular statistics to investigate how many neurons are required to represent continuous spatial variables reliably (Noorman et al., 2024), to explore the neural mechanisms underlying visual illusions (L.-Q. Zhang et al., 2025), and to model how different visual features of an object could be jointly represented in neural activity (Schneegans & Bays, 2017).

The neural control of movement is another area where analysis of circular variables has been fruitfully employed. Georgopoulos et al. (1983 Georgopoulos et al. ( , 1984) ) used circular statistics to study firing patterns of motor cortical neurons. They investigated the activity of neurons in the primary motor cortex (M1) of monkeys making reaching movements in the horizontal plane. They found neurons whose firing rate varied as an approximately cosine function of movement direction, each with a preferred direction that induced maximum activity. By treating each neuron's instantaneous firing rate as the length of a vector pointing in its preferred direction, and summing across all neurons, they obtained a "population" vector whose direction closely matched the actual reach direction on that trial. Analysis of oscillatory neural activity ("brainwaves") is a common use case for circular statistics. A striking example of periodic neural representation of linear space is phase precession (Figure 4B-4E), first observed in single-unit recordings referenced to the hippocampal theta local field potential (LFP) in rats navigating in an enclosure. In this phenomenon, certain neurons in the hippocampus (so-called place cells) fire at times progressively earlier in the theta wave (∼4-12 Hz) cycle as the rat moves through the cell's preferred location (its place field; O'Keefe & Recce, 1993; Skaggs et al., 1996). For analyzing phase-precession data, a combination of a circular representation of the periodic phase variable and a Euclidean representation of position in space has proven reliable (Kempter et al., 2012), and subsequently provided evidence for phase precession in human episodic memory (Doeller et al., 2010; Zheng et al., 2024).

Other Applications

Most examples we discussed above reflect physical measurements of time or space that have a circular or periodic structure. However, there are also more abstract perceptual, cognitive, and behavioral variables for which a circular representation is appropriate or has proven useful. For instance, circular statistics have been employed in social psychology to examine interpersonal interactions. This includes the synchronization of body movements to those of an interaction partner (Koul et al., 2023; Macpherson et al., 2024), interactions in romantic relationships (Sadikaj et al., 2015; Vowels et al., 2018), and interpersonal behavior of teachers (Cremers et al., 2021; van der Lans et al., 2020). The Interpersonal Circumplex (IPC) is an established model of interpersonal relationships that describes social behavior and traits along two orthogonal dimensions: agency and communion, referring to the degree of power or control and the degree of friendliness or affiliation an individual conveys in interaction with others, respectively. Together, these two dimensions form a circular space in which interpersonal behaviors are located as angles on a circle (Gurtman, 2009; Leary, 1957; Wiggins, 1991). Figure 5A displays the IPC with anchor words describing the type of behavior located in each part of the interpersonal circle. While agency and communion are often analyzed separately, Cremers et al. Working With Circular Data

In this section, we describe the fundamental characteristics of circular data to provide the foundation for subsequent sections. We explain the advantages of describing circular values as vectors of unit length in a two-dimensional plane and three standard forms of this vector representation: in polar coordinates, Cartesian coordinates, and on the complex plane. There is no universally correct choice for representing a given circular dataset; the optimal representation depends on the context and purpose of the analysis. For instance, angular measurements are often more intuitive for data visualization. If the methods described here are correctly applied, conclusions drawn from circular data will remain valid regardless of the chosen representation.

Identifying the Periodicity of Data

Finding the appropriate cycle length requires careful consideration, as an unsuitable choice might bias results. One cycle needs to contain every distinct point in the space of possible data values exactly once. Points that are separated by a whole number of cycles must be identical or at least equivalent for the purpose of the analysis. Selecting too narrow a period risks obscuring important aspects of the data by treating data points as equivalent when they are not: For instance, if temporal data are analyzed on a daily level, but they actually displays weekly rhythms. On the other hand, selecting too wide a period will mean the periodic nature of the data is not properly accounted for. Liu and West (2016) discuss a method for identifying an appropriate cycle length for circular analysis of temporal data.

Particular care is needed when working with data that in their raw form are angular or directional, because the appropriate period for circular analysis may not correspond to 360 • in the units of the data. Representations of Circular Data

Having identified the periodicity of our circular data, we can map each observation to its corresponding angle or phase, indicating where it falls within one full period or cycle. This requires us to select a data value that will correspond to an angle of 0, and a direction in the data space that will correspond to the positive direction of increasing angle. In some cases these choices are obvious (e.g., for temporal data with a period of one day, one would usually assign 12 o'clock midnight to the zero angle and increasing positive angular values to later times in the day), but in others they may be arbitrary, or the researcher may have to choose between several viable alternatives.

Angles are typically measured in degrees ( • ), in which case one cycle covers a range of 360 • , or radians (rad), in which case (for reasons explained below) one cycle covers 2𝜋 (≈ 6.28) rad. As discussed above, for data consisting of directions or orientations in space, the angular units of the circular space may differ from those of the raw data. The units in which angular values are reported are a matter of choice, subject to conventions of the field, but some mathematical operations are simplified by using radians. To convert an angle in degrees to radians, multiply by the ratio 𝜋 180 , and divide by the same ratio to convert the other way. Representing a circular value in polar coordinates simply involves supplementing the angular representation of data, 𝜃, with a distance or norm, 𝑟, resulting in the polar coordinate pair (𝑟, 𝜃). Technically, this is an embedding of the circular space (S 1 ) in the real plane (R² ). We can now appreciate the basis for radian units: an angle of 𝑋 rad corresponds to a distance 𝑋 measured on the circumference of the circle, with a full period corresponding to the full circumference of 2𝜋. Periodicity, Wrapping, and the Modulo Operation

As described above, the defining characteristic of circular data is its inherent periodicity, meaning that measurements that differ by a whole number of cycles are considered equivalent. This property fundamentally distinguishes circular from Euclidean space, where proximity and ordering follow conventional numerical relationships. For example, in Euclidean space, 1 is always closer to 3 than 10. In circular spaces, values that may appear numerically distant can in fact be close, for example, 1 • and 359 • differ by only 2 • because 1 • and 361 • are equivalent angles.

While conceptually any observation in a circular space can be represented by infinitely many angular values, differing by whole numbers of cycles, for practical purposes, it is usually constrained to one unique value within a specific periodic interval. According to the mathematical convention, angular values are constrained to a positive interval [0, 2𝜋) rad. Operations on Angles

When working with Euclidean variables, there exist many operations that map real numbers to other real numbers. By contrast, for circular variables, the need to respect periodic equivalence means there are very few operations that map angles to other angles: Essentially, these are limited to addition, subtraction, and multiplication by integer quantities (equivalent to repeated addition or subtraction). While it is possible to perform other operations on numerical representations of angles, the results will depend on the particular interval you have chosen for representation. For instance, 0 • and 360 • refer to the same angle, but if you attempt to divide them by 2 you will get 0 • and 180 • , which are different angles.

Having specified a range of angles in which to represent circular data, we will want to represent the results of angular addition and subtraction within the same interval. Practically, this just means applying a "wrapping" or modulo operation, as described above, after the usual arithmetic operation of addition or subtraction. A familiar example of modular arithmetic is the hour hand on a clock dial. If we subtract 2 hours from 1 o'clock, we get 11 o'clock, not -1 o'clock. Visualizations of Circular Data

Visualizing circular data requires approaches that respect its inherent periodicity and directional nature. A key decision to make is whether data are unwrapped onto a line and represented in a Euclidean coordinate system or represented in a two-dimensional polar coordinate system -where angle is mapped to direction (𝜃) and magnitude to radius (𝑟). In some cases, linear and polar visualizations convey similar information, whereas in others, one format provides a much clearer or more intuitive representation of the data. This section will outline common approaches and potential pitfalls in the visualization of circular data, concluding with a discussion of methods for visualizing summary statistics. We also provide practical guidance on producing these visualizations in commonly used programming languages.

Linear Representations of Circular Data

One common way to visualize circular data is by unwrapping it into a linear histogram along the circular dimension, using binned counts (absolute or relative frequencies) or continuous density estimates. This requires two key decisions: selecting the interval and -as for Euclidean data -defining the bins. Both the standard interval [0, 2𝜋) and the signed interval [-𝜋, 𝜋), are common choices for linear visualizations. The latter is especially useful if there is a meaningful zero point, such as for errors relative to a reference angle. The choice of the interval determines the wrap-around point, which critically affects how clearly the structure of the data is displayed. When data are unimodal and highly concentrated, a linear histogram can closely resemble a standard histogram of non-circular data and often provides a simple, interpretable summary. In such cases, the interval should be chosen so that the region of highest density lies in the center of the 𝑥-axis. For multimodal distributions, boundaries should, where possible, be positioned in regions of low density to ensure that all modes are clearly represented. For instance, in Figure 9A, the shape of the distribution around the dominant mode is clearly presented, while the secondary mode is positioned too close to the boundary, leading to density spilling over to the opposite side of the plot.

As for Euclidean data, the choice of bin size further shapes the appearance of the histogram: wider bins "smooth" over noise but may conceal fine structure in the data, whereas narrower bins reveal more detail but are susceptible to spurious fluctuations (Figure 9B). Common rules for selecting the size and number of bins in Euclidean space (e.g., Freedman & Diaconis, 1981; Scott, 1979 Scott, , 2010;; Sturges, 1926) are sometimes applied to circular data by treating angular values as lying on the real number line. However, heuristics based on dispersion measures may perform poorly in circular space and even yield pathological results, such as vanishing bin widths for near-uniform data. Currently, no universally accepted bin-selection rule exists for circular histograms. In practice, researchers often use a fixed number of bins. Crucially, bin widths and locations should be selected so that edges coincide with the boundaries of the presentation interval. Another consequence of mapping circular data to a linear axis is that subgroup visualization no longer requires any circular-specific treatment; the same methods used for Euclidean data apply directly, including grouped, overlaid, or stacked histograms (e.g., Figure 9I).

A related method is to plot a linear continuous density curve, which provides a smoothed representation of the distribution. For sufficiently dense data, continuous density curves intuitively summarize the overall structure of the distribution without being tied to arbitrary bin edges. However, as for bin size in histograms, the choice of smoothing parameter strongly influences the result: too much smoothing can mask structure, while too little smoothing exaggerates noise (Figure 9C). Additionally, a smoothing algorithm that takes into account the connectedness of the two ends of the interval must be chosen. As for the Euclidean case, circular continuous density plots are not appropriate for discrete or heavily rounded data or for sparse data, where density estimates can be unstable or misleading. For a short review of nonparametric methods for smooth density estimation of circular data, see Chaubey (2022).

The empirical cumulative distribution function (eCDF) summarizes the data without requiring a choice of bin size or smoothing parameter (Figure 9D). It shows the cumulative proportion of observations along the circular axis and can help highlight changes in density while avoiding some of the visual noise of histograms. Like other linear representations, however, eCDFs still require choosing an interval, and selecting the interval so that the wrap-around point lies opposite the region of highest concentration helps to center the distribution.

For producing linear plots of circular data, readers can use the same software and functions familiar from visualizing Euclidean data. For this, it is essential that the data are correctly wrapped (i.e., mapped to their representative values within the interval selected for visualization), and if applicable, that density estimation is conducted using methods accounting for the periodicity of the data. Once the circular data are appropriately preprocessed, linear plots can be generated by using the same software for (binning and) plotting as for Euclidean data. Polar Representations of Circular Data

We saw that linear visualizations such as histograms and eCDFs are intuitive and effective for concentrated, unimodal data. However, they may obscure structure in more dispersed or multimodal distributions, especially near wrap-around points. In such cases, polar visualizations often provide a clearer representation.

Polar histograms are widely used for polar representation of circular data (Figures 9E and 9F). The rose plot is a type of polar histogram that represents frequency data as radial wedges of equal angular width radiating from the center of a circle (Figure 9E). The height of each wedge indicates the number or proportion of observations in that angular bin. Unlike linear histograms, this approach preserves the continuity of the circular scale and avoids edge artifacts introduced by unwrapping. It also makes features such as the central tendency and directional biases easier to see. However, the representation is still influenced by the choice of bin size and the placement of the bin edges (for an illustration, see Figure 1 in Wells, 2000).

Analogous to its linear equivalent, a polar continuous density curve maps the smoothed density around the circle (Figures 9G and 9H). This representation conveys the same information as the linear density plot but emphasizes the cyclical structure of the data and does not require choosing a wraparound point. As before, the choice of smoothing parameter strongly influences the result, and continuous density plots are also unsuitable for sparse data and data rounded to a coarse resolution, where density estimates can become unstable. A key consideration in polar representations is how magnitude is mapped to radial distance. Because the area of a polar region (i.e., a wedge or the area under a smooth curve) grows with the square of its radius, mapping frequency or density directly to radial height causes area to scale quadratically (Figure 9G). This distorts the apparent magnitude because small differences in height translate into large differences in area. As a result, dense regions are spatially overrepresented, while sparse areas are understated and difficult to distinguish. The problem arises for both binned counts and smooth densities. To correct this, radial height can be mapped to the square root of frequency or density (Mardia & Jupp, 2009). This ensures that the area of each sector is linearly proportional to the actual quantity, making sector areas directly comparable across angles. However, this comes at the cost of interpretability of the radial axis: because radial distance now represents the square root of frequency or density, values can no longer be read directly from the radius. Figure 9H shows continuous density plots of the same data as Figure 9G, using an areaproportional representation. For a more extensive discussion on this topic, see Xu and Wang (2020).

A version of polar histograms that avoids a distorted representation of area is wrapped bar histograms, which use rectangular bars arranged around a reference circle instead of wedges (Figure 9F). The base of each bar is aligned with the reference circle's circumference, and the radius of the reference circle is set arbitrarily to ensure that the bars fit around its perimeter. This method effectively wraps a linear histogram around a circle, preserving the height of each bar relative to its linear counterpart. This approach avoids the distortions of apparent magnitude typical of wedge-based rose plots, because both height and area are proportional to frequency. However, the gaps between adjacent bars complicate overlaying smooth density curves: to remain consistent with bar heights, density must be mapped to height rather than area; as a result, area under the density curve can misrepresent magnitude, with a disproportionately larger area plotted around longer bars compared to shorter bars. For a more detailed discussion of polar histograms and their design considerations, see Sanderson and Peacock (2020).

Stacked polar histograms (Figure 9I) can be used to show subgroup compositions within each angular bin. Annular sectors corresponding to different groups are layered radially one on top of another. However, this design inherits the same distortion mentioned previously: subgroups farther from the center occupy disproportionately more area, even when counts are equal. This occurs because area increases with the square of the radial distance, so visually comparing sector sizes can give a misleading impression of the underlying proportions. Stacked linear histograms show subgroup magnitudes in a clearer way (Figure 9J), even though the stacked representation still makes it difficult to compare the same subgroup across bins or relative subgroup contributions within a bin. For these comparisons, alternatives such as grouped polar bars, unstacked linear histograms, or proportion-normalized plots provide more reliable summaries.

To produce polar plots, MATLAB provides the built-in function polarplot. In R, a linear plot generated with ggplot2 (Wickham, 2016) can be transformed into a polar representation by adding coord_polar() as a coordinate layer; alternatively, polar plots can be directly created using the polar.plot function from the package plotrix (Lemon, 2006). In Python, the visualization library matplotlib (Hunter, 2007) supports polar plotting via the polar projection in matplotlib.pyplot.subplot. For special considerations when creating polar plots of timeseries data, refer to Lee Rodgers et al. (2014).

Visualizations of Cylindrical Data

Data consisting of paired circular and linear variables is referred to as cylindrical. Examples include the pairing of movement direction and amplitude, and pairs of visual features like edge orientation and spatial frequency. One way of visualizing cylindrical data is a polar scatter plot, where each point is positioned according to its angle around the circle and its distance from the center (Figure 9K). For large datasets or continuous variables, scatter plots can become cluttered and difficult to interpret. In these cases, polar heatmaps offer a useful alternative (Figure 9L). Such plots summarize data density or intensity smoothly across both the circular and radial dimensions, making it easier to identify patterns in the data. Cylindrical data can also be represented using twodimensional Cartesian plots, where one axis corresponds to one period of the circular data displayed in linear form.

Polar scatter plots can be generated using MATLAB's built-in function polarscatter, the polar projection with scatter in matplotlib (Hunter, 2007) in Python, and geom_point() combined with coord_polar() in ggplot2 (Wickham, 2016) or radial.plot from plotrix (Lemon, 2006) in R. Polar heatmaps can be implemented by first converting values to Cartesian coordinates using the transformations defined in the https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/5n64t section and then applying standard two-dimensional binning and rendering functions familiar from generating heatmaps of Euclidean data.

Visualizing Summary Statistics

While histograms and density plots illustrate the overall distribution of data, visualizations of summary statistics convey key properties, such as the dominant direction in the data or how strongly clustered the observations are. Some of the concepts that we will introduce in this subsection (e.g., mean vector and circular median) are explained in greater detail in the https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/5n64t section. For now, the focus is on how these statistical summaries can be effectively represented in visual form, rather than on details of their calculation.

Rayleigh plots (Figure 10A and 10B) provide a simple and informative way to visualize both individual data points and summary statistics in a single plot. They are related to circular histograms but -in addition to individual observationsalso visually summarize central tendency and spread. Instead of grouping observations into bins, Rayleigh plots represent each data point as a mark positioned at its corresponding angle on the circumference (Figure 10A). While typically used for ungrouped data, Rayleigh plots can be adapted for grouped data. In such cases, or when multiple observations share the same angle, instead of being overlaid, points can be stacked radially to show frequency (Figure 10B). Stacking points makes individual observations easier to distinguish compared to the classic Rayleigh plot, where overlapping points can obscure one another. A distinctive feature of the Rayleigh plot is the mean vector, drawn as an arrow from the center of the circle. The angle of this arrow indicates the direction of central tendency, while its length reflects the spread of the data. Thus, it provides an intuitive summary of both the main direction and the consistency of observations around it. For a more detailed mathematical definition of the mean vector, see the https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/5n64t section.

For Euclidean data, a box plot typically displays lower quartile (Q1), median, upper quartile (Q3), with additional elements indicating the spread beyond the quartiles and potential outliers. Circular box plots (Figure 10C and 10D) aim to represent similar features but face unique challenges due to the periodic nature of angular data. A critical challenge is that, in circular space, the median and quartiles are not always uniquely defined, particularly in multimodal or uniform distributions (see the https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/5n64t section). If these order statistics are not uniquely defined, a circular box plot cannot be uniquely defined either. Despite this limitation, various implementations attempt to visualize the angular spread and central range using arcs or radial sectors to represent interquartile ranges, with additional markers for extreme values when identifiable. Like other visualizations of circular data, circular box plots can be displayed either on a circular (Figure 10C) or linear scale (Figure 10D). The circular format can be particularly useful for comparing multiple circular variables or groups, where box plots are positioned at increasing radii. However, comparing such box plots can be misleading: box plots plotted farther from the origin will be physically longer than those closer in, even if their angular spreads are identical. This can distort the perceived variability and exaggerate or diminish differences between groups. In the example shown in Figure 10C and 10D, the three groups appear more different in terms of spread when displayed on a circular plot compared to a linear one. To support interpretation, circular box plots can include radial grid lines and concentric circles, which serve as visual reference points for comparing angular spread and position across groups plotted at different radii.

The implementation of Rayleigh plots follows the same approach as described for polar scatter plots, with the radius of each point fixed to a constant value for ungrouped data or incremented radially for each observation sharing the same angle for grouped data. The mean vector can be added as an arrow using quiver in MATLAB, annotate with arrowprops in matplotlib in Python, and geom_segment() in ggplot2 in R. For a more detailed discussion of possible ways to implement circular box plots, refer to Abuzaid et al. (2012) and Buttarazzi et al. (2018).

Central Tendency

Circular Mean

A basic requirement of any statistic calculated from circular data is that it respects the equivalence of angles separated by a whole number of cycles, for example, that it is unchanged if we describe a data point as 360 • instead of 0 • . The usual definition of the arithmetic mean 1 𝑛 ∑︁ 𝑛 𝑖=1 𝑥 𝑖 does not fulfill this requirement. However, the problem of identifying a circular counterpart to the Euclidean mean turns out to be even more fundamental in nature. Consider a data sample consisting of the four cardinal directions (0 • , 90 • , 180 • , and 270 • ). Intuitively, no single direction can be identified as its average. It is clear that a different conception of mean value is required for circular data: the centroid, or center of mass, fulfills the requirements.

In Euclidean space, the arithmetic mean is equivalent to the centroid of the set of points 𝑥 𝑖 on the real number line. This idea can be illustrated using the analogy of a seesaw: Placing equally weighted balls at each location 𝑥 𝑖 on a beam, the mean corresponds to the center of mass, or the position of a fulcrum that balances the system. We can extend this concept to circular data by treating each angle 𝜃 𝑖 as a point on the unit circle (the embedded representation introduced in Section Polar Coordinates). In this case, the centroid lies inside the circle rather than on its circumference (purple dots in Figure 11). The analogy is to a disk with equally weighted balls placed along its edge at angles 𝜃 𝑖 . Placing a small plinth underneath the disk to keep it balanced would require positioning it closer to the center and not on the edge. The position of the plinth reflects the location of the center of mass.

The direction of the center of mass from the origin defines the circular mean -a generalization of the linear mean that respects the periodic and directional nature of circular data. It is the same as the direction of the resultant vector (the sum of the 𝑛 unit vectors corresponding to the data; see Section Vector Summation). The two-dimensional location of the centroid can be obtained by scaling the resultant vector by 1 𝑛 . The length of this mean resultant vector contains information about the spread of the data, as we will see in Section Measures of Dispersion. If the mean resultant vector has length 1, the circular mean is undefined, as this indicates complete directional cancellation, as in a uniform distribution.

Circular Mode

The circular mode of a sample is the most frequently occurring angle, taking into account the equivalence of angles separated by whole numbers of cycles. As in the Euclidean case, the circular mode is strictly only defined for discrete data, but the term may also be used in a looser sense to describe peaks in a histogram or smoothed density estimates of continuous data. Whereas the global mode is rarely used to estimate central tendency for continuous circular data, local mode estimates are relevant for several applications, including multimodality testing (Ameijeiras-Alonso et al., 2019; Bolón et al., 2025) and mode-based clustering approaches (Chang-Chien et al., 2012; Y. Zhang & Chen, 2021).

Measures of Dispersion

Circular Variance

As mentioned above and displayed in Figure 11, the mean resultant length R conveys information about the spread of a data sample, by quantifying how tightly the angles cluster around the mean direction. Specifically, R lies in the range0 ≤ R ≤ 1, with R = 1 indicating perfect alignment of all angles (i.e., maximum concentration) and R = 0 implying complete directional cancellation (i.e., maximum dispersion). The sample circular variance is defined as reflecting the inverse relationship between R and spread.

The circular variance is equal to the average cosine dissimilarity between the data points and their circular mean, in the same way that the Euclidean variance is the average squared deviation from the arithmetic mean.

Unlike many other circular statistics, the circular variance of clustered data is not approximately equal to the corresponding Euclidean measure. Instead, for data that are concentrated within a small arc of the circle, circular variance is approximately half the value of the Euclidean sample variance (red dashed line in Figure 12C). This is a consequence of the fact that for small 𝛿, 1cos(𝛿) ≈ 𝛿 2 2.

Circular Moments

To calculate circular mean, variance, and standard deviation, we used the mean resultant vector, which can also be characterized as the first circular moment of the data distribution. Circular moments, also called trigonometric moments, form an important class of summary statistics. They act as a "fingerprint" of the distribution, allowing complex patterns to be summarized by a few interpretable numbers.

In Euclidean statistics, the 𝑝-th moment of the sample distribution is the arithmetic mean of the 𝑝-th power of the data values, 1 𝑛 ∑︁ 𝑖 𝑥 𝑝 𝑖 . However, this definition does not transfer directly to circular data, because it ignores periodicity and directionality. To properly capture the geometry of the circle, powers of 𝜃 are replaced with powers of the complex exponential 𝑒 i𝜃 = cos 𝜃 +i sin 𝜃, which is periodic with period 2𝜋.

The 𝑝-th sample circular moment is therefore defined as

The first circular moment, ˆ︁ 𝑚 1 , is the mean resultant vector (Section Circular Mean), its modulus | ˆ︁ 𝑚 1 | is the mean resultant length R, and its argument or phase arg( ˆ︁ 𝑚 1 ) is the circular mean θ. More generally, the modulus and argument of each circular moment ˆ︁ 𝑚 𝑝 yield interesting statistics. The argument of the p-th circular moment, corresponds to the mean direction of the data when considered modulo 2 𝜋 𝑝 , that is, when directions separated by 2 𝜋 𝑝 are treated as equivalent.

Higher-order moment arguments generalize the circular mean to capture dominant symmetries in circular data. For instance, θ2 gives the dominant axis when opposite directions are treated as equivalent, whereas θ3 points to the orientation of a threefold (trimodal) arrangement.

The modulus or length of the 𝑝-th moment measures how strongly the data cluster when considered modulo 2 𝜋 𝑝 .

Across orders, a value of R𝑝 = 0 indicates complete uniformity around the circle. R𝑝 = 1 corresponds to perfect alignment of the data wrapped onto a circle of length 2 𝜋 𝑝 , which has different implications for different values of 𝑝. For 𝑝 = 1, this means all observations fall at the same angle, for 𝑝 = 2 they can be split between two opposite angles, and so forth.

While the argument and the modulus of the first circular moment describe central tendency and spread, higher-order circular moments allow us to characterize further aspects of a distribution's shape and symmetry. We turn to these measures in the following sections.

Outlier Classification and Exclusion

As in Euclidean statistics, an outlier in circular data is an observation that deviates markedly from others. In behavioral and cognitive research, such values may reflect measurement errors, misunderstandings of the task, lapses in attention, or meaningful extreme values. In circular statistics, detecting outliers requires special care because the ordering of the data is not unique and minimum and maximum values are not defined. Outliers should be excluded sparingly to avoid biasing the results. In this section, we briefly survey some methods for detecting outliers, from formal statistical tests to more algorithmic classification techniques.

One approach to detecting outliers in circular data is to use statistical goodness-of-fit tests that check whether the sample is drawn from an expected distribution. These tests can be appropriate if researchers have clear expectations about the data's distribution based on previous empirical findings or theoretical considerations. If the data deviate significantly from an expected distribution, this may indicate the presence of one or more outliers. Resources on suitable tests are provided in Section Beyond This Tutorial. While a significant result suggests the presence of outliers, statistical tests do not pinpoint which observations might be outliers.

In this section, we introduce probabilistic foundations and modeling approaches for random variables in a circular space. The distribution of a circular random variable can be modeled by a probability density function (PDF) in a similar way to a Euclidean variable. Convergence of Sample Statistics to Population Parameters

The trigonometric moments of an independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) sample, including the mean resultant vector, converge to their population counterparts as the sample size increases (see Section Central Limit Theorem). As a result, sample statistics that are continuous functions of the mean resultant vector (circular mean, variance, and standard deviation) and of higher-order trigonometric moments (circular skewness and kurtosis) inherit convergence via the continuous mapping theorem. The only case where convergence of these parameters is not assured is when the true parameter is not uniquely defined. For example, although we can calculate the circular mean of a sample drawn from the circular uniform distribution, the population mean resultant vector has length 0, so the mean direction is undefined and the sample mean direction has no population counterpart to converge to.

For parameters that are not moment-based, the question of convergence is more complicated. The circular median also converges when the true value is uniquely defined and the density around it is well-behaved, that is, continuous and sufficiently smooth (for a detailed discussion, see Purkayastha, 1995). Convergence results for local circular mode estimators are given in Y. Zhang and Chen (2021).

Circular Uniform Distribution

On the real line, a random variable can have a uniform distribution only with respect to a particular bounded interval, with PDF 𝑓 (𝑥) = 1 𝑏-𝑎 for 𝑥 in the interval [𝑎, 𝑏) and 𝑓 (𝑥) = 0 outside it (Figure 15 left). The normal or Gaussian distribution family is fundamental in Euclidean space, underpinning many statistical models due to its well-behaved mathematical properties, including closed-form expression, defined moments, and closure under translation, convolution, and scaling. For circular variables, no single family of distributions possesses all the desirable properties of the Gaussian, and some of its properties are not shared by any family of circular distributions. The closest circular equivalents to the Gaussian are the wrapped normal distribution and the von Mises distribution (sometimes referred to as the circular normal). Flexible Distribution Families

We next introduce two families of distributions that can serve as flexible models for circular data that are asymmetric or heavy-tailed. Both are four-parameter families of distributions varying in tailedness and skewness as well as location and scale. The wrapped stable family generalizes the wrapped normal distribution and retains the property of closure under convolution, meaning that sums of random variables belong to the same family. The sine-skewed Jones-Pewsey family generalizes the von Mises distribution and its PDF has an analytic expression based on trigonometric functions. Particular attention will be given to the wrapped Cauchy distribution, which uniquely belongs to both families.

Central Limit Theorem

The central limit theorem (CLT) states that, under quite general conditions, the sum or mean of a sample of Euclidean random variables converges to a normal (Gaussian) distribution as the number of observations in the sample grows, even if the individual observations do not come from a normal distribution. This fact is often called upon to justify the use of the normal distribution family as a default model for a wide range of behavioral and neural observations, and also underlies many parametric statistical tests. Analogous results to the CLT for circular variables are therefore of considerable interest; however, the topic is complex.

In circular spaces, the limiting distribution depends on precisely what operation is performed on the random variables. It should be plain that the angular sum of increasingly many independent circular random variables, regardless of their individual distributions, will in general grow in dispersion and ultimately converge to the circular uniform distribution. Of more interest is the limiting distribution of the mean resultant vector, the direction of which is the closest circular analog of the Euclidean mean. For i.i.d. circular variables, the mean resultant vector converges to a bivariate Gaussian centered on the mean resultant vector of the distribution they are sampled from, and with covariance that can be calculated from its first two circular moments (Jammalamadaka & SenGupta, 2001, Section 3.4). In particular, if that distribution is symmetrical, the bivariate Gaussian is uncorrelated, so the tangent of the resultant direction converges to a Gaussian distribution. If the distribution of resultant direction is narrow enough (which it will be for sufficiently large numbers of samples, as long as they are not uniformly distributed), then it will have approximately the same distribution as its tangent.

Based on this, we can deduce that the limiting distribution of the circular mean of symmetrically distributed circular random variables is approximately Gaussian in shape. Unfortunately, this is not sufficient to identify a specific circular distribution. In particular, both wrapped normal and von Mises PDFs converge to Gaussian as their width approaches 0, so we cannot identify either of these circular analogs of the Gaussian as the unique limiting distribution.

Choosing an Appropriate Circular Distribution

After being introduced to a range of established circular distribution families and their properties, the reader should now be able to choose suitable distributions when building statistical models of circular data. If the data come from a source with no directional bias, the circular uniform distribution should be used. If not, two criteria can be employed for selecting one of the distribution families discussed above: first, whether the data can be expected to have an approximately normal distribution; second, whether the purposes of analysis better suit the properties of wrapped distributions or those defined intrinsically on the circle. If a normal distribution can be assumed (based on inspection or because the conditions described in Section Central Limit Theorem are met), then the von Mises or the wrapped normal is appropriate; if not, and assuming the data are nonetheless approximately unimodal, the Jones-Pewsey or wrapped stable should be chosen for their ability to capture asymmetry and tailedness of data. Wrapped distribution families are more appropriate for data obtained by wrapping Euclidean random variables or when the analysis involves addition or subtraction of circular variables (because these families are closed under convolution). Distributions that are defined intrinsically on the circle are appropriate if the reader needs an analytically and computationally tractable model for statistical inference, such as MLE or Maximum A Posteriori (MAP) estimation, or if they want a closed-form PDF, entropy or Fisher information. If theoretical and practical considerations do not identify a single distribution family, we recommend selecting based on how well the different possible distributions fit the data, noting, however, that differences in fit between wrapped and intrinsically circular distributions are typically small.

Sampling Random Values From Circular Distributions

Sampling from circular distributions is essential for generating synthetic data and experimental stimuli and for use in simulations and model recovery. For wrapped distributions, the usual method is to draw samples from the corresponding distribution in Euclidean space and then wrap the generated random values onto the circle. Distributions that are defined intrinsically on the circle require different sampling methods. For the von Mises distribution, the rejection sampling algorithm introduced by Best and Fisher (1979) has become the standard. This method samples values from an auxiliary distribution on [0, 2𝜋) whose density is proportional to exp (𝜅 cos 𝜃) (a distribution closely resembling the von Mises), and then uses a rejection criterion to ensure that accepted samples exactly follow the von Mises distribution. This algorithm is implemented, for example, in the function rvm in the R package CircStats (Lund & Agostinelli, 2002) and in scipy.stats.vonmises.rvs in the Python library SciPy (Virtanen et al., 2020).

More generally, inverse transform sampling provides a universal framework for generating random variables from any continuous distribution with a known CDF: generate a random value 𝑈 from a uniform distribution in the interval [0, 1], and return the smallest number 𝑋 for which 𝐹 (𝑋) ≥ 𝑈, where 𝐹 is the CDF of the target distribution. This method can be used for sampling from circular distributions with the CDF defined over any specified interval of length 2𝜋.

This tutorial was intended to provide a practical foundation in circular statistics tailored to behavioral and cognitive research. This tutorial did not cover circular inferential statistics. A wide range of tests assess whether circular data deviate from expected distributions -for an accessible overview, see Landler et al. There are circular versions of many of the familiar tests for comparing samples. These include the Watson-Williams Test (a circular equivalent to the two-sample two-sided t-test; Watson and Williams, 1956), analyses of variance (ANOVA; Harrison et al., 1986), and multivariate analyses of variance (MANOVA; Landler et al., 2022). Landler et al. (2021) evaluate different statistical tests comparing two independent samples of circular data in terms of Type-I error rate and statistical power. Tasdan and Yeniay (2018) discuss Bonferroni corrections for circular data. For regression models of circular variables with circular predictors, there are methodological guidelines for frequentist (p-value) tests (Downs, 2002; Jammalamadaka & SenGupta, 2001, Chapter 8; Cremers et al., 2020), and Bayesian statistics (Jha & Bhuyan, 2021). For a review of methods for principal component analysis of circular and higher-dimensional directional data, we recommend Nodehi et al. (2026).

Clustering algorithms offer a way to identify subgroups or outlier candidates based on angular proximity. There are circular versions of many established clustering algorithms, including circular versions of k-means (Charalampidis, 2005), fuzzy c-means (Kesemen et al., 2016), and the machine-learning algorithm mean-shift (Y. Zhang & Chen, 2021). Machine learning methods have also been adopted to predict (Lang et al., 2020) and simulate (Wessel et al., 2026) circular variables.

Some specific cases of circular data require special considerations to avoid biases and distorted results. While this tutorial focused on continuous circular variables, treating noncontinuous data as continuous can substantially bias results (Mardia & Sriram, 2023), even with a fine-grained scale such as rounding to the nearest full degree (Landler et al., 2019). Mardia and Sriram (2023) introduce different families of circular discrete distributions, including the circular lattice distribution, which is defined over a finite set of equally spaced points on a circle (Mardia & Jupp, 2009, Chapter 3.5.2). For the classification and time-series modeling of ordinal circular data, refer to Gehlot and Laha (2025) and Jha and Biswas (2018), respectively. Adaptations for grouped or rounded data of tests that assess whether circular data deviate from expected distributions are discussed by Jammalamadaka et al. (2024) for uniformity testing and by Landler et al. (2020) for testing against expected distributions in general.

Incomplete or nonuniform sampling can also bias results; for instance, if a variable is collected only during a constrained time window of a 24-hour cycle (e.g., during daytime). Alsammani et al. (2024) discuss methods to mitigate these measurement biases. For the analysis of circular longitudinal and time-series data, some special considerations are warranted. Refer to Artes and Toloi (2009), Fisher and Lee (1994), Nuñez-Antonio and Gutiérrez-Peña (2014), and Zhan et al. (2025) for guidelines on time-series modeling, as well as to Abuzaid et al. (2014) for outlier-classification methods in circular time-series models.

Other forms of data closely related to circular data are also relevant for specific topics in behavioral and cognitive research but go beyond the scope of this tutorial. Cylindrical data comprise a circular component and a linear component. Cremers and Klugkist (2018) provide an introduction to cylindrical data in behavioral research. For an introduction to circular-linear correlations, refer to Jammalamadaka and SenGupta (2001, Chapter 8) and Mardia and Jupp (2009, Chapter 3.5.2). For regression models of circular variables on linear predictors, there are methodological guidelines for frequentist statistics (Fisher & Lee, 1992; Jammalamadaka & SenGupta, 2001, Chapter 8; Cremers et al., 2020), as well as Bayesian statistics (Cremers et al., 2018; Mulder & Klugkist, 2017). For perspectives on directional data analysis that include spherical and higher-dimensional data, refer to Fisher et al. (1993), Mardia and Jupp (2009), and Pewsey and García-Portugués (2021).
