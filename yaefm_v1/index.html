How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Statement of Contribution

What is already known on this subject?

• Perceived institutional corruption and institutional distrust are positively associated with conspiracy beliefs.

• Conspiracy beliefs tend to be more prevalent in countries characterized by higher corruption and weaker institutional integrity.

• Whether the association of individual-level corruption perceptions with conspiracy beliefs depends on national institutional context remains largely unexplored.

What does this study add?

• Corruption perceptions predict conspiracy beliefs more strongly in low-corruption contexts.

• Institutional distrust is more strongly linked to conspiracy beliefs in low-corruption countries.

• Conspiracy beliefs reflect an interplay between individual perceptions and institutional context.

Corruption Perceptions Predict Conspiracy Beliefs More Strongly in Low-Corruption Contexts

Conspiracy beliefs-broadly defined as the conviction that powerful groups secretly coordinate harmful actions against the public-are reliably associated with perceptions of institutional corruption (Alper, 2023; Alper & Imhoff, 2023; Alper, Yelbuz et al., 2024; Cordonier & Cafiero, 2024), yet the predictive value of perceived corruption or institutional distrust may depend on the institutional context in which that perception occurs. We propose that perceptions of corruption or institutional distrust are more predictive of conspiracy thinking in relatively clean societies than in highly corrupt ones. In environments where corruption is widespread, perceiving corruption may reflect common experiences with institutional dysfunction and distrust to institutions and therefore may carry less information about whether individuals hold a conspiratorial worldview. In contrast, in societies where institutions are widely perceived as clean and trustworthy, believing that institutions are broadly corrupt and cannot be trusted may be more closely linked to a more generalized suspicion toward elites and social systems. From this perspective, the relationship between perceived corruption and conspiracy beliefs should depend on the broader institutional environment: perceptions of institutional corruption should predict conspiracy thinking more strongly in low-corruption contexts than in high-corruption contexts. The present research tests this context-sensitive account across two studies using both cross-national comparisons and multilevel analyses of large-scale survey data. Study 1 investigates this as an initial first test through perceived institutional corruption comparing the UK and Türkiye, while Study 2 provides a partial replication extending the investigation to the predictive role of institutional distrust (as a proxy for institutional corruption) using ESS data, acknowledging the potential differences and implications between institutional distrust and perceived corruption.

Corruption broadly refers to the misuse of public authority for private benefit, including practices such as bribery, favoritism, clientelism, and the manipulation of public institutions for personal or political gain (Rose-Ackerman & Palifka, 2016; Rothstein & Varraich, 2017). Research on corruption often distinguishes between objective corruption, typically captured by expert-based indices of institutional integrity, and perceived corruption, which reflects citizens' subjective beliefs about the prevalence of corrupt practices among societal actors (Gutmann et al., 2020; Olken, 2009). Although these constructs are related, they capture different aspects of institutional life: objective corruption reflects structural conditions within institutions, whereas perceived corruption reflects individuals' interpretations and evaluations of those conditions. In psychological research, perceived corruption is particularly relevant because it can shape how individuals interpret political events, evaluate institutions, and form broader beliefs about the functioning of society.

Institutional trust is a closely related, yet conceptually distinct, construct: citizens who perceive public institutions as corrupt tend to report lower levels of trust in those institutions.

Accordingly, the two constructs are frequently examined together in comparative research (e.g., Anderson & Tverdova, 2003). The constructs are nonetheless distinct. Low institutional trust may also arise from perceived incompetence, dissatisfaction with governmental performance, ideological disagreement with those currently in office, low political efficacy, or a broader sense of institutional alienation, none of which necessarily involves the belief that institutions are corrupt. We return to this distinction in Study 2, where institutional trust items serve as the available indicator of citizens' evaluations of institutional integrity.

A growing literature demonstrates that perceptions of corruption are associated with conspiracy beliefs (Alper, 2023; Alper & Imhoff, 2023; Hornsey & Pearson, 2022; Mari et al., 2022; van Prooijen et al., 2022). Conspiracy beliefs involve the conviction that powerful actors secretly coordinate harmful actions against the public (Douglas et al., 2017). One explanation for the association between corruption perceptions and conspiracy thinking is that perceiving corruption undermines institutional legitimacy and increases suspicion toward authorities and elites. When individuals believe that institutions are dishonest, cannot be trusted or self-serving, they may become more inclined to interpret political and social events as products of hidden coordination or manipulation. From this perspective, perceptions of corruption and institutional distrust may render institutional actors plausible candidates for hidden wrongdoing, thereby fostering conspiratorial explanations of social outcomes.

At the same time, conspiracy beliefs may also be considered as understandable and adaptive under certain contextual conditions. Scholars have argued that suspicion toward powerful actors can be a useful psychological strategy in environments where exploitation or deception is likely (van Prooijen & van Vugt, 2018). From this perspective, conspiratorial thinking may also reflect heightened vigilance toward potential threats posed by powerful groups. Supporting this idea, cross-national studies have found that conspiracy beliefs tend to be more prevalent in societies characterized by higher levels of corruption or weaker institutional integrity (Alper, 2023; Cordonier & Cafiero, 2024). These findings suggest that environments marked by institutional dysfunction may foster generalized distrust and suspicion toward authorities.

However, an important question remains largely unexplored: Does the predictive value of perceived corruption depend on the broader institutional environment in which those perceptions occur? In other words, the same belief-"institutions in my country are corrupt"-may not be linked to the same implications and explanations in societies where corruption is rare as it does in societies where corruption is widespread.

In highly corrupt environments, perceiving corruption and distrust in institutions may reflect more understandable common experiences with institutional dysfunction and therefore may be more likely to be shared by many individuals regardless of their broader worldview. In contrast, in relatively clean institutional environments, perceiving widespread corruption and distrust in institutions may represent a stronger deviation from prevailing institutional realities and therefore may be linked to a more generalized tendency toward suspicion and conspiratorial interpretation.

One way to illustrate this difference is through the idea of signal diagnosticity (Batailler et al., 2022; Green & Swets, 1966; Tagand et al., 2026). Signals acquire meaning partly through their base rates within the environments in which they occur. When a signal is common and widely expected, it provides relatively little information about individuals' underlying dispositions or interpretive frameworks. When a signal deviates from prevailing conditions, however, it may reveal more about how individuals interpret the social world (Cone & Ferguson, 2015; Funder, 1995; Skowronski & Carlston, 1987). Applied to corruption perceptions, this logic suggests that the predictive value of perceiving corruption or dishonesty may vary across institutional contexts. In environments where corruption is widespread, perceiving corruption and not trusting in institutions may be more understandable and likely given the likely observations about institutional life. In environments where corruption is relatively rare, perceiving corruption and distrust in institutions may instead be linked to higher tendency to infer hidden coordination or institutional misconduct, processes closely related to conspiratorial thinking. We treat signal diagnosticity as a theoretically plausible interpretation of the moderation we test, not as a mechanism the present designs can adjudicate.

This perspective suggests that the relationship between perceived corruption (and institutional distrust) and conspiracy beliefs should depend on institutional context. In highly corrupt societies, perceiving corruption or trusting less in institutions may be relatively common and therefore may be less informative about whether individuals hold conspiratorial worldviews. In contrast, in relatively clean societies, perceiving widespread corruption may signal a broader distrust of institutions and elites, possibly making such perceptions more strongly associated with conspiracy beliefs. In this sense, the same perception-"my country is corrupt"-may reflect more understandable vigilance towards institutions and powerful actors in highly corrupt environments, but less so in relatively clean ones.

The present research advances an interactionist account of conspiracy beliefs by integrating individual perceptions with institutional context. Rather than treating corruption perceptions (or institutional distrust) as a universal indicator of conspiratorial thinking, we argue that their predictive value depends on how they relate to broader institutional conditions. We test this hypothesis across two studies.

In Study 1, as an illustrative first investigation, we analyze data from Türkiye and the UK, two countries that differ substantially in their levels of institutional corruption according to widely used cross-national indices (Transparency International, 2023). This design allows us to examine whether the association between perceived institutional corruption and conspiracy beliefs differs between a relatively high-corruption context and a relatively lowcorruption one. The two countries were not selected from a larger set on theoretical grounds; both samples come from longitudinal studies that were already underway. The pairing is nonetheless informative, because the two countries sit far apart on the most widely used indicators of corruption. In the 2023 Corruption Perceptions Index, the UK scored 71 out of 100 and ranked 20th of 180 countries, whereas Türkiye scored 34 and ranked 115th (Transparency International, 2023).

The ordering is the same on the World Bank's Control of Corruption indicator, on which the UK is among the highest-scoring countries and Türkiye falls below the global median (Kaufmann & Kraay, 2024). The contrast therefore allows an initial test of whether perceived corruption is more strongly associated with conspiracy beliefs where such perceptions are less congruent with prevailing institutional conditions. However, we also acknowledge that a two-country design cannot tease apart corruption from all other national and sample differences; therefore, Study 1 should be read as an initial, illustrative investigation of the proposed relationships. Study 2 extends the investigation using data from 34,356 participants across 20 European countries. Using multilevel modeling, we test whether the relationship between institutional distrust and conspiracy beliefs systematically varies as a function of country-level corruption while accounting for potential cross-national confounds. Therefore, Study 2 is an extension of the first test and partial replication of Study 1, as it uses institutional distrust as a proxy in 20 different countries. Because institutional distrust is itself an established correlate of conspiracy beliefs (Mari et al., 2022), we note at the outset that our focal test is not the main effect of distrust but its interaction with country-level corruption. Across both studies, our goal is to demonstrate that conspiracy beliefs cannot be fully understood by examining either individual perceptions or structural conditions in isolation. Instead, the predictive value of perceived corruption (Study 1) and institutional distrust (Study 2) depends on the institutional environments in which those perceptions arise. By situating individual beliefs within their broader institutional context, the present research provides a more context-sensitive account of when and how perceptions of corruption are associated with conspiratorial thinking. Study 1 Method Data, the analyses scripts, and the materials for Study 1 are openly available at https://osf.io/bx8hg/overview?view_only=84c46e89135846bda29039a42579731b.

Participants

Samples in Study 1 are from the initial waves of ongoing longitudinal studies. As the two samples can systematically differ from each other in different characteristics that we did not measure as well, the comparisons are interpreted as an initial test. No respondents were excluded from either sample, and there were no missing data on any of the measures analyzed here. All analyses reported in the main text and in the Supplementary Material (SM.3 and SM.4) therefore use the full sample of 3,401 respondents (2,442 Turkish and 959 British); the degrees of freedom reported for each model correspond to this sample. All participants signed an online informed consent form before participating.

Turkish sample.

The sample consisted of data of an online survey on a Turkish convenience sample of 2,442 participants (908 male, 1,526 female, 8 other; Mage = 31.50, SD = 9.73). Data were collected by a research company, and participants were reached via social media. To incentivize participation, we announced to the participants that randomly selected 10 participants would be given gift cards worth 150 Turkish Liras. Assuming a statistical power of .90 and a two-tailed alpha of .05, samples were sensitive to detect correlations as small as .07.

British sample.

A sample of 959 participants (476 male, 475 female, 8 other; Mage = 42.98, SD = 14.03). We recruited the participants from Prolific (http://www.prolific.co/) and paid £1 for each participant. Assuming a statistical power of .90 and a two-tailed alpha of .05, samples were sensitive to detect correlations as small as .10.

Materials Conspiracy beliefs.

We used the 15-item (e.g., "A small, secret group of people is responsible for making all major world decisions, such as going to war.") the Generic Conspiracy Beliefs Scale (Brotherton et al., 2013). Its Turkish translation was previously conducted by Alper et al. (2021). The scale uses a 5-point response format (1 = definitely not true,5 = definitely true). A sum score was calculated. Cronbach's alpha was .910 for the Turkish sample and .942 for the British sample.

Perceived corruption.

Participants were asked "Among the following groups of people, how many do you believe are involved in corruption? Tell me for each group if you believe it is none of them, few of them, most of them or all of them?". On a 4-point scale (1 = none of them,4 = all of them), participants evaluated the extent of corruption among state authorities, business executives, local authorities, civil service providers (police, judiciary, civil servants, doctors, teachers), and journalists and media. The measure was retrieved from the World Values Survey (Wave 7; Haerpfer et al., 2020). A sum score was calculated.

Cronbach's alpha was .827 for the Turkish sample and .793 for the British sample. Thus, it could be interpreted that Türkiye is significantly more corrupt than the UK.

Control measures.

Although these variables were not central to our research question, because they were in the dataset of an ongoing longitudinal study, we used them in exploratory analyses to test whether the association between perceived corruption and conspiracy beliefs remained significant after controlling them. Each of these variables has been linked to conspiracy beliefs in prior research and could therefore confound the association of interest. We do not, however, have a causal model that licenses treating all of them as confounders: some are conceptually close to perceived corruption and may act as mediators rather than confounders (generalized social trust is the clearest case), and none of them was measured with this adjustment in mind. We therefore treat the controlled models as exploratory robustness checks rather than as estimates of an adjusted causal effect. They included sex (male, female, other), education level (last school completed), perceived socioeconomic status (perceived standing on a 10-step ladder representing the society), ideology (ranging from far left to far right), religiosity (ranging from not religious at all to very religious), right-wing authoritarianism (Bizumic & Duckitt, 2018), social dominance orientation (Pratto et al., 2013), cognitive reflection (Sirota & Juanchich, 2018), science literacy (McPhetres et al., 2019), the Big Five (Atak et al., 2013; Gosling et al., 2003), the Dark Triad (Jonason & Webster, 2010; Özsoy et al., 2017), and generalized social trust (Haerpfer et al., 2020).

Analysis Procedure

We first checked measurement invariance, specifically configural and metric invariance, to test if our measures of perceived corruption and conspiracy beliefs have the same factor structure and factor loadings across two samples. Following Cheung and Rensvold (2002), invariance was evaluated by comparing the fit of the nested configural and metric models (ΔCFI, ΔRMSEA, ΔSRMR) rather than by inspecting the fit of the metric model alone; the rationale for testing metric but not scalar invariance is given in the Supplementary Material (section "Approach to Measurement Invariance"). Then, we carried out two types of main analyses. First, we report the bivariate correlations between perceived corruption and conspiracy belief. Second, we compared two samples, Türkiye and the UK, in terms of how strongly perceived corruption is related to conspiracy beliefs. We used dummycoded sample (Türkiye vs. the UK) as a moderator of this association.

Discussion

Results of this initial test of our account showed that perceived corruption was a significant associate of conspiracy beliefs, even after adjusting for control measures (see Tables SM3.3 and SM3.4), and this association was relatively stronger in the UK, compared to Türkiye. This is reasonable as higher perceived corruption could be more likely to relate to a more generalized tendency toward suspicion in a country with relatively low corruption, such as the UK.

However, comparison of two countries, Türkiye and the UK, is insufficient to draw broad conclusions regarding the moderating effect of country-level corruption. Another potential concern is that the moderation effect observed in Study 1 could be a statistical artifact of a "ceiling effect": If there is little variance in perceived corruption in highly corrupt countries, this could explain why it does not account for variance in conspiracy beliefs. A further concern is that corruption is not the only difference between Türkiye and the UK; countries that differ in corruption are also likely to differ in other country-level factors-such as wealth, inequality, and human development-that might actually drive conspiracy beliefs (Hornsey & Pearson, 2022).

In Study 2, we analyze data from 34,356 participants across 20 countries using European Social Survey (ESS; ESS Round 10: European Social Survey, 2023) data to test whether this moderation extends in larger, more representative samples by using institutional distrust as a proxy for perceived corruption. We acknowledge that perceived corruption and institutional distrust are conceptually distinct constructs. We therefore treat Study 2 as a conceptual extension and partial replication of Study 1, rather than as a direct replication. We also show that the relationship remains consistent after controlling for other potentially confounding cross-country factors, such as wealth, inequality, and human development.

Study 2 Method

The Study 2 dataset (ESS Round 10) is publicly available at https://doi.org/10.21338/ess10e03_2. The analysis scripts for Study 2 are available in the OSF repository.

Participants

Data were retrieved from the European Social Survey (ESS Round 10: European Social Survey, 2023). France and Montenegro are present in the ESS Round 10 dataset but data on all of the variables used here were not available for them, so they are excluded from all analyses. The resulting dataset comprised 34,356 participants from 20 European countries (see Table 2). Because a small proportion of respondents did not answer one or more of the conspiracy belief items, the analytic sample varied slightly across models. The country-level sample sizes in Table 2 are the totals before this listwise exclusion. The multilevel models reported below were estimated on the 33,476 respondents with complete data on the conspiracy-belief and trust items (see the notes to Tables SM5.1-SM5.4).

Materials Conspiracy beliefs.

Three items in the ESS dataset were considered as conspiracy beliefs: "Small secret group of people responsible for making all major decisions in world politics" (Secret Groups Conspiracy); "Groups of scientists manipulate, fabricate, or suppress evidence in order to deceive the public" (Scientists' Manipulation Conspiracy); and "COVID-19 is result of deliberate and concealed efforts of some government or organization" (COVID-19 Conspiracy). Participants used a 5-point scale (1 = agree strongly,5 = disagree strongly) but their ratings were reversed so that higher scores indicate higher belief.

A mean score of these three items was calculated (α = .794). Note. Values are presented as mean (standard deviation), except for country-level corruption. Countrylevel corruption scores were multiplied by -1, so that higher scores indicate higher corruption.

Institutional distrust. Direct measures of perceived corruption were not available in the ESS dataset. Instead, we relied on institutional trust items as indicators of citizens' evaluations of institutional integrity. A substantial literature shows that perceptions of corruption are closely tied to institutional trust, as corruption erodes confidence in public institutions and leads citizens to view them as self-serving or dishonest (Anderson & Tverdova, 2003; Mishler & Rose, 2001; Rothstein & Eek, 2009; Uslaner, 2002). From this perspective, institutional trust reflects citizens' broader assessments of whether institutions operate fairly and without corruption. Consequently, low institutional trust can be interpreted as indicating perceptions of institutional misconduct or corruption (Chang & Chu, 2006; Morris & Klesner, 2010; see also Ciziceno & Travaglino, 2019). Although institutional trust and perceived corruption are conceptually distinct constructs, both capture citizens' evaluations of institutional integrity and are frequently used in comparative research to assess perceptions of institutional functioning.

At the same time, institutional trust is not a pure indicator of perceived corruption.

Low trust in parliament, the legal system, the police, politicians, and political parties may also reflect perceived incompetence, dissatisfaction with governmental performance, ideological disagreement with those currently in office, low political efficacy, political polarization, or a broader sense of institutional alienation. Because institutional trust is itself a documented correlate of conspiracy beliefs (Mari et al., 2022), the individual-level association reported below should not be read as a direct estimate of the association between perceived corruption and conspiracy beliefs. Our focal test, however, concerns the interaction rather than the main effect. Whatever conceptual overlap exists between institutional trust and conspiracy beliefs is a property of the two constructs and does not itself vary across countries, and so cannot on its own explain why the strength of their association depends systematically on country-level corruption. We accordingly present Study 2 as a conceptual extension and partial replication of Study 1 using a related proxy, rather than as a direct replication, and we return to the limits of this operationalization in the General Discussion.

There were five items on trust in national institutions: trust in (1) country's parliament, (2) legal system, (3) the police, (4) politicians, and (5) political parties.

Responses ranged from 0 (no trust at all) to 10 (complete trust). Items were reverse-coded so that higher scores reflected higher institutional distrust, making the measure conceptually related to, though not equivalent to, the perceived corruption measure used in Study 1. The scale showed high internal consistency (α = .904).

Country-level corruption.

Country-level corruption scores were retrieved from Transparency International's (2023) Corruption Perceptions Index (CPI). Although the CPI is formally based on expert perceptions rather than direct behavioral indicators, it can be considered a relatively objective benchmark compared to lay respondents' perceptions, as it aggregates systematic assessments from multiple independent sources. Thus, while not "objective" in a literal sense, the CPI provides a widely used and reasonably standardized approximation of institutional corruption across nations. For comparability with the individual-level measure, scores were multiplied by -1 so that higher values represent higher corruption.

To ensure that our findings do not depend on a single corruption index, we conducted additional analyses using alternative country-level indicators of institutional integrity (e.g., the World Bank's Control of Corruption indicator; Kaufmann & Kraay, 2024).

Analysis Procedure

We first conducted a multi-group confirmatory factor analysis and checked measurement invariance, specifically configural and metric invariance, to test if our measure of institutional distrust has the same factorial structure and factor loadings across samples.

Invariance was evaluated as in Study 1, by the change in fit between the configural and metric models. Responses to the three conspiracy belief items were averaged into a single conspiracy beliefs score. A linear mixed model analysis was then conducted with this composite as the dependent variable. Institutional distrust, country-level corruption and their interaction were entered as predictors. Data were clustered based on country and a random intercept was defined so that intercepts were free to vary between clusters (countries).

We conducted additional checks including wealth (GDP per capita), income inequality (Gini Index), and human development (Human Development Index) as covariates. Including these country-level factors did not alter the key interaction between institutional distrust and country-level corruption. We also estimated an alternative model with a random slope for institutional distrust in addition to random intercepts for each country, which yielded the same pattern of results, although with a less precisely estimated interaction (see Results). The results should be interpreted with caution because the number of clusters (countries) is still limited for multilevel modelling, especially given that the covariates could create collinearity with country-level corruption.

Results

For the institutional distrust measure, we fitted a single-factor model to the five trust items across the 20 countries retained for analysis. Residual covariances between distrust in the legal system and the police, and between distrust in politicians and political parties, were freed in all groups. These two pairs were flagged by modification indices and are also substantively expected, as each pair refers to institutions operating within a single domainlaw enforcement in the first case, electoral politics in the second. The configural model, χ²(60) = 237.338, p < .001, CFI = .998, TLI = .994, RMSEA = .042, SRMR = .006, and the metric model, in which loadings were constrained to equality across countries, χ²(136) = 1064.802, p < .001, CFI = .991, TLI = .987, RMSEA = .065, SRMR = .038, both fit acceptably. The change in fit between the two models was mixed. ΔCFI = -.007 fell within the .010 criterion proposed by Cheung and Rensvold (2002), whereas ΔRMSEA = .023 and ΔSRMR = .032 exceeded the supplementary criteria of .015 and .030 proposed by Chen (2007). Two considerations bear on how this pattern should be read. First, ΔCFI is the index that Cheung and Rensvold (2002) found to be least affected by sample size and model complexity, and Chen (2007) treats ΔRMSEA and ΔSRMR as supplements to it; on that reading, the loadings can be regarded as approximately invariant. Second, the configural model fits almost perfectly (SRMR = .006), so even a small absolute change in the residuals crosses the ΔSRMR threshold, and the metric model itself remains well within conventional standards of absolute fit (CFI = .991, RMSEA = .065, SRMR = .038). On a stricter reading, in which any index exceeding its cutoff counts against invariance, full metric invariance across all 20 countries is not established. We did not test partial metric invariance, and the composite score used below weights the five items equally in every country. We therefore treat the cross-country comparability of the distrust measure as approximate rather than exact, and we return to this point in the Limitations section.

Testing the Alternative Explanation of a Ceiling Effect

In Study 1, we found that the association between perceived corruption and conspiracy beliefs is weaker in a relatively more corrupt country (Türkiye) than in the less corrupt one (the UK). In Study 2, we found that the association between institutional distrust and conspiracy beliefs is also weaker in highly corrupt countries. One alternative explanation for this finding is a potential ceiling effect: It may be that in highly corrupt countries, nearly everyone might have similarly high levels of distrust in institutions, which would reduce variability among participants. If this were the case, the lack of variation in distrust in institutions would make it less likely to correlate with differences in conspiracy beliefs. To test this explanation, we calculated country-level standard deviations of distrust in institutions.

We found that they correlate positively, not negatively, with country-level corruption, r(18) = .635, p = .003. Thus, variation in institutional distrust is actually higher in countries with greater corruption. However, this does not rule out other distributional explanationsnonlinearity, skewness, or clustering of responses in particular regions of the scale -and we therefore read it as evidence against a simple ceiling effect rather than against distributional accounts more generally.

Testing the Alternative Country-Level Factors

Another possible explanation for the findings is that countries with low and high levels of corruption may differ not only in corruption but also in other country-level factors.

Covariation among such factors has previously been identified as a major challenge in identifying national-level predictors of conspiracy beliefs (Hornsey & Pearson, 2022). In our case, highly corrupt countries are also likely to be poorer, less equal, and less developed, for example. To account for some of these potential covariates, we conducted exploratory analyses including GDP (as a measure of wealth; World Bank, 2025a), the Gini Index (as a measure of income inequality; World Bank, 2025b), and the Human Development Index (a composite measure of life expectancy, education, and income; UNDP, 2025). Including these variables in the model did not change the results (Tables SM5.1 and SM5.2). In a further model that allowed the slope of institutional distrust to vary across countries, the interaction remained significant, but, as expected with 20 clusters, it was estimated with considerably less precision than in the random-intercept model, b = -.002, SE = .001, 95% CI [-.003, -.001], z = -3.518, p < .001 (Tables SM5.3 and SM5.4). We therefore regard these supplementary models as convergent with the main analysis rather than as independent confirmation of it.

To ensure that our findings were not contingent on a single corruption index, we conducted additional analyses using the World Bank's Control of Corruption indicator (Kaufmann & Kraay, 2024). These analyses produced substantively identical patterns of results: the association between institutional distrust and conspiracy beliefs was stronger in countries with higher levels of control of corruption (i.e., lower levels of corruption; Tables SM6.1 and SM6.2).

Discussion

We extended the finding from Study 1, an initial first test, by revealing that institutional distrust is a strong predictor of conspiracy beliefs, but this association is stronger in objectively low-corruption countries. The results of Study 2 also spoke against a simple ceiling effect as a potential explanation for this pattern. Overall, these findings suggest that institutional distrust is more closely associated with conspiracy beliefs among individuals living in countries with objectively lower levels of corruption than among those living in more corrupt countries.

This implies that susceptibility to conspiracies is related not only to perceptions or national corruption levels in isolation, but also to the interaction between individual perceptions and institutional context. We also found that (i) these results do not appear to reflect restricted variance in institutional distrust; (ii) a model with a random slope for institutional distrust yielded the same pattern, with a less precise interaction estimate; and (iii) including other country-level factors, such as GDP, inequality, human development, and other measures of country-level corruption did not alter the conclusions.

General Discussion

This research investigated the relationship between perceived institutional corruption (and institutional distrust) and belief in conspiracy theories across national contexts varying in country-level corruption as indexed by expert assessments. Our findings highlight that the predictive value of perceiving corruption and distrust in institutions is not fixed but depends on the broader institutional environment in which individuals form their judgments about conspiracies. Rather than treating corruption perceptions and institutional distrust as a universal indicator of conspiratorial thinking, the present results suggest that their predictive value varies across contexts. Specifically, perceiving widespread corruption and distrust in institutions appears to be more strongly associated with conspiracy beliefs in relatively clean institutional environments, where such perceptions may be less congruent with prevailing institutional realities.

Using data from Türkiye and the UK (Study 1) and a representative sample from 20

European countries (Study 2), we found that perceived corruption (Study 1) and distrust in institutions (Study 2) are stronger predictors of conspiracy beliefs in countries with relatively lower levels of corruption. This interaction remained significant even after controlling for a comprehensive set of individual difference variables-including demographics, ideological orientation, cognitive style, personality traits, and generalized social trust-known to be associated with conspiracy beliefs (Douglas et al., 2017; Goreis & Voracek, 2019; Imhoff & Bruder, 2014; Lantian et al., 2021). The interaction was also significant for alternative model specifications (including a random slope for institutional distrust), the inclusion of countrylevel covariates (GDP, inequality, human development), and tests of measurement invariance.

With only 20 country-level units, however, and with these covariates themselves correlated with country-level corruption, the supplementary models are estimated with limited precision and are better read as convergent than as decisive.

This pattern contributes a key refinement to prevailing dual-process and motivational accounts of conspiracy beliefs by situating individual suspicion within its institutional context. Although some of the control variables may overlap with the focal constructs or act as mediators, the interaction was similarly observed in the controlled models (Tables SM3.3 and SM3.4). We also acknowledge the low internal consistency of the SDO measure in the Turkish sample (α = .406); however, a sensitivity analysis excluding this measure yielded a virtually identical interaction (Tables SM3.5 and SM3.6), so the controlled models do not appear to depend on it. While prior research has emphasized the role of individual-level factors-such as intuitive reasoning (Pennycook et al., 2012), epistemic suspicion (Lewandowsky et al., 2013), or low institutional trust (Mari et al., 2022; van Prooijen et al., 2022)-our findings suggest that the predictive value of perceived corruption and institutional distrust hinges on the congruence between subjective perception and broader institutional context. In low-corruption environments, perceiving widespread corruption and higher institutional distrust may be less congruent with prevailing institutional conditions. In contrast, in high-corruption settings, high perceived corruption and institutional distrust may be more understandable and less predictive of a generalised conspiratorial mindset, as they are more congruent with prevailing institutional conditions (Alper & Imhoff, 2023; Alper & Yilmaz, 2023 , 2024).

This context-sensitive account addresses a critical gap in the literature. While prior research has linked national corruption levels to conspiracy beliefs (Alper, 2023; Alper, Yelbuz et al., 2024; Cordonier & Cafiero, 2024), far fewer studies have asked whether the predictive values of perceiving corruption and institutional distrust change depending on institutional context. Our findings suggest that conspiracy thinking is not only linked to perceptions of corruption and institutional distrust or to country-level indices in isolation, but also to their interplay.

Importantly, because corruption indices are themselves perception-based, this pattern should not be overstated as a strict mismatch between lay perceptions and expert-based indices; it is better read as evidence that the predictive values of corruption perceptions and institutional distrust vary across contexts. This context-sensitive account suggests that conspiracy beliefs gain traction when individual worldviews and institutional signals diverge, possibly with increased likelihood that people seek alternative explanations (Alper, 2023; Alper, Yelbuz et al., 2024; Cordonier & Cafiero, 2024). In high-corruption settings, elevated conspiracy beliefs may be linked to generalized distrust given their congruence with prevailing institutional conditions. In low-corruption contexts, by contrast, strong perceptions of corruption and institutional distrust may be more likely to be linked to isolated experiences of institutional betrayal or to a more generalized disposition toward suspicion. Conceptually, this moves the field beyond treating corruption or institutional distrust as a main-effect predictor and toward understanding institutional context as a moderator that clarifies when perceptions of corruption (and institutional distrust) are most psychologically consequential.

Implications

These findings extend two important strands of literature. First, they build on work suggesting that conspiracy beliefs serve adaptive epistemic and existential functionsparticularly under conditions of uncertainty, lack of control, or systemic threat (Douglas et al., 2017; van Prooijen & van Vugt, 2018). Our results refine this view by suggesting that conspiracy beliefs may be less closely tied to prevailing institutional conditions in lowcorruption societies where perceived institutional failure could be inconsistent with the overall conditions of the context. In these settings, belief in conspiracies may arise not in relation to justified threat appraisal, but in relation to distrust-driven heuristics or ideologically motivated cognition (Lewandowsky et al., 2013; Pummerer et al., 2022). Conversely, in highcorruption contexts, conspiratorial worldviews may be more intelligible -an insight supported by findings that even highly educated individuals in corrupt environments report elevated distrust in science and scientists (Alper, Yelbuz et al., 2024). Whether conspiracy beliefs are epistemically warranted in some contexts more than others is a substantive question that has been taken up elsewhere, and the possibility that suspicion is better calibrated where institutions are demonstrably less trustworthy is a reasonable one (Alper & Imhoff, 2023; Alper & Yilmaz, 2023; Cordonier et al., 2021; van Prooijen & van Vugt, 2018).

Establishing warrant for this question would require assessing whether the specific propositions endorsed are true, and the propositions available in a cross-national surveythat secret groups direct world politics, that scientists suppress evidence, that COVID-19 was deliberately concealed -do not admit of a country-by-country truth assessment that would be comparable across 20 settings. Our claims therefore concern contextual intelligibility rather than epistemic justification: whether suspicion makes sense given prevailing institutional conditions, not whether it is borne out by the facts.

Second, our findings contribute to debates surrounding the ecological validity and limitations of using country-level variables in psychological research. Critics have warned of the ecological fallacy-inferring individual behavior from group-level indicators (Brewer & Venaik, 2014; Claessens et al., 2023). By examining interactions between individual perceptions and national-level indices, we avoid this fallacy and offer a multilevel model consistent with calls for more contextually embedded psychological science (Henrich et al., 2010; Muthukrishna et al., 2020).

This work has both theoretical and practical implications. First, it suggests that the same perception-"my country is corrupt" or "institutions cannot be trusted"-has different predictive values depending on where it is expressed. In countries such as Finland or Norway, where institutional corruption is consistently rated as low by expert indices, perceiving widespread corruption (or distrust in institutions) is less congruent with prevailing institutional conditions and appears to be more closely tied to a generalized suspicious worldview, which is consistent with its stronger association with conspiracy beliefs in these countries. In high-corruption contexts such as Bulgaria or Türkiye, the same belief and distrust may be more understandable given the institutional context (Alper & Yilmaz, 2023).

Second, the findings caution against treating conspiracy beliefs as a uniform phenomenon across contexts. As historical events like Watergate, COINTELPRO, and the Iran-Contra scandal demonstrate, conspiracies sometimes do occur, and in corrupt contexts, suspicion toward institutions is both more common and more contextually understandable (Alper & Imhoff, 2023; Alper & Yilmaz, 2023; Cordonier et al., 2021). This does not imply that any particular conspiracy claim is thereby warranted; it implies only that the same level of suspicion carries different information in different settings.

If this pattern holds up in further work, it would suggest that blanket strategies for misinformation correction may not travel well across low-and high-corruption contexts, and that interventions may need to take institutional conditions into account. The present cross-sectional data cannot, however, identify which interventions would work where.

Individual Differences in Context

Our results underscore the importance of interpreting individual-level predictorssuch as cognitive style, personality, and ideology-in light of contextual variables. While intuitive reasoning and low cognitive reflection are consistently associated with conspiracy beliefs (Bayrak et al., 2025; Swami et al., 2014; Yelbuz et al., 2022), our findings suggest that their associations with conspiracy beliefs may be stronger in contexts where perceptions of institutional failure are less congruent with prevailing conditions. Similarly, education is negatively related to conspiracy beliefs in low-corruption contexts but appears less protective in high-corruption settings (Alper, Yelbuz et al., 2024). Accordingly, psychological vulnerabilities to conspiracy thinking cannot be fully understood-or effectively mitigatedwithout attention to the institutional environments in which they operate.

Limitations and Future Directions

Several limitations merit consideration. First, our cross-sectional design precludes causal inference.

Longitudinal or experimental studies could clarify whether perceived corruption (and institutional distrust) fosters conspiracy beliefs, or whether conspiratorial thinking leads individuals to overestimate institutional decay. Second, the generic conspiracy scale used in Study 1 may mask important distinctions across conspiracy types; Study 2 partially addressed this with more specific items, but future work should include both political and non-political conspiracies, as well as implausible ones (e.g., flat-earth belief; Alper & Yilmaz, 2023). Third, our models assume that the association between these perceptions and conspiracy beliefs is linear within each context. The within-country distributions of institutional distrust differ in both location and spread across countries (see Table 2 and

Figure 3), and although the greater variance observed in more corrupt countries speaks against a simple ceiling effect, we did not model nonlinear forms of the association. Whether the association departs from linearity in particular contexts remains an open question, and specifications that allow for nonlinear associations (e.g., quadratic terms for institutional distrust and their interaction with country-level corruption) are a natural next step.

Fourth, although country-level corruption indices were our primary contextual moderator, we additionally probed alternative national covariates (GDP per capita, the Gini index, and the Human Development Index) in exploratory models; the focal interaction between individual-level institutional distrust and country-level corruption remained significant when these covariates were included and when a random slope for institutional distrust was allowed. Nevertheless, other structural features-such as inequality (Salvador Casara et al., 2022), polarization (Nera et al., 2021), and media trust (De Coninck et al., 2021)-may still interact with institutional distrust to shape conspiracy endorsement and merit direct tests as moderators rather than covariates. Integrating these factors as simultaneous moderators in preregistered multilevel models would yield a more comprehensive picture of when conspiratorial thinking emerges and how it can be countered-above and beyond the links with corruption indices.

A further limitation concerns the operationalization of perceived corruption as institutional distrust in Study 2. Because the ESS dataset does not include a direct measure of corruption perceptions, we relied on institutional trust items as indicators of citizens' evaluations of institutional integrity. Although institutional trust and perceived corruption are conceptually distinct constructs, they are closely intertwined in both theory and empirical research. Perceptions that public institutions engage in corruption or misconduct consistently erode institutional trust, and citizens often use trust judgments to evaluate whether institutions operate fairly and without corruption (Anderson & Tverdova, 2003; Mishler & Rose, 2001; Rothstein & Eek, 2009; Uslaner, 2002). At the same time, distinguishing between these constructs empirically is challenging because both capture broader evaluations of institutional integrity and governance quality (Morris & Klesner, 2010; Rothstein & Stolle, 2008; van der Meer, 2017). Consequently, while institutional trust cannot be considered a perfect substitute for perceived corruption, its use as an indicator of perceived institutional integrity is justified in large-scale comparative surveys where direct corruption measures are unavailable. A related caveat is that full metric invariance of the five trust items across the 20 countries was only partly supported (see the Study 2 Results); cross-country comparisons of the distrustconspiracy association therefore rest on approximately, rather than exactly, comparable measurement. We therefore do not treat Study 2 as evidence that the moderation generalizes across operationalizations of perceived corruption. Institutional distrust may capture evaluations of institutions that extend well beyond corruption, and it is itself associated with conspiracy beliefs, so the individual-level association in Study 2 is not a clean estimate of the corruption-conspiracy link. What the two studies share is the pattern of moderation rather than a common measure: in both, the association with conspiracy beliefs was weaker where country-level corruption was higher. Because the conceptual overlap between institutional distrust and conspiracy beliefs does not itself vary across countries, it does not offer a ready account of this contextual variation. A direct test would nonetheless require a measure of perceived corruption administered across a comparable set of countries, and we regard this as the most important next step for the present account.

Finally, the theoretical predictions guiding the present research were developed prior to conducting the primary analyses, but the studies should nevertheless be interpreted as providing initial evidence for the proposed context-sensitive account because of their nonpreregistered nature. Future preregistered research will be important for further testing the robustness and boundary conditions of these findings.

Conclusion

This research highlights a critical boundary condition in the psychology of conspiracy beliefs: the interaction between subjective perception and institutional quality as assessed at the national level. Perceived corruption and institutional distrust are robust predictors of conspiratorial thinking, but their predictive value is contingent on context. In low-corruption societies, they may be more closely tied to a generalized disposition toward suspicion, because such perceptions diverge from prevailing conditions; in high-corruption settings, they may reflect a more contextually understandable, more widely shared, and correspondingly less informative concern and be less strongly linked to conspiratorial thinking. By embedding individual perceptions within broader institutional settings, we advance an interactionist model of conspiracy belief and clarify when perceptions of corruption and institutional distrust are most psychologically consequential. These findings contribute to a growing literature on the context-sensitive nature of belief formation and call for future work that integrates individual and institutional perspectives to better understand the potential roots and rationality of conspiracy beliefs (Acemoglu & Robinson, 2013; Henrich et al., 2010).
