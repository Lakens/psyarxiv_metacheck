Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Marginal Significance works

List all sentences that describe an effect as ‘marginally significant’.

The marginal module searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The list of terms is a subset of those listed in a blog post by Matthew Hankins. The module returns all sentences that match terms describing ‘marginally significant’ results.

Some of the terms identified might not be problematic in some contexts, and there are ways to describe ‘marginal significance’ that are not detected by the module.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. Some differences come from the ground truth: it lacks p-values with a correction name (“p Bonf = .061”), and counts partial eta squared written with its subscript after it (“η 2 p = .05”), “p = .05”, and some table notes and figure legends (“† p < .10, *p < .05”), which this module leaves out, as non-significant p-values. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. Full text

Abstract

At some point in our evolutionary history humans gained the ability to communicate using language. Most accounts of language evolution focus on its role in communication. This review proposes an alternative: that the decisive step was the internalisation of utterance as a tool for directing thought. A hierarchy of animal communication is developed to sharpen this proposal. Evidence from the development of language in children supports the view that internalisation is central to language acquisition. The success of chain-of-thought reasoning in large language models provides a further, independent demonstration of the importance of language as a tool. These proposals together suggest that language is patterned on the structure of thought rather than shaped primarily by the demands of communication.

1 INTRODUCTION

It is incredible that we are able to express ideas and experiences in language and can do so even when what we want to express is complex, subtle, and novel. Language is also the subject of two important interlocking mysteries: what is the origin of our linguistic ability, and why do languages have the structure they have? The purpose of this review is to address these mysteries by presenting two ideas and one implication. The two ideas are that, firstly, it is important to consider language as a tool for thought and inference as well as for communication and, secondly, that the corresponding inward-directed use of utterances prompted the evolution of language. The potential implication is that language is patterned on thought. None of these ideas is new but the purpose of this review is to propose that, taken together, they provide a comprehensive account of the role, origin, and nature of language.

The evolution of language and the origin of its structure have attracted a great deal of theoretical attention but direct evidence remains elusive, see for example (Christiansen and Kirby, 2003; Fitch, 2010). Proposals for how language evolved typically outline a plausible sequence of adaptive steps and identify communicative or cognitive pressures that might have driven each transition. These proposals include ecological pressures such as the need to communicate about displaced resources (Bickerton, 2009), the emergence of cooperative behaviour (Tomasello, 2008) and the demands of tool manufacture (Greenfield, 1991; Stout and Chaminade, 2007). Beyond these there are more speculative proposals such as the Bow-Wow, Heave-Ho, Ding-Dong theories, each identifying a different putative origin for the first words. A more extreme view is to deny that language is the direct result of Darwinian adaptation and to contend that it emerged all of a sudden and by accident, as a byproduct of other cognitive changes (Hauser et al., 2002).

In this review I will try to specify the proposal that human language differs from communication in other animals, I will survey the evidence from the development of language in children that supports this proposal and I will suggest that the success of chain-of-thought reasoning in large language models also demonstrates the importance of language for thought. However, before any of this, the review begins with a description of the Iterated Learning Model, which was the original motivation for this review.

2 THE ITERATED LEARNING MODEL

The emphasis given here to the inward-directed use of language was prompted by recent work on the iterated learning model (ILM) (Bullock and Houghton, 2024; Bunyan et al., 2025; Lee et al., 2026). The ILM is a model (Kirby and Hurford, 2002) of language transmission and evolution which has been applied to simple artificial language learning tasks in which meanings and signals are typically limited to short bitstrings (Kirby, 2001; Kirby and Hurford, 2002). A tutor agent trains an initially naïve pupil agent by exposing it to a finite set of utterances, each pairing a meaning with the signal that expresses it in the tutor's language. After training, the pupil becomes a tutor and trains a new pupil. A language-learning bottleneck is a key feature of the model: the pupil only partially learns the tutor's language and so, when it becomes a tutor, it is forced to generalise to produce exemplars with which to train its own pupil.

Different variants of this model have been successful in that they simulate the development of compositionality, a crucial feature of real languages. In languages, compositionality describes the way individual words, affixes and grammatical rules retain their meaning across contexts. The ILM suggests that this property develops in languages because the learning bottleneck forces the pupil to generalise. There are several versions of the ILM, but a typical model due to Kirby and Hurford (2002) uses bit-strings, (1, 0, 1, 1, 1, 0, 0, 1) and so on, as both signals and meanings. Each language agent has an encoder map which maps meanings to signals and a complementary decoder map which maps signals back to meanings. signals meanings encoder decoder There are many different possible languages, each corresponding to a different encoder map. Under the right conditions, languages arise within the ILM that are expressive, compositional and stable. These are important properties of real language and one useful aspect of this ILM is that these properties can be defined for these simple bit-string languages, for example, in the case of a bit-string language, compositionality means that specific bits in the meanings are mapped to specific bits in the signals. The central insight provided by this version of the ILM is that only compositional languages can remain stable and expressive when the pupil is not given the opportunity to learn all meaning-signal pairs: any language that lacks compositional grammar cannot be consistently generalised.

One challenge in designing an ILM of this sort is that the encoder and decoder maps should be coupled and should act, in some rough sense, as inverses of each other. In fact, in the original ILM the encoder was explicitly constructed to make sure this happens: the decoder map is a neural network which learns during training whereas the encoder is constructed at the end of training as a look-up table using a robust inversion-like process called obversion. This obversion step is computationally costly. It is also unrealistic, it is akin to a child needing to complete their language learning by dreaming up all possible sentences and performing an impossibly huge mental computation to populate a vast look-up table. Our aim in proposing the semi-supervised ILM (Bunyan et al., 2025) was to couple the decoder and encoder without relying on obversion. The approach that worked was to include an unsupervised autoencoder learning step, akin to reflection. In the ILM the agent has two neural networks, one each for the encoder and decoder. The encoder and decoder can be chained together to form an autoencoder which maps meanings through signals back to meanings: meanings signals meanings encoder decoder This autoencoder uses the same neural networks as the encoder and decoder, but during autoencoder training the goal of training is to map the meaning to a signal and back to the same meaning without a tutor-specified signal. The semi-supervised ILM reformulates language learning as a combination of supervised and unsupervised episodes. The supervised episodes mimic a tutor pointing to a picture of a pedlar wearing many hats and saying "Look, the pedlar has many hats!". This helps a pupil train both their decoder mapping and their encoder mapping. The unsupervised learning episodes correspond to a pupil considering "How would I describe what the monkeys are doing with the hats, and what image does that description convey to me?". This reflection step pushes the language towards the representational richness required to transmit the meaning faithfully.

In so far as the ILM can be considered a model of real language learning, removing the obverter makes it closer to reality. Often accounts of how languages are learned and how they evolve emphasise the external role of language. In most accounts, languages are learned through exemplars and their evolution is prompted by the instrumental use in communication. The ILM suggests that the inward-directed use of language may also play a role in language learning and language evolution.

3 DIFFERENT TYPES OF SIGNALS

A hierarchy is apparent in animal communication and as an approach to clarify what is meant by inward-directed speech it is useful to create a hierarchical classification of signal types. At the bottom are accidental signals or cues, behaviours that other animals can observe and which therefore act as a signal even if their primary purpose is not communication. One animal running might prompt others to flee even if running is an escape behaviour, not a behaviour intended to communicate danger.

Next in this hierarchy are indices and what could be referred to as prompts. Indices are often the elaboration or ritualisation of a cue and are adapted for communication but animals produce them automatically or reflexively in response to environmental stimuli (Lorenz, 1950; Tinbergen, 1952). Rats, for example, produce 22 kHz ultrasonic distress calls which are triggered by fear or pain, these calls play a role in behavioural responses of conspecifics (Iznov et al., 2025) but originate in the periaqueductal grey and appear to bypass cortical initiation (Brudzynski, 2005). Prompts are instrumental signals that are employed much as an action is used, to produce a specific result. In canids, the behaviours most obviously associated with communication, teeth baring and barking, are often directed at non-conspecific animals; domestic dogs bark far more in interactions with humans than with other dogs (Yin and McCowan, 2004) for example, indicative of an instrumental use.

Finally, there are strategic signals, signals the animal directs at conspecifics to achieve a social outcome; much as a sophisticated animal might plan tool use to achieve a goal, a social animal might plan the use of an intentional signal. This sort of signal could be referred to as a bid. Of course, the distinction between each category is fuzzy, but the existence of planned signals is demonstrated by "lying" in animals, the intentional misuse of a signal to achieve a personal goal, using an alarm call to scare conspecifics away from a food source (Byrne and Whiten, 1990).

Here, a further stage in this hierarchy is proposed: inwarddirected signals in which signals are not just planned for, but form part of the mechanics of planning; the ability of the signal to carry a "fragment of meaning" is exploited as part of inference. These signals will be referred to as tokens. Thus, at the level of the bid, signalling is deployed instrumentally in social interaction whereas at the level of the token, signalling becomes reified as a manipulable unit that can be deployed both socially and internally. Roughly, a bid is an action and is useful in social interaction but a token is an object and is useful in inference.

The hypothesis proposed in this review is that the use of tokens is a prerequisite for language and the use of tokens is an advantage because it supports planning and inference. The advantages of tokens as a cognitive toolkit are manifold; one interesting demonstration of this discussed below is the way that language has been exploited for inference in machine learning systems. To give another concrete example, it is demonstrated by Lupyan and Thompson-Schill (2012) and Edmiston and Lupyan (2015) that words allow an appropriate level of abstraction. In picture verification experiments the word 'dog' primes the broad category of dogs, whereas the sound of a dog barking only primes for dogs likely to have produced a bark with its particular qualities of pitch and tempo; thus, the token provides a handle for a category that can be manipulated during cognition.

This classification of animal signals carries with it an implicit evolutionary story: a series of steps leading to language. The early part of this story is easily told. As animals develop more sophisticated representations of the world they come to include representations of how other animals behave. In social animals where flocking or herding aids survival and consanguinity makes the survival of conspecifics an evolutionary benefit, cues become indices. Sophisticated social animals develop complex behaviours around their interactions, mutual grooming and play fighting, for example, and indices evolve into bids.

The final step is not a standard part of this account: in this review language is identified with the use of tokens, so that the critical evolutionary transition occurs when speech becomes self-directed and internally manipulable. The decisive shift is not the strategic deployment of signals in social interaction; it is their use as objects for cognition: signals that can be produced, heard, and subsequently redeployed within ongoing inference. Such a transition might plausibly arise once an organism begins to treat its own vocal output as input, such as an utterance used to mean 'careful' employed when alone and doing something tricky. This idea has clear precedents in accounts that emphasise the inward-directed use of social speech (Luria, 1961; Vygotsky, 1962), the role of inner speech in self-monitoring and reasoning (Carruthers, 2002), and the view that language functions as a cognitive tool that restructures thought (Clark, 1998; Donald, 1991).

It remains a curious fact that humans appear unique in their linguistic capacities. This suggests that some consequential shift in cognitive architecture may have functioned as a narrow evolutionary pass, opening onto a new landscape of combinatorial and inferential possibilities. In the present account, that pass was the self-directed use of utterances: the transition from strategically deployed bids to internally manipulable tokens.

4 DEVELOPMENT

In Russell Hoban's Bread and Jam for Frances (Hoban, 1964) the main character, Frances, is an anthropomorphised badger who represents a child of around five years. In the story Frances sings to herself as she prepares a treat: "Jam on biscuits, jam on toast, jam is the thing that I like most". This is a fictional example of private speech; the self-talk we often overhear from young children and celebrate for its cuteness: the guileless quality which springs from its unperformative, self-directed intent. In fact, it is this unperformative intent that makes private speech valuable as an insight into the development of language in the child. Private speech often includes fragments of description or self-instruction as well as experiments in grammar and sentence structure. Based in part on the observation of private speech, Vygotsky proposed that language begins as social speech, becomes private speech, and is ultimately internalised as inward-directed speech (Luria, 1959 (Luria, , 1961;; Vygotsky, 1962).

Language use typically starts towards the end of the first year with instrumental speech: simple interjections that secure help from caregivers, 'um-um' to request food, 'ma-ma' to summon a parent. At around three years this progresses to audible private speech that children use to plan, monitor and inhibit their own actions; longitudinal studies show that children of around 30 months begin to accompany block-stacking or puzzle-solving with audible self-directed instructions like "put on top . . . no, turn" with an increase in the frequency of private speech for more cognitively demanding tasks (Berk and Garvin, 1984; Winsler et al., 1997). As the child approaches six, this private speech becomes inward-directed speech, the condensed inner speech that silently organises complex voluntary thought (Luria, 1959; Vygotsky, 1962; Winsler, 2009). In this, we see the development of language reprise the course we are hypothesising for the evolution of language. The early interjections are instrumental and external, the child uses commands much as they would use a limb movement; they do not appear to form part of the child's thought, they are, rather, the result of thought, just as reaching or grasping would be. In the next stage, in private speech, these instrumental commands are employed to help control the child's own thoughts and the child's own actions, speech becomes part of the machinery of thought, a machinery we can eavesdrop on while private speech remains self-directed, but not, strictly, internal.

As children acquire speech they gain a new psychological instrument for directing their own behaviour and thought. This was an important theme in the work of Luria (1959). Luria's programme for uncovering the self-regulatory power of speech relied on a series of deceptively simple laboratory games. As an example, Tikhomirov (1958) used a Go / No-Go task with a panel that lit up red or blue at irregular intervals which became shorter and shorter. Three-year-olds could initially follow instructions to "press on red, wait on blue" but soon the excitement from the flashing lights and button pressing would cause them to forget the inhibitory part of the instruction and they would press for both red and blue. However, children asked to shout "press!" or "don't press!" as appropriate were able to sustain their performance of the task. Furthermore, when 3and 4-year-olds do problem-solving tasks, they not only talk to themselves but also monitor and repair multi-clause utterances such as "I need the yellow one 'cause it's smaller . . . no, the blue goes first". This demonstrates on-line self-correction (Manfra et al., 2016).

Diary studies vividly demonstrate that, in addition to selfdirection, children's self-speech is used in language learning. Once the rudiments of instrumental social language are in place, a child's solitary talk becomes a tool in language development. A study described in Weir (1962) examines transcripts of solitary pre-sleep speech by the author's own 30-month-old child. Often the child is observed to practise grammatical constructions, with substitution exercises, "hi Bob / that's Bob / big Bob / little Bob", and experiments in word order. In another study of fourteen longitudinal cases (Kuczaj, 1983), private speech included morphological play; words like "goed" and novel verb-argument constructions appeared more often in crib speech than in concurrent social talk. This rehearsal and linguistic exploration has been observed in other studies, for a discursive review see Berk (1994). Again and again, the remarkable feature is that children play with language and improve their own performance without external supervision and language learning is genuinely bidirectional with public language seeding private rehearsal.

While it lasts, self-speech gives us a listening-post that allows us to observe the use that children make of private speech. What we overhear demonstrates first, that child speech progresses from an instrumental to a self-directed stage and second, that there are two aspects of self-directed speech: language as a tool and language learning through self-directed speech.

5 CHAIN-OF-THOUGHT REASONING

In many everyday reasoning problems, large language models produce visible linguistic scaffolding as part of their answer. For example, when asked: Áine and Brian each own a different dog, a whippet and a spaniel, and live in different houses, red and blue. Brian does not live in the red house. The owner of the whippet lives in the red house. Who owns the whippet? a language model will not usually reply "Áine" but will articulate intermediate steps: Brian doesn't live in the red house, so Áine lives in the red house. The whippet owner lives in the red house, so Áine owns the whippet.

This additional text may appear to be explanatory padding for the user, but it also functions computationally. By generating intermediate linguistic structure, the model clarifies the constraint relationships and conditions subsequent token prediction on them. This is chain-of-thought reasoning; a broad class of strategies, some visible, some not, that allow large language models to produce more sophisticated responses.

As an example, until mid-2023, at a time when large language models could translate between natural languages, unless explicitly asked to provide a lookup table, a Morse-code prompt often elicited a strange and inaccurate response: a kind of spy-novelese word-salad. The system dutifully produced a pastiche of the adventure fiction that presumably made up its Morse-code exemplars, rather than decoding the message. Morse translation demands a small but crucial step: the task needs to be recognised as cipher-decoding requiring a symbolby-symbol lookup. The behaviour of the models changed abruptly. After mid-2023 the same prompt that previously triggered a nonsense spies-and-signals reply started to give an accurate plain-English decoding: the model moved from direct associative completion to a self-directed plan of action, identify cipher → build table → translate In parallel, tool-using interfaces such as code execution and function-calling made it easier for the system to act on any plan through tiny, reliable computation (Gao et al., 2023; Yao et al., 2023). The important point here is not tools but the emergence of a sort of reasoning. Once the system was encouraged to generate explicit intermediate steps and to invoke a simple helper script, it ceased free-associating around the narrative context and instead used inward-directed language to specify a procedure: treating Morse code as a character-by-character cipher.

When it describes intermediate steps to itself in language, the model can turn its raw associative capacity into directed, goal-driven inference: the statistical relationships it learns through training on next-word prediction power its inference, but are not the totality of it. To perform complex tasks, large language models often use something similar to inwarddirected speech. Of course, the use of language in large transformers is complex and evolving. Indeed newer models may possess sufficiently rich internal representations to decode Morse-code inputs without recourse to stepwise linguistic decomposition or tool use and the deployment of language in responding to queries is situation-dependent. Nonetheless, chain-of-thought reasoning does demonstrate the utility of selfdirected speech in inferential processes.

At present, when large language models reason step-by-step, they do so in a normal human language chosen to match the apparent preferences of the user. There is nothing inevitable about this: it is a consequence of training rather than selection. Tool use is instructive here: when models are given access to a symbol-manipulation layer, a Python interpreter for example, they often route some of the response through that layer rather than through prose, suggesting that natural language is not always the most efficient substrate for the kinds of structured inference these systems perform. This does raise questions about human languages: what gave human languages the properties they have, what set of expectations about language structure guides learning in infants and what is the substrate for these expectations?

6 LANGUAGE PATTERNED ON THOUGHT

A central tenet of the Chomskyan programme is that human beings come equipped with a Universal Grammar, whose principles are taken to be arbitrary. Just as the particular choice of word to match a particular meaning is arbitrary (de Saussure, 1916), there is, as the argument runs, a great variety of conceivable systems of universal rules, but evolution has canalised us into just one uniquely human version (Chomsky, 1965 (Chomsky, , 1986)). During first language learning the child's experience of language merely triggers a pre-wired schema. This schema does not arise from experience, it represents innate knowledge of which particular principles govern the structure of human language.

The clarity with which Chomsky identified the challenge childhood language learning presents remains important, and the vast quantity of text large language models require to acquire grammar only sharpens it (Houghton et al., 2023), but the Universal Grammar view has been contested on multiple fronts; in particular, it is not obvious that this arbitrariness is either necessary or plausible. A contrary view is that any gram-matical properties that are found across the grammars of diverse languages are present precisely because they mirror the structure of the world we experience and the forms of thought we deploy when making inferences about that world (Bybee, 2010; Piaget, 1952; Tomasello, 2003). As a simple and obvious example, the most elementary grammatical distinction, the separation of content words into noun and verb, tracks the real-world distinction between objects and actions.

More elaborate hallmarks such as deep recursion and hierarchical phrase structure can likewise be interpreted as the linguistic footprints of the part-to-whole constructions that organise human reasoning (Greenfield, 1991; Lashley, 1951; Miller et al., 1960). In Piaget's programme cognitive growth proceeds up a hierarchy with each stage embedding and reorganising earlier structures into a higher-order whole (Piaget, 1952). It is possible that the child's ability to coordinate actions and represent relations among actions provides the conceptual scaffolding for nested phrase structures and dependency relations in syntax, while, running the other way, emerging linguistic hierarchies are used as internal symbols in increasingly hierarchical reasoning.

Space and spatial organisation seem to form a key part of this story: spatial representation is linked to conceptual and linguistic structure (Barsalou, 2008; Jackendoff, 1983 Jackendoff, , 2002;; Tversky, 2019). Indeed, the structure of space mirrors logical relations, for example, "the nest is on the tree, the tree is in the bog" implies that the nest is in the bog, just as A ⊂ B and B ⊂ C imply A ⊂ C and spatial representation can form a template for the representations used in logical inference. Similarly, expressions and structures derived from spatial structure are found across unrelated language families and the recurrence is unlikely to be coincidental (Anderson, 1971; Bopp, 1816; Lyons, 1967). It is often easier to notice the peculiarities of languages if they are less familiar and, as an illustration, an example from Irish (Gaeilge, ISO 639-3 gle) is presented. In English the phrase "I am reading" uses the present participle "reading", the equivalent Irish phrase is táim ag léamh. Here léamh is the verbal noun of léigh, the Irish verb meaning "read". The verbal noun behaves like a noun and ag means "at" so the literal translation of táim ag léamh is "I am at the state-of-readingness".

At this point there is a potential confusion between thought directed by language and the term 'language of thought', introduced by Fodor (1975). The language of thought does not refer to inner speech or language-directed thought in the sense used throughout this review; it hypothesises that there is a prelinguistic system of symbolic, compositional mental representations that underlies thought in general: the cognitive architecture within which inference takes place regardless of whether language is involved. Fodor uses the word 'language' for thought, to reflect the view that thought is compositional and encodes logical relationships. This remains a contentious idea, but in the context of this review, it is proposed not only that this is true but that language has evolved to subserve the needs of complex inference as well as communication and therefore finds a template in the language of thought. This template gives it a compositional structure and an approximate logical calculus. This is similar to Millikan's teleosemantic programme (Millikan, 2005), in which the con-tent and structure of linguistic expressions are related to their function and their evolutionary history. The key difference is that, for Millikan, the function of language is communication.

A compelling recent proposal suggests that the neural ingredients for symbolic, compositional thought are already present in the spatial navigation system (Kazanina and Poeppel, 2023). Kazanina and Poeppel argue that cells in the hippocampus of rodents implement precisely the kind of abstract predicates that a Fodorian language of thought requires. This suggests that the brain already possesses a system capable of the symbolic, compositional computation that underpins inference, and that this system is organised spatially. It is suggested here that if tokens inherit the structure of the inferential system they serve, then the spatial organisation of the language of thought provides a template from which language structure derives.

7 DISCUSSION

This review proposes that language is best understood as an inward-directed tool as well as an outward-directed channel for communication and that a key function of language is the scaffolding of thought and inference. This suggests that words provide useful labels and that grammars create expectations for words: in English an adjective is often followed by a noun. These expectations can be thought of as providing useful priors for inferential reasoning.

The relationship between language and thought is complex and contested. As an example, the Irish author Sally Rooney writes about the snooker player Ronnie O'Sullivan (Rooney, 2025):

In that particular frame, after potting that last red, how did O'Sullivan know that the cue ball would come back down the table that way and land precisely where he wanted it? Of course it was only obeying the laws of physics. But if you wanted to calculate the trajectory of a cue ball coming off an object ball and then a cushion using Newtonian physics, you'd need an accurate measurement of every variable, some pretty complex differential equations, and a lot of calculating time. O'Sullivan lines up that shot and plays it in the space of about six seconds.

A lot of sophisticated thought clearly occurs without language. In fact, a lot of thought is perceived by the thinker to be "out of contact" with language-directed thought. The passage quoted above captures this vividly. Ronnie O'Sullivan is not calculating explicitly and not reasoning in any form that is accessible to introspection or reportable in words. The same point applies more broadly: animals plan, deceive, and solve problems without language, and humans perform countless skilled acts, catching a ball, reading a face, in ways that are entirely opaque to verbal reflection. If language constituted all of thought, none of this would be possible.

However, the claim here is not that all thought is linguistic but that language provides a manipulable representational layer that extends and organises thought. Consider how Ronnie O'Sullivan became Ronnie O'Sullivan. The instinctive mastery he now displays was built through years of practice in which shots were almost certainly described, evaluated, and rehearsed in language, muttered at the table, discussed with friends, replayed in inner speech during the long hours of solitary practice. The language may have disappeared from the finished performance, and this performance may feel like it lies beyond the realm of words, but along the way private speech scaffolded the acquisition of skills that are subsequently automatised and internalised leaving no linguistic trace. While the interface between language-directed thought and non-linguistic thought may appear to lie beyond our perception of our own thought, this does not mean that it is absent.

Many thoughts not only benefit from language but could not be easily accomplished without it. Indeed the scaffolding of thought often includes externalised language: talking things out, writing notes, constructing to-do lists are all part of the machinery of thought. Mathematics gives us a useful analogy. It is very hard to do mathematics without writing it down. We often make small metaphorical drawings and scribbles before inspiration strikes but to understand our own mathematical ideas and to check they are correct we need to write them down and to use the logical relationships encoded in the symbols to validate the chain of deduction. Similarly, language is a set of tokens with learned relationships; they may lack the precision of mathematical symbols but, nonetheless, they encode useful structure.

Many people report that they think primarily in images rather than words, and some report thinking in neither (Heavey and Hurlburt, 2008). If inner speech is a central cognitive tool, what are these people doing? This is genuinely difficult to assess, but there are reasons for caution about introspective reports of this kind. The unreliability of introspection about cognitive processes is well established (Nisbett and Wilson, 1977) and what people describe when asked how they think may reflect their phenomenological experience of thinking rather than the underlying computational processes. Indeed, it would be surprising if people differ so profoundly in their inferential processes rather than just differing in how they understand those processes to operate and so the reported differences in the nature of inner thought might resemble this diagram:

In each case the cross stands for the place the subject locates their thought process even if the inferential machinery in each case incorporates inner speech and continuous representations. These considerations connect to larger questions about the nature of consciousness that lie beyond the scope of this review; the principal one being the relationship between inwarddirected speech, our "inner voice", and consciousness. It is tempting to define consciousness broadly, extending it to any system capable of representing its environment and acting on those representations. Such definitions are useful for discussing animal cognition and have the advantage of continuity with evolutionary biology. However, definitions which blur the boundary between consciousness, sentience, awareness and attention may not capture what we mean when we reflect on our own conscious experience, the felt sense of an inner life in which thoughts are available to us as objects of attention. The hierarchy proposed in this review suggests a corresponding hierarchy of awareness. In this view, cues, indices and bids span the range from attention to sentience while tokens are a prerequisite for consciousness. Tokens give us the capacity to make our own mental states the object of thought and to construct the running inner commentary that is central to our lived experience of conscious thought and which creates the human form of reflective consciousness.

Finally, it is interesting to note that this does not suppose that consciousness is constructed of a 'private language'. Wittgenstein argued that there is no private language: the parts of thought accessible to language are accessible through a language developed in language games, social contexts with public criteria of correctness (Wittgenstein, 1953). However, this maps naturally onto the token concept: tokens are a scaffold for thinking whose structure uses the structure of thought as a template but which develop meaning as part of a communal language game.

The question is how to decide whether these ideas are useful and valid. Perhaps the most obvious weakness of these proposals is the one mentioned above: the variation in how people describe their own thought and the tendency for some people to describe their thinking as picture based. This suggests an experimental programme; the famous rotation experiments of Shepard and Metzler (1971) demonstrate that there is an unconscious and unavoidable visual element to thought when performing visual tasks; the challenge is to find analogous planning or reasoning tasks to demonstrate that thought also has an inevitable language element.

Beyond that, in mathematical modelling, the ILM could form the basis for a more sophisticated agent-based model which uses its signal space as an inferential tool. In cognitive science, the ongoing efforts to probe the structure of thought more directly and to develop neural measures of the relationship between inner speech and reasoning would allow the central claims of this review to be tested more rigorously. Linguistically the relationship between spatial cognition and language structure deserves systematic cross-linguistic investigation. Finally, there is the question of whether any non-human animal possesses anything like inward-directed signalling. Is the gap between bids and tokens absolute or do animals possess some version of tokenised thought?
