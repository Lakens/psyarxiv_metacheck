Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. Full text

Abstract

Cooperative behavior is often viewed as evidence of altruistic motivation. Yet, cost-free, non-binding signals of intended cooperation -known as cheap talk -can also shape others' future behavior to one's advantage. Distinguishing unconditional altruistic cooperation from this more strategic cooperative signaling is difficult experimentally, because the same action can reflect either motive. We present a modified trust game that dissociates these motivations by allowing participants to emit cost-free cooperative signals that neither alter immediate payoffs nor commit them to future cooperation, but that could encourage future investments. In three online samples of adults (n = 253 / 488 / 827), participants learned to use cheap talk strategically, signaling greater willingness to cooperate when doing so could encourage future investment. Overall, exploitativeness, a facet of antagonism, positively predicted cheap talk, whereas callousness and aggression showed weaker, negative associations. Cooperative behavior therefore serves not only as an expression of altruistic motivation, but also as a strategic tool for shaping others' expectations and future choices. More broadly, these findings demonstrate how the social-cognitive capacities that sustain human cooperation can also be deployed instrumentally to influence others.

[div-01]

Cooperation is fundamental to human social life, allowing people to form relationships, coordinate their behavior, and pursue shared goals. Yet, cooperative behavior does not always reflect cooperative motives. Consider a new colleague who goes out of their way to help you with an important project. The gesture appears generous, but is it necessarily so? They may genuinely wish to help, feel bound by social norms, or be cultivating trust in anticipation of needing your assistance later. In each case, the behavior is identical, but the underlying motivation differs dramatically.

This ambiguity reflects a fundamental challenge in understanding human cooperation: cooperative acts strongly influence our decisions about whom to trust, yet those acts may themselves be shaped by an interest in appearing trustworthy.

Cooperation as Social Information

When deciding whom to trust, assist, transact with, and select as future interaction partners, people often look to past cooperative behavior (Allen et al., 2024; Chang et al., 2010; FeldmanHall et al., 2018). Indeed, the evolutionary mechanisms of direct and indirect reciprocity, reputation-based cooperation, and partner choice all depend on individuals' tracking others' cooperative behavior and using it to guide subsequent decisions (Nowak & Sigmund, 2005; Trivers, 1971). From the actor's perspective, this creates an incentive to behave cooperatively when doing so can shape how others behave in future interactions. Consistent with this perspective, people cooperate more in experiments when their actions are observable, carry reputational consequences, or affect access to future opportunities (Barclay & Willer, 2006; Fehr & Fischbacher, 2003; Milinski et al., 2002; Wedekind & Milinski, 2000). Thus, cooperation functions not only as an allocation of resources, but also as a way to shape a partner's future behavior.

For a cooperative signal to be useful, however, others must find it credible.

Signaling theory (Spence, 1973) proposes that costs help to maintain credibility by making them more difficult -or less worthwhile -to produce for people who lack the quality or intention being signaled (Gintis et al., 2001; Grafen, 1990; Smith & Harper, 2003). For example, staying late to finish a task with a colleague is stronger evidence of cooperativeness than simply offering to help because staying late requires giving up personal time -a cost that only a truly cooperative person would typically be willing to bear.

Not all social signals are costly, however. Merely offering to help your colleague (without actually doing so) can foster an impression of cooperativeness without requiring any immediate sacrifice. In game theory, cost-free, nonbinding communication is often described as cheap talk (Crawford & Sobel, 1982; Farrell & Rabin, 1996).

Because cheap talk neither alters immediate payoffs nor commits the sender to subsequent behavior, it can easily be used to claim cooperative intentions that the sender does not actually hold. Nevertheless, sender-receiver models show that cheap talk can convey information when the parties' interests are at least partially aligned -for example, when both colleagues benefit from a productive relationship (Crawford & Sobel, 1982). As interests diverge, however, cheap talk becomes less informative because the sender has more to gain by misrepresenting their intentions. Cheap talk can therefore communicate genuine cooperative intentions, but it can also be used strategically to cultivate trust and encourage favorable treatment.

Antagonism and Strategic Cooperation

Critically, the tendency to use cooperative signals as cheap talk should vary across individuals. In dimensional models of personality and psychopathology, agreeableness-antagonism captures individuals' ability to align their goals with those of others and sustain positive social relationships (DeYoung, 2015; Lynam & Miller, 2019).

Across classic economic games and real-world contexts, self-reported agreeableness predicts more cooperative, prosocial, and forgiving behavior, whereas antagonism is typically associated with selfishness, defection, and conflict (Carlo et al., 2005; Habashi et al., 2016; Yarkoni et al., 2015; Zhao & Smillie, 2015). Factor analyses distinguish at least three related facets of agreeableness-antagonism: compassion v. callousness, which contrasts empathy and altruism with a lack of concern for others, pacifism v. aggression, which contrasts nonviolence and conflict avoidance with aggression, and politeness v. exploitativeness, which contrasts honesty and humility with dishonesty, selfishness, and grandiosity (Allen et al., 2017; Crowe et al., 2018; Sleep et al., 2021).

These facets exhibit divergent social cognition profiles. For example, callousness and aggression are typically associated with poorer performance on classic theory of mind or mentalization tasks, whereas exploitativeness is associated with better performance (Allen et al., 2017; Blain et al., 2021 Blain et al., , 2025)). This divergence also extends to social learning. Using a modified repeated trust game, we found that learning signals tracking how well participants anticipated a partner's cooperative behavior were weaker among individuals higher in callousness and aggression but stronger among more exploitative individuals. These findings suggest callousness and aggression reflect reduced sensitivity to cooperation incentives, whereas exploitativeness is linked to more effective learning about others' behavior. These individual differences may also have consequences for the use of cheap talk. Cheap talk separates the benefits of appearing cooperative from the costs of committing to cooperation, making it potentially attractive to individuals who are both inclined to use others instrumentally and particularly adept at predicting how others will interpret and respond to one's behavior. Thus, exploitative individuals should be particularly prone to using cooperative cheap talk to influence social partners, whereas callous and aggressive individuals may be less inclined to do so.

Measuring Cooperative Cheap Talk

A central obstacle in cooperation research is that identical cooperative acts can arise from fundamentally different psychological processes. In many experimental paradigms, the same action can both benefit another person and influence how the actor is perceived or treated in the future, making altruistic and strategic motives difficult to disentangle (https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?nYsbHD Bourgeois-Gironde & Corcos, 2011; Snijder et al., 2026). For example, in repeated trust games, reciprocating an investment both benefits the investor immediately and may encourage greater trust on subsequent interactions (King-Casas et al., 2005 , 2008). Similarly, in reputation-and partner-choice paradigms, cooperative acts simultaneously benefit others and improve one's reputation or access to desirable partners (Fehrler & Przepiorka, 2016; Milinski et al., 2002; Serdarevic et al., 2021). This same ambiguity characterized our own prior trust game studies: participants' cooperative choices reflected learning from a partner's past behavior, but the underlying motive -strategic influence, norm adherence, or unconditional altruismremained unclear (Allen et al., 2024; Vanyukov et al., 2019).

To address this limitation, we developed a multi-round trust paradigm that distinguishes cooperative cheap talk from costly cooperation. In a standard trust game, cooperation by the trustee is costly: returning part of an investment benefits the investor but reduces the trustee's immediate payoff. Our task manipulates this cost while holding the expressed cooperative intention constant. In one condition, participants decide whether to return an investment before learning whether the investor shared it, meaning that their decision may carry monetary consequences. In another, participants whose partner has already declined to invest indicate whether they would have returned the investment had it been shared. This counterfactual response is communicated to the partner but cannot alter the participant's immediate payoff or commit them to future cooperation; it therefore constitutes cheap talk in the game-theoretic sense.

Critically, observing cheap talk alone cannot reveal whether it reflects strategic signaling or a general tendency to cooperate. A general preference for cooperation will promote both cost-free expressions of willingness to cooperate and cooperation that carries actual costs. We therefore operationalize individual differences in cooperative cheap talk as the extent to which people selectively express cooperation when it is cost-free relative to when it is costly -a pattern consistent with strategic signaling.

Manipulating partner reciprocity additionally allowed us to examine whether the use of cheap talk varied with the partner's responsiveness to cooperative signals.

The Present Study

The present study leverages this new paradigm to ask whether people strategically use cooperative cheap talk to influence others, and whether this behavior varies across facets of antagonism. We first tested whether participants expressed greater willingness to cooperate when doing so was cost-free and nonbinding, but could plausibly encourage future investment. We also examined whether cheap talk varied with the partner's reciprocity, which could affect the perceived value of attempting to influence that partner. Finally, building on our prior findings, we hypothesized that exploitativeness would predict greater cheap talk, reflecting a propensity to use cooperative signals instrumentally. In contrast, aggression and callousness were expected to predict reduced cheap talk, reflecting either diminished concern for social coordination or reduced sensitivity to social contingencies.

Methods

Participants

Samples consisted of adult participants (Sample 1: n = 300; Sample 2 n = 548; Sample 3: n = 904) enrolled in online studies of social decision-making conducted on Prolific (see Table S1 for demographic characteristics). After exclusions based on attention checks and quality controls (see Table S2), Sample 1 consisted of 253 participants (Mean age = 37.24, SD = 11.77; 49% female), Sample 2 consisted of 488 participants (Mean age = 45.78, SD = 15.62, 52% female), and Sample 3 consisted of 827 participants (Mean age = 37.12, SD = 9.91, 58% female).

Measures

Antagonism and its lower-order facets were measured via a battery of self-report measureshttps://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?xywQUe including the Big Five Aspect Scales (BFAS; DeYoung et al.https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?DHVyk6 2007) https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?VgjVvF Externalizing Spectrum Inventory Brief Form (https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?DHVyk6 Patrick et al., 2013) , Interpersonal Reactivity Index (https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?VgjVvF Davis, 1980) , and Personality Inventory for DSM-5-Brief Form (https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?1TLppt Maples et al., 2015). Relevant subscales from each measure were selected for use in analyses based on their theoretical placement within the antagonism domain and our prior modeling of antagonism's facet structure (Allen et al., 2017 (Allen et al., , 2019 (Allen et al., , 2024;; Blain et al., 2021). These scales initially included: BFAS Compassionfoot_0 ; PID-5 Deceitfulness, Manipulativeness, Grandiosity, Attention-Seeking, and Callousness; IRI Perspective-taking and Empathic Concern; ESI Fraud, Relational Aggression, Destructive Aggression, Physical Aggression, Lack of Empathy, and Lack of Honesty (see Tables S3-S6 for self-report exclusions and descriptive statistics).

Experimental Task

The Cooperative Signaling Task (CST; Figure 1) is a 222-trial modified trust game designed to assess the strategic use of cost-free cooperative signals (i.e., cheap talk) to influence a partner's future behavior. Participants play as trustees ("leprechauns") interacting with three ostensible investors ("merchants") who reciprocate at fixed rates (75%, 50%, or 25%, otherwise responding randomly). On each trial, the investor receives one gold coin and decides whether to keep or share it. If shared, the coin is multiplied (1×, 3×, or 5×; randomly assigned), and the participant chooses whether to return half or keep the full amount. The multiplier is revealed only after the participant's decision, preventing conditioning on payoff magnitude and dissociating expectations about the investor's behavior from monetary outcomes. Participants complete three runs of two block types that differ in the timing of information. In the Investment-Aware block (akin to the classic trust game), the investor's decision is revealed before the participant responds. If the investor shares, the participant decides whether to return half or keep the multiplied amount. If the investor keeps, participants indicate whether they would have returned had it been shared. Because this response neither affects payoffs nor commits the participant to future cooperation, it constitutes cooperative cheap talk. In the Investment-Blind block, participants decide whether to return before learning the investor's choice; if the investor shares, their precommitted decision is carried out. Thus, in the Investment-Blind block, participants cannot condition their choice on the investor's immediate behavior, and return decisions provide a baseline index of general cooperativeness under uncertainty.

Our primary outcome, cheap talk, is operationalized as the interaction between block type and investor decision, indexing the extent to which the Investment-Aware versus Investment-Blind difference in return behavior is greater on investor-keep than investor-share trials. Participants complete three runs, one per investor type; the 50% investor always comes first, with the 75-25% investors counterbalanced and block order randomized. Participants are led to believe they are interacting with real partners. The task is identical across samples except that Sample 3 did not include the 25% reciprocating investor (n trials = 148), and button placement was counterbalanced in Samples 2 and 3 to rule out response-side bias as a potential confound.

Statistical Approach

Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was conducted to evaluate whether selected scales were strong indicators of the latent constructs of callousness, aggression, and exploitativeness. In the initial CFA model in Sample 1, Callousness was indicated by BFAS Compassion, IRI Perspective-Taking, IRI Empathic Concern, PID-5 Callousness, and ESI Lack of Empathy; Exploitativeness by PID-5 Manipulativeness, PID-5 Deceitfulness, PID-5 Grandiosity, PID-5 Attention-Seeking, and ESI Lack of Honesty; and Aggression by ESI Relational Aggression, ESI Physical Aggression, ESI Destructive Aggression, ESI Theft, and ESI Fraud.

Model fit for the CFA was determined using established criteria for acceptable and good fit: comparative fit index (CFI) ≥ .90/.95, the root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA) ≤ .08/.06, and the standardized root mean square residual (SRMR) ≤ .10/.08 (Hu & Bentler, 1999; Kline, 2016). Modification indices were inspected to identify sources of misfit. Adjustments (e.g., residual correlations) to the model were permitted only when theoretically defensible. Indicators exhibiting substantial cross-loadings without theoretical justification were removed to preserve interpretability.

The final CFA model established in Sample 1 was specified identically in Samples 2 and 3. Model fit and model adjustments were handled using the same criteria in all samples.

To test behavioral effects on the task and examine whether they were moderated by personalityhttps://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?G2FIxH we used Bayesian multilevel structural equation modeling (https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?G2FIxH Sadikaj et al., 2019) , which allows trial-level effects to be estimated as purely within-person processes while simultaneously modeling between-person heterogeneity (Lüdtke et al., 2008). Within-person predictors included block (Aware versus Blind), investor decision (keep versus share), and their interaction (cheap talk). We first examined models in which investor-type indicators were included as covariates, and then examined models where investor type was allowed to moderate cheap talk. To account for task progression effects, models included centered within-block trial and global trial. Each progression variable interacted with block, investor decision, and block × investor decision, allowing cheap talk to change both within blocks and across successive investors. Models included a participant-specific random intercept and random slopes for all within-person predictors, with random effects allowed to correlate.

To examine personality moderation, latent factors representing callousness, exploitativeness, and aggression were included at the between-person level. These traits were specified as simultaneous predictors of both the random intercept and slopes. This specification allowed personality to moderate both cheap talk and its change over task progression. By modeling random slopes as latent variables and integrating the measurement model directly into the structural model, this approach reduces measurement error and minimizes cross-level confounding, providing a rigorous test of whether antagonism facets account for individual differences in strategic cheap talk.

All MSEM models used Bayesian estimation with noninformative priors in Mplus Version 8.7 (Muthén & Muthén, 2019). Bayesian estimation uses all available data and provides similar results to full information maximum likelihood in accounting for missing data (Asparouhov & Muthén, 2010). Because our primary hypotheses made directional predictions, we computed one-tailed Bayesian p-values, defined as the posterior probability that the focal effect was in the direction opposite to that predicted. We report these directional Bayesian p-values together with 95% credible intervals to characterize uncertainty in effect-size estimates. Note that Bayesian posterior probabilities quantify the extent to which the data support a given hypothesis, which allows for stronger inference than frequentist approaches that quantify the probability of observing the data under the null hypothesis.

Results

Behavioral Evidence for Interpersonal Coaxing

Multilevel structural equation modeling was used to examine our hypothesis that participants would engage in cheap talk when given the opportunity to signal cooperation at no cost. Cheap talk was operationalized as the within-person block × investor decision interaction predicting trial-level return decisions, with the expectation that return rates would be higher in the Investment-Aware block relative to the Investment-Blind block specifically on trials in which the investor kept.

In all three samples, the block × investor decision interaction was significant, indicating that the effect of block was contingent on the investor's behavior (Supplemental Table S7). Probing this interaction revealed that when investors kept, participants returned at significantly higher rates in the Investment-Aware block relative to the Investment-Blind block. Thus, participants selectively indicated willingness to cooperate in the Investment-Aware block, where return decisions on keep trials were cost-free, relative to the Investment-Blind block, where return decisions could entail monetary consequences (see Figure 2). In all samples, participants also returned less often in the Investment-Aware block relative to the Investment-Blind block when the investor had shared, consistent with greater temptation or cost salience once an investment is known. There was a main effect of investor reciprocity, such that participants returned more with the 75% reciprocal investor than the 50% (all samples) or 25% (Samples 1 & 2) investor. Participants also returned less as the block progressed and, in Sample 1 only, as the task progressed. Cheap talk increased both within each investor interaction, and as participants encountered subsequent investors, both of which are consistent with learning. Separate Bayesian MSEMs showed no evidence that cheap talk varied by investor reciprocity in any sample (Table S8).

Taken together, these results represent strong evidence, across three independent samples, that participants selectively engaged in cheap talk, using cost-free cooperative signaling to plausibly influence their counterpart's future investment behavior.

Confirmatory Factor Analysis of Lower-Order Antagonism Facets

To examine associations between antagonism and cheap talk at the latent level, we first established a three-factor measurement model that could be carried forward into structural analyses. Figure 3 presents the standardized factor loadings for the final model across all three samples. The initial CFA in Sample 1 exhibited poor fit, χ²(87) = 268.61, p < .001, CFI = .89, RMSEA = .09, SRMR = .09. The two largest modification indices suggested that fit could be improved by allowing ESI Lack of Honesty to cross-load on Callousness and PID-5 Callousness to cross-load on Exploitativeness.

Because these cross-loadings were not theoretically justified, we removed the two indicators rather than introducing the suggested parameters. The revised model demonstrated improved fit in Sample 1, χ²(62) = 159.89, p < .001, CFI = .93, RMSEA = .08, SRMR = .06. We then evaluated the revised model in Samples 2 and 3 without further modification. Model fit was comparable across both samples, although RMSEA remained somewhat elevated in Sample 2 (Sample 2: χ²(62) = 275.03, p < .001, CFI = .92, RMSEA = .09, SRMR = .06; Sample 3: χ²(62) = 372.85, p < .001, CFI = .92, RMSEA = .08, SRMR = .06).

Moderation of Cheap Talk by Antagonism Facets

To test whether cheap talk varies as a function of antagonistic traits, we extended our original Bayesian MSEM by adding latent between-person callousness, aggression, and exploitativeness factors as predictors of heterogeneity in the random slopes in the model, including the within-person cheap talk effect (the slope for the block × investor decision interaction). In Sample 1, consistent with our hypotheses, exploitativeness positively moderated the cheap talk effect (Figure 4). In contrast, aggression was associated with reduced cheap talk, and there was no effect of callousness (see Table S9). This pattern of findings replicated in Sample 3, where exploitativeness was again positively associated with cheap talk, aggression was negatively associated, and callousness showed no association. Follow-up models allowing these trait effects to vary by investor reciprocity indicated that neither the positive association with Exploitativeness nor the negative association with Aggression differed across investor types in Samples 1 and 3 (Table S10).

Effects were weaker in Sample 2. For exploitativeness, 85.1% of the posterior distribution lay above zero, falling short of the pre-specified 95% criterion, and providing only weak evidence for a positive association. Aggression did not predict individual differences in cheap talk in Sample 2, whereas callousness negatively moderated the cheap talk effect. This implies that participants at the lower pole of callousness -who were more empathic and compassionate -showed an increase in cooperative signaling from the Investment-Blind to the Investment-Aware block on investor-keep trials.

In follow-up analyses, we examined whether associations between antagonistic traits and cheap talk varied by investor reciprocity. No significant moderation effects emerged in Sample 1. In Sample 2, higher Callousness was associated with relatively less cheap talk with high-and low-reciprocating investors compared with medium-reciprocating investors, although neither investor-type contrast was significant overall. In Sample 3, higher Exploitativeness attenuated the greater cheap talk observed with high-relative to medium-reciprocating investors (Table S10). Thus, evidence for moderation by investor reciprocity was inconsistent across samples.

In sensitivity analyses probing the attenuated effect of exploitativeness on cheap talk behavior in Sample 2, we uncovered two contributing factors. First, greater statistical suppression by aggression in Sample 2 appeared to obscure the effect of exploitativeness. Second, there were 41 participants who did not perceive the merchants as credible human co-players (i.e., mean credibility rating of 0 across merchants), and the presence of these participants further attenuated the association.

When we excluded these 41 participants and removed aggression from the model, exploitativeness significantly predicted cheap talk behavior in Sample 2 ( = 0.14, 95% β CI [0.01, 0.27], p = 0.02).

Antagonism Facets and Strategic Cheap Talk in the Combined Sample

Finally, we pooled data from all three samples to test whether antagonism facets moderated cheap talk while accounting for sample differences. Before estimating structural effects, we examined measurement invariance of the three-factor antagonism model across Samples 1, 2, and 3 using multigroup confirmatory factor analysis (N = 1,533). The unconstrained confirmatory factor analysis for our 3-factor latent trait model (i.e., configural model) demonstrated acceptable fit ( 2 (186) = 824.83, p < 0.001, CFI = χ 0.92, RMSEA = 0.08, SRMR = 0.06). Successively constraining factor loadings, indicator intercepts, and residual variances to equality produced no substantively meaningful decrement in fit based on established guidelines for invariance testing (https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?v8lbQ0 Chen, 2007). Thus, the model supported strict measurement invariance (see Table S11), indicating equivalent measurement of antagonism facets across samples.

Having established measurement invariance, we estimated a Bayesian MSEM in the combined dataset (N = 1,568; 264,092 trials). Sample was represented by two between-person dummy-coded covariates, and the model retained the progression controls used in the sample-specific analyses. The block × investor decision interaction remained positive, providing robust evidence of cheap talk even after controlling for sample (β = .22, p < .001, 95% CI [.21, .23]). Exploitativeness positively moderated cheap talk (β = .18, p < .001, 95% CI [.09, .27]), whereas aggression negatively moderated cheap talk (β = -.15, p < .001, 95% CI [-.23, -.06]). There was weak evidence for callousness negatively moderating cheap talk, though the credible interval included zero (β = -.05, p = .07, 95% CI [-.13, .02]).

Discussion

Our findings suggest that cooperation may reflect not only altruistic motives but also strategic efforts to signal one's cooperativeness to others. Across three independent samples, participants selectively increased their willingness to cooperate when doing so carried no monetary cost but could plausibly influence their partner's future behavior. Although return behavior varied with investor reciprocity, the magnitude of cheap talk did not reliably differ across investor types. Moreover, cheap talk increased both within and across investor interactions, consistent with learning.

Together, these findings challenge the assumption that cooperative behavior necessarily reflects prosocial preferences, instead demonstrating that individuals can learn to express cooperativeness strategically to influence others.

Our findings complement evolutionary and economic accounts of cooperation as a conditional and forward-looking strategy (McNally et al., 2012; McNally & Jackson, 2013; Thöni & Volk, 2018). However, most experimental paradigms confound strategic social signaling with reinforcement history or intrinsic prosocial motivation. By introducing situations in which cooperative signals were both cost-free and nonbinding (i.e., they did not require participants to actually return an investment), our experiment isolated a behavioral signature that is difficult to explain in terms of payoff maximization or learned value alone. Participants selectively increased cooperative signaling only in contexts where doing so could plausibly affect their partner's future decisions -a pattern consistent with strategic social signaling rather than a stable preference for cooperation. Conceptually, this resembles game-theoretic cheap talk: cost-free, nonbinding communication that can nevertheless influence another player's beliefs or behavior (Farrell & Rabin, 1996). The key difference is that in our task, the signal was behavioral rather than verbal: participants indicated that they would have returned an investment even after the merchant had already kept. Thus, unlike ordinary reciprocal cooperation, cheap talk is valuable only insofar as it alters the counterpart's expectations or future choices.

We found that strategic cooperation varied across facets of antagonism. In line with our hypotheses, exploitativeness positively predicted cheap talk in Samples 1 and 3 and in the combined dataset, although evidence was weaker in Sample 2. This pattern is consistent with prior work linking exploitativeness to enhanced social-cognitive abilities, including theory of mind and social learning, and supports a view of exploitativeness as reflecting a capacity to use social information and social behavior instrumentally (Allen et al., 2017 (Allen et al., , 2024)). From this perspective, cooperation may serve as one of several tools through which exploitative individuals shape the behavior of others to advance their own interests.

Conversely, aggression showed a relatively consistent negative association with cheap talk, predicting reduced cooperative signaling in Samples 1 and 3 and in the combined dataset, although not in Sample 2. This contrast with exploitativeness suggests that these traits may reflect distinct strategies for navigating social interactions. Whereas exploitative individuals may use cooperative signals instrumentally to encourage favorable treatment, aggressive individuals may be more inclined to respond antagonistically to perceived noncooperation rather than attempting to cultivate future cooperation. Evidence for callousness was considerably weaker.

Callousness predicted reduced cheap talk in Sample 2 but not in Samples 1 or 3, and its negative association in the combined dataset was small and uncertain. Importantly, cooperative signaling need not always reflect selfish intentions; it may also serve prosocial goals, such as encouraging others to cooperate and establishing mutually beneficial relationships. Greater cheap talk among less aggressive individuals may reflect a preference for cooperative rather than conflict-oriented strategies for influencing others. Whether a similar tendency characterizes more empathic and compassionate individuals remains uncertain given the inconsistent findings for callousness.

Broadly, our findings clarify why exploitativeness may predict cheap talk rather than mere reciprocity. In our prior work, exploitative individuals showed stronger default network learning signals and greater behavioral reciprocity, suggesting enhanced sensitivity to a counterpart's cooperation history (Allen et al., 2024). However, that pattern could be explained without invoking a representation of the counterpart's mind: exploitative individuals may simply have learned the counterpart's return rate more efficiently and adjusted their policy based on that rate alone.

The present results make this interpretation less plausible. On trials in which the investor kept the endowment, returning could not improve the participant's immediate payoff and yielded no new information about the investor's return rate. Instead, the sensitivity of this behavior to investor reciprocity points to a communicative function: it could lead the reciprocating investor to believe that the participant would return a future investment. Thus, cheap talk depends not merely on learning what the partner tends to do, but on representing how one's own signal may alter the partner's beliefs, expectations, or future choices. In this sense, cheap talk provides a bridge between prior findings linking exploitativeness to social reinforcement learning and theory of mind, respectively (Allen et al., 2017 (Allen et al., , 2024)).

The primary methodological contribution of this study is isolating the signaling component of cooperative behavior. By manipulating the cost and long-term return on cooperation, our task differentiates cooperation motivated by immediate incentives from that intended to influence others. This distinction matters because many cooperative acts are behaviorally identical but psychologically different: the same choice may reflect direct payoff maximization, prosocial motivation, or an attempt to shape a partner's future behavior.

Several limitations should be noted. First, although participants were led to believe they were interacting with real partners, the use of simulated agents introduces uncertainty about how strongly participants engaged with the social aspects of the task.

Second, our measure of cheap talk necessarily infers strategic intent from patterns of behavior rather than assessing it explicitly. As such, our design cannot determine whether cheap talk was motivated by the monetary value of influencing the partner, by a desire to appear cooperative to the partner or oneself, or by both. Third, the assessment of antagonism relied on self-report measures, which is subject to rater biases and may not fully capture the trait's behavioral manifestations.

In sum, the present study demonstrates that cooperation is sometimes used strategically to influence others, and that this tendency constitutes one of the behavioral manifestations of trait exploitativeness. Our findings characterize cooperation as a flexible social tool that can be deployed in the service of diverse goals. More broadly, our findings suggest that the social-cognitive capacities that support human cooperation also allow cooperation to be used as a means of influence, and that individuals with greater social-cognitive ability may be most likely to use it this way.
