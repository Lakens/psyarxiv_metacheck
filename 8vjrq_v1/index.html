To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 3 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

p = .0945 Children of mothers with higher anxiety symptoms had a nonsignificant trend to greater exposure to fear talk in the toddler sample (r = .255, p = .0945).
p>.3 No associations were observed in the infant or child samples (all p>.3).

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

The everyday ecology of children's emotional experience Emotional experiences are fundamental inputs to human development, yet we know surprisingly little about their distribution in everyday life. Research typically characterizes children's emotional environments using global reports or brief observations, obscuring the real-time contexts in which emotions arise. Here, we used artificial intelligence to automatically transcribe and annotate daylong audio recordings from three samples totaling N=159 families with children aged 5 months-8 years old (48.43% female). Our findings reveal the architecture of children's everyday emotional environments: positive speech was concentrated during play and music, and 25% of speech segments conveyed frustration, concentrated during routine caregiving. Mothers' mental health did not predict affective speech, but lower child effortful control predicted more frustration in our toddler sample. Frustration peaked in early toddlerhood, suggesting that children's growing independence at this age may reorganize family emotional environments. Overall, emotional speech appears to emerge from everyday activity, with meaningful shifts across development.

Children's exposures to everyday emotions -including emotion words and emotional statements and expressions by caregivers, siblings, or peers -are thought to shape the foundations of lifelong mental health and wellbeing (LoBue & Ogren, 2022). However, we know surprisingly little about the everyday processes by which these experiences arise. Theories of social emotional development state that the household emotional climate, including parents' expression of emotional states and their ability to manage their emotions to provide loving and supportive care -provides learning inputs for children's own abilities to regulate, key to adaptive responding to challenges throughout development (Morris et al., 2017; Sroufe et al., 2009). One hypothesized mechanism by which family context can affect children's mental health is through repeated exposures to expressed emotions in everyday home environments (Morris et al., 2007). For example, maternal anxiety has been associated with greater infant attention bias toward angry faces (Morales et al., 2017), whereas infants of mothers with depressive symptoms looked longer at happy than sad expressions, potentially reflecting greater familiarity with sadness (Hernandez-Reif et al., 2006).

Children's emotional exposure could arise through multiple pathways. Emotion exposure may reflect relatively stable characteristics of family members -such as mothers' mental health or children's own temperament. Emotional exposure might also arise from more dynamic features of everyday activities in which interactions unfold (Tamis-LeMonda & Masek, 2023). Understanding the contributions of these different influences speaks to a central question about human behavior: to what extent do our experiences reflect more or less enduring dispositional traits, and how much do they dynamically fluctuate in response to the situations around us? However, most of what we know about children's emotional environments uses parent reports or structured observations which cannot fully capture the processes that lead to emotional exposure. Gold-standard video assessments provide unparalleled access to multimodal parent-child activity in high definition (Adolph, 2020). Yet, because these assessments involve recording children and their parents for brief periods of time in researcher-structured circumstances, they cannot capture many of the everyday activities from which emotions may arise in real-world interactions. Since emotions are inherently subjective, surveys that evaluate individuals' own experiences are arguably one of the most meaningful ways to assess emotions (Teoh et al., 2023). However, traditional surveys are not suited for capturing within-person variation in emotions over time (Shiffman et al., 2008). Repeated sampling approaches, such as ecological momentary assessments, reveal that emotions shift dynamically throughout the day (Mehl et al., 2024; Reitsema et al., 2022). Nevertheless, this type of assessment can only provide sporadic insight into emotional experiences and may therefore miss much of the richness of everyday emotional life, including fleeting but potentially important moments of affect. Overall, past work in this area rarely captures the volume of highresolution data required to examine how emotions emerge through everyday activities and interactions.

Mobile and wearable sensors offer new opportunities to capture these processes in real time. In the last decade, researchers have increasingly leveraged child-worn sensors to capture long stretches of children's everyday experiences in unconstrained, real-world settings (de Barbaro, 2019; Fausey et al., 2016; Franchak et al., 2024; Laudańska et al., 2025). In such studies, children may wear, for example, a lightweight audio recorder or head-mounted camera to capture what is in view -or earshot -of the child across the day, week, or month in real world settings.

Two related insights from this emerging field inform the current work. First, expansive time, space, and responsibilities at home can change the ways that caregivers interact with their children at home relative to brief structured assessments (de Barbaro & Fausey, 2022). For example, mothers are known to speak more to children in structured interactions than at home (Tamis-LeMonda et al., 2017), where they move more freely in and out of shared attention and proximity with their child over the day. Structured assessments can also bias children's emotional environments. For example, up to 70% of infants do not show negative affect within free play sessions commonly used to study parent-child interactions (Leerkes et al., 2009; McElwain & Booth-LaForce, 2006). Similarly, maternal negative affect is rare during free play, occurring less than 1% of the time in one study (Somers & Luecken, 2022). In everyday settings, parents may sit down and play with their children, but they must also ensure that their child is dressed, fed, and ready for the day, while simultaneously caring for themselves and potentially other children. These caregiving activities may challenge or frustrate children and their caregivers as they may require children to interrupt preferred activities, tolerate limits or delays, and comply with caregiver demands. Second, parent and child behavior is dynamic and emerges in real time through everyday interactions. For example, while most developmental research highlights the importance of between-person differences in language use to children's language development (Anderson et al., 2021), parents do not speak uniformly to their children across the day: rather, there are periods of time that there is lots of speech and other periods where there is less (Abney et al., 2017; Warlaumont et al., 2022; see also Abney et al., 2025). High-density sensor data can reveal the moment-to-moment predictors of speech within-person: by synchronizing audio recordings with cell phone screen logs, Mikhelson et al. (2024) recently revealed that periods of mothers' phone use at home were associated with 16% fewer words spoken around the child, relative to periods when the same mother was not on the phone. Similarly, wearable audio recordings revealed that across the day, maternal sensitivity was highly variable within person, and that mothers were less sensitive during high-intensity emotional episodes, such as tantrumming siblings, relative to calmer occasions in the same families (de Barbaro & Madden-Rusnak, 2026). Such findings highlight the potential of longform wearable sensors to capture the dynamic within-person processes by which parent-child interactions -including everyday affect expressions -unfold at home.

Drawing from ecological frameworks of family emotional development, children's emotional exposures should also be shaped by more stable between-person regularities in children's and caregivers' characteristics and behavior (Morris et al., 2007). Children actively shape the flow of the interactions around them, including by eliciting feedback from caregivers. Stable individual differences in children's temperament, or propensities for particular patterns of affect, attention and action, may therefore contribute to the emotional environments they experience. For example, high levels of child negative affectivity, as well as low levels of effortful control, i.e. difficulties with regulating behavior and emotions, have been linked with greater parenting stress (Jańczak et al., 2025; Oddi et al., 2013). Additionally, both child negative affectivity and surgency, i.e. high activity, impulsivity and approach behaviors, have been associated with greater parental distress, particularly in response to child negative emotion (Fields et al., 2017). Individual differences in parent mental health may also predict regularities in children's emotional exposures. In structured interactions, depressed mothers exhibit more negative and less positive affect, are more withdrawn, and can be more irritable or prone to anger and conflict (Aktar & Bögels, 2017; Lovejoy et al., 2000). Maternal anxiety is associated with more threat-focused parenting, including greater attention to danger and more fear-or threat-related communication in ambiguous or potentially threatening situations (Muris et al., 2010). Together, these findings suggest that maternal mental health may systematically shape the emotional content of children's everyday interactions.

Finally, children's emotional exposure may be distinct across developmental time periods. In infancy, the onset of independent locomotion around the end of the first year of life is thought to organize substantial transitions in children's communication and social-emotional experience (Campos et al., 2000; Karasik et al., 2014; Schneider & Iverson, 2022). As children begin walking and crawling, they gain novel possibilities to explore the world on their own, including access to sources of potential danger or harm. In turn, parents report expressing more anger at their infants and being more likely to scold their infants or make demands of them, relative to parents of children who do not yet independently locomote (Campos et al., 2000). We know less about the affective environments of older children. However, across early to middle childhood, children's executive functions, including impulsivity and inhibitory control, show meaningful improvement paralleling gains in emotion understanding and regulation as well as prosocial skills (Liu et al., 2026; Stucke & Doebel, 2024). Parents report that parenting stress is highest during the ages 2-3 and begins to decrease by age 4 (E. J. Kim et al., 2021), with some data suggesting that decreases in behavior problems across this period drive this shift (Neece et al., 2012). Overall, these findings suggest that children's emotional environments are likely to shift with development as children's behavior -and parents' responses to that behavior-change over time.

Recent breakthroughs in artificial intelligence paired with longform child-worn audio recordings mean that we can now obtain unprecedented access into children's realworld emotional environments, including markers of the everyday activities from which individual emotional expressions arise in the moment. Advances in automated transcription now allow speech within daylong audio recordings to be reliably transcribed at scale (Bain et al., 2023; Radford et al., 2023). And large-language models are increasingly being used to annotate text -including natural speech-for various dimensions of content with high reliability, even for off-the-shelf models (Gilardi et al., 2023; Rathje et al., 2024).

The current study leverages these tools to characterize the emotional content of speech produced by and around children in everyday home environments, regardless of speaker identity or whether speech is directed towards the child. We examine how children's exposure to different emotions is distributed across the day, and the extent to which children's emotional exposures are predicted by relatively stable characteristics of parents and their children versus emerging dynamically in the moment from activity contexts.

We had three main hypotheses: 1) Children's emotional exposures will dynamically fluctuate across the day and be predicted by ongoing everyday activities in real time.

2) Children high in negative affectivity and surgency, and those low in effortful control/regulation will experience higher rates of negative affect in everyday speech. 3) Children of mothers with high anxiety symptoms will experience higher rates of fear-talk in everyday interactions and children of mothers with high depression symptoms will experience higher rates of sad-talk in everyday speech.

By examining these questions in three cross-sectional datasets ranging from infancy to middle childhood, we also observed the extent to which the organization of children's emotional environments differs across the first 8 years of life.

Results

Descriptives

We analyzed full-day child-worn LENA audio recordings, collected in everyday realworld environments, from 159 participants across three comparable samples, with participants aged 5-11 months ("infants", n=70), 6 months -3.5 years (92.2% over 12 months old; "toddlers", n=51), and 3-8 years ("children", n=38; see also SM1). Speech content present in each participants' audio recording was transcribed by WhisperX (Bain et al., 2023; Radford et al., 2023) and segmented into 30-second bins, which were then automatically annotated using GPT4.1mini (OpenAI, 2025) for everyday household activities and five categories of emotion, including enjoyment, frustration, anger, fear and sadness. To protect participant privacy, we used only secure enterprise software or software that could be run on secure servers managed by UT Austin.

LENA audio recording duration totaled 3196.66 hours, with a mean of 20.10 hours recorded per participating family (SD = 6.66; median = 24.00; range = 1.24-24.04 hours), and 70.44% (112/159) of recordings over 21 hours (see SM2). After segmenting and transcription, we identified 167,742 distinct 30-second bins containing speech across the 159 participants, covering 43.73% of the total recording duration. Considering all segments containing speech, affect was detected in 40.36% of bins and activity was detected in 29.38% of bins (see SM3 for sample specific statistics).

Over 25% of everyday speech segments contained frustrated speech

Across the 167,742 30-second bins containing speech collected from all 159 children, frustration was the emotion most commonly detected in everyday speech, occurring in 25.65% bins, followed by enjoyment (12.66%). Sadness, fear, and anger each appeared in fewer than 1.5% of coded bins. This pattern replicated across our three age groups, with frustrated speech detected in 22.19% of coded bins in the infant sample, 28.68% in the toddler sample, and 26.96% in the child sample, followed by enjoyment (detected in 14.23%, 9.96%, and 14.44% of speech bins, respectively). Frustration was also the most commonly detected emotion in 139 of 159 (87.42%) participants (IQR = 20.07-30.20%).

Caregiving routines (7.60%) and play (6.88%) were the two most commonly detected activities in our recordings, occurring at similar rates in the combined sample, with other activities, including mealtimes (4.65%), music/singing (1.8%), and media (1.7%) occurring less frequently in speech bins. This overall pattern of detected activity was similar across samples. Complete statistics on affect and activity rates for each sample can be found in the supplemental materials (SM4). Expressed emotions are dynamically organized with ongoing activities

Across all samples, emotional speech was highly associated with activity context. First, we observed that rates of positive and negative affect differed across activity contexts (Figure 2). Paired family-level t-tests revealed significant differences in the rates of emotional speech co-occurring with each activity: among bins where play was detected, positive affect was significantly more likely than negative affect in speech (41.2% vs. 22.8%). Positive affect was also more likely than negative affect during periods of music/singing (42.1% vs. 11.9%). By contrast, negative affect was significantly more likely than positive affect during caregiving routines (41.7% vs. 15.0%) as well as in mealtimes, media, and sleep (see SM5).

Betweenfamily differences in the rate of caregiving routines were unrelated to overall rates of frustration. We also discovered positive within-child associations between music/singing and enjoyment in all three samples, and lower within-child odds of frustration during play (see SM6).

Individual differences in everyday speech exposure

We found relatively few correlations between emotional speech and parent and child characteristics (SM7). Rates of negative affect in speech were not associated with child negative affectivity or surgency in any of our three samples. However, in our toddler sample, children with lower regulation scores were exposed to more negative emotional speech (r = -.407, p = .0229). Given that frustration accounted for the majority of negative affect, and was particularly prevalent during routine caregiving activities, we conducted an exploratory analysis examining child regulation specifically in relation to frustrated speech. Toddlers with lower regulation were exposed to significantly more frustrated speech (r = -.428, p = .0164), consistent with the possibility that children's emerging regulatory capacities help shape the emotional interactions occurring around them. However, the broader negative-affect association was not significant for toddlers following the Benjamini-Hochberg corrections for nine tested correlations (BH q = .206) and was not observed in our infant or child samples. The frustration-specific finding was exploratory and requires independent replication.

Next, we examined several hypothesized correlations between affective speech and maternal mental health. Maternal depression was unrelated to sad speech in all samples (all p>.05). Children of mothers with higher anxiety symptoms had a nonsignificant trend to greater exposure to fear talk in the toddler sample (r = .255, p = .0945). No associations were observed in the infant or child samples (all p>.3).

Differences across samples are suggestive of meaningful developmental trends

Frustrated speech was significantly lower in the infant sample (22.01% of speech bins) than the toddler and child samples (28.87% and 26.14%, respectively). There was no significant difference in the rate of frustrated speech between our toddler and child samples. To further examine the relationship between age and frustration, we fit a natural cubic spline to our combined N=159 dataset, finding that the peak of frustrated speech occurred in early toddlerhood, reaching its maximum at approximately 27 months of age (see Figure 4). We found no significant differences in the rates of anger or sadness between our samples. However, fear-coded bins were significantly more frequent in the child sample (1.62%) than in the infant (1.00%) and toddler samples (1.08%).

Next, we found significantly higher rates of play detected in the child sample (12.08%) relative to play in the infant and toddler samples (5.21% and 5.05%, respectively). Rates of enjoyment, however, were matched between the infant and the child samples (13.88% and 14.25%, respectively), which both had significantly higher enjoyment than the toddler sample (9.74%). Finally, media and reading were more frequent in the child sample relative to infant and toddler samples, and speech about sleep was most common in the infant sample (see SM8 for full statistics).

Discussion

The current study examined everyday emotional speech in thousands of hours of childworn audio recordings drawn from 159 children spanning five months to 8 years of age.

Our findings reveal that affect is pervasive in everyday speech, and that children's emotional environments have a rich ecological structure. Different emotions have vastly different base rates of expression, with frustration and enjoyment by far the most common in everyday home interactions. We found limited associations between children's emotional environments and relatively stable characteristics of family members, alongside strong evidence that emotional expression was tied to families' activity in the moment. These patterns were broadly similar from infancy to school age, speaking to their generalization across the wide variation in developmental stages. At the same time, we observed key areas of distinction between samples, suggesting children inhabit distinct emotional ecologies across ages.

Approximately 18% of all thirty-second recorded segments included a spoken expression of emotion, including both overnight periods and silence. In segments with speech, children experienced high levels of frustration (25.65% of bins) and enjoyment (12.66% of bins), with substantially fewer expressions of anger, sadness, and fear. These findings contrast with past work which has generally found that children's exposure to markers of emotion is relatively rare in real-world interactions (Hoemann et al., 2026; Ogren & Sandhofer, 2021), and that positive emotions are much more likely to be expressed than negative emotions in both structured and unstructured parent-child interactions (Jackson & Geangu, 2026; Somers & Luecken, 2022). Two main differences between our work and past studies are likely to account for these discrepancies.

First, past studies of real-world emotional exposure have examined the use of explicit emotion labels (e.g. happy, sad, frustrated; Nencheva et al., 2023; Ogren & Sandhofer, 2021), or facial expressions in view of infants (Hoemann et al., 2026; Jackson & Geangu, 2026; Palmer et al., 2026). However, neither parents nor children label each emotion that they express, meaning that emotion labels are likely an undercount of emotion expression in speech (Nencheva et al., 2023). Similarly, faces are not often in view of young children (Deak et al., 2014; Fausey et al., 2016) and thus observed facial expressions may simply not provide a high volume of emotional exposure for children. By leveraging a large language model to detect more subtle linguistic expressions of emotions in parent and child speech, we observed emotional content not captured by prior studies. More broadly, given that sound can be heard irrespective of where a child is looking, auditory signals may provide children more pervasive access to ongoing emotional information in their environments. Indeed, sounds -via words and/or tonesmay alert children to orient to faces at key moments, a behavioral pathway that may be critical for supporting learning about emotional facial expressions.

Second, many past studies of parent-child emotion draw from researcher-constrained recordings of dyadic interactions, including structured free play sessions. There is a long practice of capturing "unstructured" home interactions in developmental psychology, ranging from 1-2 hours to full day or overnight recordings (Ainsworth et al., 1978; Schuengel et al., 1999; Soska et al., 2021; J. Sullivan et al., 2021; Teti et al., 2010). However, a number of considerations are likely to affect the nature of emotional expression in such recordings. First, relative to recordings captured remotely, the presence of researchers can decrease the insensitivity of parent child interactions (Lee et al., 2017). Next, the absence of other family members may change interactions at home, including for example, decreasing sibling conflict, a common source of frustration and prohibitions. Finally, recordings may differ in the extent to which they capture routine caregiving activities, major contextual drivers of expressed frustration in our sample. Semi-structured observations of mealtimes, bedtimes, and clothing or diaper changes (Jian & Teti, 2016; Maas et al., 2013; Messina et al., 2020; Saltzman et al., 2018) indicate that these may provide developmentally meaningful access to variation in frustration, hostility, and parenting insensitivity more broadly. The emotional speech captured in our sample likely reflects a combination of these factors, highlighting the unique potential of daylong recordings of real-world interactions to capture a representative sample of everyday family activity.

Next, we observed that emotions were structured dynamically by ongoing activities occurring around the children. Enjoyment was more likely to appear during play and singing. Frustrated speech was much more likely to appear during mealtimes and other routine caregiving tasks including bathing, dressing and getting out of the house. In post-hoc analyses, we further determined that frustration often emerged during prohibitions or conflict between family members (SM9). Where much past work has examined emotional expression as a relatively stable characteristic of parents or children, this work indicates that everyday activities are a potent dynamic organizer of children's real-world emotional experiences. Our findings are more consistent with work leveraging high-density survey designs like EMA which document dynamic fluctuations in emotions across the day (Nencheva et al., 2026; Reitsema et al., 2022). However, EMA surveys require active response by participants, which means that even on the high end they are relatively sparse, ranging from 8-15 reports per day. By instead annotating emotional speech from continuous audio recordings, we were able to gain access to thousands of datapoints per day from each participant -approximately two orders of magnitude more data on emotional expression than EMA studies can provide. These high-density annotations provided access to repeated instances of emotional expression paired with ongoing activities at scale, allowing us to observe the dynamic structure by which emotions emerged over the course of the day.

The high volumes of frustration observed in our samples -with 75% of children experiencing frustration in at least one in five speech-containing bins-suggest that this is a relatively normative dimension of young children's emotional environments in North American families. The developmental implications of this will require longitudinal samples. However, while past work has linked parent hostility with worse child outcomes (Pinquart, 2017; Polcari et al., 2014), the pervasiveness of frustration in our sample means it is unlikely that frustration alone is an indication of maladaptive family dynamics. Critically, our annotations distinguished between speech containing frustration versus anger, where frustration included speech indicating annoyance, irritation or impatience, whereas anger codes required hostility, aggression or rejection. And although exposure to frustration was pervasive, exposure to anger was relatively rare: accounting for 0.15%-0.63% of bins for 75% of all families. Most broadly, these findings suggest that the context of observation may change the behaviors we observe: in relatively brief, "minimally" structured interactions where researchers are present, even behaviors that are relatively common at home may be substantially less likely to surface. This may be particularly relevant for studies of emotion or social emotional development, where caregivers' responses to challenging or distressful situations are often more predictive of attachment security and childhood outcomes than their responses in neutral contexts (Leerkes et al., 2009; McElwain & Booth-LaForce, 2006).

Third, several findings were suggestive of developmental effects. First, we observed the least amount of frustrated speech in our infant sample, with significantly higher frustration in both our toddler and child samples, appearing to peak during toddlerhood. This is consistent with past work linking the onset of infant locomotion to increases in parental frustration and prohibitions in self-report and structured observations (Biringen et al., 1995; Campos et al., 1992; Zumbahlen & Crawley, 1996). However, to our knowledge this is the first time that large-scale daylong recordings have been used to demonstrate an increase in frustration with toddlers relative to infants. Interestingly, we also observed relatively high amounts of enjoyment in our infant sample, suggesting something of a "honeymoon" period at this age. By contrast, enjoyment was lowest in our toddler sample before rebounding in the child sample. Broadly, these findings are consistent with the idea that children's growing independence, activity, self-regulation and family responsibilities change, so do the distributions of emotions that children encounter (Campos et al., 2000). However, given that our three samples were collected independently and age and sample were confounded, the possibility remains that these differences reflect sampling differences rather than age differences per se.

Finally, relatively stable characteristics of mothers and children made more limited contributions to children's emotional environments. In our toddler sample only, lower effortful control predicted a higher rate of frustrated speech. Frustrated speech often contained prohibitions, likely from caregivers (SM9). However, following replication in other samples, future work should attempt to distinguish different potential sources of this speech -including from caregivers, the child's own speech, or the speech of other nearby children -to pinpoint how and why these increases in frustrated speech may emerge at this age. The association between regulation and frustrated speech did not generalize to infants or older children. However, heightened issues around compliance in toddlerhood may highlight individual differences in self-regulation. These issues may be less salient in infancy, where autonomy is still limited, and with older children, as they gain communication skills and autonomy, for example. Consistent with this, survey research has documented challenges with child compliance and tantrumming typically begin around 12 months, peaking between 18-24 months (Potegal & Davidson, 2003; M. W. Sullivan & Lewis, 2012).

We saw no significant relationships between mothers' anxiety or depression symptoms and children's affective environments. Future efforts to isolate maternal speech input in the recording may reveal additional associations. For example, our human coders identified that media was a common source of fear talk in many families, likely contributing noise to associations with maternal mental health. We did not attempt to distinguish sources of emotional speech input in the current work, as media can and should be considered a valid source of children's emotional input in real-world environments. However, examining the emotional content of maternal speech may be particularly useful when considering mothers speech input as a potential behavioral pathway of intergenerational transmission of depression and anxiety symptoms (LoBue & Ogren, 2022).

Limitations.

We acknowledge several limitations. First, we used transcripts from child-worn audio recordings to characterize children's emotional exposures, meaning emotions expressed through vocal tone, facial expression, or other bodily signals were not captured by our approach. Recent work confirms that speech content conveys much meaningful information about affect (Koch et al., 2026; Wolf et al., 2023). However, multimodal emotional signals may change the meaning of speech content, for example when someone speaks sarcastically (Yadavalli et al., 2026). Similarly, while speech often accompanies activity, some activities are done silently, and other times, speech about a given activity does not signify that that an activity is occurring in the moment. As such our activities should be interpreted as not referring to observed activities per se but rather speech suggesting the probability of an activity.

More broadly, while AI transcription and annotation have substantially improved in recent years, AI recall rates were not high across all emotion categories and human-AI reliability was somewhat lower than gold-standard human-human annotation (Kappa = .8; Bakeman & Gottman, 1997) for some annotations, although not out of the range of acceptability. While there are well-known accuracy-scale tradeoffs presented by all automated models (de Barbaro et al., 2026), improvements in AI models may increase annotation reliability, providing more accurate data.

Next, our analyses do not account for who was expressing detected affect. As such, expressed emotions combine those expressed by parents or other caregivers with those expressed by the target child and any other children or other individuals in range of the LENA audio recordings. Future work should attempt to distinguish these different potential sources of children's emotional inputs.

Finally, while leveraging data from three samples allowed us to examine generalization of emotional environments and their organization across developmental periods, the fact that our samples were recruited independently means that sample is confounded with age change and we do not have clean age comparisons. We note that all data was collected in Austin, and sample demographics across the three groups was largely similar, although education and income were highest for our oldest sample, which was also whiter than our other samples. As such, observed distinctions across ages should be examined in additional samples that can better control for such differences in future work.

Conclusions

Together, our findings reveal that children's everyday emotional environments are systematically organized around day-to-day family activities. Frustration was a common feature of everyday speech, particularly during caregiving routines, while positive emotional speech was concentrated during play and music. Differences across developmental samples further suggest that these environments reflect the changing demands of childhood. By documenting emotional exposures as they unfold in realworld settings, this work provides a foundation for understanding how everyday emotional experiences contribute to children's development.

Method

Participants

The current study included N= 159 total children aged 5 months to 8 years, drawn from three independent samples each including child-worn audio recordings, child temperament and parent mental health measures. Our infant sample was collected between 2025-2026 and included N=70 families from the BABYConnect Study, an ongoing longitudinal study with 24-hour audio recordings with infants aged 5-11 months (M=7.6, SD=1.0). Our toddler sample was collected beginning in 2022 with the ongoing Whole Communities Whole Health Project and included N=51 families with a child aged between 7 months to 3.5 years old (M= 2.0, SD=0.8; 47/51 or 92.2% of target children were over 12 months old). Our child sample was collected from 2024-2026 as part of the Caregiver-Child Dynamics and Socioemotional Development Study and included N=38 families with a child aged 3-8 years old (M=5.6, SD=1.3).

All study procedures were approved by the Institutional Review Board at the University of Texas at Austin. Written informed consent was obtained from the parents or legal guardians of all participating children.

Demographics and eligibility criteria were unique to each sample. Full demographic information is available in Supplemental Materials SM1.

Infant Sample (BABYConnect; IRB#: STUDY00005017). Infant sample participants (n=70) were drawn from the BABYConnect (Building Bonds with Your Baby through Everyday Connections) project, an ongoing longitudinal study following 140 mothers from the third trimester to 18 months postpartum. Mothers were recruited during pregnancy from the Austin metropolitan area from a maternal-fetal medicine clinic and Facebook advertisements. Eligibility criteria included planning to speak Spanish, English, or both languages to their children; not being pregnant with multiples; and the infant being expected to be home without medical assistance by 6 months postpartum.

All participating families who shared LENA audio by July 31, 2026 were included in the current analyses. Infants in this sample ranged from 5.0-11.1 months old (M= 7.6, SD=1.0), with most being 6-8 months old, see age histogram S1); 42.9% were female. Mothers were predominantly non-Hispanic White (52.2%) and Hispanic (35.8%) with the remainder being Black (6%), Asian (3%), or multiracial (3%). 79.1% of included mothers primarily spoke English and 20.9% spoke Spanish. The sample had a diversity of income levels, with 29.8% reporting earning less than $50,000 a year, 19.4% between $50,000 and $100,000, 25.4% between $100,000 and $200,000 and 25.4% greater than $200,000. Mothers ranged in age from 20 to 48 (mean = 33).

Based on reported annual household-income categories (n = 42), 31.0% reported incomes below $50,000, 11.9% reported $50,000-$99,999, 47.6% reported $100,000-$199,999, and 9.5% reported $200,000 or more. Procedure

Protocols varied across samples, however in all samples families were instructed to collect and share a 24-hour LENA (Language ENvironment Analysis) home audio recording and to complete survey measures including family demographics, child temperament, and maternal depression and anxiety symptom questionnaires. As surveys were not necessarily completed at the same time as the LENA recordings (detailed below), we report child age at the time of the LENA recording for each sample.

Infant Sample.

In the BABYConnect study, LENA audio recordings were collected following a home visit occurring when infants were approximately 6-8 months of age. Mothers completed questionnaires as part of the longitudinal study, including demographic characteristics (57 prenatally, 13 postnatally, 0.4-5.2 months, M = 1.7 month of age), mental health symptoms (5.78-10.41 months of age, M = 7.64), and infant temperament (.5-7.3 months of age, M = 2.41). The mean time between the LENA recording and child temperament measure was 5.69 months (SD = 1.71, range = 0.95-9.43; n = 50). Two children were excluded from the temperament analyses because they completed their assessments very early in infancy, at 10 and 14 days after birth. The mean time between the LENA recording and maternal depression and anxiety symptom surveys was 0.22 months (SD = 0.28, range = 0-1.74; n = 65).

Toddler Sample.

In the WCWH longitudinal cohort study, families collected waves of data for up to five years during their participation in the study. These data included daylong LENA audio recordings twice per year and surveys relating to parent and child characteristics, including child temperament and maternal mental health, also occurring twice per year. While surveys and LENA recordings were both collected at biannual assessment waves, they were not completed concurrently. For the current study, for each child we selected the longest recording. Then, we matched the LENA recording to the temperament and mental health measurement completed closest to the recording date. We prioritized measurements within 6 months before or after the recording date. If no questionnaire was collected within this time window, we selected the closest available assessment. The mean time between the LENA recording and child temperament measure was 7.20 months (SD = 5.36, range = 0.72 -21.55, n = 32, range). The corresponding intervals for maternal depression and anxiety symptom assessments were 5.80 months (SD = 4.53, range = 1.05-21.03; n = 46) and 5.83 months (SD = 4.58, range = 1.05-21.03; n = 45), respectively.

Child sample.

In the CDSD study, caregiver-child dyads participated in laboratorybased longitudinal study with 4 waves, each 6 months apart. At wave 1, demographic information, standardized measures of maternal mental health and child temperament were collected online a few days prior to an in-person laboratory visit. Families received the LENA audio recorder during the laboratory visit and had two weeks to complete their recording. The mean time between the LENA recording and child temperament measure was 0.75 months (SD = 0.70, range = 0.10-3.58; n = 31) for the child temperament analysis and 0.73 months (SD = 0.65, range = 0.10-3.58; n = 38) for both the maternal depression and anxiety symptom analyses.

Naturalistic Audio Data Collection

Across the three samples, all participating children wore a LENA recording device during one full day in their natural home environments. Caregivers were provided the same recording instructions across all three samples. Specifically, caregivers were instructed to turn on the recorder within one hour of waking up and keep it on throughout the recording period, allowing children to engage in their usual activities throughout the day. The target recording duration was 24 hours per family. To protect participants' privacy and autonomy, families were also instructed that they could pause or stop the recorder at any time for any amount of time, and that they could choose to delete all or any part of the recording after they had completed recordings.

The recorder was worn by children in a pocket on a vest provided by the study team. Caregivers were allowed to remove the device as needed for activities in which wearing the recorder was not appropriate. After recording, the audio files were saved locally, uploaded to the LENA system for processing, and subsequently deleted from LENA storage in accordance with individual study IRB protocols.

The mean LENA recording duration was M = 18.29 hours, SD =7.71, range = 1.24-24.04 hours for our infant sample, M = 22.20 hours, SD = 4.57, range = 3.04-24.01 hours for our toddler sample, and M = 20.96 h, SD = 5.51 h, range = 6.73 -24 for our child sample.

Measures Maternal Anxiety and Depression Symptoms.

Each sample collected different measures of maternal mental health. For the infant sample, mothers were assessed for postpartum and anxiety symptoms with the Edinburg Postnatal Depression Scale (EPDS; Cox et al., 1996) and the GAD-7 (Spitzer et al., 2006). The EPDS is a 10question survey with 4-point Likert scales where higher scores indicated more severe symptoms. Participants were instructed to answer questions about their depressive symptoms in the past 7 days. Example items include "I have felt sad or miserable" and "I have blamed myself unnecessarily when things went wrong." The GAD-7 is a 7-item survey with 4-point Likert scales where higher scores indicated more frequent symptoms. Participants were instructed to answer questions about often they experienced anxiety symptoms over the last 2 weeks. Example items include "I have blamed myself unnecessarily when things went wrong" and "Worrying too much about different things." The EPDS (n = 62; Cronbach's α = .84, 95% CI [.76, .90]; McDonald's ω = .86) and GAD-7 (n = 65; α = .84, 95% CI [.74, .89]; ω = .84) demonstrated good internal consistency.

For the toddler sample, maternal anxiety and depression were assessed using the Mood and Anxiety Symptom Questionnaire (MASQ; Watson et al., 1995). The measure assessed symptoms related to anxiety and depression using 62 items rated on a 5-point Likert scale. Scores were calculated by summing the relevant MASQ-62 subscale items according to the standard scoring procedure, where higher scores indicated greater severity of anxiety and depression symptoms. Internal consistency was good to excellent across all four scales: General Distress-Anxiety (α = .87, 95% CI [.83, .89], ω = .87), Anxious Arousal (α = .87, 95% CI [.81, .91], ω = .88), General Distress-Depression (α = .94, 95% CI [.92, .95], ω = .94), and Anhedonic Depression (α = .92, 95% CI [.90, .93], ω = .92).

In the child sample, we assessed maternal anxiety using the trait anxiety items from the State-Trait Anxiety Inventory, Form Y (STAI-Y; Spielberger, 1983). The trait anxiety measure includes twenty items rated on a 4-point Likert scale. Higher scores indicate higher anxiety symptoms. STAI-T internal consistency was excellent (α = .92, 95% CI [.89, .95], ω = .92). Caregiver depressive symptoms were assessed using the Beck Depression Inventory-II (BDI-II; Beck et al., 1996). This 21-item measure asks to rate symptoms from 0 to 3, with higher scores indicating higher levels of depressive symptoms. Internal consistency demonstrated good reliability (α = .88, 95% CI [.83, .93], ω = .90).

Child temperament. Across all three samples, child temperament was assessed using the Infant Behavior Questionnaire-Very Short Form (IBQ; for all children under 12 months), the Early Child Behavior Questionnaire-Very Short Form (ECBQ; for all children 18-36 months of age) and Children's Behavior Questionnaire-Short Form (CBQ; for all children over 36 months), as per the age recommendations for each form. The IBQ (Putnam et al., 2014) is a 37-item measure of infant temperament. The ECBQ (Putnam et al., 2010) is a 36-item measure of child temperament. The CBQ (Putnam & Rothbart, 2006) is a 94-item measure of child temperament. All measures were used to calculate three parallel dimensions of temperament using the standard subscale calculations: Surgency/Extraversion, Negative Affectivity, and Effortful Control/Regulation. Higher scores indicate higher expression of the temperamental trait.

Internal consistency was acceptable to good across all three samples, except for ECBQ Negative Affectivity in the toddler sample, which showed relatively low reliability (α = .56, ω = .62). IBQ reliability estimates in the toddler sample were also less precise due to the small sample size, and McDonald's ω could not be reliably estimated (see SM10 for complete table ).

Speech Processing Pipeline

Transcription. Raw audio files from LENA were processed through a multi-step WhisperX (Bain et al., 2023; Radford et al., 2023) transcription pipeline. First, recordings were saved as WAV audio files and then divided into 5-second segments and transcribed using WhisperX version 3.7.4. The transcription pipeline included voice activity detection, automatic transcription, and time alignment. The resulting data included segment start time, segment end time, and transcribed text for each segment.

Transcripts were cleaned by removing or redacting personally identifiable and sensitive information, including names, locations, workplaces, and other real-world identifying details, and replacing such information with "***". To balance temporal precision with sufficient contextual information within each segment, 5-second segments were grouped to create 30-second segments. After processing, the final dataset included 167,742 valid 30-second transcript segments across 159 families: 70 in the infant sample, 51 in the toddler sample, and 38 in the child sample, which we combined into 30-second bins in order to provide our annotation model additional context for making emotion and activity judgements.

Transcription Accuracy. English-language transcription quality was evaluated against ground-truth human reference transcripts for 75 30-second segments randomly selected from five daylong recordings in the FauseyTrio HomeBank corpus (Fausey & Mendoza, 2018). The human transcription and annotation procedures used to produce these reference transcripts are described by Montag et al (2026). Median unigram precision was 82.8%, with median bigram, trigram, and 4-gram precision of 67.7%, 57.1%, and 47.6%, respectively, indicating substantial overlap between the words produced by WhisperX and those in the human reference texts. Recall was lower, with median unigram, bigram, trigram, and 4-gram recall of 54.5%, 40.0%, 34.8%, and 28.1%, respectively. Mean word error rate was 60.9%, and automated transcripts averaged 69.4% of reference length. Overall, this pattern of results indicates that while the automated transcription produced text that was generally reliable, it also systematically missed a portion of what was said on the recording, indicating non-trivial errors and omissions. Human review of segments with relatively low recall values indicated that this was often related to quieter speech or speech in the background of the recording.

Spanish-language transcription quality was evaluated against ground-truth humanreference transcripts for 150 segments 30-second segments. Segments were randomly selected from transcripts of nine Spanish speaking participants from the BABYConnect study. Two native Spanish speakers each transcribed the segments independently and then collaboratively conferenced their transcriptions to create the ground truth sample. Median unigram precision was 64.5%, with median bigram, trigram, and 4-gram precision of 37.8%, 20.0%, and 8.6%, respectively, indicating moderate overlap between the words produced by WhisperX and those in the human reference texts that declined sharply for longer word sequences. Recall was lower, with median unigram, bigram, trigram, and 4-gram recall of 45.9%, 26.7%, 14.7%, and 8.0%, respectively. Mean word error rate was 64.7%, and automated transcripts averaged 75.2% of reference length. Overall, this pattern of results indicates that Spanish-language transcription was somewhat lower quality than English-language transcription and that while the automated transcription captured many individual words it also missed or altered some portion of what was said, particularly in multiword sequences.

Automated Affect and Activity Detection

ChatGPT-based annotation was used to identify affect and activity from each transcript segment using UT Austin's Enterprise Technology, which has a variety of data protections, including limited data retention periods, local university administration, and by default not using inputs to train models. Combined with our PII removal pipeline, this allowed us to leverage ChatGPT functionality while also maintaining control of participant's data, protecting their privacy, and preventing their data from being used by for-profit companies. The model received the segmented transcript generated by the WhisperX transcription pipeline together with a structured prompt that included instructions for identifying affect and activity based on linguistic information in the transcript.

Dictionary-based methods, including for example LIWC (Tausczik & Pennebaker, 2010) and the lexicon-based tools in the syuzhet R package (Jockers, 2015; H. Kim, 2022), estimate affect by matching text to a list of predefined emotion or sentiment words. Bagof-words approaches similarly represent text through word occurrences or frequencies while disregarding their order (Manning et al., 2008). These approaches have difficulty distinguishing meanings that depend on context. For example, a dictionary that includes "hot" as a synonym for anger could misclassify a reference to "Hot Wheels" as emotional language. Contextual encoder models such as BERT represent an advance by interpreting words in relation to surrounding text, with classification typically accomplished through fine-tuning on labeled examples (Devlin et al., 2019). Instructionfollowing generative LLMs, such as those underlying ChatGPT, extend these capabilities by applying detailed coding instructions directly to passages and drawing on learned knowledge of situations and relationships to interpret implied meaning. Their value for emotional language analysis lies in identifying what speakers communicate through an exchange, including emotions that are never explicitly named.

Evidence supports both the interpretive capacity of these models and their effectiveness for large-scale annotation. GPT models have outperformed dictionary-based approaches in psychological text analysis and fine-tuned BERT-based classifiers on some implicit-emotion recognition tasks (Boutouta et al., 2025; Rathje et al., 2024).

When given the same coding instructions, ChatGPT also classified tweets and news articles more accurately than crowd workers-by approximately 25 percentage points on average across four datasets-and produced greater agreement across repeated annotations at substantially lower cost (Gilardi et al., 2023). This combination of contextual interpretation and scalable annotation is especially valuable for studying children's emotional language environments. A parent's statement, "Stop that-I've already told you five times to stop," can communicate frustration without containing an explicit emotion word. LLM-assisted analysis offers a way to capture these indirect expressions systematically across extensive recordings, expanding measurement from the emotional vocabulary children hear to the emotional meaning conveyed in everyday speech.

Model and Prompt details.

To obtain automated affect and activity annotations, we submitted each 30-second transcript segment to OpenAI's GPT-4.1 mini model using a Python script to automate API requests, accessing GPT through the university's secure enterprise AI service. For each segment, the prompt provided detailed instructions as to our coding scheme and instructed the model to consider explicit linguistic cues as well as the surrounding conversational context within each segment. When there was insufficient linguistic or contextual evidence to make a reliable classification, the model was instructed to assign an NA label. The model was also instructed to provide an evidence note documenting the basis for the classification or the lack of sufficient evidence. We share our script, including complete coding prompts, derived from a publicly available script by Rathje et al. (2024) in SM 13.

Human Annotation and Validation.

To assess the reliability of automated GPT annotations, we trained human research assistants on both activity and affect coding schemes. Following training, subsets of transcript segments were selected for human annotation. Initial assessments revealed that certain affect categories appeared rarely, meaning selected transcripts were highly skewed containing few examples of each affect or activity category. Thus, to increase the robustness of our reliability values we used GPT predictions for each category to randomly select segments that contained positive predictions. For each emotion or activity category in each set, the reliability subsets contained 50% positive predictions from GPT randomly shuffled with 50% randomly selected segments from the database. Human coders were blind to GPT predictions.

We used between 40-60 transcribed 30-second segments for each category of emotion or activity. Reliability segments were independently coded by 2-3 trained human annotators with final scores representing average performance 1) among all human coders and 2) between each human coder and our GPT model. In keeping with the gold standards within each field, human-human reliability was evaluated using Cohen's Kappa and ChatGPT-Human reliability was evaluated using accuracy, precision, recall, specificity, F1 score, balanced accuracy, and Cohen's kappa.

For both affect and activity, both human coders and GPT were instructed to use a threepoint scale in which 0 indicated that the target affect or activity was absent, 1 indicated mild confidence that the target affect or activity present (i.e. that it was likely), and 2 indicated strong confidence that the target affect or activity was present. For all reliability calculations and analyses, we binarized each rating (0 = absent; scores ≥1 = present; except for anger/frustration, see below), as detailed below. Complete reliability values for human-human and human-GPT are presented in SM11.

Affect coding. Using the script described above, we prompted ChatGPT to annotate five categories of emotion spanning two broad dimensions of affect: Positive affect, i.e. enjoyment, and negative affect, including fear, frustration, anger and sadness. Initially, each emotion was coded on a three-point scale and frustration and anger were not differentiated. However, early results indicating large volumes of predicted frustration/anger prompted us to further distinguish these. In our final coding scheme, frustration and anger are derived from a five-point scale, where 0 indicated no detected frustration/anger, 1-2 indicated frustration, and 3-4 indicated anger.

Each affect category was evaluated independently, allowing multiple emotions to cooccur within a single 30-second segment. Ratings captured both explicit references to emotions and indirect linguistic expressions of affect, interpreted within the surrounding conversational context. References to emotions experienced by others, hypothetical situations, and media content could also be coded. However, isolated emotion words or situations that might reasonably elicit emotion were not sufficient without relevant emotional meaning in context. Ambiguous but meaningful evidence of affect could receive a lower intensity score. When multiple expressions of the same affect occurred within a segment, the highest supported intensity was assigned rather than averaging across expressions.

Across human-human rating pairs, Cohen's kappa for the 30-second reliability segments was .795 for enjoyment, .599 for frustration/anger, .667 for sadness, and .800 for fear, indicating acceptable agreement between our human coders. Among human-GPT precision was .792 for enjoyment, .852 for frustration/anger, .867 for sadness, and .848 for fear, and recall was .760 for enjoyment, .821 for frustration/anger, .788 for sadness, and .875 for fear for the 30-second reliability segments and (see SM 10 for complete table). Moderate-to-high precision combined with very high recall for enjoyment and frustration/anger indicates that GPT identified nearly all human-coded instances of these emotions, although some positive predictions were not confirmed by human coders. In contrast, higher precision but substantially lower recall for sadness and fear indicates that GPT's positive classifications were generally consistent with human judgments, but it failed to identify approximately half of the instances identified by human coders.

Enjoyment. Enjoyment encompassed expressions of happiness, pleasure, amusement, affection, excitement, and delight. Speech was rated on a 0-2 scale:0 = no enjoyment,1 = possible or mild enjoyment, and2 = clear enjoyment. Evidence included explicit positive emotion language, affectionate expressions, celebration, and enthusiastic engagement in enjoyable activities. Routine politeness, praise, positive conversational content, or formulaic expressions of affection (e.g., "love you, bye" at the end of a phone call) were not sufficient without additional contextual evidence of enjoyment.

Frustration and Anger.

Frustration and anger were coded along a shared angerrelated affective continuum, ranging from mild annoyance and irritation to explicit anger and hostility. Speech was rated on a 0-4 scale:0 = no frustration or anger,1 = possible frustration,2 = clear frustration or irritation,3 = clear anger, and4 = strong or escalated anger. For analyses, scores of 1-2 were classified as frustration, while scores of 3-4 were classified as anger. Critically, anger (scores 3 and 4) required explicit linguistic evidence of hostility, aggressive blame, threats, or emotional escalation, whereas frustration included expressions of impatience, irritation, or exasperation without these features. Ratings were based on linguistic evidence of affect in context. Although routine directives and prohibitions were not considered sufficient evidence of frustration, repeated prohibitions or requests could indicate possible frustration (score 1), particularly when suggesting impatience, urgency, or repeated blocked effort.

Fear. Fear encompassed expressions of anxiety, worry, apprehension, alarm, or concern about actual or anticipated harm, danger, or unwanted outcomes. Speech was rated on a 0-2 scale:0 = no fear,1 = possible or mild fear, and2 = clear fear. Evidence included explicit references to fear as well as indirect expressions of concern, such as warnings about potential harm (e.g., "Be careful, you're going to get hurt!"). Fear-related discussions involving other people, hypothetical situations, or media content could also be coded. However, neutral descriptions of potentially dangerous situations were not considered sufficient evidence of fear.

Sadness.

Sadness included expressions of emotional hurt, disappointment, loneliness, grief, loss, or sorrow. Speech was rated on a 0-2 scale:0 = no sadness,1 = possible or mild sadness, and2 = clear sadness. References to crying, loss, or sadness in others could also contribute to ratings, with ambiguous expressions generally receiving a score of 1. Physical discomfort, tiredness, or general negativity alone were not considered sufficient evidence of sadness.

Activity coding.

We coded seven categories of everyday activities: mealtimes, play, reading, music/singing, caregiving routines, sleep, and media. Categories were not mutually exclusive, and multiple activities could be coded within the same 30-second segment when supported by the transcript. Coding was based on linguistic evidence of activities, including descriptions, discussions, or indications of ongoing activities, as detailed below. Because activity annotations were based on transcripts rather than direct observations of behavior they should be interpreted as transcript-based estimates of concurrent activity rather than direct behavioral observations.

Across human-human rating pairs, Cohen's kappa values were .607 for mealtimes, .500 for play, .845 for reading, .796 for music/singing, .698 for caregiving routines, .750 for sleep, and .325 for media, indicating acceptable agreement between our human coders. Among human-GPT, pooled human-GPT precision for the 30-second segments was .825 for mealtimes, .700 for play, .775 for reading, .825 for music/singing, .800 for caregiving routines, .875 for sleep, and .800 for media (see SM11 for complete results).

Mealtimes.

Included talk that referenced eating, drinking, meal preparation, or related conversations about food consumption ("pass me a fork"). Mealtime activities were coded separately from general caregiving routines.

Play. Included talk referencing toys, games, drawing, coloring, and outdoor or playground activities, as well as playful interactions with caregivers, siblings, or other children (i.e. playful or silly speech, e.g. "I'm a carrot, no, you're a carrot").

Reading. Included reading aloud, discussions of books or reading, and other references to ongoing reading activities. To distinguish reading from other media, coders used linguistic features characteristic of written text (e.g. the phrase "she said" at the end of a speech quote).

Music/singing.

Included transcriptions of singing, songs, and other musical vocal activities, as well as explicit references to music or singing. Rhythmic, patterned vocal repetition could indicate singing even without identifiable lyrics, whereas ordinary conversational repetition was not sufficient. Coders did not distinguish between music/signing from media vs human-generated.

Sleep.

Included text indicating bedtime and nap routines, i.e. preparations for sleeping and attempts to settle a child to sleep or back to sleep. References indicating an anticipated or ongoing sleep routine were included, whereas general discussions of sleep unrelated to a current or upcoming routine ("she's been sleeping so badly this week") were excluded.

Caregiving routines. Included speech referencing everyday child-directed care activities such as dressing, hygiene, toileting, bathing, and transitions associated with leaving the house (e.g., putting on shoes, gathering belongings, or getting into a car seat). Coding required evidence of an identifiable caregiving activity rather than isolated instructions or ambiguous interactions. Household chores unrelated to childcare were excluded. Caregiving associated exclusively with meals or sleep was classified under those more specific categories.

Media.

Included text-based evidence of television, videos, tablets, phones, or other electronic media use, including speech originating from media. When media content depicted activities such as play, eating, or caregiving, these activities were not coded unless there was independent evidence that they were also occurring in the child's immediate environment.

Analytic Strategy

All Analyses were conducted in R.

Descriptive analyses. Descriptives summarized participant demographic characteristics, LENA audio recording duration, maternal mental health scores and child temperament. Affect and activity categories were summarized for each family using frequency and rate per hour considering all bins where speech was automatically detected.

Affect and activity distributions. The overall distributions of affect and activity categories were examined using the raw count (Figure 2) or percentage of speechcontaining segments assigned to each category (for all analyses). Segments labeled as None were retained in the denominator when calculating the prevalence of each affect or activity category. Associations between affect and activity were examined by comparing the occurrence of predicted affect categories in segments that also contained predictions for specific activity contexts. Differences in positive affect and negative affect across activity contexts were tested using two-sided, paired family-level t-tests with Benjamini-Hochberg (B-H) correction for multiple comparisons. Positive affect included enjoyment, and negative affect had frustration, anger, sadness, and fear. Between-sample comparisons of family-level proportions used Welch's t-tests, with BH correction across all emotions and activities separately.

Activity-affect associations. We used multilevel mixed-effects logistic regression models to examine associations between everyday activities and emotional speech, separately for the infant (n = 70), toddler (n = 51), and child (n = 38) samples, and including sample interactions to estimate sample-specific associations. Models predicted the presence or absence of affect in each 30-second speech segment from activity, distinguishing within-family and between-family associations. To separate these effects, we included both a within-family predictor (the binary activity indicator centered around each family's mean proportion of segments containing that activity) and a between-family predictor (the family's mean proportion of segments containing that activity). Thus, within-family effects captured differences in emotional speech when a given activity was present versus absent, whereas between-family effects captured associations between families' overall activity prevalence and emotional speech. Models also included child age at recording centered within sample, sample group, interactions of sample group with two activity predictors (within and between), and a random intercept for each child. We conducted modeling for two affects (frustration and enjoyment) with eight activities. Between-family odds ratios were in per 10% increase in activity prevalence.

For visualization, we generated model-predicted probabilities of emotional speech. Within-family predictions compared segments with and without each activity, holding family activity proportions at their sample means. Between-family predictions varied activity proportions from the 5th to 95th percentile within each sample, holding withinfamily deviations at zero. Predictions were calculated at the sample mean age, with random intercepts set to zero, and 95% Wald confidence intervals were estimated and transformed to probabilities. Statistical significance was assessed using two-sided Wald tests of sample-specific coefficients against zero, with Benjamini-Hochberg correction across 96 comparisons (two emotions × eight activities × three samples × two effects).

Affect-Child and Parent Characteristics. Associations between maternal mental health and child temperament with family emotional speech were examined using Pearson correlations. The maternal mental health variables were anxiety and depression scores from each sample. The child temperament variables were Orienting/Regulation, Negative Affectivity and Surgency. The emotional speech outcomes included the rate per hour of the full LENA recording time for each relevant affect category. Analyses controlled for child age. Statistical significance was set at p < .05. Negative affect was examined in relation to the three temperament dimensions; sadness was examined with maternal depression, and fear with maternal anxiety. BH correction was applied.

For the affect-age analysis, frustration proportions in family level were modeled in a pooled natural cubic spline with three degrees of freedom (N = 159). Model inference used HC3 robust standard errors.

SM 9. Post-Hoc Analysis on Frustration and Prohibitions/Conflict

To examine the potential contexts of frustrated and angry speech we annotated conflict/prohibitions in each 30-second speech bin using GPT. Conflict/prohibitions were coded using the same process as affect and activities detailed in the methods, using the following definition.

Conflict/prohibitions.

Included interpersonal conflict, arguing, discipline, correction, scolding, resistance, limit-setting, and prohibitions.
