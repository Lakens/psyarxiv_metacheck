Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. Some differences come from the ground truth: it lacks p-values with a correction name (“p Bonf = .061”), and counts partial eta squared written with its subscript after it (“η 2 p = .05”), “p = .05”, and some table notes and figure legends (“† p < .10, *p < .05”), which this module leaves out, as non-significant p-values. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. Abstract

Universal school-based mental health interventions involve delivering mental health content to all young people within a class, regardless of need. They are typically evaluated in terms of average e[ects, yet there is growing evidence that individual young people may experience the same intervention very di[erently. In this narrative review, we consider five illustrative sources of individual variation in response to universal school-based mental health interventions: social relationships within the classroom, baseline mental health status, special educational needs and disabilities (SEND), family circumstances and gender. For each factor, we review relevant theoretical and empirical literature to explore how each factor might contribute to positive, negative and/or neutral experiences during universal school-based mental health interventions. The review is additionally informed by reflections from a Young People's Advisory Group of five 14-18-year-olds with experience of school mental health lessons, whose perspectives are integrated throughout. We conclude that understanding individual variability is crucial for evaluating the safety and e[ectiveness of universal school-based mental health interventions, and for determining whether and how these interventions can be adapted to meet the diverse needs of young people.

Introduction

Schools are increasingly a place for mental health early intervention and prevention (Deighton et al., 2026; Hoover & Bostic, 2021a; Lever et al., 2024). They are an equitable and convenient place to deliver mental health interventions, especially given long waiting times to enter child and adolescent mental health services within the NHS (Children's Commissioner for England, 2024). School-based approaches include universal mental health interventions, which are lessons delivered to whole classes irrespective of need.

These sessions are designed to promote mental health and wellbeing, reduce mental health symptoms and/or prevent them from developing. In England, these lessons may be taught as part of the Relationships, Sex, and Health Education (RSHE) curriculum or as additional sessions (e.g. delivered in lunchtimes or after school), and may be delivered The evidence of the e[ectiveness of universal school mental health interventions to date is mixed. A recent meta-analysis found that they lead to small improvements in anxiety and depression on average (71 studies, N=63,041; (Hayes et al., 2025). However, the quality of the evidence is often low. For instance, Hayes and colleague reported that only 13% of studies included in their meta-analysis showed low risk of bias (Hayes et al., 2025). An overview of systematic reviews and meta-analyses of school mental health interventions for depression prevention (including both universal and targeted prevention interventions but excluding depression treatments) found only 10.3% of reviews showed low risk of bias (Grant et al., 2025). Recent high-quality trials, including those that draw on therapeutic techniques such as cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT) or mindfulness, have typically show null or sometimes negative e[ects (Andrews et al., 2023; Deighton et al., 2026; Guzman-Holst et al., 2025; Harvey et al., 2023; Kuyken et al., 2022; Werner-Seidler et al., 2025).

There is much that remains unknown about universal mental health interventions in schools (Foulkes et al., 2026a). First, it is unclear which components of di[erent interventions are more likely to lead to average positive or negative e[ects. Second, the underlying mechanisms of di[erent e[ects have been hypothesised, but not definitively assessed. Third, and the focus of this paper, it is unclear whether the mixed or small average e[ects may mask individual di[erences in responses to an intervention (Foulkes et al., 2026a). Universal interventions rest on the assumption that all students in a classroom can understand, implement and benefit from the taught content, but emerging evidence from subgroup analyses and qualitative research indicates that di[erent young people have highly varied experiences in these lessons. Some find the lessons positive and helpful, others report finding them irrelevant or inadequate, and still others report that they find it distressing to learn about mental health at school, particularly in front of their peers (Bailey et al., 2023; Bastounis et al., 2017; Miller et al., 2023; Montero-Marin et al., 2022 , 2023).

Given the new statutory guidance for RSHE in England states that all pupils should be taught about mental health in schools (Department for Education, 2025), and given the increased research focus on school as a site for mental health intervention and prevention (Hoover & Bostic, 2021b; Weist et al., 2023), it is crucial to understand individual di[erences in response to universal approaches. This will clarify who is likely to benefit most from whole-class interventions, who the interventions may not help, and for whom the interventions may be potentially harmful. In turn, this information can be used to design inclusive, accessible mental health interventions that are safe and e[ective for all, and the adaptations that may be needed to achieve this goal.

Alternatively, such evidence may support a need to minimise universal delivery in schools in favour of more targeted one-to-one or small group support. However, to date, research on individual di[erences is limited and mixed, and more attention to this issue is needed.

This paper presents a narrative synthesis of potential sources of individual variation in response to universal school-based mental health interventions. We focus on five individual di[erence factors: 1) social relationships in the classroom; 2) baseline mental health status; 3) special educational needs and disabilities (SEND); 4) family circumstances; and 5) gender (see Figure 1). Individual di*erence variables that may predict response to universal lesson-based mental health interventions Social relationships in the classroom

The first individual di[erence factor that may shape a pupil's experience of universal interventions is their social experiences within the classroom. Schools are complex social systems with strict social norms and hierarchies (Adler & Adler, 1996; Foulkes & Andrews, 2025; Van Den Berg et al., 2020). Bullying is common, and on average 20% of children and adolescents report feeling unsafe at school, with considerable variation in study estimates (6% to 69%; Mori et al., 2021). Adolescents are particularly sensitive to social evaluation and typically find it more di[icult than adults to engage in behaviours or express opinions that distinguish them from their peers or put them as risk of ridicule (Andrews et al., 2020). Self-disclosure activities are common in school mental health interventions, but this requires young people to trust their peers, which some intervention participants report is not the case (Kvist Lindholm & Zetterqvist Nelson, 2015b). Qualitative research highlights that social dynamics within a classroom can make it a challenge to deliver mental health interventions, with young people reporting that peers make jokes or negative comments in the lesson, which increases stigma for those with mental health problems (Aspeqvist et al., 2024).

The YPAG highlighted that fear of judgement from peers was a major barrier to opening up in mental health lessons. "[A young person] might be judged by their peers as well, like they may not be comfortable talking about all of that with their friends and having like side eyes, negative eyes kind of stuC going around." "I think lots of people aren't going to want to be vulnerable and actually talk about their mental health when sometimes it's a topic that you might not even talk about with like your closest friends. So I think sometimes schools expecting people to just volunteer to be really vulnerable with people they don't know is kind of maybe not feasible."

In particular, they highlighted that for pupils being bullied, the fear of peer judgment and subsequent hesitancy to open up in class would be heightened: "I would say like a lot of bullying cases that I've known, it's basically because someone doesn't fit into the social norms or certain hierarchy levels. … Since fitting in is one of the most important things that a lot of teens see, I figure that just because of that, some people won't speak up about their problems or some people think, 'Oh, it's not that important. It's okay if I feel this way. It doesn't aCect anyone else. If I speak up about it, everyone's going to like look at me weirdly.'" "When someone is being bullied, they might go into danger mode and be in constant fear and all of that. So that might aCect how they respond to mental health lessons, because they might not respond positively and be enthusiastic about the topic in general."

The YPAG acknowledged that teachers would make allowances for pupils to leave the class if they felt uncomfortable or upset, but they highlighted that re-joining the class later on would gain unwanted attention from peers: "If people do leave a lesson, then it is like, where do they go? … People think, "oh, well, you must struggle with that or have some sort of like, background with that experience" and then it might lead to questions from your peers and then you end up maybe having to explain it or talk about it and that's not what you want."

Data from a large-scale survey in England shows that pupils with more mental health di[iculties are less likely to feel safe at school and are more likely to have been bullied in the last year (Newlove-Delgado et al., 2022). Recent evidence also shows that pupils eligible for free school meals and those who have special educational needs and disabilities (SEND) reported lower levels of school belonging (Thornton et al., 2026); lower school belonging in turn is associated with worse wellbeing over time (Khanna et al., 2026). This suggests that students who are being bullied or feel unsafe at school may simultaneously need more mental health support but also be less comfortable engaging with universal mental health interventions intended to help them. Conversely, young people with the strongest peer relationships, who feel safe and comfortable at school, may be in the best position to benefit from universal mental health interventions. It is well established that young people with high-quality friendships and a good sense of belonging at school tend to achieve more academically, likely because these relationships are a support system that encourage collaboration, knowledge sharing and academic motivation (Alotaibi, Wentzel). It is possible that this extends to mental health lessons, where young people who feel more socially secure are better able to engage with and implement the classroom content.

Relatedly, these young people may be more likely to benefit from universal mental health interventions because they can use the taught techniques to support each other. This was highlighted by the YPAG: "A student might find it positive when they apply the lessons not for themselves but instead someone who they know that relates to the struggles or situations discussed in the mental health lessons, giving them an idea on how to help that person out if they are in trouble."

Baseline mental health status

In any one classroom, there will be substantial variation in individuals' baseline mental health status: from students who are thriving and flourishing to those who have clinical levels of symptoms. A sizeable minority of young people in every class will already meet the criteria for a mental disorder -the estimated prevalence of common mental disorders among adolescents is 25 to 31% -and a larger percentage will show elevated but subthreshold symptoms (Andrews & Foulkes, 2025; Silva et al., 2020). It is therefore crucial to consider how universal mental health interventions might di[erentially impact young people across this spectrum.

Research on this question to date has shown mixed findings. First, there is some evidence to suggest that for students with greater baseline mental health problems, universal school mental health lessons may worsen their mental health symptoms (Montero-Marin et al., 2022; Stallard et al., 2012; Stoppelbein, 2003). The converse to this is that those with no or minimal baseline mental health problems may benefit more from these interventions. One study of a universal school-based intervention based on CBT showed some support for this (Pössel et al., 2004a). Specifically, they found that the intervention had a preventative e[ect for students with minimal depressive symptoms at baseline, while symptoms increased in the equivalent participants in the control group; in contrast, students with clinically-relevant baseline symptoms showed no significant improvement following the intervention, while symptoms decreased among their counterparts in the control group (Pössel et al., 2004b). This idea was supported by the YPAG, who said that more general tips and content taught in school mental health lessons may be helpful for those without mental health conditions, but may be insu[icient to support those with higher mental health needs: "If people don't struggle as much and or maybe just aren't particularly anxious or maybe don't have any sort of specific mental health conditions, then maybe learning stuC and lessons about breathing techniques or mindfulness for before a public talk or interview might be helpful, but for people who maybe have more complex needs, it might just seem like those things that [the teachers are] suggesting in a lesson might be too small or they've tried them before and they're not actually going to make a diCerence."

In the literature, several mechanisms have been proposed to explain the insu[icient or detrimental impact of universal mental health lessons on those with worse baseline mental health (Foulkes et al., 2026). First, some have suggested that increased symptom reporting post-intervention reflects a better understanding and acknowledgement of their existing di[iculties (Guzman-Holst et al., 2025). Second, negative e[ects may be because interventions teach students to be aware of negative thoughts and feelings, already elevated in this subgroup, without su[iciently addressing how to challenge or update these beliefs (Foulkes & Stapley, 2022; Hailwood, 2020; Montero-Marin et al., 2022; Stallard et al., 2012; Stoppelbein, 2003).

Third, it is possible that those with mental health di[iculties, or who have otherwise had recent traumatic or di[icult events, may find the content of mental health lessons actively upsetting or distressing. During a universal social and emotional learning curriculum taught in primary schools, some children found lessons upsetting when they prompted reflection on challenging experiences, in particular grief and loss (Demkowicz et al. (2026). Similar experiences have been reported in multiple qualitative evaluations of secondary school interventions, with some pupils saying that the lesson content triggered distressing thoughts and memories (Garmy et al., 2015; Kvist Lindholm & Zetterqvist Nelson, 2015a; Miller et al., 2023).

The YPAG supported this saying and said it may be especially the case for those who have had a "rough past". "It may trigger negative thoughts or maybe memories for those who may have had a rough past."

Another reason o[ered by the YPAG as to why those with mental health conditions in a class may not benefit from universal mental health lessons is due to inaccurate teaching of mental health conditions that may perpetuate stereotypes within their peer group: "I think maybe if a lesson is about raising awareness of a mental health condition, but maybe it's one like OCD that's often very misunderstood, like in the media. And I think if the teacher explains it using stereotypes and there's someone in the lesson who has experienced that, they almost feel like it's kind of teaching it wrong and then other people are also going to have these kinds of stereotypes about it."

The YPAG highlighted that mental health lessons may heighten people's awareness of the severity of existing symptoms which may be worrying for them, making the students feel worse.

"say you address something that applies to a person [in the class], instead of taking it the good way, they take it in a way like, "oh, my issue is being discussed here, so it's kind of serious." [This] could lower their self-esteem so rather than wanting to improve their mental health, it's like kind of worsening it, making it like more of a bad eCect on them."

It is important to note other mixed findings regarding baseline mental health. First, there is some evidence that those with worse baseline mental health may experience more benefit from universal school mental health interventions. Specifically, one study found that the universal FRIENDS for Life programme, which is based on CBT principles, reduced worry in the small subgroup of young people who scored above the clinical threshold on a baseline anxiety and depression measure (Wigelsworth et al., 2026).

Conversely, in a mindfulness intervention, those with better baseline mental health (low levels of depression or weight/shape concerns) reported higher levels of anxiety at 3month follow up compared to equivalent adolescents in the control group (Johnson & Wade, 2021). The authors speculate this may be due to an increase in awareness of emotional states in these groups, which may become ruminative. Another study found that the increase in depressive symptoms following a universal intervention based on Dialectical Behaviour Therapy (DBT) was more marked in those with low (relative to high) levels of baseline anxiety; the increase in emotion dysregulation was also more marked in those with low (relative to high) levels of baseline anxiety and depression (Harvey et al., 2023). The mixed findings to date highlight the urgency for large scale, systematic analysis to better understand the relationship between baseline mental health and outcomes from universal mental health interventions. It is possible that there is an optimal level of mental health problems at which young people are most likely to benefit from universal interventions, but more research is needed to assess this.

Special Educational Needs and Disabilities (SEND)

SEND pupils typically find school more challenging than their peers, particularly if appropriate adjustments for them have not been made. This includes the social aspects, the physical environment and the task of classroom learning (Cipriano et al., 2023). It is reasonable to hypothesise that these challenges might extend to mental health lessons, a[ecting their ability to experience the intended benefits. Indeed, there is qualitative evidence, from primary school teachers delivering universal mental health or socioemotional learning (SEL) interventions, that these lessons are not su[iciently accommodating for SEND pupils (Demkowicz et al., 2026; Hamilton et al., 2025). Yet there has been limited systematic investigation of this. In a review of 269 SEL interventions, only 20 (7.4%) studies considered how outcomes might di[er for students with SEND (Cipriano et al., 2023). Of these studies, the analyses and definition of SEND was widely heterogenous and no reliable conclusions could be drawn about the experience of any of these pupils (Cipriano et al., 2023). More research is urgently needed to understand how di[erent SEND pupils experience universal mental health interventions. Below, we highlight two groups in particular that should be exploredthose with language di[iculties and autistic students -while acknowledging that many other SEND pupils may also have di[erential experiences in these lessons.

Language di*iculties

To implement and benefit from taught content in any lesson, a young person must first understand what the teacher is saying. It is well established that young people with receptive or expressive language di[iculties, such as those with Developmental Language Disorder (DLD), struggle to understand lessons at school. Young people with DLD report a constant struggle to keep up with classroom lessons and peer conversations (Hobson & Lee, 2023; C. Norbury et al., 2024), and their academic outcomes su[er as result (McGregor, 2020; Ziegenfusz et al., 2022). Teachers delivering SEL lessons have reported needing to make ad-hoc adaptations for pupils with SEND, because these pupils find the content too di[icult to understand (Hamilton et al., 2025) Stapley teachers) or are not able to participate in group discussions, often a core activity in universal interventions (Demkowicz et al., 2026). SEND pupils may thus struggle to access the benefits of these interventions if they are not adapted for their needs.

It is already established that one-to-one CBT, mindfulness-based therapy and other talking therapies involve nuanced emotion vocabulary, the use of metaphors and abstract language, and complex grammar to represent the causal and temporal relationships between feelings and actions, which are di[icult to understand for those with language comprehension di[iculties or DLD (Hill et al., 2025; Norbury et al., 2024).

Many universal mental health interventions are based on CBT and mindfulness (Guzman-Holst et al., 2025). This indicates that any SEND pupils with language di[iculties may struggle to understand and implement content from mental health lessons, but this urgently needs to be empirically assessed.

The YPAG highlighted the inaccessibility for some students of the group discussion components of mental health lessons, as well as di[iculties with reading and writing tasks: "If you're quite nervous anyways and then being asked to talk or if it's some sort of like writing or reading tasks and people find that diCicult, then it just might not be accessible."

Autism and ADHD

To date, there has been very limited research investigating how neurodivergent young people, such as those who are autistic and/or have ADHD, experience universal mental health interventions in schools. However, a number of indicators suggest that they may have more di[icult experiences in these lessons. Firstly, they face more challenges at school in general, particularly mainstream schools (Billington et al., 2024; Greer et al., 2026; Gwernan-Jones et al., 2016; Horgan et al., 2023; Norbury et al., 2024). For autistic young people, this includes navigating school as an unpredictable, complex and demanding social environment (Billington et al., 2024; Horgan et al., 2023). Young people with ADHD report challenges inherent to the behavioural expectations of the classroom context -i.e. the requirement to sit still and concentrate for prolonged periods -and the escalating cycle of punishment and stigma that arises when they repeatedly fail to meet these expectations (Gwernan-Jones et al., 2016). Lukito et al. (2025) found that autistic and/or ADHD students experienced twice the school-based emotional burden as their peers, which in turn was significantly correlated with symptoms of depression and anxiety.

These di[icult school experiences may impact the extent to which neurodivergent young people are able to learn or benefit from universal school mental health interventions, which again require them to sit still and pay attention for prolonged periods of time. In support of this, secondary school teachers in a large universal intervention trial in England reported that students with attention di[iculties struggled to concentrate or understand certain terms or concepts (Stapley et al., 2026), which is a preliminary indication that pupils with ADHD might experience a barrier to benefiting from these interventions.

The second indicator relates to the challenges with emotional understanding and expression seen in some neurodivergent young people. Teachers involved in a primary school universal intervention study reported that the intervention was not e[ective for autistic children in their class, because they had more di[iculties identifying and labelling emotions, which was required by the intervention (Hamilton et al., 2025). The teachers also reported that the lessons required a certain level or type of social skills that autistic pupils did not have, and were "too abstract" for some of the pupils to follow (Hamilton et al., 2025). Relatedly, neurodivergent young people often have co-occurring alexithymia, a di[iculty in identifying bodily signals and emotions and distinguishing between the two (Vaiouli et al., 2022). Mindfulness programmes in particular require young people to notice and focus on their breathing and their body, including during meditation practices. These abstract representations of internal and emotional experiences, and the requirement to pay attention to them, may be di[icult for neurodivergent young people with alexithymia.

Outside of the school context, there is evidence that people with autism, ADHD and/or alexithymia benefit from adaptations to one-to-one CBT and mindfulness interventions (Birtwell et al., 2025; Hartley et al., 2022; Ogrodniczuk et al., 2011; Spain & Happé, 2020).

These include reduced use of metaphors, changing the duration of the sessions, more therapist-led sca[olding for cognitive exercises, and o[ering mindful movement exercises as an alternative to breath-focused meditation (Birtwell et al., 2025; Hartley et al., 2022; Ogrodniczuk et al., 2011; Spain & Happé, 2020). If future research reliably demonstrates that neurodivergent young people find universal school mental health interventions more challenging, then these recommendations could be extended to the school-based context (Demkowicz et al., 2026; Hamilton et al., 2025; Stapley et al., 2026).

We asked the YPAG their thoughts on how neurodivergence might a[ect how pupils respond to universal mental health lessons, and they highlighted how the school and classroom environment may feel unpredictable and not feel emotionally safe for neurodivergent young people: "Those emotional topics require emotional safety. So if the environment or classroom feels unpredictable or like the teacher [doesn't] feel trust [ed], then the student [shows] their vulnerability and it might be even more intense for neurodivergent people."

It is important to note that there is one trial assessing an SEL curriculum that specifically assessed outcomes for a subgroup of young people who had a range of developmental disabilities and neurodivergent conditions (Katz et al., 2020). The intervention combined mental health literacy and techniques from dialectical behavioural therapy. This subgroup analysis found positive e[ects on all three outcomes -self-esteem, coping skills and perceived social support (Katz et al., 2020); mental health outcomes such as anxiety or depression symptoms were not the focus of the trial.

It is therefore important for future research to identify exactly what components of interventions can e[ectively and safely be taught to SEND students, what outcomes can be expected, and what adaptations, if any, need to be made.

Family circumstances

Socio-economic

There is mixed evidence regarding how family circumstances may impact response to universal interventions. Primary school teachers suggested that universal socioemotional lessons were especially beneficial for children from disadvantaged families with di[icult home lives, as these children had greater support needs (Demkowicz et al., 2026). In support of this is some evidence from a universal SEL primary school programme that focused on coping strategies (Holen et al., 2012a). This study found that children from low SES families (i.e. with lower parental education) reported a greater improvement in coping than children from high education families (Holen et al., 2012b).

Similarly, Hamilton et al. (2025) reported that an SEL intervention was helpful for children from deprived backgrounds as it provided an opportunity to talk about problems at home.

The YPAG highlighted the relevance of family circumstances, focusing more specifically on potential stress at home: "If a student is already going through a hard time, for example something may be happening at home, the coping methods taught at school may help them get their minds oC of things and help them relax." However, a di[erent study found that children eligible for free school meals (an indicator of low parental income) reported increased depression and anxiety symptoms following a universal school-based CBT anxiety intervention, while their counterparts in the control group did not (Wigelsworth et al., 2018). It is unclear why this might be. Teachers who delivered a universal intervention reflected that multiply disadvantaged children (for example, children experiencing low attainment intersecting with SEND and trauma) may struggle to apply content from mental health lessons to their lives due to having more complex needs, and that these pupils would require further intervention or support (Demkowicz et al., 2026; Hamilton et al., 2025). Other researchers have proposed that young people experiencing structural adversity (e.g. poverty, food insecurity, housing instability) may face ongoing pressures that limits the e[ectiveness of school based mental health interventions, and that this group might need other school-based support alongside classroom-based universal interventions (Mansfield et al., 2023) The relevance of the content in the lessons to children's home lives may also be important. For example, in one primary school SEL intervention, content in some of the example stories were perceived by teachers as too "middle-class", focusing on going shopping to buy new clothes, going on holidays and playing in the garden, which the teachers reported lacked relevance to those from deprived backgrounds (Hamilton et al., 2025). Other stories were considered inappropriate or harmful for those experiencing certain adversities (e.g. removal from the family home, bereavement) (Hamilton et al., 2025). In an ethnographic study of mindfulness lessons in secondary school, one pupil shared in class that her brother had just gone to prison, and the teacher responded by asking the class for examples of more general things that caused stress (Hailwood, 2020). Bailey et al. (2023) found that only 49% of students thought storylines in a secondary school intervention were 'completely' or 'somewhat' relevant to their lives.

Outside of the school context, there is significant literature on cross-cultural di[erences in the conceptualisation of mental illness and mental health stigma (Elshamy et al., 2023; Knifton, 2012; Lauber & Rössler, 2007; Ng, 1997), which may lead to di[erences in how families and communities think about, discuss and respond to mental health problems.

For example, a US study found that ethnicity moderated the relationship between parents' attitudes and perceived stigma and intentions to seek child mental health services (Turner et al., 2015). More positive attitudes were associated with greater helpseeking intentions among European-American parents, but not African-American or Hispanic-American parents, while greater perceived stigma was associated with lower help-seeking intentions among Hispanic-American parents only (Turner et al., 2015). In the school intervention context, there may be meaningful ethnic di[erences in parents' attitudes towards mental health lessons in schools, and how they respond to this (e.g. supporting with homework tasks), but to date this has not been assessed.

In qualitative studies, some Black African young people in the UK report limited discussion about mental health in their families, although experience varies across individuals (Craddock et al., 2023; Degge et al., 2025; Meechan et al., 2021). It is possible that more open mental health conversations and less stigma at home lead to greater benefits from universal school based mental health interventions, and that the converse is also true, but more research is needed to comprehensively compare family communication about mental health across di[erent ethnicity groups and how this impacts the e[ectiveness of school mental health interventions. In a qualitative study, teachers reported that children's at-home emotional expression context, not specific to culture or ethnicity, was reported by teachers to influence the e[ectiveness of a universal socio-emotional learning programme (Demkowicz et al., 2026).

The YPAG highlighted that being from a background where mental health is not openly discussed or is stigmatised at home may mean a young person feel less able to open up in class.

"Some students may come from my background where mental health isn't openly discussed. So maybe when teachers are discussing about mental health and all of that, they might not be open enough to share what they feel." "I would say if they're in a situation at home where they feel quite uncomfortable to talk about their problems with their parents or siblings or carers, it kind of correlates to how they act in school where they may not feel comfortable around teachers discussing topics like that."

As well as cultural di[erences and mental health-related stigma, a young person's first language may also impact their response to universal mental health interventions taught in English, with those who have English as a first language likely to benefit more than those who do not. In a qualitative study, educational mental health practitioners (EMHPs) in England reported that small-group school-based mental health interventions were less accessible for those with a first language other than English (Howard & Moore, 2026), so it is reasonable to hypothesise that the same might apply to universal interventions. The EMHPs reported that key terminology in the lessons did not have a direct translation in the child's first language, for example panic attacks, and that some concepts therefore risk being 'lost in translation' (Howard & Moore, 2026). A universal school-based mental health literacy intervention found that English proficiency predicted knowledge acquisition from the intervention, and that students with English as a second language reported increased stress following the intervention; this suggests that English proficiency may influence both learning outcomes and how the lessons is experienced (Wei et al., 2024). Regarding a universal primary school intervention, teachers perceived the intervention as helpful to develop emotional literacy for children with a first language other than English, but reported that these children may have missed out or broader benefits due to the inaccessibility of content and group discussions (Demkowicz et al., 2026).

Gender

Some trials of universal school mental health interventions have found that gender is associated with di[erent outcomes, with girls typically showing better outcomes than boys. There is evidence that girls benefit more than boys from CBT-based interventions (Barrett et al., 2006; Pössel et al., 2011) and an SEL intervention (Coelho et al., 2017), and other trials of CBT-based and mindfulness-based universal interventions found that boys responded more negatively than girls (Guzman-Holst et al., 2025; Johnson et al., 2016; Klim-Conforti et al., 2021).

These di[erences may be due to gender di[erences in emotion expression and regulation, particularly in the social settings of a classroom. Specifically, teachers report that girls are more willing to talk openly about their feelings, while boys are reluctant to be seen doing this by peers and so feigning disengage to mask their unease (Hamilton et al., 2025). Other qualitative work reiterates that socialised gender norms play a role, for example male high school students reported that a barrier to engaging in a school-based mindfulness programme is a social message that men who practice mindfulness and compassion are "silly" or "weak" (Colaianne & Falcone, 2025).

When asked about gender, the YPAG also focused on how gender stereotypes could impact both boys and girls when asked to open up in class about mental health. They confirmed that the stereotype that boys are emotionally stronger might prevent boys from opening up in class, but also highlighted that girls being labelled as "dramatic" may also limit how much girls open up. They also highlighted that gender-diverse young people may not feel able to express their feelings in mental health lessons, in line with studies reporting discrimination experienced by these students within the UK school environment (Bower-Brown et al., 2023).

"I would say gender plays quite a big role in it regarding mental health because there's the big stereotype that guys should be stronger and they shouldn't really care about their emotions." "Girls are more likely to be labelled as dramatic and boys are more likely to be labelled as strong […] so they might not be able to express their emotions. And then non-binary people […] could also bottle up their emotions and not be able to express how they really feel about their mental health."

More research is urgently needed to understand how gender might influence the experience and e[ectiveness of universal school mental health interventions, and whether adaptations can be made to reduce the impact of gender stereotypes on young people's willingness to discuss mental health in classroom groups.
