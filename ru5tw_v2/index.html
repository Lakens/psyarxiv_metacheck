Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. Introduction

Adult attachment research is strongly organized around attachment anxiety and attachment avoidance (Fraley et al., 2000; Li & Chan, 2012; Mikulincer & Shaver, 2016).

These dimensions describe stable individual differences in hyperactivating and deactivating tendencies, but they do not uniquely specify the micro-temporal process through which a person interprets a relational event, compares the current condition with a desired condition, acts, receives a partner response, and recovers or remains dysregulated.

The need for a dynamic account is not itself new. Bowlby's theory was explicitly goal directed and feedback regulated (Bowlby, 1982); control-theory analyses were applied to adolescent attachment and emotion regulation decades ago (Kobak et al., 1993); computational implementations have formalized attachment set-goals and feedback (Gagliardi, 2022; Petters, 2019; Stevens & Zhang, 2009); and adult relationship theories already describe risk regulation, partner responsiveness, coregulation, and partner buffering of insecurity (Murray et al., 2006; Overall et al., 2022; Sbarra & Hazan, 2008; Simpson & Overall, 2014). We refer to the proposed framework as Attachment Regulation and Control (ARC). Here, control denotes feedback-based regulation toward relational goals in the control-theoretic sense; it does not denote interpersonal dominance, coercion, or a generalized need for control. ARC therefore does not present feedback control as a new idea.

The problem addressed here is narrower and empirical. Existing ideas are distributed across different levels of analysis and are rarely joined in a measurement architecture that can be tested as a temporally ordered process in adult romantic relationships. ARC asks whether person-level attachment orientations predict how repeatedly measured relational discrepancies are translated into action, how partner feedback updates subsequent relational state, and whether a positive secure-base representation adds recovery information beyond established insecurity dimensions.

This framing also limits claims about security and disorganization. Secure-base script research provides positive representational content for security beyond merely low anxiety and avoidance (H. S. Waters & Waters, 2006; T. E. A. Waters & Roisman, 2019; T. E. A. Waters et al., 2025). This justifies prespecifying positive secure-base representation as a candidate construct, but does not yet justify making it a required ARC component because its incremental value for recovery beyond anxiety and avoidance remains unestablished. Adult disorganization, by contrast, remains heterogeneous in definition and measurement (Pollard et al., 2023). ARC therefore treats recovery as a dynamic outcome to be measured, positive security as a candidate to be tested against it, and disorganization as an independent validation criterion rather than a quantity generated by the model.

ARC does not require a particular computational controller family. P, PI, PID, state-space, active-inference, reinforcement-learning, and related formulations can be treated as competing mechanistic or observation models when their identifying predictions are available. The psychological framework is instead defined by measurable relations among perceived state, desired state, discrepancy, action, feedback, state updating, and recovery. This separation allows the framework to remain testable even when several latent mechanisms are observationally equivalent.

Contribution and scope

The strongest defensible contribution of ARC is integration and empirical specification

Theoretical Foundations Control-system attachment is prior theory, not ARC novelty

Bowlby's attachment system was formulated as goal directed and feedback regulated (Bowlby, 1982). Kobak and colleagues later used control theory to analyze attachment strategies and emotion regulation during mother-teen problem solving (Kobak et al., 1993).

Petters reviewed the control-system lineage and its computational implications (Petters, 2019). ARC adopts this lineage rather than claiming to originate it.

Computational and dynamical precedents

Stevens and Zhang modeled infant attachment as a dynamic system with explicit state evolution and recovery-like behavior (Stevens & Zhang, 2009). Gagliardi implemented attachment as a multidimensional control system with representational set-goals and agent interactions (Gagliardi, 2022). Olar and colleagues used an adaptive network model for anxious-avoidant relationship dynamics (Olar et al., 2026). These precedents show that targets, state updating, feedback, and dyadic dynamics are already computationally plausible.

Their existence changes the novelty question. ARC should not be evaluated by whether it can reproduce another simulated attachment trajectory. It should be evaluated by whether its proposed variables can be measured repeatedly in people and whether the resulting temporal model adds information beyond static attachment scores and simpler time-series baselines.

Risk regulation, responsiveness, and coregulation

Adult relationship regulation also has established psychological precedents.

Risk-regulation theory describes how dependence and perceived acceptance shape decisions to seek connection or protect the self (Murray et al., 2006). Coregulation and partner-buffering accounts emphasize that adult attachment, distress, separation, and recovery unfold through DYNAMIC ADULT ATTACHMENT REGULATION coupled interpersonal regulation rather than isolated intrapersonal control (Overall et al., 2022; Sbarra & Hazan, 2008; Simpson & Overall, 2014). ARC therefore treats partner feedback as a measured component of the process rather than background noise.

Closeness and autonomy are not assumed to be opposites ARC distinguishes experienced autonomy from interpersonal distance.

Close-relationship research shows that autonomy and relatedness can be jointly satisfied rather than occupying opposite ends of one continuum (Kluwer et al., 2020). This supports a possible multidimensional relational-state representation while stopping short of establishing which coordinates ARC requires. The scalar closeness pilot therefore remains the first test; a closeness-autonomy extension must earn its added complexity empirically.

Positive security as a candidate core construct Secure-base representations encode expectations that support can be available and effective and that exploration or constructive activity can resume (H. S. Waters & Waters, 2006; T. E. A. Waters & Roisman, 2019; T. E. A. Waters et al., 2025). This provides a strong theoretical reason to test positive secure-base representation, S, as distinct from simply reversing L and U . It does not, however, establish that S is necessary for the ARC architecture or that it predicts recovery beyond dimensional insecurity. ARC therefore prespecifies S as a candidate core construct rather than a required component. Recovery is treated as an observable dynamic outcome against which this candidate can earn or fail to earn incremental validity.

Candidate moderators rather than additional core constructs

Mentalized affectivity concerns identifying, processing, and expressing affective states (Greenberg et al., 2017). Emotion-regulation flexibility concerns context-sensitive adaptation (Mosannenzadeh et al., 2024). Both are relevant to ARC, but their role is deliberately limited. Mentalized affectivity is a candidate moderator of relational-state inference quality; flexibility is a candidate moderator of context-and feedback-sensitive policy adaptation.

Neither is required for the core feedback loop to be defined.

A minimal transition model is ri,t+1 = f i (r i,t , a i,t+1 , p i,t+1 , c i,t+1 ) + ω i,t+1.

Empirical Evidence and Falsifiable Predictions

What is already established ARC builds on components that should not be presented as novel. Attachment anxiety and avoidance are established dimensional descriptions of adult attachment, and meta-analytic evidence links both dimensions to romantic relationship quality (Fraley et al., 2000; Li & Chan, 2012; Mikulincer & Shaver, 2016). Positive secure-base representations have an independent theoretical and measurement tradition (H. S. Waters & Waters, 2006; DYNAMIC ADULT ATTACHMENT REGULATION T. E. A. Waters & Roisman, 2019; T. E. A. Waters et al., 2025).

Control-system interpretations of attachment predate ARC (Bowlby, 1982; Kobak et al., 1993; Petters, 2019), and computational models already implement targets, state updating, feedback, and recovery-like dynamics (Gagliardi, 2022; Olar et al., 2026; Stevens & Zhang, 2009). Adult relationship theories also provide established accounts of risk regulation, coregulation, and partner buffering of attachment insecurity (Murray et al., 2006; Overall et al., 2022; Sbarra & Hazan, 2008; Simpson & Overall, 2014).

The empirical contribution of ARC therefore depends on joining these strands in a single temporal measurement architecture and testing whether the proposed dynamic variables add information beyond simpler alternatives.

Relational discrepancy has empirical precedent, but H1 remains under-tested

The distinction between experienced and desired closeness has direct empirical precedent. Longitudinal work using actual and ideal closeness showed that smaller discrepancies were associated with better relational well-being and mental health, and that moving closer to one's ideal over time accompanied improved outcomes (Frost & Forrester, 2013). Dyadic response-surface analyses likewise showed that closeness discrepancy is consequential for relationship quality, with especially unfavorable outcomes when experienced closeness is lower than desired (Pusch et al., 2022). Population-based evidence further indicates that wanting more closeness is more strongly associated with poorer relationship characteristics than experiencing more closeness than desired (Pietras et al., 2024).

These findings support the construct validity of a perceived-desired discrepancy, but they do not establish the ARC-specific causal-temporal claim. Most discrepancy studies predict relationship quality, well-being, or longer-term change rather than the next regulatory action within the same individual. H1 is therefore not the claim that closeness discrepancies exist or matter. It is the narrower claim that momentary discrepancy contains incremental information about subsequent regulation after current state, context, and behavioral inertia have been accounted for.

Why the difference score must itself compete A simple score e t = r * t -rt imposes a substantive constraint: desired state and perceived state are assumed to enter the action model with equal magnitude and opposite sign. Existing evidence also makes directional asymmetry plausible. Wanting more closeness than currently experienced may not be dynamically equivalent to wanting less closeness than currently experienced (Pietras et al., 2024; Pusch et al., 2022). ARC therefore treats positive and negative discrepancy components as a mandatory sensitivity analysis rather than assuming one globally linear slope.

Perceived partner responsiveness supports the feedback pathway

The feedback component also has strong adjacent evidence. In a two-week daily diary study, within-person increases in perceived partner responsiveness covaried with greater emotional expression to the partner; an experimental study in the same program showed that induced responsiveness increased expression of anxiety (Ruan et al., 2020). A yearlong intensive longitudinal study further showed that perceived partner responsiveness participated in within-and between-person commitment dynamics and was associated with attachment-related individual differences (Segal & Fraley, 2016).

These studies make the ARC feedback pathway plausible, but they also highlight a measurement distinction that the framework must preserve.

Attachment orientations plausibly shape both response magnitude and response channel

The idea that L and U modify relational dynamics has direct within-person precedent and is consistent with interactionist accounts in which attachment effects depend on relational context and activation conditions (Campbell & Marshall, 2011). Event-contingent work found that attachment anxiety strengthened, and attachment avoidance weakened, affective reactivity to agreeable interpersonal behavior, particularly with romantic partners (Sadikaj et al., 2011). Daily and intensive longitudinal studies likewise show attachment-related differences in relationship feedback, commitment processes, and responses to relational cues (Beck et al., 2024; Carnelley et al., 2025; Segal & Fraley, 2016).

More recent dyadic experience-sampling work using dynamic structural equation modeling reports attachment-related moderation of emotional interdependence between partners (Xie et al., 2025).

This evidence argues against representing attachment only as a shift in one signed action coefficient. Anxious regulation can be expressed through overt proximity seeking, reassurance seeking, protest, or heightened interpersonal engagement, while avoidant regulation can appear as weaker overt responding, distancing, suppression, or internal disengagement. The same relational discrepancy can therefore be translated into different response channels. ARC consequently treats H2 as a multivariate policy claim. The key test is whether L and U explain reproducible heterogeneity in channel-specific discrepancy-to-action mappings after context and action history are controlled. The strongest directional prediction concerns DYNAMIC ADULT ATTACHMENT REGULATION positive discrepancy, where a person wants more closeness than is currently experienced: higher L should preferentially strengthen overt approach-oriented responses, whereas higher U should reduce overt approach and increase distancing or internal disengagement. Effects for closeness surfeit remain less constrained and should be estimated rather than assumed. This literature makes H2 plausible, but does not establish the ARC-specific e t → a t+1 moderation. Secure-base processes justify a candidate, not yet a required core construct Adult secure-base functioning can be measured behaviorally. In engaged couples, the Secure Base Scoring System captured secure-base use and support in observed problem-solving interactions and predicted relationship variables beyond a generic communication coding system (Crowell et al., 2002). Daily dyadic work likewise found reciprocal associations between relationship-specific security and support seeking, provision, receipt, and felt support (Davila & Kashy, 2009).

There is also direct evidence that attachment is associated with recovery from relational stress. Cortisol trajectories after relationship conflict vary with attachment insecurity (Pietromonaco et al., 2013; Powers et al., 2006), positive dyadic coping predicts faster cortisol recovery and interacts with attachment anxiety (Meuwly et al., 2012), and observed recovery from romantic conflict has developmental links to earlier attachment security and partner functioning (Salvatore et al., 2011). Coregulatory and partner-buffering accounts independently place interpersonal regulation and recovery at the center of adult attachment processes (Overall et al., 2022; Sbarra & Hazan, 2008; Simpson & Overall, 2014).

Relationship experiences can also contribute to longer-term changes in attachment security, supporting a distinction between momentary regulation and slower representational change (Arriaga & Kumashiro, 2019; Bayraktaroglu et al., 2023). H3 is therefore deliberately stronger than the existing evidence and serves as a promotion test rather than as an assumption required to define ARC. Missing-data sensitivity

The primary analysis uses observed valid same-day lags and never reconstructs missed prompts. Because ordinary GEE can be sensitive to informative observation processes, a prespecified sensitivity analysis uses stabilized inverse-probability-of-observation weights estimated from response history (Chen et al., 2010; Cole & Hernán, 2008). Both the numerator and denominator response models are fit using days 1-11 only, and the training-derived model and truncation bounds are then applied unchanged to days 12-14.

The denominator model uses only information available before each scheduled prompt, including prompt stratum, study day, prior response rate, immediately previous response status, and last observed negative affect, absolute discrepancy, and absolute signed action. The current unobserved prompt state is never used to predict its own availability.

Weight distributions, fitted response probabilities, truncation frequency, and effective sample size are reported.

This weighted analysis is interpreted as a missing-at-random sensitivity conditional on the specified observed history. It is not claimed to identify or correct a missing-not-at-random mechanism in which response depends on the contemporaneous unobserved relational state or action. The exact-design Monte Carlo deliberately retains such a contemporaneous state/action-dependent nonresponse condition as a stress test.

Robustness across unweighted and weighted analyses is informative, but agreement between them does not establish that missingness is MAR.

Analytic progression

The confirmatory H1 estimator is a Gaussian generalized estimating equation with identity link, couple-level clustering, an independence working correlation, and a bias-reduced sandwich covariance estimator (Mancl & DeRouen, 2001; Zeger & Liang, 1986).

The mean model contains explicit within-person deviations and participant means, the locked context terms, prompt stratum, elapsed time, and the relevant M0-M4 relational predictors. This estimates population-average temporal associations while allowing repeated observations from both partners in a couple to remain statistically dependent.

The choice is deliberately narrower than a full latent dynamic model. H1 asks whether the average state/target architecture adds predictive information and which observation representation is warranted; it does not require estimation of a person-specific latent controller. Couple and person random intercepts and relational-slope heterogeneity are nevertheless included in the Monte Carlo data generator. The confirmatory GEE must therefore demonstrate acceptable bias, interval coverage, false-positive behavior, and predictive model selection under richer heterogeneity than it explicitly parameterizes.

The working correlation is set to independence so that the within-couple covariance structure is not selected from the data. Robust sandwich inference is used for correlation misspecification, with a finite-cluster correction prespecified for primary inference (Mancl & DeRouen, 2001; Zeger & Liang, 1986). Software versions, convergence tolerances, iteration limits, and Monte Carlo execution parameters are frozen in the reproducibility package and study protocol rather than repeated in the theoretical manuscript. There are no Bayesian priors and no required random-effects distribution in the primary H1 estimator.

ARC is intended to become smaller when evidence does not support added structure.

If proximal support predicts recovery while S does not, the recovery account should be simplified toward episode-level coregulation. If S predicts relationship quality or secure-base behavior but not the preregistered primary recovery outcome, the positive construct may remain theoretically relevant, but it should remain outside the ARC core and its placement in the dynamic recovery pathway should be removed. Secondary outcomes cannot be substituted post hoc for a failed primary promotion endpoint.

Mentalized affectivity and flexibility

M and F should be excluded, merged with adjacent constructs, or redefined if they fail discriminant validity or do not add incremental information to the specific pathway assigned to them. ARC does not depend on preserving these moderators.

Dynamic disorganization extension

Dynamic indicators should not be described as attachment disorganization if they fail to improve held-out prediction of an independent disorganization criterion beyond L, U , their interaction, and simpler static summaries. High variability by itself is insufficient evidence of disorganization.

Mechanistic non-uniqueness

Even successful prediction of the ARC temporal sequence would not uniquely identify a latent computational mechanism. If P/PI/PID, state-space, active-inference, reinforcement-learning, or other models remain observationally equivalent, the defensible conclusion is about temporal prediction and feedback organization rather than a unique psychological algorithm. This identifiability problem is addressed separately in Manuscript 2.

Discussion

What ARC contributes

ARC is best understood as an empirical integration framework. Control-system attachment, secure-base representations, interpersonal risk regulation, dyadic coregulation, and computational attachment models are established precedents (Bowlby, 1982; Gagliardi, 2022; Kobak et al., 1993; Murray et al., 2006; Petters, 2019; Sbarra & Hazan, 2008; Stevens & Zhang, 2009). The contribution proposed here is to place these ideas into one parsimonious sequence with variables that can be repeatedly measured in adult romantic relationships and with explicit conditions under which components should be rejected.

This positioning deliberately lowers the novelty claim while strengthening falsifiability.

The central question is not whether feedback regulation is a new metaphor for attachment.

It is whether measured relational discrepancy and feedback provide reproducible information about subsequent action and recovery beyond static attachment dimensions and generic temporal baselines. Throughout this account, control and controller are cybernetic terms for feedback regulation toward a reference condition; they do not denote interpersonal dominance, coercion, or a controlling personality style.

Where the existing human evidence stops

Several pieces of the proposed loop already have human support. Actual-ideal closeness discrepancies are associated with relational outcomes (Frost & Forrester, 2013; Pietras et al., 2024; Pusch et al., 2022); perceived partner responsiveness fluctuates within people and predicts interpersonal expression and relationship dynamics (Ruan et al., 2020; Segal & Fraley, 2016); partner-buffering research further shows that relationship partners can attenuate insecurity-related difficulties (Overall et al., 2022; Simpson & Overall, 2014); and attachment orientations moderate within-person affective or dyadic responses to relational cues (Beck et al., 2024; Carnelley et al., 2025; Sadikaj et al., 2011; Xie et al., 2025).

What is not yet established is the specific ARC chain linking a momentary, explicitly measured desired-perceived discrepancy to subsequent regulatory action and then to partner feedback and state updating. This distinction prevents adjacent evidence from being treated as direct confirmation.

It also motivates a stricter treatment of the discrepancy construct. Difference scores are compact, but they encode equality and sign constraints on the effects of their

DYNAMIC ADULT ATTACHMENT REGULATION components. ARC therefore treats the difference score as a parsimonious hypothesis that must compete against the raw components and asymmetric discrepancy models. A failure of the simple difference representation would revise the mathematics without necessarily eliminating the broader feedback-regulation account. Attachment dimensions are policy moderators, not controller labels ARC does not interpret anxiety and avoidance as fixed controller types. The current formulation assigns them a narrower empirical role: they may explain why the same momentary discrepancy is translated into different regulatory channels. This distinction matters because overt approach, interpersonal distancing, and internal disengagement are not interchangeable manifestations of one scalar action tendency. Existing within-person and dyadic evidence supports attachment-related heterogeneity in responses to relational cues, and interactionist theory emphasizes that these effects depend on context and activation conditions; the specific ARC cross-level interactions nevertheless remain untested (Campbell & Marshall, 2011; Carnelley et al., 2025; Sadikaj et al., 2011; Xie et al., 2025).

This formulation also provides a clear failure mode. If L and U predict only stable mean differences in behavior and do not alter within-person discrepancy-to-action mappings, ARC should retain them as descriptive attachment dimensions while dropping their proposed dynamic policy role.

Positive security is a stronger claim than low insecurity

The secure-base literature supports positive representational and behavioral aspects of security (Crowell et al., 2002; H. S. Waters & Waters, 2006; T. E. A. Waters & Roisman, 2019). Separate literatures also link attachment and dyadic coping to recovery from conflict and physiological stress (Meuwly et al., 2012; Powers et al., 2006; Salvatore et al., 2011).

These literatures justify keeping S as a prespecified candidate rather than dropping positive security from the research program. What remains uncertain is the bridge between these lines: whether a positive secure-base representation predicts recovery after anxiety and avoidance are already modeled.

H3 therefore intentionally adopts an incremental-validity criterion. A zero-order association between S and recovery is not sufficient. Nor is evidence that secure partners recover better when security is operationalized only as low insecurity. The positive-security construct earns promotion to the ARC core only if a separately measured S construct improves a preregistered primary recovery outcome beyond L/U , adds out-of-sample predictive value, and the increment is independently replicated or supported by a second preregistered design. This predictive criterion does not by itself establish a causal effect of S.

Stronger mechanistic interpretation would require experimental or otherwise identification-oriented evidence, and the recovery outcome must distinguish genuine state/function improvement from mere retreat of the desired relational target.

Adult romantic relationships as the empirical target

Much computational attachment work has been developed around infant-caregiver systems or simulation. Adult romantic relationships add different targets, reciprocal agency, relationship-specific histories, explicit communication, autonomy goals, and partner-specific regulation. Autonomy need satisfaction can coexist with relatedness in close relationships rather than representing its simple opposite (Kluwer et al., 2020). ARC therefore treats dyadic reciprocity as a substantive empirical issue rather than an automatic extension from infant models.

Why the framework is deliberately small

ARC retains only L and U as required person-level variables, together with the minimal momentary loop needed to test feedback regulation. S is a prespecified candidate for promotion after H3, not a required construct. M and F are optional moderators.

Relational-control propensity is evaluated in a separate psychometric program, while the recoverability of P/PI/PID and other candidate mechanisms is a separate identifiability question.

These neighboring research programs can inform later extensions of ARC but are not prerequisites for the framework itself. This separation prevents a failure of one speculative DYNAMIC ADULT ATTACHMENT REGULATION component or one mechanistic model from being hidden by reinterpretation of another.

Security and disorganization

Positive secure-base representation and recovery provide a way to test whether security contributes information that cannot be reduced to low insecurity. This is an integration of established secure-base ideas with repeated temporal measurement, not a claim that positive security is newly discovered or already required by ARC.

The disorganization extension is more tentative. Poor recovery, context-residual inconsistency, persistent approach-avoidance conflict, and maladaptive switching are candidate trajectory properties, not definitions of adult disorganization. Their value depends on incremental prediction of independent criteria.

Limitations

The framework has no human validation data. Momentary targets may be reactive to measurement, relationship goals may change rapidly, partner effects create reciprocal causation, and the sampling interval can change the apparent dynamics. Repeated prompts that ask participants to compare perceived and desired relational states may increase discrepancy salience, shift the desired target, or alter regulatory behavior. The pilot therefore treats study-day trends in rt , r * t , and e t as reactivity diagnostics and examines whether primary conclusions are sensitive to temporal-trend adjustment. Such trends cannot uniquely identify measurement reactivity, because genuine relational change is observationally similar, so they are interpreted as a limitation and sensitivity check rather than a correction.

Measurement error in rt and r * t directly contaminates discrepancy. Person-specific processes may also differ enough that group-level coefficients conceal important heterogeneity.

A further limitation is observational equivalence. Multiple computational mechanisms can produce similar action trajectories. ARC therefore prioritizes predictive and discriminating evidence over mechanistic labels.

Immediate empirical priority

The next scientific step is a feasibility pilot centered on H1. If perceived and desired relational states cannot be measured reliably on matched coordinates, or their discrepancy does not predict subsequent action beyond context and action history, the model should be simplified before any large-scale test of moderators or disorganization.

Conclusion

The ARC earns explanatory value only if these dynamic measurements improve reproducible, held-out prediction beyond simpler alternatives. Its first decisive test is therefore empirical rather than computational: whether repeatedly measured relational states and targets predict subsequent regulatory action in human relationships, and whether the proposed discrepancy representation is actually warranted by the data.
