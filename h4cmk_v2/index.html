Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. Some differences come from the ground truth: it lacks p-values with a correction name (“p Bonf = .061”), and counts partial eta squared written with its subscript after it (“η 2 p = .05”), “p = .05”, and some table notes and figure legends (“† p < .10, *p < .05”), which this module leaves out, as non-significant p-values. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

If you want to extend the package to be able to download files from additional data repositories reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Ideological conflict threatens democracy and social cohesion worldwide. Although intergroup contact can reduce prejudice between ideological groups, significant barriers prevent effective contact. In this theoretical review, we propose that intellectual humility (IH)-the awareness of one's intellectual limitations-may help overcome these barriers. We present a framework arguing that IH could promote effective contact through three pathways: (1) increasing other-focused cognitions and behaviors, (2) reducing reflexive defensiveness, and (3) reducing information bias. We review evidence drawn primarily from US horizontal affective polarization research that is consistent with this framework. We also outline four research priorities that are necessary for future work on IH and ideological conflict, including establishing causality through experimental designs, resolving measurement and conceptual ambiguities, testing generalizability across contexts and populations, and demonstrating real-world impact and scalability. This framework and research agenda provide a roadmap for investigating IH as a potential lever for improving intergroup relations across ideological divisions.

[div-01]

Ideological conflicts pose a unique challenge for societies. These conflicts-rooted in deeply held moral worldviews (Brandt et al., 2019)-resist easy resolution and fuel lasting animosity (e.g., Garrett & Bankert, 2020). In the United States (US), ideological conflict has become a defining feature of political life, reflected in rising levels of horizontal affective polarization-animosity between ordinary citizens who hold opposing political views (e.g., Finkel et al., 2020). For example, positive feelings towards political outgroup members have dropped from nearly 50 (out of 100) in the 1970s and 1980s, to around 20 in 2020 (Druckman & Levy, 2022; Iyengar et al., 2019). This animosity is not unique to the US: Polarization is tearing at democracies around the world, from Brazil and India to Poland and Turkey (Carothers & Donohue, 2019; see also Boxell et al., 2024). These divisions have serious consequences. Across 83 democracies, higher levels of political polarization predict greater likelihood of political violence (Piazza, 2023). Beyond the threat of violence, polarization can fracture communities, strain interpersonal relationships, and undermine the foundations of democratic governance (Levitsky & Ziblatt, 2018; Voelkel et al., 2024).

How can we begin to address this animosity between political opponents? Political scientists (Mutz, 2002), psychologists (Allport, 1954), and policymakers agree that positive contact with those with whom we disagree can attenuate ideological conflict. Research on the intergroup contact hypothesis has demonstrated robust benefits across diverse contexts, including between political counterparts (Pettigrew & Tropp, 2006; Paluck et al., 2019; Strother et al, 2021; Voelkel et al., 2024). However, there are significant barriers that prevent both the occurrence and productivity of contact between ideological counterparts. People must first be willing to seek out contact with those who disagree with them, and when contact does occur, it must be characterized by more positivity than hostility for benefits to emerge (Barlow et al., 2012; Paolini et al., 2010). Given these challenges, it is critical to identify factors that can reduce barriers to seeking out contact and engaging constructively during contact interactions.

We propose that intellectual humility (IH)-the awareness of one's intellectual limitations-may be a critical factor for promoting more frequent and constructive contact between people who hold opposing political views. While IH is a growing area of psychological research, to our knowledge there are no comprehensive reviews examining its role in attenuating ideological conflicts (see Porter et al., 2022b for a general IH review; see Sgambati & Ayduk, 2022 for a short review on IH and polarizationfoot_0 ). Because this is a relatively new area of inquiry, this review pursues two major goals. First, we present a theoretical framework for why IH might reduce barriers to effective contact, arguing that IH could work through three pathways: (1) increasing other-focused cognitions and behaviors, (2) reducing reflexive defensiveness, and (3) reducing information bias. We review existing evidence that is consistent with this framework, while emphasizing that this evidence is suggestive rather than conclusive due to its primarily correlational nature and US-centric focus. Second, we identify gaps and limitations in existing research and provide a set of four research priorities for future work. By identifying both the promise and limitations of current research, we provide a clear agenda for determining whether and under what conditions IH can meaningfully reduce affective polarization.

Affective Polarization

Scholars distinguish between two types of political polarization-ideological and affective. Ideological polarization refers to growing distance between political partisans in their policy preferences, though some researchers argue that claims of ideological polarization are overstated (e.g., Fernbach & Van Boven, 2022). By contrast, there is clear evidence for increases in affective polarization-negative emotions toward members of the political outgroup (Druckman & Levy, 2022; Iyengar et al., 2019). Our framework focuses primarily on affective polarization rather than ideological polarization because improving the frequency and quality of contact may help reduce animosity between political opponents. Specifically, we focus on horizontal affective polarization (animosity between ordinary citizens) rather than vertical affective polarization (animosity between citizens and political elites or parties as a whole).

Although these constructs tend to be highly correlated, they are distinct (see Areal & Harteveld, 2024). Horizontal affective polarization encompasses both animosity toward supporters of opposing political parties (party-based affective polarization) and toward those who hold opposing positions on contentious political issues (issue-based affective polarization). Although these forms of polarization have become increasingly intertwined in the US as party identity and issue positions have sorted together (e.g., Newport, 2023), they may be more distinct in other political contexts. In the Research Priorities section, we call for work examining whether IH may also influence ideological polarization and vertical affective polarization, as well as how its effects may differ across political contexts in which party-based and issue-based affective polarization are less closely intertwined.

Affective polarization encompasses diverse forms of animosity and is captured by diverse measurement approaches. Many researchers measure affective polarization using simple feeling thermometers assessing how warm and cold participants feel towards the ingroup and outgroup on 101-point scales. Affective polarization is then calculated as the difference score between the ingroup rating and the outgroup rating, or simply the outgroup rating (Bowes et al., 2020; Finkel et al., 2020; Iyengar et al., 2019; Voelkel et al., 2024). However, researchers also examine theoretically related constructs including social distance (e.g., comfort with one's child marrying a political outgroup member; Iyengar et al., 2012; 2019) and dehumanization of outgroup members (Cassese, 2020; Landry et al., 2024; Martherus et al., 2021).

Despite measurement heterogeneity, converging evidence across various indices reveals concerning levels of ideological hostility. Affective polarization has intensified over time, with the difference in warmth ratings between partisans' own party (out of 100) and the opposing party (out of 100) doubling from around 25 in the 1970s to around 50 in 2020 (Druckman & Levy, 2022; Iyengar et al., 2019). Dislike for one's outgroup now exceeds liking for the ingroup (Finkel et al., 2020). This pattern extends beyond the United States. Examining linear trends of affective polarization over four decades in 12 developed democracies, researchers found that although the US exhibited the largest increase in affective polarization (slope of affective polarization over time: .56), five other countries (Switzerland slope: .51, France slope: .26, Denmark slope: .21, Canada slope: .15, and New Zealand slope: .14) also showed increases (Boxell et al., 2024). In their examination of political polarization across the globe, Carothers and O'Donohue (2019) describe it as a "widespread phenomenon, with common negative consequences for democracy across diverse national contexts" (p. 1). Providing examples that span the globe, they note that many countries in Latin America (e.g., Bolivia, Brazil, Colombia, Venezuela), South and Southeast Asia (e.g., Bangladesh, India, Indonesia), the Middle East (e.g., Egypt, Israel, Syria, Turkey), Africa (e.g., Cameroon, Côte d'Ivoire, Uganda), and Europe (e.g., France, Great Britain, Poland) are all marked by deep ideological divisions. However, despite the global challenges posed by political polarization, most research on IH and ideological conflict has been conducted in the United States. We therefore focus primarily on the US context while integrating evidence from other national contexts where available. We return to questions of generalizability and the critical need for cross-national research in our discussion of future directions.

Contact as a Solution

Research on the intergroup contact hypothesis-that interactions between members of different groups will reduce intergroup prejudice-began in the 1930s through research on racial desegregation (Dovidio et al., 2003). After several studies showed preliminary evidence that intergroup contact reduces prejudice, Allport (1954) developed the intergroup contact hypothesis, arguing that intergroup contact increases accurate knowledge of outgroup members and counteracts stereotypic views, thereby reducing prejudice.

Since its conception, intergroup contact has become central to research on conflict resolution. By 2006, Pettigrew and Tropp identified 515 studies examining the benefits of intergroup contact, finding consistent reductions in prejudice across diverse contexts. Paluck and colleagues (2019) reassessed the evidence with 27 studies and found that, while effects varied in magnitude, the benefits of intergroup contact were robust. Contact scholars have expanded on Allport's theory by identifying mechanisms through which contact works (Pettigrew & Tropp, 2008) and developing alternative methods of establishing contact when direct interaction is not feasible. For example, Wright and colleagues (1997) found that knowing an ingroup member who has a friendly relationship with an outgroup member reduces prejudice (extended contact; see also Cameron et al., 2011; Cernat, 2011; Gómez et al., 2011; Paolini et al., 2004; Zhou et al., 2019). Others showed that merely imagining having contact with outgroup members reduces prejudice (imagined contact; Harwood et al., 2011; Husnu & Crisp, 2011; Miles & Crisp, 2014; Vezzali et al., 2012). Finally, recent work has found similar benefits from virtual contact over a video call (Bruneau et al., 2021; Maunder et al., 2019; White et al., 2015).

Some evidence suggests that contact can also improve attitudes between political outgroup members (Mutz, 2002; Strother et al., 2021; Warner & Vallamil, 2017). In a recent mega-study testing 25 interventions designed to reduce political hostility (Voelkel et al., 2024), the intervention with the largest impact on affective polarization was based on intergroup contact (Cohen's d on partisan animosity of -.53). Participants watched a video of U.K. dyads with opposing views on climate change, feminism, and transgender identity cooperate on a task before engaging in an open-minded discussion over a beer. Participants who viewed these positive contact experiences showed substantial decreases in self-reported partisan animosity. In another study, Wojcieszak and Warner (2020) found that extended contact reduced social distance toward outgroup members. Brooks and colleagues (2025) found that exposure to mediated intergroup contact reduced political hostility. Recent work has also employed technologymediated contact that effectively reduces political hostility through anonymous conversations with an outgroup member over an app (reducing affective and issue polarization; Combs et al., 2023) or even mediated by an AI chatbot (increasing willingness to respect outgroup views; Argyle et al., 2023). There is also evidence for the effectiveness of contact in reducing political hostility in other countries including Canada (Amsalem et al., 2022), Denmark (Thomsen & Thomsen, 2023), the UK (Tausch, et al. 2024), and Mexico (Greene et al., 2025).

Despite these successes, we argue that there are barriers that prevent both the occurrence and productivity of contact between ideological counterparts. Put simply, people must be willing to have contact, and, when they do, the contact experience cannot be characterized by strong hostility. For example, in seeking to understand why prolonged contact can sometimes fail to improve intergroup relations, Barlow and colleagues (2012) demonstrated that negative experiences of contact can harm intergroup relations more than positive experiences improve them. Negative contact experiences also make group categories more salient (Paolini et al., 2010) and are more common in areas of ongoing conflict (Guffler & Wagner, 2017). Contact experiences can therefore backfire if they are characterized by stronger negativity than positivity. This is perhaps why Santoro & Broockman (2022) found that contact fails to be effective in the political context when partisans discuss areas of disagreement during their interaction, likely leading to more negativity.

Considering these challenges, it is important to identify factors that can support the effectiveness of ideological contact by reducing the barriers to approaching contact opportunities and engaging constructively with ideological counterparts during contact experiences. Because there are structural barriers that prevent involuntary or spontaneous contact (Paolini et al., 2024), researchers have focused on the predictors of, and barriers to, volitional contact seekingactively seeking out and engaging in intergroup contact (Paolini et al., 2018). Researchers divide these predictors and barriers into three levels: micro-level (e.g., individual motivations to engage in contact, such as self-expansion), meso-level (e.g., intergroup attitudes towards the outgroup; ingroup norms around contact), and macro-level (e.g., misinformation; broader societal norms around contact; Kauff et al., 2021; Paolini et al., 2018; Ron et al., 2017).

We argue that IH is an important micro-level factor that might encourage both more frequent and more productive intergroup contact in this context. In developing this framework, we do not intend to downplay the role of other relevant psychological factors (e.g., Right Wing Authoritarianism; Social Dominance Orientation, Need for Closure, Extraversion). Indeed, many of these have been examined in other reviews (see Turner et al., 2020) and some even correlate with IH (e.g., r = -.28 with Right Wing Authoritarianism; Krumrei-Mancuso, 2018). However, we believe IH deserves focused attention because its emphasis on intellectual fallibility makes it especially well-suited to addressing conflicts rooted in ideological differences. Further, although IH is likely best characterized as a micro-level factor, it may influence how people respond to meso-level and macro-level factors. For example, below we describe how IH is associated with lower susceptibility to misinformation.

In the next section, we first define IH and review key measurement considerations before presenting our theoretical framework for how it might reduce barriers to effective contact.

Intellectual Humility

Research on IH has exploded over the last 15 years, yet scholars continue to debate its definition and measurement. Ballantyne (2021) organizes IH definitions into four categories: attitude management (how we create and hold attitudes), realistic self-assessment (recognizing our inquisitorial fallibility), low self-concern (regulating how intellectually important we think we are), and mixed definitions that combine elements of the other three categories. However, while variation exists across definitions, most definitions of IH share a common theme. Porter and colleagues (2022a) reviewed 18 definitions of IH and found that the majority (11 of 18) focus on recognizing one's intellectual limitations. Other definitions emphasize interpersonal features of IH (8 of 18) and not being overly focused on one's limitations (6 of 18). In the current review, we focus primarily on the limitations-owning definition, where IH involves an awareness of one's intellectual limitations (Leary, 2018; Porter et al., 2022b). Critically, recognizing one's limitations is not the same as having low confidence in one's beliefs, but rather concerns how one holds their beliefs in the face of new evidence or different views (Haggard et al., 2018; Leary, 2018). Additionally, although IH shows theoretical and empirical overlap with related constructs, such as open-mindedness, wisdom, need for closure, authoritarianism, and dogmatism, it remains conceptually and statistically distinct from these constructs (see discussion of distinctiveness in Research Priority 2).

Measurement approaches to IH are similarly diverse. Porter and colleagues (2022a) identified 20 different measures of IH. Several validated scales assess general IH, including the General Intellectual Humility Scale (a unidimensional scale that focuses on awareness of one's intellectual limitations; Leary et al., 2017), the Comprehensive Intellectual Humility Scale (with four factors: independence of intellect and ego, openness to revising one's views, respect for others' views, and lack of overconfidence; Krumrei-Mancuso & Rouse, 2016), the Multi-Dimensional Intellectual Humility Scale (with four factors: open mindedness, modesty, corrigibility, and engagement; Alfano et al., 2017), the Limitations-Owning Intellectual Humility Scale (with three factors: limitations-owning, lacking discomfort, and love of learning; Haggard et al, 2018), and a recent scale that assesses situated intellectual humility across multiple contexts (Rudnev et al., 2026). In addition to general scales, researchers have also developed domainspecific measures of IH, such as the Specific Intellectual Humility Scale for use in domains like politics and religion (Hoyle et al., 2016). This measurement heterogeneity raises significant challenges. As Porter (2023) notes, the field faces "jingle-jangle" problems, where measures with similar labels tap different constructs (jingle fallacy) or where measures with different labels tap the same construct (jangle fallacy).

The multidimensional nature of several scales also raises questions about whether IH is best understood as a unitary construct or a collection of related but distinct facets. Moreover, general trait measures may function differently from state-level or domain-specific assessments.

Whereas trait IH reflects a stable dispositional tendency, state IH can vary considerably across situations and topics (Rudnev et al., 2026; Zachry et al., 2018). This distinction connects to broader debates about how virtues should be measured in psychology. For example, the current approach of treating higher IH scores as better may not align with how virtues function in Aristotelian ethics, where virtues are defined by situation-specific optimality-the right amount of a quality in a given context (Ng & Tay, 2020). From this perspective, trait-level assessments do not effectively capture this situational variability. Further, current assessments-whether state or trait-do not distinguish optimal from insufficient or excessive levels of IH across contexts.

Given these conceptual and measurement issues, we face a challenge in synthesizing the existing literature. To manage this challenge, we take a pragmatic approach. Despite variation across measures, they tend to correlate strongly with each other and predict similar outcomes (e.g., Bowes et al., 2020; Koetke & Schumann, 2025), suggesting they assess a common underlying construct. Accordingly, we document the specific measures used in each study we review (Table S1) but treat findings as relevant to IH broadly construed. However, we acknowledge that this approach has limitations and return to measurement challenges in our discussion of future directions.

The Promise of IH for Improving Ideological Contact: Theoretical Predictions and Supportive Evidence

We now turn to our central question: Why might IH reduce barriers to effective ideological contact? We propose IH as an independent predictor that promotes more frequent and constructive contact through three pathways: (1) increasing other-focused cognitions and behaviors, (2) reducing reflexive defensiveness, and (3) reducing information bias. For each pathway, we develop the theoretical rationale for why IH should reduce specific barriers to contact, then review existing evidence that is consistent with these theoretical predictions. We organize our review around eight specific demonstrations of how IH might manifest within these three pathways (see Figure 1 for an overview). These demonstrations are not intended to represent distinct facets of the construct of IH, but rather specific cognitions, motives, and behaviors that IH might predict. Although not an exhaustive list of correlates and effects of IH in the political domain, these demonstrations illustrate a framework through which we can understand the benefits of IH for attenuating political conflict through more productive contact.

Notably, these three proposed pathways should both increase the frequency of contact (by making people more willing to seek it out) and improve the quality of contact (by promoting more constructive engagement). Although some demonstrations are more directly linked to contact-seeking (e.g., demonstration 1 explicitly examines motivation to seek contact) while others focus primarily on what happens during contact (e.g., conflict resolution), we view these as interconnected rather than discrete processes. For instance, having more benevolent views of the outgroup should both increase willingness to initiate contact and improve the tenor of interactions when they occur. Moreover, these processes might create positive feedback loops, where more constructive initial contact experiences might increase willingness to seek future contact, while greater contact frequency provides more opportunities to practice and refine constructive engagement skills. In this way, to the extent that IH can promote any of these pathways, it might set in motion self-reinforcing cycles that compound over time.

Before reviewing the framework and evidence, several caveats are essential. First, most studies we review are cross-sectional and correlational (see Table S1), limiting causal inferences and keeping questions regarding directionality and third variables open. Second, most evidence comes from US samples, raising questions about generalizability to other nations and ideological conflict contexts. Third, wherever possible, we present evidence from research directly examining IH in the political domain. However, we also draw on evidence from ideologically similar domains, such as religion or politicized science topics, and, in some cases, from nonideological interpersonal contexts. Fourth, the strength of evidence varies considerably across the eight demonstrations, from commonly replicated findings to preliminary single studies. Finally, questions remain about whether IH is prevalent or similarly beneficial among those with the most extreme political beliefs, or whether the benefits of IH fail to extend to those who are most polarized. We return to this critical boundary condition in our discussion of research priorities.

Given these caveats, we provide an evidence strength index in Figure 1 and pertinent methodological details and effect sizes in Table S1 to help readers evaluate the empirical support for each demonstration. We encourage readers to interpret the evidence as suggestive of the potential of IH rather than as definitive support for its effects. Note. "Moderate" indicates some support but either limited replication, mixed findings, or findings from outside the political domain. Pathway 1: Increasing Other-Focused Cognitions and Behaviors

Ideological conflict erodes people's willingness to engage constructively with opposing viewpoints. Political sectarianism-the "tendency to adopt a moralized identification with one political group and against another" (Finkel et al., 2020, p. 533)-encourages negative stereotyping, avoidance, and hostility toward outgroup members. In contemporary political discourse, political animosity has become normative and socially rewarded, further discouraging genuine engagement with political opponents (Iyengar et al., 2019). IH shows promise in diminishing this hostility and avoidance. Researchers theorize that IH acts as "social oil" for interpersonal interactions, helping interactions proceed more smoothly and with less conflict (McElroy et al., 2014; Van Tongeren et al., 2019). Empirical work consistently shows that those higher in IH exhibit more prosocial and other-focused orientations (e.g., Porter et al., 2022b). This might occur because, through recognizing their own limitations, people with higher IH place more value on the experiences and perspectives of others. This other-focused orientation might be particularly crucial when interacting with political outgroup members, motivating people to consider different views while avoiding an inflated sense of self that places them and their group above others. We identify four demonstrations of how IH might manifest in other-focused cognitions and behaviors in the context of political contact: higher motivation to seek out contact, more benevolent cognitions toward the outgroup, increased empathic effort, and better conflict resolution.

Higher Motivation to Seek Out Contact

A fundamental barrier to positive intergroup contact is simple avoidance. In general, we observe low levels of natural contact between members of different political groups and high levels of homophily-the preference to affiliate with similar others (Goldenberg et al., 2022).

People report discomfort with being close to ideological outgroup members (e.g., their children having an inter-party marriage; Iyengar et al., 2012), prefer to date those who share their views (Huber & Malhotra, 2017), and avoid political outgroup members on social media (Barberá et al., 2015). People also engage in ideological migration-moving from ideologically discordant neighborhoods into concordant ones (Gimpel & Hui, 2015; Motyl et al., 2014)-further reducing the probability that they will spontaneously encounter ideological outgroup members.

Why might IH reduce this avoidance? If recognizing one's intellectual limitations leads

people to value diverse perspectives as opportunities for learning, those higher in IH might be more willing to engage with ideological outgroup members. Rather than seeing these interactions as worthless or threatening, people with higher IH might approach these contact experiences with curiosity. Consistent with this prediction, people with higher IH indicate greater willingness to befriend political outgroup members who disagree with them even on contentious issues, such as abortion (Stanley et al., 2020). Similarly, socio-political IH is associated with greater interest in and appreciation for political discussions (Krumrei-Mancuso & Newman, 2020; 2021), while cultural humility is associated with greater appreciation for diversity and commitment to intercultural competence in the adjacent ideological domain of religion (Paine et al., 2022).

Moreover, in longitudinal work, IH measured at time one predicts having a more politically heterogeneous social network at times two through four, which in turn predicts less attitude polarization six to eight months later at time five (Sgambati & Ayduk, 2023). Preliminary evidence from other national contexts is also consistent with these patterns. In Germany, IH is associated with more approach behaviors toward political outgroup members (e.g., wanting to spend time with them; Knöchelmann & Cohrs, 2025).

More Benevolent Cognitions about the Outgroup

Negative perceptions of outgroup members create substantial barriers to constructive contact. Because people identify with and tie their self-esteem to their groups, they are motivated to reject other groups and bolster their group's standing (Tajfel & Turner, 2004). In political contexts, people view outgroup members as stereotypical and homogeneous (Crawford et al., 2013; Graham et al., 2012), lacking intelligence and moral character. These negative stereotypes contribute to mistrust (Chambers & Melnyk, 2006) and affective polarization (Ahler, 2014), consequently leading to both an avoidance of contact and stressful, hostile contact interactions (Paolini et al., 2004; 2016; Trawalter et al., 2009).

When people recognize that their own understanding is limited and potentially biased or flawed, they might become less certain that disagreement necessarily signals foolishness or immorality in others. Those with higher IH might therefore be less likely to assume the worst about political outgroup members, attributing their perspectives to different values or reasoning processes rather than to intellectual or moral inadequacy. Supporting this possibility, people higher in IH, socio-political IH, and political humility show greater tolerance toward ideological outgroups and are more likely to identify positive aspects of another person with whom they disagree on important ideological topics (Hodge et al., 2021). People higher in IH are also more likely to see those who disagree with them on ideological topics as intelligent (Stanley et al., 2020), credible, and trustworthy (Krumrei-Mancuso & Newman, 2021), and are more likely to ascribe morality and positive character traits to political outgroup members (Bowes et al., 2020). Moreover, those higher in IH make more respectful attributions for why people disagree with them in the context of both intellectual and personally-relevant political disagreements (Porter & Schumann, 2018). Extending to religious ideological conflict, IH is associated with religious tolerance (Lubis & Sianipar, 2022; see Choe et al., 2023 for a review) and more positive views of those who disagree with them on religion (Hopkin et al., 2014; Rodriguez et al., 2019).

Increased Empathic Effort

Another major barrier to effective contact is that people have little motivation to empathize with those with whom they disagree. Empathy is malleable and motivated (Cameron et al., 2019; Zaki, 2014), and people often choose to disengage from opportunities to empathize with members of ideological outgroups (Schumann et al., 2014). This empathy avoidance has consequences for both whether contact occurs and how productive it is when it does occur, which is unfortunate because empathizing with others predicts altruistic behavior (Batson & Shaw, 1991) and decreased stereotyping (Galinsky & Moskowitz, 2000). The failure to empathize during contact can therefore exacerbate negative interpretations and reduce the benefits of the contact experience.

Because people high in IH recognize the limitations of their own knowledge, they might direct their attention toward understanding others' perspectives, particularly those who bring a different perspective than their own. This possibility is similarly supported by research linking IH to cognitive flexibility (Zmigrod et al., 2019). Rather than dismissing alternative views without consideration, people high in IH may have the cognitive flexibility to invest effort in understanding why others hold different positions to gain a more complete understanding of a topic. This greater empathic motivation should make outgroup contact more appealing and less threatening. Further, when interactions do occur, empathic effort should increase their quality by orienting people toward listening and learning about the outgroup's perspective.

Consistent with this prediction, IH is associated with more empathic responses toward people who express opposing views, including greater willingness to listen to someone who disagrees with them on intellectual or personally relevant political issues (Porter & Schumann, 2018). In the Israeli-Palestinian conflict context, people higher in IH exhibit greater empathic accuracy towards ideological outgroup members (Lehmann et al., 2025). Outside the political context, IH is associated with trait empathy (Krumrei-Mancuso, 2017) and greater empathic effort in interpersonal conflict situations (Ludwig et al., 2022). People higher in IH are also less likely to want to aggress against someone who challenges their religious beliefs (Van Tongeren et al., 2016) and feel closer to those with whom they have had conflict (Peetz & Grossmann, 2021), possibly because of greater empathy for these counterparts. More generally, they are more likely to endorse prosocial values such as altruism (Krumrei-Mancuso, 2017; Jongman-Sereno et al., 2023), suggesting a general orientation toward others.

Better Conflict Resolution

During interactions with ideological outgroup members, people might do or say things to each other that are offensive (e.g., losing one's temper; talking over the other person; disrespecting the other person's views). How people respond to these transgressions matters considerably for whether the relationship can be repaired and whether future contact will be pursued and will be productive. Apologies and forgiveness are important reparative responses that predict a host of positive outcomes after a perceived offense (Blatz & Philpot, 2010; Exline et al., 2007; Exline & Baumeister, 2000). However, both responses are difficult and might be especially challenging in ideological contexts. Barriers to apologizing include wanting to maintain power and a positive self-image, or lacking empathy toward the person one has offended (Leunissen et al., 2014; Okimoto et al., 2013; Schumann, 2018). Similarly, forgiveness is especially difficult when offenses are perceived as severe or intentional or when victims experience anger or low empathy toward the transgressor (Fehr et al., 2010)-perceptions and emotions likely to be present during adversarial ideological exchanges. Thus, poor conflict resolution during or after contact might escalate hostility toward the outgroup member and reduce motivation for future contact with that individual or other political outgroup members.

People with higher IH should be better equipped to constructively manage their conflicts due to their awareness of their potential fallibility and greater empathic motivation. They might find it less threatening to acknowledge their own mistakes and more natural to extend understanding to others who have erred. Supporting this possibility, IH is associated with both apologies and forgiveness. In two studies, people who scored higher on IH offered higher quality apologies in response to imagined interpersonal offenses, including an intellectual disagreement (Ludwig et al., 2022). More studies have examined the benefits of IH for forgiveness. In the political domain, political humility is associated with greater forgiveness toward people with whom participants had experienced a political conflict (Hodge et al., 2020). IH is also associated with forgiveness in the adjacent ideological domain of religion. People higher in IH are more likely to forgive a religious leader who committed a transgression (Hook et al., 2015; McElroy et al., 2014), and IH is associated with forgiveness for past religious conflict (Zhang et al., 2015).

In a longitudinal study, state-level IH also predicted forgiveness during non-ideological interpersonal conflicts (Grossmann et al., 2016). Interestingly, victims are also more likely to forgive transgressors whom they perceive as having high IH (Hook et al., 2015 , Zhang et al., 2015), suggesting that displays of IH may facilitate reconciliation. Beyond apologies and forgiveness, IH is also associated with reporting more frequent use of constructive conflict management strategies (e.g., communicating openly) in non-ideological conflicts (Koetke et al., 2024).

Pathway 2: Reducing Reflexive Defensiveness

Even when contact between political opponents occurs, defensiveness can undermine its quality by making people less willing to consider opposing perspectives. One reason for this defensiveness is that people often perceive their ideological beliefs as objective and universal truths (Ross & Ward, 1995). When these beliefs are challenged, people often feel a strong inclination toward defensiveness-a tendency to dogmatically protect one's views and reject contrasting perspectives without consideration. IH may reduce this reflexive defensiveness. Some definitions of IH specifically define it as a "non-threatening" awareness of their limitations (Krumrei-Mancuso & Newman, 2020; Krumrei-Mancuso & Rouse, 2016), and one measure includes a subscale assessing the separation of one's intellect and one's ego (CIHS; Krumrei-Mancuso & Rouse, 2016). If people can recognize that their understanding is limited without experiencing this as a threat to their selfworth, they should feel less defensive in response to discordant information and perspectives (Bowes et al., 2022; Krumrei-Mancuso et al., 2020; Porter et al., 2022b). This reduced defensiveness should manifest in two key ways: greater open-mindedness when encountering different perspectives and increased willingness to revise one's beliefs in light of new information.

Increased Open-mindedness

A lack of open-mindedness toward other viewpoints can undermine constructive contact between political opponents, making them less willing to listen to and engage in meaningful dialogue with those who disagree with them. One reason for this close-mindedness is that people often perceive their own views as universally correct and opposing views as universally incorrect (Skitka et al., 2015). Such perceptions are associated with reduced willingness to compromise (Mullen & Skitka, 2006; Ryan, 2017), more negative judgements of opposing views (Tappin & McKay, 2019), and avoidance of views that feel threatening to one's moral identity (Cohen et al., 2000).

Because people high in IH recognize that their knowledge is limited, they should be more open to considering alternative viewpoints. Rather than immediately rejecting views that differ from their own, they may approach such views with curiosity and see disagreement as an opportunity to refine their thinking. However, it is important to acknowledge that openmindedness has strong conceptual overlap with IH, and some measures of IH include openmindedness as a subscale (e.g., the CIHS; Krumrei-Macuso & Rouse, 2016). This raises the question of whether IH predicts open-mindedness, whether open-mindedness is a component of IH, or whether they are overlapping elements of a broader metacognitive network (Grossmann et al., 2020; Smith et al., 2025). We treat open-mindedness here as a likely behavioral outcome of IH (Porter et al., 2022b), though we acknowledge that the conceptual boundaries between these constructs remain unclear (see later discussion of this issue in Research Priorities section).

Keeping this conceptual caveat in mind, IH is correlated with a constellation of characteristics that capture greater open-mindedness, including open-minded thinking (Krumrei-Mancuso et al., 2020), openness to experience (Hodge et al., 2021; Porter & Schumann, 2018), general openness and epistemic curiosity (Leary et al., 2017), intellectual engagement (Krumrei-Mancuso et al., 2020), less close-mindedness (Haggard et al., 2018), and need for cognition (Leary et al., 2017; Krumrei-Mancuso et al., 2020). Beyond more generalized characteristics and traits, IH is associated with greater behavioral openness to learning about opposing political perspectives (Porter & Schumann, 2018). In the religious context, IH is associated with less religious fundamentalism (Hodge et al., 2019).

Greater Willingness to Revise Beliefs

The fruitfulness of contact interactions depends on people's willingness to revise their beliefs about the outgroup. People enter contact experiences with assumptions and stereotypes about ideological outgroup members (Crawford et al., 2013; Graham et al., 2012). The benefits of contact are limited if people are unwilling to revise their perceptions of the outgroup based on what they learn during the interaction (Stathi et al., 2017). These revisions can open people up to future contact (Paolini et al., 2018) and set the tone for more productive future contact due to lower misperceptions and hostility.

Recognizing one's intellectual limitations may soften the threat of admitting one was wrong. Those higher in IH might view belief revision as intellectual growth rather than personal failure, making them more willing to update their views when contact experiences provide new information about outgroup members.

Existing research supports the prediction that people with higher IH show greater willingness to revise their viewpoints in the face of new evidence. IH and socio-political IH are associated with openness to changing one's views on contentious political (Krumrei-Mancuso & Newman, 2020; 2021) and religious (Rodriguez et al., 2019) topics after hearing different opinions. People with higher IH are more accepting of those who change their views on political issues (Bowes et al., 2022; Leary et al., 2017). Outside of politics, IH is associated with changing one's views in response to strong evidence (Leary et al., 2017), and, more broadly, people with higher IH tend to hold mindsets that orient them toward constantly learning and revising, including growth mindsets towards their knowledge and intelligence (Porter and Schumann, 2018) and mastery goals in challenging learning contexts (Krumrei-Mancuso et al., 2020; Porter et al., 2020). These mindsets should facilitate belief revision when people encounter information that challenges their current views.

Pathway 3: Reducing Information Bias

People tend to seek out information that conforms to their ideological beliefs, a pattern that can shape their perceptions of political outgroups even before they encounter them. This selective exposure further entrenches people in their stereotypes and meta-perceptions of the ideological outgroup. It also limits the common ground people have when entering a contact interaction, as they may base their views on different and often biased information sources (Barthel et al., 2016). These distorted information environments fuel misperceptions that make people less willing to seek out contact and less able to engage constructively when contact does occur. However, in recognizing the limitations of their knowledge, people with high IH prioritize accuracy of knowledge rather than confirmation of existing beliefs (Koetke et al., 2023; Porter & Schumann, 2018). We identify two demonstrations of how IH might reduce biased information seeking in this context: more balanced information consumption and greater discernment of misinformation.

More Balanced Information Consumption

People are biased in the information they seek out and consume, likely amplifying stereotypes of political outgroups and reducing contact likelihood. They engage in selective news exposure, preferring sources that confirm their ideology (e.g., Knobloch-Westerwick & Meng, 2009). People also engage in motivated reasoning and myside bias to protect their views (Kunda, 1990; Stanovich et al., 2013) and tend to only trust sources of information that confirm their beliefs (Jurkowitz et al., 2020).

These biases are amplified by social media. People curate their online experiences to encounter ideologically concordant views, creating echo chambers (Barberá et al., 2015; Boutyline & Willer, 2017). For example, Mosleh and colleagues used a social media experiment to show that people were almost three times more likely to follow back someone who shared their political identity than someone who did not (2021). They are also more likely to share emotional, moral, and outrageous content (Brady et al., 2017)-often among those who also share their views (Gentzkow & Shapiro, 2011)-contributing to increased polarization (Bail et al., 2018). These biased social media experiences and news diets make people more rigid in their own views and more likely to discount opposing perspectives (Guess et al., 2021). They also fuel misperceptions about the degree of division among the electorate and the number of outgroup members who espouse extreme views (Wilson et al., 2020). These biases thwart the motivation to seek out outgroup contact and contribute to hostile contact experiences.

Given their prioritization of accuracy and recognition that their own views might be inaccurate, people high in IH should be less threatened by encountering discordant views and more interested in understanding the full landscape of perspectives on an issue. Consistent with this prediction, IH is associated with greater news literacy and media proficiency, including using more sources when searching for information and being more comfortable when searching for information (Gorichanaz, 2021). People higher in IH are also more likely to use and trust reputable news sources for information about politicized topics (Koetke et al., 2022; Ryu et al., 2023). Moreover, IH is correlated with spending more time reading statements that express ideologically inconsistent views (Deffler et al., 2016; Porter & Schumann, 2018) and reacting less negatively to these views (Hopkin et al., 2014). Relatedly, those higher in IH are more likely to support free speech for ideological outgroups (De keersmaecker et al., 2021) and are more likely to appreciate balanced (rather than biased) views on general issues (Leary et al., 2017).

Greater Discernment of Misinformation

A closely related barrier to productive contact is political misinformation-false or misleading information about political figures, systems, or events which may further lead to avoidance of political outgroup members. Political misinformation is rampant and dangerous (Lazer et al., 2018; Van Bavel et al., 2021). Misinformation contributes to inaccurate assumptions about ideological outgroups, including stereotyped characterizations and exaggerated perceptions of their political positions (Ahler & Sood, 2018; Fernbach & Van Boven, 2022). These distorted perceptions might lead people to avoid ideological outgroup members whom they believe are immoral or hostile to their group (Lees & Cikara, 2021; Moore-Berg et al., 2020). These perceptions also increase support for political violence (Mernyk et al., 2022) and outgroup dehumanization (Landry et al., 2022).

If recognizing one's intellectual limitations increases motivation for accuracy over confirmation, those higher in IH should be more careful about verifying information. People high in IH should invest effort in vetting news and information to increase the probability that their beliefs are accurate and based on solid evidence, potentially leading to better discernment of true versus false claims.

Existing research suggests that people with higher IH are better able to discern the accuracy of misinformation (Bowes & Tasimi, 2022; Prike et al., 2024), possibly because IH helps people recognize the strengths in arguments (Leary et al., 2017; Montez & Harwood, 2025). People higher in IH are also more likely to investigate misinformation that they encounter. IH predicts self-reported and behavioral investigation (i.e., fact checking) of polarized COVID-19 misinformation (Koetke et al., 2022) and both ideologically concordant (aligned with one's ideology) or discordant (counter to one's ideology) political misinformation headlines (Koetke at al., 2023). Notably, these investigative behaviors improve people's ability to discern which headlines are fake (Koetke et al., 2023), suggesting that people with higher IH may acquire more accurate perceptions of the outgroup over time.

Possibly because people higher in IH are more discerning of misinformation, they are also more likely to side with scientific evidence on polarized issues. Higher IH scores are associated with less belief in pseudoscience and less conspiratorial ideation (Bowes & Tasimi, 2022). IH is also associated with more positive attitudes towards vaccines (Preston & Khan, 2024; Senger & Huynh, 2021), greater willingness to vaccinate (Huynh & Senger, 2021), stronger likelihood of social distancing during the COVID-19 pandemic (Koetke et al., 2021; Jongman-Sereno et al., 2023; Ryu et al., 2023), and greater trust in science (Koetke et al., 2021; Plohl & Musil, 2023). Critically, these associations remain significant when controlling for political orientation (e.g., Plohl & Musil, 2023; Preston & Khan, 2024).

Research Priorities

The evidence reviewed above suggests that IH may offer one solution to reducing horizontal affective polarization. The research community has made impressive progress over the past 15 years, establishing clear correlational patterns and beginning to develop promising intervention approaches. Nevertheless, fundamental methodological and theoretical questions remain unresolved. Does IH causally improve pathways to more effective contact? What distinguishes IH from related constructs, and how should it be conceptualized and measured?

Under what circumstances might IH be ineffective or perhaps even counterproductive? Can it be reliably cultivated to produce lasting improvements in intergroup relations? The current evidence base-while promising-has limitations that constrain causal inferences, generalizability, and our theoretical understanding of how and when IH shapes contact processes. We now outline four critical research priorities that address these limitations through methodological advances and theoretical development, with the goal of strengthening the evidence base and deepening our understanding of the potential IH holds for improving intergroup relations across ideological divides (see Table 1 for summary of Research Priorities).

Priority 1: Establishing the Causal Role of IH in Ideological Contact

Perhaps the most pressing priority is to move beyond correlational evidence to establish whether IH causally influences intergroup outcomes. Most studies reviewed here are crosssectional and correlational (see Table S1), leaving open critical questions about causality and directionality. People who score higher on IH tend to be more empathic, more curious about diverse viewpoints, and less defensive to opposing perspectives. But this pattern is also consistent with alternative explanations. For example, consider the association between IH and political misinformation discernment (Bowes & Tasimi, 2022; Koetke et al., 2023). Although IH might cause people to be more discerning of false information, this association might also be explained by third variables such as need for cognition or political orientation (e.g., both misinformation discernment and IH show modest associations with liberal orientation; Guess et al., 2021; Koetke & Schumann, 2025). Alternatively, it is possible that people who are better at discerning misinformation become more intellectually humble over time through continual discovery of the prevalence of false information. While some studies control for likely third variables such as political orientation, correlational designs cannot definitively rule out alternative accounts. Without experimental evidence demonstrating that increases in IH produce changes in key outcomes, we cannot determine whether IH represents a causal lever for improved ideological contact. Moving forward, researchers could pursue several complementary approaches to establishing the causal influence of IH.

Developing and validating experimental manipulations. First, researchers need to develop experimental manipulations that reliably increase IH and test whether these increases improve intergroup outcomes. Here we make two distinctions. First, IH has been conceptualized as both a trait (consistent across situations) and a state (specific to a context or situation; Zachry et al., 2018). Second, we distinguish between brief experimental manipulations that aim to momentarily increase IH for the sake of establishing causal connections (aiming for state-level changes), and more in-depth interventions that aim to durably increase IH (aiming for trait-level changes). In this section, we focus on brief experimental manipulations that likely impact statelevel IH, and return to more extensive interventions in Priority 4. Several approaches for such manipulations show promise, including growth mindset interventions (Porter & Schumann, 2018), fallibility salience exercises (Koetke et al., 2023), self-affirmations (Hanel et al., 2023), and self-distancing techniques (Grossmann et al., 2021). However, these manipulations often have modest effects and have only been tested on a limited range of outcomes in relatively few studies, with minimal replication across independent labs. Moreover, a critical challenge is ensuring that manipulations increase IH rather than work through unintended mechanisms. For instance, Crawford & Ruscio (2021) found that asking people to explain policy mechanisms in detail-which could theoretically induce IH by heightening awareness that one does not understand policy details-did not affect political outcomes. However, it is unknown whether this manipulation actually increased IH even momentarily, as IH was not measured and a similar manipulation in a different study did not enhance IH relative to a control (Knöchelmann et al., 2025). Similarly, although interventions that target other constructs (e.g., growth mindset, selfaffirmation) have been shown to increase IH, these approaches make it difficult to determine whether subsequent changes in outcomes are driven by increases in IH or by other psychological processes affected by the interventions. Future experimental work must include manipulation checks, measure potential alternative mechanisms to demonstrate the effects are attributable to changes in IH specifically, and prioritize replication to establish the reliability of promising manipulations.

Strengthening longitudinal designs.

Beyond experiments, correlational designs can also be improved through more rigorous longitudinal approaches. Establishing genuine within-person change requires at least three measurement occasions per person to capture trajectories (Grossmann et al., 2025), and most longitudinal work reviewed here falls short of this standard.

Moreover, researchers might leverage naturally-occurring variation-such as being assigned a politically diverse roommate, participating in dialogue programs, or relocating to a politically heterogeneous neighborhood-to test whether experiences theorized to enhance IH predict IH gains over time, and whether those gains predict improvements in intergroup outcomes. While such designs cannot definitively speak to causality, they provide stronger evidence than crosssectional correlations by establishing temporal ordering. A longitudinal approach will also allow researchers to better understand core theoretical questions such as how stable IH is across the lifespan, how IH changes co-occur with societal changes (e.g., changes in societal polarization), and whether changes in IH predict or fail to predict subsequent changes in other constructs of interest (e.g., attitudinal extremity, open-mindedness, etc).

Priority 2: Resolving Measurement and Conceptual Ambiguities

A second critical priority is achieving greater clarity around how IH should be measured and how it relates to adjacent constructs. As described earlier, heterogeneity in how IH is defined and measured limits the ability for this body of work to offer a unified understanding of its effects (Porter et al., 2022a). Although IH measures tend to correlate, they often claim to measure different conceptualizations of IH, include different subscales, or vary in focus (e.g., trait vs. state). Moreover, several related constructs with distinct labels exist in the literature. For the field to build a more rigorous body of evidence, it is important to clarify the relationships between these constructs and develop stronger measurement consensus in the following ways.

Testing construct distinctiveness. Resolving these conceptual ambiguities requires both theoretical and empirical work. Some progress has already been made. IH shows theoretical overlap and statistical associations with several related constructs yet appears to capture something distinct. Like IH, open-mindedness (r = .43; Hoyle & Davisson, 2023) places an emphasis on considering alternative views, but IH uniquely emphasizes recognition that the bases of one's reasoning may be "inadequate or untrustworthy" (Hoyle & Davisson, 2023, p. 106), which goes beyond mere openness to other perspectives. Dogmatism (rs = -.20 to -.60; Hoyle & Davisson, 2023; Leary et al., 2017) involves asserting one's opinions as absolute truths and being intolerant of opposing views (Rokeach, 1954). While someone low in dogmatism and someone high in IH might both be willing to admit their opinion is incorrect, the individual high in IH might also be willing to update their belief (Hoyle & Davisson, 2023). Need for closure (r = -.14; Leary et al., 2017) reflects discomfort with ambiguity (Kruglanski et al., 1993), while IH specifically concerns intellectual limitations. Authoritarianism (r = -.28; Krumrei-Mancuso, 2018)-defined by rigid conventionalism, uncritical submission to authority, and aggression toward non-conformists (Adorno et al., 1950)-reflects a broader orientation toward conformity and deference to authority rather than recognition of one's own intellectual fallibility. Finally, wisdom or wise reasoning (Grossmann et al., 2020) may represent a broader metacognitive network of which IH is one component.

Beyond establishing incremental predictive validity, an important theoretical task is to identify whether and why IH influences ideological contact differently or through different psychological processes than related constructs. We propose that recognizing one's intellectual fallibility may be particularly consequential in situations where people encounter challenges to their existing beliefs, potentially reducing their defensiveness and encouraging them to reconsider their assumptions about the beliefs and intentions of political opponents. Future research should test whether these processes help explain IH's effects on ideological contact and whether they distinguish IH from related constructs such as open-mindedness or low dogmatism.

Empirically, future research should more systematically test whether different constructs show divergent patterns. Studies comparing multiple measures simultaneously would help clarify where genuine distinctions exist. For a recent example, Knöchelmann and colleagues (2026) tested the incremental validity of IH on affective polarization beyond several conceptually similar measures (e.g., openness, need for cognition, etc.) in the German context. They found that while some IH scales predict affective polarization over and above these other covariates, others showed unexpected positive associations. These findings underscore the importance of examining the unique predictive contributions of different IH measures.

Determining optimal measurement approaches. Researchers must also determine how best to operationalize IH. Should IH be measured as a unidimensional construct or as multidimensional with distinct subscales? Do domain-specific measures (e.g., political IH) function differently-potentially offering more predictive precision-than general IH? Further, substantial within-person variability exists within IH (Grossmann et al., 2016; Rudnev et al., 2026; Zachry et al., 2018), raising questions about whether trait-level assessments adequately capture the construct or whether state-level or repeated measurements across contexts are necessary (e.g., Hoyle et al., 2016). Some argue that IH is best conceptualized as neither a state nor a trait, but rather a density distribution, consistent with Whole Trait Theory (see Fleeson & Jayawickreme, 2015; Zachry et al., 2018). Relatedly, current approaches typically treat higher IH as universally better, but treating IH as a virtue in an Aristotelian sense suggests it is optimal only when expressed appropriately for the situation (Ng & Tay, 2020). The field would benefit from clearer guidance about the defining features of IH and how best to measure them, facilitating more consistent and cumulative research. At the same time, different research questions may call for different measurement approaches, particularly when assessing stable individual differences versus situational variability or considering the appropriateness of IH expression. Future research should therefore work toward greater measurement standardization while systematically evaluating when alternative approaches are theoretically justified.

Considering the level of analysis.

Recent work distinguishes between individual and collective IH-the "tendency of a collective's members to attend to each other's intellectual limitations and to the limitations of their collective's cognitive efforts" (Krumrei-Mancuso et al., 2025). Our framework focuses on individual-level IH and its role in shaping how individuals navigate contact with political outgroup members. However, collective IH likely also matters, and may be particularly valuable in ideological conflict contexts given that much political thinking and engagement occurs in group settings rather than in isolation. Collective IH might contribute to perceived norms that encourage contact-seeking, promote constructive dialogue during contact, or reduce social penalties for engaging with political outgroup members.

Moreover, collective IH may be more controllable through environmental scaffolding (e.g., assigning a devil's advocate; Krumrei-Mancuso et al., 2025), potentially making it a promising target for intervention. Although the empirical study of collective IH is just beginning, future research should examine how individual and collective IH independently or synergistically shape contact processes.

Priority 3: Identifying When and For Whom IH Promotes Constructive Contact

A third priority is determining the scope and boundaries of the benefits of IH for reducing ideological conflict. Most research reviewed here comes from US samples, raising questions about generalizability to other national and cultural contexts. Moreover, critical questions remain about whether IH functions similarly at different levels of political extremity, across the ideological spectrum, in contexts of power imbalance, and for people of different demographic backgrounds. More generally, it is critical to assess the potential downsides of IH. Roberts and Wood argue that "in the long run, just about everybody will be epistemically better off for having, and having associates who have, epistemic humility" (p. 251, 2003). However, even the most prosocial psychological constructs have downsides in certain contexts (e.g., forgiveness, McNulty, 2011). IH likely requires appropriate application to specific contexts to lead to beneficial outcomes (as might be suggested by a virtue framing; Ng & Tay, 2020) and therefore may backfire when people are intellectually humble in inappropriate contexts (e.g., Koetke & Schumann, 2026). Systematically mapping these boundaries both theoretically and empirically is essential for understanding where IH can meaningfully improve contact and where its benefits may be limited or even counterproductive.

Testing cross-national generalizability.

The US-centric focus in existing research represents a clear constraint to the generalizability of this research for other ideological conflict contexts. Recent research has focused on understanding similarities and differences in interpretations and perceptions of IH across nations and cultures (Christen et al., 2019; Rudnev et al., 2024). Although still few, these studies suggest that IH might be similarly relevant to contact processes across the globe. However, more work is needed to examine whether there are contexts where IH may not show benefits for contact. For example, does IH predict more constructive contact in contexts with long histories of violent conflict or in collectivistic cultures where norms around disagreement and humility may differ? Cross-national collaborations using standardized measures and study designs would help establish whether IH facilitates ideological contact across diverse contexts or primarily within specific cultural and political systems.

Examining boundary conditions related to political extremity.

A critical unresolved question is whether IH is prevalent among-and effective for-those with the most ideologically extreme beliefs. Some evidence suggests curvilinear associations in which IH is lowest among those at the ideological extremes and highest among moderates (Bowes & Tasimi, 2025), whereas other research has found that IH is only weakly and unreliably associated with political identity strength (Koetke & Schumann, 2025). One possible explanation for these divergent findings concerns how political extremity is operationalized. Studies measuring ideological position along "extremely liberal" to "extremely conservative" continuums have found curvilinear associations, whereas those using continuous measures of belief strength or conviction (e.g.,0 = "not at all strong" to100 = "extremely strong") have found weaker or no curvilinear effects.

These findings point to an important theoretical distinction between the extremity of one's political position and the strength of one's political convictions. We propose that strong political convictions are not inherently incompatible with IH: people may be deeply committed to their political beliefs while still recognizing the limitations of their knowledge. The mixed findings reviewed above suggest that IH may be more closely related to ideological extremity than to strength of conviction, but future research is needed to establish whether this pattern is reliable. Clarifying these relationships could help determine whether IH is fundamentally at odds with strong or extreme political commitments or whether they can coexist.

A related but distinct question concerns whether IH is equally effective at reducing political hostility across levels of ideological extremity. This is particularly important because those at the ideological extremes tend to show the highest levels of affective polarization (e.g., Enders & Lupton, 2021). If IH is less prevalent among extremists, its potential to reduce polarization at the societal level may be limited without successful intervention. However, increasing IH in these individuals could be particularly impactful, given their potentially greater levels of political hostility. It therefore remains an important direction for future research to determine whether IH can be successfully cultivated among those with extreme views and whether doing so produces especially pronounced reductions in political hostility (see Priority 4).

It is important to note that most citizens in the US-and likely in other nations-do not hold extreme political beliefs (Brenan, 2025; Hawkins et al., 2019), yet still exhibit substantial hostility toward political outgroups (e.g., Finkel et al., 2020; Martherus et al., 2021). Thus, even if the benefits of IH are concentrated among moderates, addressing affective polarization within this majority would remain a meaningful contribution.

Testing for ideological asymmetries. Future research should also examine whether IH functions similarly across the ideological spectrum. While some evidence suggests symmetry in intolerance across liberals and conservatives in the US (Crawford & Brandt, 2020; Wetherell et al., 2013), more recent research indicates growing asymmetries, with liberals expressing greater hostility than conservatives (Nair et al., 2026). This recent finding reflects the belief among liberals that conservative policies threaten harm to disadvantaged groups (Nair et al., 2026).

Moreover, IH itself shows modest associations with liberal political orientation (Koetke & Schumann, 2025), raising questions about whether its benefits for intergroup contact extend equally across ideological groups. Do the three pathways identified in our framework operate similarly for liberals and conservatives? Although some studies test for moderation by political orientation, systematic examination of this question is lacking. We would predict that, despite ideological differences in baseline levels of IH and affective polarization, IH would have similarly beneficial effects across political orientations, as the psychological processes underlying these pathways are not inherently specific to liberals or conservatives. Consistent with this prediction, in their recent mega-study on reducing ideological conflict, Voelkel and colleagues (2024) found baseline partisan differences in several outcomes (e.g., support for partisan violence) but little evidence that political ideology moderated the effectiveness of their interventions to reduce ideological conflict. We therefore would expect similar levels of symmetry in the effectiveness of IH for encouraging and benefiting from contact.

Examining effects across different forms of polarization. Another important question

is whether IH has different effects across various forms of political polarization. Our framework focuses primarily on horizontal affective polarization, given its relevance for reducing barriers to effective contact between political opponents. However, certain pathways might also be wellsuited to reducing ideological polarization (i.e., divergence in political attitudes and policy preferences). For example, reducing information bias might expose people to more balanced policy information, potentially facilitating greater convergence in their political views. Future research should also examine whether IH can reduce vertical affective polarization. Potential benefits might emerge directly through more open-minded and less biased evaluations of opposing political leaders, or indirectly through reductions in horizontal affective polarization.

For example, more constructive contact with ordinary citizens who hold opposing political views may foster more favorable perceptions of the political leaders who represent them. Consistent with this possibility, Areal and Harteveld found that horizonal and vertical polarization tend to be highly correlated (r's ranging from .71 -.88 depending on the country). It therefore follows that intervening on one of these may meaningfully impact the other.

Additionally, future research might examine whether IH has differential effects on partybased vs. issue-based affective polarization. IH may be particularly effective at reducing animosity rooted in disagreements over specific political issues, where recognizing the limitations of one's knowledge and beliefs could foster greater openness toward opposing viewpoints. By contrast, animosity rooted in partisan identity may be less responsive to IH given the powerful role of social identity and group loyalties (e.g., Iyengar et al., 2019; Van Bavel & Pereira, 2018). Examining these possibilities in political contexts where party affiliation and issue positions are less closely aligned could help clarify whether and how IH operates differently across these forms of affective polarization.

Considering power dynamics and demographics.

Almost no work has examined how IH operates in contexts of power imbalance or how its effects vary by demographics (e.g., gender, race, socioeconomic status) of the people expressing or perceiving IH (but see Priebe & Van Tongeren, 2023; see Michalec and colleagues, 2021 for a similar call for more research on this topic). The benefits of IH might not extend to intergroup conflicts characterized by major power imbalances where marginalized group members might reasonably fear being further exploited by members of the more powerful group if they were to show them openness or empathy. High IH individuals might also be reluctant to engage in openminded or productive contact with members of extreme ideological groups who appear intransigent in their views. Supporting this possibility, research examining IH and prejudice toward different groups in society (e.g., "Elderly") found that those higher in IH displayed more prejudice, potentially because they saw outgroups as less intellectually humble and therefore potentially more rigid in their views (Colombo et al., 2021). Future research should examine whether the benefits of IH depend on relative group status, demographic characteristics, or perceptions of the outgroup's rigidity. Failing to understand these dynamics risks promoting IH in contexts where it may be ineffective or even harmful, particularly for members of marginalized groups.

Priority 4: Demonstrating Real-World Impact and Scalability

The current framework explains how IH may promote more productive ideological intergroup contact through three major avenues. Although substantial work remains to establish IH's causal influence, generalizability, and boundary conditions, existing research also points to IH as a potential target for intervention. However, the field is just beginning to understand whether IH can create meaningful change in naturalistic contexts or whether interventions can be scaled effectively and produce enduring benefits. To determine whether IH can serve as a practical lever for reducing political hostility, research must more carefully assess real-world applicability and intervention scalability and durability.

Moving from lab to field. The evidence reviewed here comes predominantly from selfreport surveys and hypothetical interactions tested with student or online samples (see Table S1).

While these approaches are common and offer substantial advantages (e.g., Stagnaro et al., 2026), they limit our ability to assess the impact of IH in the real world. IH research should better mirror approaches from contact research by using field studies in various educational, workplace, and community settings to test the effectiveness of IH in actual ideological contact situations (e.g., Goldenberg et al., 2017; Paolini et al., 2022; White et al., 2021). Does IH predict more constructive interactions during real political conversations or in ideologically diverse workplace contexts? Do people with high IH serve as bridge-builders in polarized communities?

Moreover, existing research has predominantly focused on IH as an individual characteristic, largely overlooking the dyadic dynamics of real-world interactions. We propose that expressions of IH may encourage greater openness and IH from outgroup interaction partners, potentially creating reciprocal patterns of constructive engagement. For example, acknowledging uncertainty or expressing genuine interest in an opposing perspective may reduce the perceived threat of disagreement, encouraging the other person to respond with greater openness even when they are not dispositionally inclined to do so. Supporting this possibility, research on receptiveness finds that displayed receptiveness (e.g., asking elaboration questions) can elicit more receptive responses from the recipient (Chen et al., 2010; Yeomans et al., 2020).

Similarly, self-reported IH is highly correlated with perceptions of participants' conversation partner's IH (Rodriguez et al., 2019), perhaps suggesting a reciprocal influence. Future research should examine whether and when expressions of IH encourage corresponding behavior from interaction partners, whether such reciprocity improves the quality of ideological contact, and whether repeated exposure to intellectually humble partners can produce more enduring changes in IH. Future research might also examine whether expressions of IH toward the outgroup by ingroup members shape perceived norms surrounding ideological contact, in turn increasing people's willingness to engage constructively with outgroups. We anticipate that this normative influence would be particularly strong when modeled by high-status ingroup members (e.g., Walsh et al., 2018) or when people perceive that expressions of IH are becoming increasingly common (Sparkman & Walton, 2017; Author et al., in prep).

Developing scalable, enduring interventions.

How can IH be cultivated? Here we focus on theory-based interventions to increase IH in lasting ways. Intervening on IH in this way is more difficult than momentarily increasing state level IH for the sake of experimental manipulation (see Hartke et al., 2026 for a recent review). Intervening in an enduring way requires making changes to someone's trait level IH, or at least enduringly impacting how people display IH in certain contexts (e.g., durably increasing IH specifically in the context of political conversations). Some proposed interventions focus on education, teaching people about their intellectual biases or the concept of IH itself. For example, a five-week philosophy course on intellectual biases increased peer-reported (but not self-reported) IH (Meagher et al., 2019), an asynchronous training program about the psychology of conflict and polarization increased IH in randomized control trials (Welker et al., 2023), and completing tasks targeting IH (e.g., modeling IH, celebrating IH) increased IH over the subsequent four months among those motivated to develop their IH (Mendonça et al., 2023). Other approaches have enhanced IH by targeting the psychological threat of acknowledging one's intellectual limitations through self-affirmation exercises (Hanel et al., 2023) or self-distancing techniques (Grossmann et al., 2021; see also Grossmann & Kross, 2014; Kross & Grossmann, 2012). Finally, some research suggests that exposure to new contexts and perspectives-such as through dialogue (Thorson et al., 2025) or living abroad (Li, 2024)-enhances IH by making people aware that their own personal experiences and perspectives are limited.

An important theoretical question is whether these intervention approaches cultivate IH through distinct psychological mechanisms, and if so, whether those mechanisms have implications for the durability and generalizability of their effects. For example, awareness-based interventions might be particularly effective for people who are genuinely unaware of gaps in their knowledge, whereas threat-reduction interventions might be more effective for those who are reluctant to acknowledge their fallibility because doing so threatens their self-image.

While these interventions show promise, determining the best strategies for increasing trait-level IH at scale and with enduring effects remains a key challenge. Moreover, certain interventions might not be similarly effective across different social and ideological groups. For example, some work suggests that IH is modestly associated with more liberal or left-leaning ideologies (Koetke & Schumann, 2025), underscoring the need to test whether IH manipulations and interventions work equally well across the political spectrum. Encouragingly, the dialoguebased intervention noted above produced comparable increases in IH among both liberals and conservatives (Welker et al., 2023). Relatedly, it remains an open question whether IH interventions are effective for the most extreme partisans-those who tend to show the highest levels of affective polarization. Thus, we still have much to learn about how to ethically and enduringly cultivate IH. Future work might consider a mega-study approach-testing a large number of interventions simultaneously (Milkman et al., 2021; Voelkel et al., 2024)-to compare IH interventions and determine which are the most reliable, scalable, and beneficial across different groups (see Knöchelmann et al., 2025 for a smaller scale version of this idea).

Additionally, intervention research should assess individual-level change trajectories rather than relying solely on group-mean comparisons, as individuals may respond heterogeneously to the same intervention. This approach avoids problematic assumptions of ergodicity and reveals for whom interventions work best and under what conditions (Grossmann et al., 2025).

Situating effects within the broader literature.

Intergroup contact research benefits from a large literature of intervention studies with calculable effect sizes, enabling meta-analytic synthesis and comparison across approaches (Paluck et al., 2019; Pettigrew & Tropp, 2006). In contrast, IH intervention work remains preliminary, with few studies testing interventions in intergroup contexts. This gap makes it challenging to assess whether IH interventions represent a particularly promising approach to reducing political hostility or simply one of many modest interventions with similar effects. Future work should consistently report effect sizes to enable meaningful comparisons, and IH researchers should attempt to situate these effects within the broader intergroup contact literature. Moreover, directly comparing IH-based interventions to other prejudice-reduction strategies-possibly through mega-study designs that test multiple approaches simultaneously-would clarify the relative promise of IH and help researchers invest in the most effective approaches.
