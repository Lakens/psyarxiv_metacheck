Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). Statcheck might still miss one-sided tests that are described in another sentence, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. Introduction

The growing uses and applications of generative artificial intelligence (AI) have been changing the actions and academic behaviors of learners and educators in higher education (Freeman, 2025; Watson & Ennion, 2026). AI has introduced new time-efficient ways for doing academic tasks. In the example of completing an essay assignment, AI can help with structuring arguments, synthesizing sources, conducting literature research, and even the writing itself (Freeman, 2025). Asking AI to search and synthesize the most cited and relevant studies related to a research question can be more appealing time-wise, when compared with manually identifying individual related papers and reading through and searching them for the potentially numerous answers to the initial research question. Many similar comparisons can be made for other educational tasks such as writing a specific part of a paper or meticulously preparing a table based on APA.

In terms of how the use of AI has influenced learners so far, previous findings are not encouraging. Fan et al. (2025) highlighted that reliance on generative AI can be conducive to dependence on it, leading to what the authors termed as "metacognitive laziness" (p. 490). It involves not expending the effort in metacognitive processes (e.g., evaluation, orientation) during task revision, that is a prerequisite for deep and durable learning. Similarly, other studies have shown that over-reliance on AI and excessive use of it are associated with a decrease in critical thinking, decision-making, and analytical reasoning (Gerlich, 2025; Tian & Zhang, 2025; Zhai et al., 2024). There seems to be an "efficiency-understanding" trade-off among learners. Abbas et al. (2024) showed that, when students realized that AI assistance can substitute independent effort, it was associated with procrastination and memory loss, alongside poorer academic performance.

The findings in the literature seem to point in the direction that the undesirable influences of AI use on learners mainly take place in the psychological and motivational levels. Importantly, previous findings showing the associations between AI use and diminished engagement entail further exploration on what carries them. If AI use is linked with decreased effort, this potential correlation is unlikely to be direct since tool use is a behavior and not a motive. A plausible account is that the use of AI is related to changes in core motivational appraisals such as whether the authentic learning is still worth doing oneself, and whether one can still do it without AI assistance. Testing such an indirect path requires measures of learning value and self-efficacy explicitly situated in the AI context.

The field lacks the tools to examine these dynamics. At first glance, it might be tempting to think that classical self-efficacy and task value measures already account for AI's presence, since today's students necessarily complete the MSLQ as learners already embedded in an AIrich environment. However, classical measures (e.g., MSLQ; Pintrich et al., 1991) assess the value of completing a course or domain and the self-efficacy of the learner within it. They do not question one's personal capability for learning and the value of it when a tool can complete the required tasks. Classical scales may therefore fail to differentiate between two forms of learning: one in which the learner completes the expected tasks, possibly by letting AI do them, versus one in which the learner values and feels capable of their personal understanding and learning in spite of the significant shortcuts that AI can provide. The question that remains open is whether this conceptual distinction translates into empirically separable constructs. It is yet to be tested whether appraisals of value and capability that are explicitly AI-contextualized are different from the classical, AI-unspecified appraisals.

Current instruments measure aspects such as AI literacy among learners, or how much learners feel capable in their use of AI, or their motivation for learning AI. These instruments are mainly intended to study AI as the objective and outcome of education. What is missing is an arguably more fundamental measurement which is intended to measure learning motivation as an outcome where AI is the context. In this study, we explore whether appraisals of task value and self-efficacy, when explicitly situated in the context of AI availability, are empirically distinguishable from their classical counterparts, and then we introduce and validate two brief measurements for two main constructs: Learning Value in the Context of AI and Learning Self-Efficacy in the Context of AI. It will be done with a large sample of traditional-age and adult undergraduate students.

Theoretical Framework

The two AI-related constructs that are the focus of this study are derived from two motivational components at the center of expectancy-value theory. In expectancy-value theory and its later updated situated version (Eccles & Wigfield, 2002 , 2020), achievement behavior is motivated by two questions on the mind of the learner: "Can I do this task?" (expectation of success) and "Do I want to do this task, and why?" (subjective value). The four compartments of the appraised subjective value include intrinsic value, attainment value, utility value, and cost, which result in how valuable a task or an outcome seems in the eyes of a learner. This dynamic can potentially be fundamentally impacted in a learning environment where generative AI can help perform the educational tasks and prepare related outcomes. Realizing that durable learning is no longer the only way for students to perform and accomplish, along with the fact that there is a new convenient way that speeds up progress can truly impact how meaningful learning is, with all four theoretical aspects of "value" being impacted. The four components of value are not necessarily impacted in the same way when AI is present in the learning context. Utility value seems to be the most vulnerable one. The reason is the appraised instrumental payoff of learning a skill can decrease when the tasks associated with that skill can be performed by AI. Similarly, the cost component is aggravated, since the time and effort invested in durable learning might feel less justified considering the faster path. Intrinsic and attainment values could be diminished less, as they are tied to the personal meaning and the identity relevance of learning. However, these two value dimensions of learning can be diminished in more gradual forms, since people's hierarchy of values could shift towards valuing AI-skills. Here, Learning Value in the Context of AI (LV-AI) can be defined as the perceived value of learning knowledge and skills in a context where AI tools can perform the associated tasks. Hence, LV-AI is not about what a learner believes about AI, it is the new belief of the learner towards learning when AI's shortcut-like facilitations are present.

The other main question, "Can I do this task?" relates to learners' expectations of success and their appraisals of personal capability, a component closely aligned with self-efficacy as described in social cognitive theory (Bandura, 1997). Previous research has repeatedly shown that self-efficacy is among the strongest predictors of academic success (Schneider & Preckel, 2017). The introduction of generative AI has equipped many learners with an enhanced sense of capability and confidence when performing learning tasks and pursuing educational goals.

However, the increased use of this tool has been found as associated with AI dependency, which is in turn associated with weakened critical thinking (Tian & Zhang, 2025). It seems to be indicative of a shift from productive tool use to problematic reliance. To clearly address this phenomenon, we explore Learning Self-Efficacy in the Context of AI (LSE-AI) defined as a learner's appraisal of their ability to learn and perform academically, without relying on AI tools.

Existing Measures and the Measurement Gap

During recent years, different psychometrics measurements have been developed for the assessment of several AI-related features, mainly including literacy, self-efficacy, and competence for using generative AI. The Artificial Intelligence Self-Efficacy Scale (AISES) developed by Wang and Chuang (2024) measures 4 subcategory of confidence in one's ability to use AI including "assistance, anthropomorphic interaction, comfort with AI, and technological skills." Other developed scales are aimed at measuring AI competence self-efficacy (Chiu, Ahmad, & Çoban, 2025; Chiu et al., 2025) and readiness to learn AI (e.g., the AI Learning Readiness Self-Efficacy scale; Oksanen et al., 2026). In all these scales, the capable self is one in which the learner feels more capable of using AI well. However, LSE-AI in this study inverts this referent. The question becomes whether the learner is confident in their individual capability of learning independently even when AI tools are available.

There is also a second group of AI-related educational measurements, which address how much a learner is motivated to learn AI. For example, AI Motivation Scale (AIMS; Li et al. 2025) uses the lens of self-determination and measures motivation to learn with AI in terms of intrinsic, identified, introjected, and external regulation and amotivation. In this measurement, the focus is the learner's engagement with AI as a tool, rather than the value of learning itself when AI is available. To the best of authors' knowledge, the scale that is the closest to the present work is the AI-Motivation and Needs scale (AIM-N; Maska et al., 2026). This scale measures the degree to which a learner perceives learning tasks and degree pursuit as redundant and unnecessary due to the existence of AI and how it can substitute the educational endeavors.

While such redundancy beliefs are conceptually close to the Learning Value in the Context of AI, there are important distinctions between these two constructs. While the redundancy beliefs involve how much AI tools make learning endeavors obsolete and substituted, LV-AI refers to how much these learning activities that lead to deeper development of understanding are still valuable. A learner can consider a skill redundant while still appraising its acquisition as intensely valuable, or vice versa. Moreover, they are grounded theoretically in a different way.

While the AIM-N originated in the self-determination theory and its account of need satisfaction, LV-AI is grounded in expectancy-value theory's account of subjective task value.

The distinction between the AI-contextualized vs. classical task value and self-efficacy lies in the condition where the motivational appraisals take place.

Two features of the added clause explain why the appraisals may come apart. First, value judgments are comparative. Asking whether learning is important invokes the alternative of not learning, which few learners endorse. Asking whether learning is worth it when AI can perform the task invokes the alternative of delegating, which is available, fast, and effective. Second, that comparison is largely a question of cost -the time and effort durable learning requires relative to the shorter route -and cost is the value dimension least represented in classical task value measures. A parallel logic applies to capability. Efficacy appraisals rest heavily on prior mastery experience, and for current students much of that experience has been AI-assisted; asking about capability without AI therefore asks learners to judge themselves against evidence they may have less of.

The distinction rests on what each appraisal takes as its object. Classical task value asks how much a learner values a course or domain; LV-AI asks how much a learner values personally acquiring the capability when a substitute for that capability exists. These are logically independent: a student may regard a program as highly valuable for their career while doubting that they need to learn its content themselves. Classical self-efficacy asks whether a learner expects to succeed in their program; LSE-AI asks whether they can learn and perform without leaning on AI. Success and unaided capability come apart precisely when a capable tool is available. Moreover, classical instruments were developed before generative AI was available to students, so their items leave the tool context unspecified and respondents supply it themselves. A classical score is therefore ambiguous about whether AI availability was considered at all, which is what an explicitly AI-situated item removes.

To summarize, the current measurements are mainly designed to assess learners' motivation for learning how to use AI. They do not examine AI-situated self-efficacy and task value which are the two constructs that gain significant meaning in this new educational era while grounded in expectancy-value theory and social cognitive theory as the core motivational features in learning. To explore the newly situated learning motivation, compare it across groups, and study related interventions, there is the need for developing scales that can measure the value of and confidence in learning when AI can do the work. Such a need becomes more salient considering the increasing evidence outlining the corroding effects of AI reliance (Fan et al., 2025; Tian & Zhang, 2025).

Traditional-Age and Adult Learners

Considering that a large portion of motivational studies take place within the context of undergraduate students, comparing the function of a new instrument among traditional-aged and adult undergraduate students provides insights into the consistency of the measurement.

Commonly defined as students aged 25 and older, adult learners experience different educational paths when compared with traditional-aged students. Such differences exist in terms of role demands, responsibilities associated with being an adult, needs, motivational profiles, and educational histories. These differences can plausibly be relevant to both of the present constructs. While the applications of AI in adult education contexts have increased (Yazdani et al., 2025), the fact that adults have for a longer time experienced pre-AI life (at the time this study is conducted) can be reflected in how they view and judge AI-independent learning, both in terms of capability and value. The present study tests configural, metric, and scalar invariance across traditional-age and adult learners, using a sample recruited to represent both age groups in approximately equal numbers.

The Present Study

In this study we examine whether appraisals of academic task value and self-efficacy when explicitly situated in the context of AI availability are different from the classical counter, developing and validating the Learning Value in the Context of AI and Learning Self-Efficacy in the Context of AI scales in a sample of 882 undergraduate students in the United States of America. The steps taken for the validation process involve exploratory factor analysis, confirmatory factor analysis, reliability estimation, convergent validity, discriminant validity against classical task value and self-efficacy, measurement invariance across student type, and criterion validity. To examine the criterion validity of the Learning Value in the Context of AI and Learning Self-Efficacy in the Context of AI scales, we explore the predictive power of these two constructs for three academic outcome variables including effort expenditure, persistence, and GPA. The predictive power of the two AI-situated motivational variables is examined above and beyond classical task value and self-efficacy, student age type (adult or traditional undergraduate), and frequency of AI use. Exploring the incremental prediction reveals whether the two AI-contextualized motivational beliefs meaningfully add information that the classical self-efficacy and task value do not already carry. Because existing work has not examined what accounts for the association between AI use and academic engagement, we adopt an exploratory basis to explore whether LV-AI and LSE-AI help explain that potential association. Guided by the above, the present study addresses the following research questions: RQ1. Do appraisals of task value and self-efficacy, when explicitly situated in the context of AI availability, form empirically distinguishable factors?

RQ2.

Are the resulting scales internally consistent, and do they demonstrate convergent validity as well as discriminant validity both from one another and from classical task value and self-efficacy?

RQ3. Do the scales function similarly across traditional-age and adult undergraduate students?

RQ4. Do LV-AI and LSE-AI predict academic outcomes (effort expenditure, persistence intention, and GPA) above and beyond classical task value, classical self-efficacy, student age type, and frequency of AI use?

RQ5.

Provided that AI use is associated with academic outcomes, to what extent are these associations accounted for indirectly through LV-AI and LSE-AI?

Method

Data were initially collected from 897 undergraduate students through the online research and data collection platform Prolific in March 2026. For the participant to be eligible to participate in the study, they had to be enrolled in an undergraduate program in the United States.

To ensure that data were representative of both age groups, we recruited approximately similar numbers of participants from each age group. After data cleaning, which will be later described, the final sample included 882 participants with 471 traditional-age students and 410 adult students (there was one case where the age was missing). Participants' age ranged from 18 to 69 years old (M = 27.09). The data collection of this study was done as a part of a bigger survey.

Each participant was compensated with 6 dollars for their participation.

Of the 882 participants, 53.4% were traditional-age students under 25 years of age and 46.5% were adult learners. Regarding gender, there were 463 (52.5%) female, 385 male (43.7%) participants with 24 (2.7%) identifying as non-binary, and 10 preferring not to share. Regarding race and ethnicity, 425 (48.2%) were White, 153 (17.3%) were Black or African American, 117 (13.3%) as two or more races or ethnicities, 97 (11.0%) as Asian, 69 (7.8%) as Hispanic, 8 (0.9%) as another race or ethnicity, 3 (0.3%) as Middle Eastern or North African, and 2 (0.2%) as American Indian or Alaska Native, while 8 (0.9%) preferred not to say. Participants were from different fields of study including engineering or computer science (n = 214, 24.3%), business or management (n = 189, 21.4%), social sciences (n = 133, 15.1%), health sciences (n = 119, 13.5%), humanities (n = 90, 10.2%), natural sciences (n = 85, 9.6%), education (n = 29, 3.3%), and law (n = 22, 2.5%). 330 participants (37.4%) were first-generation students.

Participants completed the online survey on Qualtrics, where they were initially informed about the aims and general characteristics of the study and asked to provide their informed consent. Data were collected for Learning Value in the Context of AI, Learning Self-Efficacy in the Context of AI, AI use, classical task value, classical self-efficacy, effort expenditure, GPA, and persistence intention, along with demographic items. We designed and administered the survey for this research. The instrument also included measures of additional constructs that are not reported in this manuscript. Considering that the whole survey was larger, we embedded five attention checks throughout the questionnaires so that participants' attentiveness to the study and the quality of their data could be ensured.

Data Quality Screening

Participants who failed two or more out of the five attention checks were excluded.

Moreover, we excluded the fastest 5% of the participants with one attention check or straightlining patterns. Straightlining was defined as repeatedly choosing the same response for 20+ consecutive items. These exclusion criteria led to filtering 15 individuals, yielding 882 participants as the final sample.

Measures

Item Development.

The LV-AI and LSE-AI scales were developed through an iterative process. To start, the general definition and specific aspects of the constructs were prepared by the first author. Next, a generative AI tool (ChatGPT; OpenAI) was used to assist with the preparation of candidate items based on our specifications for the two constructs. It was followed by the evaluation and revision of the candidate items for construct validity. We assessed items for clarity and for framing AI comprehensively (not being limited to one generative AI tool). Finally, a short pilot study involved 28 participants completing the survey study and 3 of them providing qualitative feedback. The completions did not show signs of fundamental and technical flaws in the online survey, and the qualitative feedback was not indicative of any major issues that were troublesome for participants when comprehending and answering the scales. All six final items are presented in Table 2.

Learning Value in the Context of AI (LV-AI).

Defined as the perceived value of acquiring knowledge and skills in a context where AI tools can perform associated tasks, Learning Value in the Context of AI is grounded in Eccles and Wigfield's (2002) expectancyvalue theory in this study. Initially, we developed four measuring items for this construct where the participants were asked to show how much the sentences were true for them on a 5-point Likert scale ("1" meaning "strongly disagree" and "5" meaning "strongly agree"). The initial and untested items involved (1) "Learning course material myself is worthwhile, even when AI tools can provide quick answers"; (2) "Even when AI tools are available, developing my own understanding of course content is important to me"; (3) "Even when AI tools can assist with my work, putting personal effort and time into learning course material is meaningful to me"; and (4) "Using AI tools does not diminish the importance I place on learning academic skills for myself." Preliminary analyses showed that item 4 exhibited a mixed loading pattern and was excluded, leading to the final scale after psychometric screening to involve the first three items.

In terms of theoretical coverage, item 1 addresses the worth and utility of learning the material oneself, item 2 is reflective of attainment value through the development of one's own understanding, and item 3 involves both the intrinsic meaning of learning and the cost of the effort and time that it requires.

Procedures and Analytic Strategy

We performed all six analytical steps in R. The full sample (N = 882) was randomly divided into the EFA subsample (n = 431) and CFA subsample (n = 445) stratified by student age type (traditional-age vs. adult learner). We then examined the item-level diagnostics for each scale prior to EFA, including corrected item-total correlations, alpha-if-item-deleted statistics, and inter-item correlation matrices. Next, exploratory factor analysis (EFA) was performed on the EFA subsample using principal axis factoring with oblique rotation, given that LV-AI and LSE-AI were expected to correlate. We conducted parallel analysis to determine the number of factors. It was done along with eigenvalue inspection and theoretical considerations. The two subsamples mentioned were only used for the factor analytic steps, where the independence of the exploratory and the confirmatory data was necessary. The measurement invariance analyses were performed on the 876 participants with valid student-type classification and complete data on the LV-AI and LSE-AI items, and the criterion validity and mediation analyses were performed on the cases with complete data on all the involved variables (n = 869).

For the confirmatory factor analysis (CFA), we used Maximum Likelihood with Robust standard errors (MLR) estimator in the lavaan package (Rosseel, 2012) to account for potential non-normality of participants' responses. The competing models involved a correlated 2-factor model (the hypothesized structure), a 1-factor model, and an orthogonal 2-factor model. Model fit was evaluated using the robust CFI and TLI, RMSEA, and SRMR. For the assessment of reliability, we computed Cronbach's α and McDonald's ω using the semTools package (Jorgensen et al., 2022). Convergent validity was evaluated through the magnitude of the standardized factor loadings, the average variance extracted, and the pattern of zero-order correlations with theoretically related constructs. Discriminant validity was evaluated using the heterotrait-monotrait ratio of correlations (HTMT; Henseler et al., 2015). Additionally, a fourfactor model involving the two AI constructs and their two classical counterparts was tested against two alternative models in which each AI construct was merged with its classical counterpart. We then examined the measurement invariance across the two age groups. To do so, configural, metric, and scalar models were tested.

For the last stage of the analysis, hierarchical linear regression was performed to evaluate criterion validity. The three academic outcome variables being predicted involved effort expenditure, persistence, and GPA. Block 1 included classical task value, classical self-efficacy, student type, and AI use as predictors. Block 2 was where LV-AI and LSE-AI were added.

Finally, we analyzed whether AI use could predict the three academic outcome variables, followed by examining whether any potential associations were mediated by LV-AI and LSE-AI post hoc with 5,000 bootstrap resamples.

This study was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board of Texas State University (protocol no. 10458). All procedures were conducted in accordance with the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and its later amendments.

Reliability, Convergent Validity, and Discriminant Validity (RQ2)

Reliability and convergent validity statistics are presented in Table 2. The internal consistency for both scales was acceptable (LV-AI: α = .80, ω = .81; LSE-AI: α = .72, ω = .72).

Moreover, the composite reliability was bigger than .70 for both LV-AI (.81) and LSE-AI (.72).

We evaluated convergent validity through the magnitude of the standardized loadings, the average variance extracted, and the pattern of associations with theoretically related constructs.

All of the six standardized loadings were significant and substantial (LV-AI: .75-.78; LSE-AI: .66-.71), indicating that items converged on their intended factors.

Average variance extracted for LV-AI was .58, but for LSE-AI it was .46, which fell marginally short of the .50 criterion.

Since all LSE-AI loadings were significant and the substantial and composite reliability was adequate for this scale, the marginal average variance shortfall seems reflective of the conservative nature of the .50 criterion for brief scales instead of a failure of convergent validity.

This is also acknowledged as a limitation. Convergent evidence was further supported at the construct level. As shown in Table 1, LV-AI and LSE-AI each correlated moderately with their classical counterparts (both r = .39) and negatively with frequency of AI use (r = -.30 and -.32, respectively). They were both positively associated with effort expenditure and persistence intention.

Heterotrait-monotrait ratio of correlations (HTMT) of .841 showed the discriminant validity between the two constructs, as it was smaller than .85 (Kline, 2011) and .90 (Gold et al., 2001; Henseler et al., 2015) thresholds. The magnitude of the correlation was expected considering that the value of authentic learning in AI-rich contexts and confidence in AI-Independent learning are closely intertwined for a learner's motivational beliefs.

To further examine the discriminant validity of the two new constructs against their classical counterparts, a four-factor model involving LV-AI, LSE-AI, classical task value, and classical self-efficacy was tested and compared with two alternative models in which each AI construct was merged with its classical counterpart. The four-factor model showed adequate fit (CFI = .917; TLI = .904; RMSEA = .077; SRMR = .053), which is expected considering the length of the two classical subscales, and it fit considerably better than both the model merging LV-AI with classical task value (Δχ²[3] = 172.75, p < .001) and the model merging LSE-AI with classical self-efficacy (Δχ²[3] = 163.14, p < .001). Moreover, the HTMT values between the AI constructs and their classical counterparts were low (LV-AI with classical task value = .486; LSE-AI with classical self-efficacy = .531), and well below the conventional thresholds. It was indicative of the two new constructs being empirically distinguishable from the classical motivational constructs from which they are conceptually derived.

Measurement Invariance Across Student Type (RQ3)

Traditional-age (n = 469) and adult (n = 407) students were compared in multigroup CFA across the configural, metric, and scalar invariance sequence (Table 3). The configural model fit well, supporting the two-factor structure in both groups. Constraining factor loadings to equality did not lead to any degradation in fit (Δχ²[4] = 3.54, p = .472; ΔCFI = .000), which was indicative of the metric invariance. Constraining item intercepts produced a significant decrement (Δχ²[4] = 12.95, p = .012), showing that full scalar invariance was not supported, despite fit index changes remaining modest (ΔCFI = -.005). Univariate score tests identified the intercept of LSE-AI item 3 ("When learning tasks are challenging, I can rely on my own abilities rather than depending entirely on AI tools") as the only parameter that was non-invariant (χ²[1] = 11.33, p = .001). A partial scalar model where only this intercept was freed fit well (CFI = .995; TLI = .993; RMSEA = .031; SRMR = .024) and did not differ from the metric model (Δχ²[3] = 1.11, p = .774), supporting partial scalar invariance with five of six intercepts constrained equal across both groups.

Criterion Validity (RQ4)

To explore the criterion validity of the scales, hierarchical regression analyses were performed to examine whether the two focal AI constructs could predict academic outcome variables (effort expenditure, persistence, and GPA) above classical task value and self-efficacy while student type and AI use were controlled (n = 869; Table 4). In block 1, all three outcome variables were significantly predicted by classical self-efficacy, while classical task value was only a significant predictor of persistence and effort. While AI use was not correlated with GPA (β = -.02, p = .489) and to persistence intention (β = .01, p = .756), it had a significant negative association with effort expenditure (β = -.09, p = .003). The AI constructs added the largest increment for effort expenditure (ΔR² = .022, F[2, 862] = 13.03, p < .001) where both LV-AI (β = .09, p = .021) and LSE-AI (β = .10, p = .013) were both significant independent contributors. For intention to persist, the AI constructs added significant incremental variance (ΔR² = .015, F[2, 862] = 7.66, p < .001), which was driven by LV-AI (β = .10, p = .025). However, LSE-AI was not independently significant (β = .06, p = .157) in predicting persistence. For GPA, while classical self-efficacy was the dominant predictor (β = .20, p < .001), the AI constructs did not add significant incremental variance (ΔR² = .003, F[2, 862] = 1.17, p = .311). The addition of the two motivational AI constructs in Block 2 altered the correlations that were found for effort in Block 1. Both AI use (β = -.09, p = .003 in Block 1; β = -.04, p = .180 in Block 2) and classical task value (β = .11, p = .003; β = .06, p = .135) were reduced to non-significance, whereas classical self-efficacy remained the strongest predictor in both blocks (β = .37 and .35, both p < .001). Therefore, the new constructs showed incremental criterion validity for academic outcomes with behavioral engagement (persistence and effort) but not for GPA.

Exploratory Mediation Analysis (RQ5)

The fact that the correlation that AI use had with effort was attenuated to non-significance raised the possibility that this association operated through the two AI motivational beliefs here.

It was tested post hoc in a path model where AI predicted LV-AI and LSE-AI, which in turn predicted effort expenditure, with classical task value, classical self-efficacy, and student type entered as covariates of all three endogenous variables and the two mediators permitted to 29 covary. The saturation of the model (df = 0) makes the fit indices not informative. Using 5,000 bootstrap resamples, the indirect effects were estimated.

AI use negatively predicted both LV-AI (β = -.25, p < .001) and LSE-AI (β = -.25, p < .001), and both constructs positively predicted effort expenditure (LV-AI: β = .09, p = .033; LSE-AI: β = .10, p = .017). While the total effect of AI use on effort expenditure was significant before the mediation analysis (β = -.09, 95% CI [-0.138, -0.024], p = .005), its direct effect was not (β = -.04, 95% CI [-0.097, 0.023], p = .211) after the mediating paths were factored in. The combined indirect effect through the two constructs was significant (β = -.05, 95% CI [-0.071, -0.021], p = .001), which accounted for about 54% of the total effect. The specific indirect effects through LV-AI (β = -.02, 95% CI [-0.042, -0.001]) and LSE-AI (β = -.03, 95% CI [-0.045, -0.004]) were each individually significant, although their confidence intervals were close to zero and they should be interpreted with corresponding caution.

Discussion

Considering the importance of how academic AI use can be associated with changes in learners' motivational dynamics, the present study introduced and validated two brief scales for the measurement of two focal learning motivational constructs in AI-rich educational contexts.

These two constructs include Learning Value in the Context of AI (LV-AI), referring to the perceived value of acquiring knowledge and skills when AI tools can perform the associated tasks, and Learning Self-Efficacy in the Context of AI (LSE-AI), described as the learner's perceived capability to learn and perform academically without relying on AI. The study was performed while also exploring and comparing the applicability of the scales among adult (above 25-years of age) vs. traditional-age (between 18-25 years of age) undergraduate students.

Identified by exploratory factor analysis and supported by confirmatory factor analysis, the suggested two-factor structure comprising three items per construct showed excellent model fit, acceptable internal consistency, and obvious superiority over one-factor and orthogonal alternatives. The results were indicative of the two scales being empirically distinguishable from each other (HTMT = .84) and from classical task value and self-efficacy. The scales also showed configural and metric invariance and partial scalar invariance across traditional-age and adult learners, and incremental prediction of persistence intention and effort expenditure beyond classical motivational constructs, student type, and frequency of AI use. Overall, the results support that the two scales are psychometrically sound instruments for studying Learning Value in the Context of AI and Learning Self-Efficacy in the Context of AI as motivational consequences of AI in higher education.

Theoretical Implications

There are a number of theoretical implications that the findings highlight. Firstly, the two AI constructs could significantly predict effort expenditure (ΔR² = .022, with both constructs contributing independently) and persistence intention (ΔR² = .015, driven primarily by LV-AI), but not GPA. Consistent with the previous studies, effort and persistence are the direct proximal behavioral expressions of motivation according to expectancy-value theory (Eccles & Wigfield, 2020). However, GPA is a distal outcome influenced by more non-motivational determinants (e.g., prior achievement, course difficulty). The AI-contextualized motivational beliefs are mainly drivers of energization and direction of motivated learning behavior rather than graded performance, which is expected to be indirectly and less strongly improved by motivational beliefs. The strongest predictor of GPA among the motivational beliefs was classical selfefficacy, which is an observation consistent with previous findings (Schneider & Preckel, 2017).

Secondly, while the two AI-influenced motivational constructs were empirically distinguishable, they were strongly correlated at the latent level (r = .85). Expectancy value theory highlights expectancy and value as two distinct constructs with reciprocity in how they influence belief systems. The strong link between LV-AI and LSE-AI shows that when students doubt the value of durable learning when AI quickly provides access to the academic products, they also feel less capable of learning those skills without AI. The opposite trend also holds true, meaning that declining confidence in one's capability to learn without relying on AI undermines the perceived value of durable learning. Whether any of these two constructs are causally prior can be studied using longitudinal designs.

Third and relatedly, the four-factor model tested in this study showed that the two AI constructs are not redundant with their classical counterparts, considering that merging each of them with its classical counterpart resulted in considerably worse fit and that their HTMT values with the classical constructs were low. It suggests that situating task value and self-efficacy in an AI-rich context does not simply reproduce the classical constructs, but rather captures motivational beliefs that the classical measures do not already carry.

Finally, the invariance results show that the scales function equivalently at the configural and metric levels among both the traditional-age and adult learners, except for one Learning

Self-Efficacy in the Context of AI item. The age group seemed to lead to different baseline endorsements of the item "When learning tasks are challenging, I can rely on my own abilities rather than depending entirely on AI tools." This deviation in how the two age groups treat the item is very much interpretable, especially considering that this is the only item measuring learners' reaction to when tasks become challenging. Adult learners tend to make judgments about themselves based on longer time periods (decades) of pre-AI contexts where academic and educational tasks needed to be handled without the help of AI. It provided them with more volume of independent master experience, hence the shift in the item's endorsement without altering the construct's meaning. Since five of six intercepts were invariant, latent comparisons across student age groups remain defensible.

The Relationship Between the New Constructs and AI Use

The inverse associations that AI use had with LV-AI and LSE-AI are important findings bearing significant insights regarding the learning motivational dynamics of the current AI-rich educational context. The fact that both of the AI constructs were negatively correlated with AI use (LV-AI: r = -.30; LSE-AI: r = -.32) can be interpreted differently. An initial interpretation can be that, when AI is introduced in educational contexts, students who feel value in learning per-se and feel capable in learning independent of AI tend to use this tool less. On the other hand, the other conceivable trend is that when students use generative AI for their educational endeavors, they tend to feel that the completion of these tasks through the more effortful durable learning is not worth it. Such a decrease in the appraisal of value can also be the result of comparing the two ways of achieving the academic goal in terms of their costs (emotional, time, effort).

Moreover, the efficiency of AI task accomplishment can make the learner think that "this close-to-perfect job done so quickly by AI would have been of less quality and more flaw if I wanted to do myself, which would have taken much longer and more effortful." In addition to time and effort, the emotional cost is another issue which one faces when one chooses the durable learning path (please note that, now, this learner has thoughts about the possibility of another easier path, an image that aggravates the cost imbalance). The emerging literature also suggests that heavy AI use may erode these beliefs over time (Fan et al., 2025; Tian & Zhang, 2025).

It still should be highlighted that these cross-sectional associations cannot adjudicate direction in a certain way before further evidence is observed. The results of the present findings can be accounted for by the previously mentioned potential dynamics, or they could be only indicative of students using AI less because they value and trust their own learning or a combination of the two. Both readings are supported by the data, and further research to distinguish them is among the most important questions these scales now make tractable.

Accounting for the Association Between AI Use and Effort

It is important to explore the role of AI use when the new AI-contextualized motivational constructs are modeled. In the hierarchical regression analyses and before modeling LV-AI and LSE-AI, the relationship between AI use and effort expenditure was significant. However, after the two constructs were entered, it fell to non-significance. Exploratory path modeling was performed to explore the mediating effects of LV-AI and LSE-AI on the relationship between AI use and effort. It showed that just over half of the AI use's total association with effort expenditure was carried indirectly through the two constructs, with no significant direct path remaining. With cautious interpretation, this suggests that the negative association between AI use and effort expenditure is largely shared with the two AI-embedded motivational beliefs here.

There are at least two ways of explaining these findings. On the one hand, it can be the case that frequent academic use of generative AI can lead to the erosion of learners' appraisal of value and self-efficacy in AI-independent learning. On the other hand, it is possible that students who already place little value on learning without AI may both turn to AI more often and invest less effort. In this way, AI use and effort are joint expressions of an already existing motivational stance rather than links in a causal chain. Considering the cross-sectional design of the study, the results are supportive of an indirect pathway but do not establish causality. Future studies with longitudinal and experimental designs can explore these possible accounts and the scales suggested in this paper can be used for that purpose.

The observed pattern has a consequential interpretation. It seems that once the learners' motivational beliefs in AI-embedded educational contexts are known, their degree of AI use has little further information to add about their effort. This has a direct practical implication. For decision-makers, institutions, and educators who are mainly worried about the degree of AI use among learners, the findings of this study warn them about being concerned about measuring the wrong thing. Instead, it points them towards the potentially more important and decisive role of learners' AI-contextualized motivational belief about learning, here being the value of learning and the confidence in one's capability to learn AI-independently. This perspective seems to be more effective when trying to enhance learners' engagement and effort in the new AI-saturated educational era.

Practical Implications

Since each of the constructs have three items, the brevity of the scales makes them practical for using in institutional and classroom settings. As evaluation instruments, these scales can be used by institutions that adopt new AI tools and policies to monitor the motivational correlates of those decisions. Data collected before and after such decisions and policy changes can provide them with such insights. In addition to such practical uses, future research can also use these scales in order to explore the correlations and intervention effects in the context of AIrelated motivational research. Finally, considering that findings show that both LV-AI and LSE-AI predict increased effort and persistence, which are important academic decisions and behaviors, this study can provide educators with insights regarding how reliance on generative AI can be associated with decreased academic persistence (withdrawal) and effort (disengagement). Addressing unfavorable previous findings about AI use and disengagement in learning, the findings of this study suggest that the practical aim is not less AI use, but AI use that leaves the value of and confidence in one's own learning intact.

Limitations and Future Directions

There were several limitations in this study. Firstly, this study was cross-sectional. Future studies with multiple data points or longitudinal designs can put the criterion validity of the scales to the test in a more detailed way. Moreover, the fact that this study relied on self-reported data is another limitation of this study. For the LSE-AI scale, while all loadings were significant and substantial and the composite reliability was found to be adequate, the average variance extracted was slightly lower (.46) than the conventional .50 criterion. The fact that the scale consists of 3 items can make the .50 AVE criterion rather too conservative for such a brief scale.

However, the AVE can be increased in longer versions of this scale where more items are added.

Relatedly, the brevity of the scale can be preventing in terms of detailed measurement of subconstructs. For example, the fourth dimensions of task value (especially cost) can be more meticulously assessed if more targeted items are included in future scales. Future versions of LV-AI and LSE-AI scales that are longer and more constructually comprehensive can benefit from further structured content validation. Finally, although the sample was large and deliberately balanced across two age groups, replicating similar studies in different cultural contexts can help explore the generalizable vs. unique findings.
