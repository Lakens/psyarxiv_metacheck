Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Marginal Significance works

List all sentences that describe an effect as ‘marginally significant’.

The marginal module searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The list of terms is a subset of those listed in a blog post by Matthew Hankins. The module returns all sentences that match terms describing ‘marginally significant’ results.

Some of the terms identified might not be problematic in some contexts, and there are ways to describe ‘marginal significance’ that are not detected by the module.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). Statcheck might still miss one-sided tests that are described in another sentence, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. Some differences come from the ground truth: it lacks p-values with a correction name (“p Bonf = .061”), and counts partial eta squared written with its subscript after it (“η 2 p = .05”), “p = .05”, and some table notes and figure legends (“† p < .10, *p < .05”), which this module leaves out, as non-significant p-values. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

If you want to extend the package to be able to download files from additional data repositories reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Abstract

Judgments of responsibility are foundational to human social life. Adults judge responsibility by considering not just what someone chose to do, but also the alternative choices available to them. Thus, the development of adult-like responsibility judgments in children depends on their capacity to reason about counterfactual choices. In this Review, we distinguish three key elements of this capacity: understanding that counterfactual choices are possible, successfully reasoning about counterfactual worlds, and identifying the most relevant counterfactuals in any given situation. This framework clarifies the relationship between counterfactual reasoning and responsibility judgments in childhood, identifies conceptually distinct elements of children's understanding of responsibility, and opens new avenues for inquiry into an essential element of children's social reasoning.

[H1] Introduction

Sally faces a vexing choice on a rainy afternoon: taxes are due and she should really spend a few hours finalizing her documents, but her favorite TV series just dropped on Netflix. After reflecting for a moment, she switches on the TV, sinks back into the pillows, and becomes a tax delinquent.

Prior research on adults suggests that most people would hold Sally responsible for her choice and its consequences: They would conclude that Sally could have chosen otherwise, and that doing so would have altered the outcome [1] [2] [3] [4] [5] [6] [7] . This is a counterfactual judgment: Sally's responsibility for the actual choice that she made, and for its consequences, is determined by considering a relevant non-actual alternative-the counterfactual possibility that she had chosen to do her taxes instead. This attribution of responsibility matters a great deal: it determines how Sally's character is perceived and whether her action is evaluated as wrong and deserving of punishment.

Judgments of responsibility (and related judgments of blame, wrongness and punishment) can arise from several different cognitive processes [8] [9] [10] [11] [12] , not all of which depend on counterfactual reasoning. But, the counterfactual process is essential to fully adult-like responsibility attribution. A key question is whether young children also can-and spontaneously do-make responsibility judgments by considering counterfactuals, or whether they are limited to making responsibility judgments in other ways (for example, simply based on what someone actually did). On the one hand, children, and in some cases even infants, can make moral judgments that track many features that are also relevant to adult judgments of responsibility (see ref. 13 for a review). On the other hand, there are notable differences in the capacity and spontaneous use of counterfactual reasoning more generally in childhood 14- 20 . Thus, there is suggestive evidence both for and against an early, adult-like conception of responsibility in young children.

Progress understanding the developmental trajectory of reasoning about counterfactual choices requires distinguishing between several processes. First, it might be that children and adults have different folk theories of choice. Specifically, it might be that certain features of choice that adults subject to counterfactual consideration are difficult, or even unavailable, for children to represent [21] [22] [23] . Second, it might be that children have general deficits in representing counterfactual timelines, and this general difficulty has a downstream effect on their ability to reason counterfactually about a person's actions 14, 16, 24 . Finally, even if children can represent counterfactuals, they might generate different counterfactuals to adults; for example, children might consider an irrelevant alternative choice someone could have made, which might change subsequent judgments of responsibility [25] [26] [27] . Thus, if adult judgments of responsibility often depend on considering counterfactual choices, then children might differ from adults because they do not employ an identical concept of choice, cannot easily represent counterfactuals, or generate different counterfactuals. These possibilities are not mutually exclusive: It is possible that children of a certain age would fail on some or all counts.

In this Review, we explore how children's emerging understanding of counterfactual choices might drive how they understand responsibility more broadly. First, we review the developmental evidence relevant to three key elements of counterfactual reasoning about responsibility: the concept of choice, the representation of counterfactuals, and the ability to identify relevant counterfactuals for the situation (Figure 1). We conceptualize responsibility as a precursor to other social judgments (such as judgments of blame, punishment and forgiveness) [28] [29] [30] [31] and therefore include studies that measure these social judgments. We then consider how and when these capacities might be integrated developmentally. We conclude with suggestions for future work that illustrates how a counterfactual choice framework of responsibility judgments can help to resolve whether, when, and how children's judgments of responsibility might differ from those of adults.

[H1] The concept of choice Many theories of responsibility attribution posit two key kinds of counterfactuals: An assessment of whether an agent controlled their behavior (that is, possessed the capacity to have decided to act otherwise) and whether the behavior caused the outcome in question 1, 28, 32.

In this section, we focus on the former: the concept that people have behavioral control over their actions and can choose how they want to act (Figure 1a).

There are a multitude of counterfactuals that result in altered choices. Some involve changes to the actual world. For instance, Sally might have chosen to do her taxes instead of watching tv if she was dating a tax accountant who emphasized the importance of the tax deadline. Intuitively, distant counterfactuals like this one do not feel relevant to judgments of responsibility because they involve such substantial changes to the actual world; that Sally could have dated a tax accountant is so removed from the actual circumstances so as to be considered irrelevant.

Alternatively, some counterfactuals only involve changes to an agent's mental state. For instance, Sally could have wanted to do her taxes more than watching TV. In that case, Sally could be held responsible for having irresponsible preferences. People likely do entertain counterfactuals of this kind ("Sally is responsible for her delinquency because she could have been the kind of person who cares more about obeying the law..."), but it is a more substantive alteration to the actual world than is necessary in typical responsibility attribution.

Finally, there are counterfactuals related to whether a person could have chosen otherwise, holding constant their beliefs and preferences (the standard elements that determine rational choice). For example, Sally could have 'just decided' to do her taxes in keeping with her obligation, even though she would have preferred watching TV. Indeed, adults can entertain the possibility of choosing against preferences. For instance, when asked whether a character could cease ongoing electrical shocks by moving his hand to the right, even though he strongly preferred for the shocks to continue, people tended to say yes, it was possible 33.

Often this reasoning process is described as an assessment of control over behavior: A person is responsible for their actions when the actions were voluntary, in the sense that they could have decided to do otherwise 1, 28 . Counterfactuals of this kind might be especially central to judgments of moral responsibility 34 . It is implicitly assumed that choosing otherwise does not require a change to the psychological inputs to the process of choice (for example, beliefs, desires, or recognition of obligation) but rather changes the process and outcome of choice itself. This particular counterfactual-whether a person could have simply chosen otherwise-might be especially important to the judgment of whether a person controlled a particular behavior because controlling one's behavior presumably entails the capacity to change it by deliberative choice 1, 33, 35.

Counterfactuals related to whether a person could have chosen otherwise are closely related to certain conceptions of free will. Notwithstanding a lively philosophical debate about whether free will exists and what it entails, there is substantial evidence that ordinary people use a practical concept akin to free will when making responsibility judgments.

Although people do sometimes express doubts about whether free will exists when questioned about in the abstract 33, [36] [37] [38] , they often claim that people are 'free to choose otherwise' when sufficiently concrete situations are described 37, 38 . Furthermore, people This is not the most recent version of this paper. In sum, the concept that one could have 'just chosen' otherwise irrespective of preferences, relevant costs, and/or rewards seems to drive adult judgments of responsibility 1, 5, 32, 39, 42.

In this section, we first review evidence indicating that children possess a folk theory of rational action according to which a person chooses actions based on their expected value -that is, the likelihood that actions will satisfy their preferences (or, in many formulations, their goal), given their beliefs and relevant costs 43, 44 . This evidence suggests that children might be able to entertain the counterfactual that a person would have chosen differently because they had different preferences, which might be relevant and sufficient for judgments of responsibility 23, 36 . However, when adults make judgments of responsibility, they might consider a counterfactual in which one acted against one's preferences-although perhaps in accord with moral obligation (that is, choosing to do otherwise, holding all else constant 33, 36, 41, 45 ). We therefore next consider whether there is additional evidence that young children believe that a person can make a deliberate choice that is contradictory to preferences.

[H2] Reasoning about expected value

Even infants seem to understand the relationship between a person's goals, preferences and values, and the actions that person chooses-what is sometimes called the naïve utility calculus [46] [47] [48] [49] [50] . From an early age, children predict that people will choose actions that satisfy their goals, preferences, and values, and also infer goals, preferences, and values from the actions that they observe people perform [50] [51] [52] . For example, 5-and 6-year-olds are able to predict that a character with a preference for a particular treat is more likely to pay a cost to get that treat than a character who does not have the same preference 53 . Other work shows that infants expect agents to prefer a goal more if they paid a higher cost to get to it, suggesting some early understanding of the relationship between costs and preference 52 . This consideration of actions with respect to expected costs and rewards might also influence children's social reasoning, such as judging if an act is nice based on whether it was easy or difficult 51Error: Reference source not found . For example, children tend to judge someone who finds it easy to help but does not meaner than someone who finds it difficult to help and does not 51 . This research suggests that infants and children make judgments of actions based on a computation of relative costs and rewards. This naïve utility calculus involves a model of choice: Infants and young children assume that, when others have multiple actions available to them, their choice is guided by a comparative cost-benefit analysis (see ref. 54 for a review). Importantly, some studies have directly examined whether children understand that an action indicates a choice, especially when other actions were possible. That is, a choice is less meaningful if other options do not exist. In these studies, infants are shown an agent being presented with two options (A and B) and then choosing one of those options. If infants infer that the agent prefers option A, they should look longer when the agent subsequently chooses against that preference (that is, chooses option B) 55 . Such looking time experiments have found that infants 3 months of age and older not only infer preference from the selected option but also consider the available options when inferring preference. That is, they will not infer a preference of option A over option B if option B was not present during the agent's initial choice 46, 47, 55, 56.

The body of work reviewed above suggests that even infants interpret agents' actions in light of the alternative available actions, in line with the folk theory of rational action: preferences among alternatives predicts action. However, reasoning that an agent prefers A over B when both are present and A is chosen does not require further reasoning that the agent could have instead decided to choose B without any alteration to their preferences or circumstances. In other words, to conclude that young children evaluate the counterfactual of 'just choosing otherwise' requires evidence that children reason about possible choices that do not align with preferences.

[H2] Reasoning about conflicting motivations

From around age 3, children begin to acknowledge the possibility of having a choice over one's actions [57] [58] [59] . For instance, in one study 3-5 year old children were told about an individual who sees money on the table and does not take it. When they were then asked whether the individual could have chosen to take the money, nearly all said "yes" 57 . In a control condition, the children were informed that the money was (unbeknownst to the individual) glued to the table, so the individual could not have physically taken it.

Nevertheless, most children still said that the individual could have taken it. This response was interpreted as evidence that the children did not mean that the individual could have taken the money in a physical sense; instead, they must mean that the individual could have chosen to take the money (trying and then, presumably, physically failing) in an agentic sense.

However, although young children reason that agents have a choice over their actions, they find it difficult to reason about agents choosing undesirable actions. For instance, when 4-7-year-olds judge characters whose immediate preferences conflict with rules [60] [61] [62] [63] , long term goals 64 , or immediate costs and rewards 65, 66 , they might assume a stricter alignment between preference and choice than adults. Moreover, only by age 6 or 7 do children report that it might feel good to follow a rule that contradicts preferences 62, [67] [68] [69] [70] . Similarly, children's understanding of practice (which requires holding conflicting motivations in mind, such as 'I want to play but I will instead do my homework') improves between the ages of 4 and 8 years [71] [72] [73] [74] . In line with this, one study found that while children aged 4 to-6 increasingly understood that it is not possible to act differently when there is a physical or epistemic constraint on choice, only 6-year-olds reasoned that it was possible to act against desires 75 . By contrast, younger children seemed to consider desires as constraints on choices similar to physical or epistemic constraints.

Consistent with this pattern, it is only by age 6 that children understand that paying an unwanted physical cost (for example, going up stairs to retrieve an item for someone) is praiseworthy, and only by age 8 that children understand that a paying an unwanted psychological cost (for example, wanting to play outside but having to help out inside) is praiseworthy 76 . These judgments might depend on the understanding that it is possible to choose against one's preferences, which is incompatible with a strict interpretation of the folk theory of rational action. If children tend to judge actions in this way, it might constrain their ability to judge that it is especially praiseworthy for someone to 'do the right thing' when they do not actually want to 77, 78 . Thus, when 3-5-year-old children assert that an actor 'could have chosen' to act differently, they might be imaging an alternative possibility that involves changes to the actor's preferences (or other mental states or circumstances). By 6 years old, children might begin to judge that a person can 'just choose' to act against their preferences, which introduces a new and potentially relevant counterfactual to consider when making judgments of responsibility.

In sum, a key variable in adult judgments of responsibility is whether the agent was in control of their behavior, and this assessment typically involves considering counterfactuals related to whether the agent could have acted otherwise. From infancy children understand how preferences guide choices, but existing evidence suggests that perhaps only from around ages 5 or 6 do children begin to reason about choices as distinct from preferences-that is, to understand that it is possible to 'just chose' differently.

[H1] The representation of counterfactuals

Sally's choice to watch TV rather than finish her taxes matters in part because of its downstream consequences: Her taxes go unfiled. However, if they would have been unfiled regardless of her choice-if, for instance the entire tax filing system was subject to a catastrophic failure-she would presumably be held less responsible. Thus, Sally's choice can be evaluated by contrasting it with a counterfactual choice and checking whether it would eventually lead to a different state of affairs in the world 1 (Box 1). However, children's counterfactual reasoning about what could have happened shows a protracted development 14, 19, 27 . Consequently, even children who understand the concept of choice might struggle to apply it.

When judgments of responsibility are based on counterfactual reasoning, two elements are necessary: Performing a mental 'intervention' on the person's actual actions (asking, 'could they have chosen to act differently') and tracing the downstream consequences of that intervention (asking, 'what would have happened had that alternative choice been made').

There are other relevant interventions, such as about the mental state of the person (what if they had known more?), how they should have acted (what would a reasonable person have done?), or uncontrollable aspects of the situation (what if it had not been raining?). In this section, however, we focus primarily on whether children can reason about whether an alternative choice could have been made and what would have happened had it been made by considering children's general ability to represent counterfactual alternatives and their ability to represent counterfactual timelines (Figure 1b).

There has been a substantial amount of research over the past decade on children's counterfactual thinking ability 14, 18, 20, 24, 79 . This work has found that counterfactual thinking about real events is substantially more challenging for children than seemingly related abilities, such as engaging in pretense. That is, although very young children (around age 2, ref 80 ) engage in pretend play about non-actual events, it is not until around age 6 that they begin to reason about real world events by juxtaposing counterfactual (that is, non-actual) alternatives 14, 80, 81 . This distinction might arise because pretend play can be entirely divorced from reality, whereas counterfactual reasoning about the real world involves anchoring nonactual possibilities to actual reality 14, 26, 81 , which might require greater inhibitory control and/or representational abilities 24, 79 . Alternatively, it might be that pretend play is in some way supported by the capacity to represent others' belief states as different from reality (Box 2), whereas the counterfactual possibilities relevant to judging responsibility are not represented as belief states.

Although children under 6 sometimes assert that past events could have gone differently or people could have done otherwise [57] [58] [59] , it is disputed how often these assertions reflect genuine or mature counterfactual reasoning (for debate, see refs 14, 81, 82 ). Indeed, the distinction between counterfactual thinking about the past and other simpler forms of reasoning 20, 83 is especially relevant because judging responsibility via counterfactual reasoning specifically requires thinking about how a real sequence of past events could have turned out differently.

Children's capacity for mental time travel (the ability to represent the actual past and hypothetical future timelines) emerges around ages 3 to 4 (ref 84, 85 ). Interestingly, it is generally easier for children to maintain representations of alternative possible future events (hypotheticals) than alternatives to an actual past event (counterfactuals), and the latter ability only begins to emerge around age 6 (for reviews, see ref. 24, 86 ). This pattern does not seem to reflect a general facility for future-oriented as opposed to past-oriented thought, because children typically find it easier to remember actual past events than to simulate future events (see ref. 87 for a review). Rather, it suggests that the conflict between representations of actual and non-actual possibilities might constrain children's counterfactual reasoning.

Moreover, children have difficulty simultaneously reasoning about multiple future hypotheticals (that might conflict with each other) until about 3-4 years old [88] [89] [90] [91] . For example, in one study, children were shown an inverted Y-shaped tube where a ball is dropped down the top and could come out of either exit. Children younger than 4 years old tend to cover only one exit at a time, whereas older children begin spontaneously covering both exits, thereby ensuring a catch 88 (for replications, see ref. 72, [92] [93] [94] ). This result might arise because younger children struggle to represent mutually exclusive possible outcomes (or possible actions 95 ). This possibility aligns with the fact that children's ability to plan for the future more generally improves between 3 and 5 years old 85 . For example, only by age 4 do children engage in episodic foresight to select an item that will help them solve a future puzzle 96, 97.

Young children might therefore struggle with counterfactual thinking because the real event has actually happened and so there is always conflict between multiple possible timelines.

Children do not reliably show adult-like patterns of reasoning about counterfactual past events until about 6 years old 16, 18, 20 . For example, in one study 17 children were shown a ball rolling towards a pig and knocking it over. When asked "what would have happened, had This is not the most recent version of this paper. However, when there were two balls and one ball knocked the pig over before the second ball arrived, only children 6 years and older consistently reasoned correctly that, had the first ball been blocked, the pig would nevertheless have been knocked down (by the second ball). Younger children answered at chance, consistent with the possibility that they represented a conditional dependency between the first ball and knocking over the pig, but that they struggled to reason about the sequence of events that would be represented in a true counterfactual simulation. These results suggest that young children struggle not only with the maintenance of multiple alternatives, but also with using a counterfactual antecedent to generate counterfactual consequences.

Given these deficits in counterfactual reasoning about physical events, it is perhaps not surprising that children also struggle to incorporate information about counterfactual choices into their moral judgments of actions [98] [99] [100] [101] . For example, children younger than 5 years old tend to judge characters who chose to do something undesirable (for example, chose to get a gift their friend does not like when they could have got one they liked) and characters who have no choice but to do something undesirable (for example, had no choice but to get the gift their friend does not like) as equally 'mean', despite differences in the availability of alternative choices 16, 98, 100, 102.

Children also show less reliable expressions of counterfactual emotions such as regret or relief until around age 6 (see ref 103 for a review), possibly because of difficultly reasoning through counterfactual timelines about alternative choices and their consequences. Children's emotions about choices that affect others also show similar developmental patterns [104] [105] [106].

These studies all specifically manipulated the counterfactual choices children could have made, and so provide additional evidence that children younger than 6 years old might have difficulty representing counterfactual choices 103.

In sum, children are better able to reason about future compared to past counterfactual possibilities, perhaps because considering alternatives that require 'undoing' what actually took place taxes executive function abilities (such as inhibitory control), representational abilities, or some combination of both 24 . Indeed, young children and other animals might simply update current models of the world based on new information, whereas older children and adults are able to reason about time as a dimension itself 107 , 79 (see refs 79, 108 for a review of children's counterfactual thoughts). [H1] Relevance of counterfactuals

Making judgments of responsibility based on counterfactual reasoning depends not only on the existence of a counterfactual choice, but also on the subjective relevance of that counterfactual choice. For example, Sally might intend to file her taxes, but just as she is about to click 'submit' a deranged accountant tells her, "If you push that button, I will shoot you". Many people would presumably admit that there is some strict sense in which it is possible for Sally to choose to file the taxes under these circumstances, even though she would surrender her life as a consequence. However, they would be unlikely to consider this a relevant possibility; they will reason as if she had no alternative. This point can be illustrated by considering the application of ordinary modal language (language governed by representations of the relevance of alternative possibilities).

It would be perfectly natural to say, "Because of the threat, Sally had to be delinquent". By contrast, it would sound unnatural to say, "Sally was free to file her taxes on time." Of course, in cases of extreme duress it is often a metaphysical possibility for a person to choose to suffer grave harm-in other words, it does not violate laws of logic or nature. But this is not the way people use ordinary modal language; what governs the use of modal terms like 'could', 'had to', 'forced', and 'free' is instead an understanding of which alternative choices are relevant and which are not.

Unpacking what exactly it means for an alternative to be 'relevant' is not straightforward and has inspired a large literature [109] [110] [111] [112] . In adults, several related factors tend to contribute to the sense that a counterfactual is relevant, and to its likelihood of spontaneous consideration. These include whether the counterfactual is close, easy to imagine, and involves ex ante probable changes from actual circumstances and whether the counterfactual involves a better outcome or more normative action than the actual event [112] [113] [114] [115].

Children's spontaneous consideration of relevant counterfactuals might differ from adults' in two key ways (Figure 1c). First, they might consider fewer counterfactuals than adults because they have less experience with the world or because they do not see a counterfactual choice as relevant at all given that the choice has already been made. Although there is little direct evidence regarding children's judgments of counterfactual relevance, there are several studies on children's representations of the relevance of other kinds of non-This is not the most recent version of this paper. In one such study, children aged 4 to 8 were asked if it was possible to find an alligator under their bed (improbable) or to eat lightning (impossible). Younger children tended to report that improbable events were impossible, whereas older children and adults acknowledged that improbable events were possible 116, 117 . Children also tend to judge that things that are impermissible (for example, stealing a candy bar) are also impossible 118, 119.

Together, these results suggest that children might have a general tendency to think that fewer things are possible than adults. Interestingly, even adults exhibit this pattern of judgment when under time pressure, suggesting that the tendency to entertain a wide set of possibilities requires time or deliberation 109, 120, 121.

Second, children might generate different and perhaps more random counterfactual possibilities than adults-in other words, they might have different standards of relevance or ignore relevance altogether. Adults judge near counterfactuals (counterfactuals that involve few or small changes to actual events) to be more relevant than far counterfactuals 111 . For instance, adults tend to expect people to feel worse after near miss events (for example, missing a flight by a couple of minutes) than far miss events (for example, missing a flight by several hours) 122-124 . Some evidence suggests that until age 6 children do not robustly reason about counterfactuals in the nearest possible world when judging causal chains 17, 20, 25.

Similarly, it is not until around age 5 that children reliably distinguish between close and far counterfactuals, and their ability to do so continues to improve with age 125, 126 . For example, in one study 125 children were told about characters who had high or low chances of winning a prize and then failed to win it. By age 5 children judged that they would feel worse after the near miss. However, this judgment was not related to their judgements of how close the better alternative outcome actually was. In fact, before age 6 children did not correctly judge how close the counterfactual alternative was at all (but see ref 126 for some work suggesting preliminary understanding of proximal closeness between possible events from age 4).

Moreover, 4-year-olds tended to think that characters who almost received a better prize would feel happier relative to characters who had less chance of getting the better prize, possibly because they reasoned that even though the character did not get a good prize their closeness represents the likelihood that they would next time.

In another study 25 , children saw a ball travel along a trajectory, bump into a block, and then change direction. They were then asked how the ball would have moved had the block not been there. Children under 6 tended to not only modify the trajectory of the ball after the block, but also the trajectory the ball could have taken before the block. Only from 6 years old did children restrict their reasoning to the counterfactual that adults would deem most relevant-one in which all elements of the situation are held constant except the removal of the block. Thus, young children's spontaneous counterfactual reasoning in this task was not guided by considering near counterfactuals as most relevant.

Adults also judge counterfactual actions to be more relevant when they are controllable by the agent in question [127] [128] [129] [130] . Controllability might be related to relevance because the outcome of counterfactual past actions under an agent's control can guide thinking about how to behave in the future 115 . This dimension of counterfactual relevance is especially important for judgments of responsibility because it helps to explain why people typically hold agents more responsible for actions under their control.

Some studies examining regret in young children have manipulated their control over an outcome by varying if they themselves make a choice or their choice is made for them (for example, by rolling a die 131 ), the actual choices available 132, 133 , or by varying the control or foreseeability of the outcomes 134, 135 . Overall, the evidence suggests that children report consistently more regret and relief for controllable vs. uncontrollable bad and good outcomes respectively from around 8 years old.

However, results are somewhat mixed with respect to how controllability of counterfactual choices impact younger children's reported regret, relief and their propensity to feel curious about an outcome. For example, some work suggests that 4-and 5-year-olds show more curiosity about controllable (vs. uncontrollable) past choices that turned out badly 136 . By contrast, another study found that 4 to 7-year-olds tended to feel worse about uncontrollable and unforeseeable counterfactual choices than controllable and foreseeable choices, whereas 8-and 9-year-olds showed the opposite (and adult-like) pattern of feeling worse about choices they could control and foresee 134 . The failure to show an adult-like pattern indicates insensitivity to the most relevant counterfactual ('what if I had chosen otherwise'), which is only relevant in cases of controllability. The reversed pattern of feeling worse about uncontrollable behaviors might arise because younger children judge it bad to lack control, perhaps due to a focus on future utility rather than their own counterfactual choices. A propensity to focus on future utility could explain the apparently conflicting findings that younger children tend to be more curious about controllable counterfactuals 136.

In sum, people cannot possibly consider all counterfactuals. Instead, a small set of relevant counterfactuals come to mind and guide judgments of responsibility. [H1] Integration and implications

Adult-like judgments of responsibility depend on at least three developmental capacities: the understanding of choice as a concept; the ability to represent counterfactual timelines; and the ability to consider the relevant counterfactual timeline. Broadly speaking, these capacities might be integrated developmentally in two ways: true conceptual change (the possibility that young children fail to deploy an adult-like concept of choice or fail to accord this concept a central role in responsibility attribution) or a gradual sharpening of cognitive capacities, such as reliably reasoning about counterfactuals and identifying the most relevant among them.

Starting with the latter, there are several good reasons to conclude that, whether or not a true conceptual change occurs, at the very least young children are constrained by their capacity to reliably reason about relevant counterfactual alternatives. First, very young children have difficulties reliably reasoning about entirely physical counterfactuals, which seems to impact their experiences of regret over their own choices and their judgments of other's choices. Second, the development of reliable counterfactual reasoning is protracted, which is inconsistent with the kind of rapid reorganization that might be predicted if children's only obstacle were conceptual attainment. (However, this evidence comes from cross-sectional rather than longitudinal studies). Third, there are general limits on reasoning in children across related measures like appropriate use and understanding of modal language (Box 3) and the ability to represent and reason about false beliefs (Box 2).

It is much less certain whether children undergo change in the concept of choice relevant to responsibility judgment. One obstacle is that there is not a definitive characterization of the adult state, which is a necessary precursor to characterizing its developmental pathway. A second obstacle concerns experimental design: Young children have difficulty identifying and reasoning about relevant counterfactuals, which necessarily constrains their performance in standard responsibility attribution tasks where they are asked to render judgment about an actual situation. An alternative methodological approach might be to explicitly present children with relevant counterfactual alternatives (so they do not have to identify and construct these themselves) prior to responsibility judgment (as in ref 25 ).

If children do, in fact, undergo a conceptual shift in their understanding of choice and responsibility, a key challenge will be to determine what precipitates this change. We focus on two possibilities among many that could be considered. First, children only begin to reliably respond that a person could choose against their own desires-a response potentially diagnostic of an adult-like concept of choice-between ages 5-8 years. This finding is notable because it is also around this time that children become especially adept at reasoning about counterfactual possibilities (Figure 2). It is possible that this co-occurrence is coincidental during a period when many other changes occur. However, it might also be the case that children's capacity for robust counterfactual representation is part of what supports their acquisition of an adult-like concept of choice (for related perspectives see refs 14, 16, 24, 86 ). For instance, perhaps becoming better able to understand and reason about the counterfactuals that adults reference when making statements of responsibility enables children to recognize the importance of the counterfactual possibility of an individual having 'just chosen' otherwise. On this view, basic representational facility comes to support conceptual attainment.

Another possibility is that young children's concept of choice is refined as they begin to make choices in their own lives differently. There are notable developmental changes in children's capacity to regulate their own behavior between ages 4 to 8. Children under the age of 6 might most often observe themselves choosing according to present, felt desires. However, they might eventually notice themselves regulating their choices based on an additional sense of obligation, propriety or duty. If so, the idea that a person can 'just choose' to do otherwise might arise from the personal experience of wanting to do one thing and yet choosing to do otherwise (for related perspectives, see refs 59, 137 ). This possibility resonates with the idea that between the ages of 3 and 5 years children begin to understand joint commitments and agreements-a kind of 'we' obligation, akin to a social contract-that introduce a categorically different kind of constraint on their behavior than personal preference 138, 139 . Children consequently begin to choose behavior not only according to their own dispositions, but according to the obligation to meet collectively agreed-upon standards and roles. Perhaps as children incorporate social obligation in their own thinking, they also begin to judge the responsibility of others by asking whether those others could have chosen to do otherwise because of obligation. This possibility is highly speculative but warrants further study.

We identified three potential obstacles to making judgments of responsibility based on counterfactual reasoning in young children: Failure to deploy an adult-like concept of choice, failure to reliably represent and reason about counterfactuals, and failure to reliably identify the counterfactuals relevant to responsibility attribution. However, many open questions remain about the development of responsibility judgments in childhood.

First, we reviewed evidence that around age 6 children might begin to acquire the concept that it is possible to choose against one's preferences and work suggesting that it is also around this age that children begin to represent counterfactual sequences of events. One question is whether these two developments are related, and in what way. For example, it could be that children have a concept of free choice but struggle to apply it counterfactually.

Alternatively, it might be that a concept of free choice is somehow dependant on a capacity for counterfactual thinking. Future work could examine these possibilities directly, for example by asking children questions about choices that go against preferences and are either counterfactual or in the future. If children are better able to answer the latter but not the former, it would suggest that children have a concept of choice, but struggle to apply it counterfactually.

Second, work on children's representation of counterfactuals has found that they do not tend to consider the nearest counterfactual world 26 . However, less work has directly explored what exactly children are considering instead. Thus, it is unknown whether children's difficulty representing counterfactual choices reflects a difficulty representing any counterfactual world, or instead difficulty preferentially representing the closest (or most relevant) counterfactuals. Moreover, even when children are able to represent a near counterfactual world, they might not do so consistently. Future work could directly test which specific counterfactual choices (if any) children generate when judging responsibility, and whether or how these change with age.

Third, we have suggested that children's emerging concept of choosing against preference around the age of 5-8 years plays a role in the development of adult-like responsibility judgments. Prior to this age, children understand others' choices between alternatives based on expected value (the best expected satisfaction of their preferences). We further suggested that children's theory of rational choice is later augmented by an additional understanding of agency that transcends preference-a deliberative process by which any preferred action can be rejected and replaced. Reconciling these seemingly conflicting findings might provide important insight into children's understanding of responsibility. Future work should directly contrast how children consider choice across these two types of judgments. Doing so could help to clarify the structure of the mature concept of free will.

Finally, judgements of responsibility can encompass distinct judgments, such as blame or punishment. Prior work suggests that judgments of responsibility are an important precursor to other kinds of social evaluation, such as judgments about blame and whether punishment was deserved 29, 140 , especially in cases of negligence [141] [142] [143] , as well as what kinds of outcomes are justified after a transgression 30, 144 . We predict that the developmental changes reviewed here will align with reported changes in children's social evaluations more broadly, insofar as they depend upon judgments of responsibility. Future research could explore if judgements of responsibility based on counterfactual reasoning differentially affect these distinct outcomes.

In addition to informing understanding of child development, answering these developmental questions could shed light on how adults judge responsibility. For instance, whereas adults consider it relevant whether-and which-alternative actions a person had available, young children's responsibility judgments are less sensitive to such manipulations.

Box 1: Alternative mechanisms

There are many ways that people can-and do-make judgments of responsibility that do not depend on contrasting counterfactual representations. These judgments of responsibility may depend only on the actual facts or events that transpired. For instance, a driver might be held responsible for speeding based on the fact that they were operating a speeding vehicle, or a soccer player might be held responsible for a loss because they struck the ball into their own goal. This causal evaluation does not necessarily require counterfactual reasoning, in part because of the series of physical events connecting an agent with subsequent harm. Indeed, causal and counterfactual reasoning have been differentiated in adults (see ref 2 for a review) and children (see ref 147 for a review). Other non-counterfactual foundations for responsibility judgment could include ownership (for example, responsibility for harms caused by a dog you owned, or a tree on your property) and promise (for example, responsibility for the failure of product delivery because of a contractual obligation). Thus, there are alternative mechanisms of responsibility judgments that do not depend on counterfactual representations that might be available to young children. These alternatives must be considered carefully before concluding that children have a capacity for counterfactual judgment based on a correspondence between their responsibility attributions and those of adults.

BOX 2: Mental states and judgments of responsibility

An extensive body of work over has examined children's capacity to represent the minds of others, known as theory of mind [148] [149] [150] [151] . This capacity is relevant to children's ability to judge responsibility. For example, by the age of 5 (and, in some cases, much earlier) children reliably incorporate information about a person's mental states-and especially their intent-into their moral judgments of character and punishment 152, 153 . Furthermore, the concepts of choice and intention are intimately connected in the folk theory of mind: choices can reveal a person's intentions 43 , and people are more likely to describe a person's behavior as a genuine choice if it reflects their intentions 154.

Given that around 5 years old children first reliably use an agent's benign intent to exonerate cases of accidental harm in vignette studies (ref 155 ), future work could explore how this ability interacts with the components of counterfactual reasoning related to responsibility judgments that come online concurrently. This relationship is of special interest because mature judgments of accidental harm (relative to intentional harm) might be particularly reliant on counterfactual reasoning. In these cases, an agent would not have caused harm had they, counterfactually, had access to full information and adequate behavioural control.

However, some evidence suggests that intent-based and responsibility-based moral judgments are distinct constructs 2, 32 . For instance, people often assign some degree of punishment even to accidental or negligent harms (harmful actions that lack intent) 32 . In these cases, punishment seems to depend on a counterfactual appraisal of whether the action caused harm.

Relatedly, to consider whether somebody could have chosen differently and thereby brought about different consequences, it is important to understand whether they believed that a different choice would have led to different consequences. In other words, people's choices are not only constrained by what is physically possible, but also what people think is possible: if someone did not know that cake was available on the menu, they could not have chosen it by ordinary deliberation, even though counterfactual beliefs would render this alternative action available 102 . The relevant counterfactual for responsibility judgments is therefore whether the agent could have reasonably deliberated and chosen otherwise while holding constant inputs to the deliberative process (such as their beliefs).

BOX 3: Modal language and reasoning about counterfactual choices

Counterfactuals are expressed using modal language such as 'could have' or 'would have'. A large body of work examines children's emerging understanding of modal language and modal thought. Although perspectives on what exactly is changing during development vary (for two perspectives see refs 157, 158 ), children's use and understanding of modal language improve between ages 2 and 4 (refs [158] [159] [160] ). Like counterfactuals, modal language describes non-actual possibilities that contradict actual states (past or present) 158, 161 . It therefore seems plausible that children's understanding and use of modal language might reflect their developing competency in reasoning about counterfactuals.

There is also some evidence that acquiring modal language scaffolds children's ability to reason about counterfactuals. For instance, differences in how counterfactuals are expressed in different languages (such as in Chinese and English) influence when children grasp the related concepts 162 . However, children younger than age 5 or 6 years struggle in tasks that demand counterfactual reasoning but involve minimal counterfactual language 17, 98.

Thus, the fundamental developmental constraints are more likely to be conceptual than lexical.

BOX 4: Culture and reasoning about counterfactual choices

If choice is a concept that children learn about through experience or explicit teaching, there might be differences in how this concept is applied or understood across cultures because of differences in language and socialization. Indeed, some work has found cross-country differences in the extent to which children judge that it is possible to act against desires or preferences (see ref 22 for a review). For example, one study 163 found that the extent to which children judge that it is possible to act against one's desires emerged later among children in Singapore and Peru compared to children in the US and China. Another study found that children in Nepal were more likely to report that it was not possible to act against a social obligation than children in the US 164.

The way in which children consider choice as relevant to their moral judgments might also differ across cultures (for evidence of such cross-cultural differences in adults, see refs 145, 146 ). For example, children in the US and China both judged that a character who caused a bad outcome and who had a better choice available to them was meaner than a character who had no other choice but to cause a bad outcome 102 . Interestingly, children in India did not clearly make this distinction and tended to judge all characters as generally positive irrespective of choice. These results suggest that the choices children consider to be relevant might differ based on culture or socialization 165 . Future work should test this possibility directly.
