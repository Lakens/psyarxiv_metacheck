Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Sustained attention is a central deficit in individuals with Attention Deficit Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD), but how their attention fluctuates-alternating between periods of good and poor attentionremains poorly characterized. In non-clinical samples, two main methods are used to study attentional fluctuations: an objective method, based on reaction time variability distinguishing between stable "inthe-zone" periods and erratic "out-of-the-zone" periods, and a subjective method, based on selfreported experiences of being focused versus mind-wandering. The goal of the current study is to investigate the subjective experiences associated with objectively defined out-of-the-zone periods, by combining performance data with self-reported measures of mind-wandering (MW), motivation, boredom, fatigue, and perceived task difficulty. Participants with ADHD (N = 23) matched with control participants (N = 23) completed a Sustained Attention to Response Task, which included probes at the end of each block to assess subjective states. Our first results replicated previous findings in controls; out-of-the-zone periods did not predict increased mind-wandering. In contrast, out-of-the-zone periods predicted mind-wandering in ADHD, as well as lower motivation, higher boredom, and greater fatigue, suggesting that RT variability is more strongly associated with a negative experience in ADHD participants. These findings revealed that individuals with ADHD exhibit a greater overlap between objectively defined out-of-the-zone periods and subjectively reported attentional disengagement, which might explain their increased frequency of attentional lapses. Together, these results highlight the importance of jointly considering both objective and subjective measures of attentional fluctuations to better characterize sustained attention in psychiatric conditions.

1. INTRODUCTION

Who has never been distracted by their thoughts while performing everyday tasks such as driving a car or reading a book? Maintaining focus over time-referred to as sustained attention-is challenging for everyone, but especially for individuals with Attention Deficit Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD). This psychiatric condition is characterized by a triad of symptoms: inattention, hyperactivity, and impulsivity, and affects approximately 3-5% of the adult population (Kessler et al., 2005). Although several studies have attempted to better characterize sustained attention deficits in participants with ADHD, a number of important advances made in recent years in neurotypical populations have yet to be explored in this clinical group. Among these, the characterization of sustained attention fluctuations -between periods of high and low attentional engagement-has been scarcely explored in patients, limiting our understanding of sustained attention deficits in ADHD.

To investigate sustained attention in experimental settings, researchers typically use go/no-go or Continuous Performance Tasks (CPTs). In these tasks, participants are instructed to press a response button when frequent go stimuli appear, and to inhibit their response when infrequent no-go stimuli are presented (Robertson et al., 1997). Lapses in sustained attention are commonly indexed by commission errors-failures to withhold a response on no-go trials.

To better understand when and why attentional lapses occur, researchers have proposed two main theoretical accounts. The overload theory posits that sustained attention relies on limited cognitive resources that become depleted over time, leading to increased errors, mental fatigue, and disengagement (Baumeister et al., 1998; Grier et al., 2003; Parasuraman et al., 1998). In contrast, the underload theory suggests that attentional failures arise from low task engagement due to boredom and insufficient stimulation, resulting in a mindless mode of processing (Manly et al., 1999; Robertson et al., 1997). While both theories account for important aspects of attentional decline, neither fully explains the diversity of empirical findings. For example, if attentional lapses were solely due to resource depletion, motivational factors such as reward should have little impact-yet rewards often improve performance (Chidharom, Vogel, et al., 2025; Chidharom et al., 2026; Esterman et al., 2016 Esterman et al., , 2017;; Esterman & Rothlein, 2019; Massar et al., 2016; Seli, Cheyne, et al., 2015). Conversely, if boredom were the primary cause, making the task less boring by increasing task difficulty should reduce lapses-yet it frequently exacerbates them (Helton & Russell, 2011 , 2013; Helton & Warm, 2008).

Although these studies have identified motivation, fatigue, boredom, and perceived task difficulty as factors associated with sustained attention performance, the contradictions between existing theories prevent accurate prediction of when lapses will occur.

These theories are largely based on the study of vigilance decline-that is, the increase in attentional lapses over the course of a task. However, recent advances have shown that attention does not only change gradually over time but also fluctuates from moment to moment, alternating between periods of high and low attentional engagement. These moment-to-moment fluctuations in sustained attention, reflected in transitions between different mental states, have proven to be strong predictors of attentional lapses with greater temporal precision than overall vigilance decline.

Two main methodological approaches have been developed to assess these fluctuations in sustained attention. One commonly used method relies on subjective self-reports, typically using thought-probes. These probes interrupt the task at random intervals and ask participants to reflect on the content of their thoughts. Responses generally fall into two categories: on-task, where attention is focused on the task, and off-task or mind-wandering, where thoughts drift away from task-related goals.

A growing body of evidence shows that people tend to make more commission errors-lapses of attention-during mind-wandering moments than when they are fully engaged (Andrillon et al., 2021; Bertschi et al., 2025; Chidharom, Bonnefond, et al., 2025; Horáková et al., 2026). These findings support the use of thought-probe methods as a valid tool for identifying different attentional states (Kane et al., 2021; Welhaf et al., 2023).

In contrast, an objective approach focuses on behavioral performance-specifically, momentto-moment changes in reaction times during sustained attention tasks. Attentional stability is assessed using the Variance Time Course (VTC), which quantifies the deviation of each correct response time from the participant's mean RT. A median split of these VTC values identifies stable, consistent response periods (in-the-zone) versus periods marked by greater variability (out-of-the-zone). The latter are associated with increased lapses rate. This pattern has been reliably observed across a variety of tasks and replicated in both neurotypical and schizophrenia samples (Chidharom, Krieg, & Bonnefond, 2021; Chidharom, Krieg, Pham, et al., 2021; Esterman et al., 2013 Esterman et al., , 2015)), establishing the VTC as a robust and objective marker of attentional state fluctuations.

Although both methods have been effective in isolating fluctuations in sustained attention, recent findings suggest that they may capture distinct types of attentional variability in some tasks, which could jointly contribute to attentional deficits (Bertschi et al., 2025; Chidharom, Bonnefond, et al., 2025). For instance, Chidharom et al. (2025) found that out-of-the-zone periods were not more frequent prior to self-reports of mind-wandering compared to on-task reports, suggesting that subjective and objective fluctuations in attention do not systematically overlap. Moreover, the negative effects of poor attention -whether subjectively or objectively defined-appeared to be cumulative, particularly in relation to cognitive control mechanisms. Specifically, post-error slowing, an adaptive response aimed at preventing future lapses, was preserved during objectively defined out-of-the-zone periods when participants reported being on task, indicating intact performance adaptation. However, the same objective state was associated with disrupted post-error slowing when participants reported being in a mind-wandering state, highlighting the additional burden of concurrent subjective disengagement. Above all, this study revealed that out-of-the-zone periods are not always driven by mind-wandering, and the subjective experience underlying these objective periods remains an open question.

In individuals with ADHD, sustained attention deficits and the increased frequency of attentional lapses have been explained through frameworks aligned with both overload and underload theories. Barkley (1997) was among the first to attribute sustained attention impairments in ADHD to deficits in cognitive control, aligning with overload accounts (Barkley, 1997). In this view, failures in inhibitory control disrupt resource allocations to other executive functions, such as working memory and selfregulation of motivation, thereby increasing the likelihood of attentional lapses. In contrast, Sonuga-Barke (2005) proposed a theoretical account more consistent with underload theories. According to his perspective, individuals with ADHD struggle to maintain attention on tasks that are unrewarding, lack immediate incentives, or are perceived as boring (Sonuga-Barke, 2005).

Although theoretical debates continue regarding the mechanisms underlying attentional lapses in ADHD, subjective and objective approaches to measuring sustained attention fluctuations have rarely been applied to this population-despite their strong predictive value for lapses in neurotypical individuals. Yet, evidence suggests that ADHD subjects exhibit stronger markers of attentional variability on both fronts. Objectively, they show greater reaction time variability compared to neurotypical individuals (Epstein et al., 2011; Kofler et al., 2013; Tamm et al., 2012), and subjectively, they report more frequent episodes of mind-wandering (Arabacı & Parris, 2018; Lanier et al., 2021; Seli, Smallwood, et al., 2015; Van den Driessche et al., 2017). However, to our knowledge, no study has combined both objective and subjective approaches to examine attentional fluctuations in this population.

The aim of this study is to better characterize the subjective experience underlying objectively defined out-of-the-zone periods, in order to improve our understanding of sustained attention lapses in both neurotypical individuals and individuals with ADHD. To this end, participants completed a SART interspersed with probes at the end of each block. These subjective probes assessed mind-wandering episodes, as well as four additional subjective factors linked to sustained attention theories: motivation and boredom (underload), and fatigue and task difficulty (overload). In neurotypical individuals, we hypothesize a replication of previous findings, showing no overlap between objectively defined out-ofthe-zone periods and reported mind-wandering, suggesting a greater influence of other subjective states such as reduced motivation or increased difficulty. In individuals with ADHD, two hypotheses may account for the greater deficits in sustained attention observed in patients. In the first hypothesis, we posit that individuals with ADHD experience a greater temporal overlap between objective and subjective attentional fluctuations, resulting in a cumulative effect of both impaired objective performance and degraded subjective attentional states. This convergence may lead to a higher frequency of attentional lapses. A second hypothesis suggests that individuals with ADHD exhibit reduced overlap between these two types of fluctuations. This increased dissociation between objective and subjective attentional periods may result in prolonged time spent in suboptimal attentional states, thereby contributing to sustained attention deficits.

2. METHOD

>METHOD

2.1 Participants

This study reanalyzes a subset of the data reported in Horáková et al. (2026), comprising 26 medication-naïve individuals with ADHD and 25 controls. Five participants (three with ADHD and two controls) were excluded because they did not report at least one on-task or one mind-wandering episode during the task. Because the present study represents a secondary analysis of an existing dataset, the sample size was determined by the original study rather than by an a priori power analysis specifically designed to test the present hypotheses. Participants were between 18 and 59 years old (mean ± SD: 31.4 ± 10.6 years), and the two groups of the final sample were comparable in terms of age, sex, and educational background. Inclusion criteria required participants to have no history of neurological or other psychiatric disorders, no previous treatment with psychostimulants, and no incidents of head injury involving loss of consciousness. Participants in the ADHD group had been diagnosed prior to study inclusion by psychiatrists specializing in adult ADHD in the Strasbourg University Hospitals, France. Diagnoses were established through a comprehensive clinical assessment based on DSM-5 criteria, supported by the semi-structured Diagnostic Interview for ADHD in Adults (DIVA). Whenever available, retrospective information on childhood symptoms was corroborated by collateral reports and/or documentary evidence, such as school reports. A comprehensive psychiatric evaluation was also conducted to assess psychiatric comorbidities and alternative explanations for ADHD symptoms. All participants gave written informed consent and were financially compensated for taking part in the study.

2.2 Stimuli and Procedure

Participants completed the Sustained Attention to Response Task (SART), a go/no-go task using single digits from 1 to 9, shown in random order with equal frequency. The digit "3" appeared in approximately 10% of the trials and acted as the no-go signal, meaning participants had to withhold their response when it appeared. All other digits were go signals that required a button press. Digits were presented in black Arial font, with sizes ranging from 100 to 180 points, and appeared slightly above a yellow fixation cross on a gray background. Each digit remained visible for 167 ms, followed by a randomly selected interval of 1500, 1700, 2100, 2300, or 2500 ms. The task was displayed on a standard 17-inch computer monitor. The full task included 810 trials, made up of 720 go trials and 90 no-go trials. The design is thus a replication of Chidharom and colleagues (2025), with the main exception that the probe appeared not every 30 trials but either every 27, 54, or 81 trials, for a total of 15 blocks per participant. The entire session took around 40 minutes. Before starting the actual task, participants were given detailed instructions and completed a 3-minute of practice trials (Figure 1A).

2.3 Measuring the fluctuations of sustained attention.

Measure of the subjective attentional states. The thought-probe method was used to measure the subjective states. At the end of every block, participants responded to a short thought probe consisting of five questions. The first question asked them to indicate whether their attention was on the task or if their mind had wandered just before the probe. This question appeared once per block, totaling 15 times. The remaining four questions asked participants to rate their motivation, boredom, fatigue, and how difficult they found the task, using visual analog scales from 0 ("Lowest level") to 100 ("Highest level").

Measure of the objective attentional states.

Objective in-the-zone and out-of-the-zone states were defined following the method described by Esterman et al. (2013). We measured how consistent each participant's reaction times were by calculating the variance time course (VTC). To standardize the data, we first normalized each participant's RTs, allowing comparisons on the same scale. The VTC was based only on correct responses during go trials, where each trial's score represented how much that reaction time differed from the participant's average RT. For trials with no responses or errorssuch as missed go trials or correctly withheld no-go trials-RT values were estimated using linear interpolation from the surrounding trials. We then split performance into two categories using the median VTC value across blocks: "in-the-zone" (low RT variability) and "out-of-the-zone" (high RT variability).

2.4 Behavioral analysis

To evaluate how well both subjective and objective methods captured changes in sustained attention, we calculated standard performance measures for the entire block before each probe, similar to the approach used by Chidharom et al. (2025). We measured the average reaction time (RT) for correct responses during go trials to assess how quickly participants reacted. The RT variability of correct responses was analyzed through the coefficient of variation (CV) by dividing the standard deviation of reaction times by the mean of reaction times. To assess accuracy, we calculated the percentage of lapses in attention, defined as commission errors-pressing the button when they shouldn't during no-go trials. The commission error rate was calculated by dividing the number of errors on no-go trials by the total number of no-go trials within each attentional state. Omission errors, which are missed responses to go trials, were rare (only 0.54%) and therefore not included in the analysis.

Finally, to see how often participants were out-of-the-zone during different subjective states, we calculated the percentage of high-variability trials that occurred during either on-task or mind-wandering periods, based on the total number of trials in each subjective state.

2.5 Statistical analysis.

Performance measures were analyzed using a repeated-measures ANOVA with two withinsubjects factors, Objective State (in-the-zone vs. out-of-the-zone) and Subjective State (on-task vs. mind-wandering), as well as a between-subjects factor Group (ADHD vs. Control). When significant interactions were found, post hoc paired t-tests were conducted. A Tukey test was also performed to control for multiple comparisons. To examine whether out-of-the-zone periods predicted the selfreported subjective state, we used a generalized mixed-effects model specified as follows:

The subjective states focused on task-related thoughts (on-task; coded as 1) versus taskunrelated thoughts (mind-wandering; coded as 0). This model allowed us to compute the percentage of out-of-the-zone trials that preceded each type of probe (on-task and mind-wandering) separately. To smooth the data, a sliding window approach was applied, averaging the percentage of out-of-the-zone states across three trials before and after each trial, similar to Chidharom et al., 2025. The trial factor included the 20 trials preceding each probe. This window was chosen because the minimum number of trials between probes was 27, allowing us to avoid including post-probe trials in the analysis. To maintain consistency in the analytical structure, the median scores for motivation, boredom, fatigue, and perceived difficulty were computed for each participant. These scores were then used to categorize trials into high (coded as 0) or low (coded as 1) subjective experience groups for further comparison.

In the control group, greater out-of-the-zone time significantly predicted higher difficulty states only in early trials (Mean -1 SD; B = -0.02102, SE = 0.01045, z = -2.011, p = .044). The effect did not reach significance at the middle trial position (B = -0.00170, SE = 0.00747, p = .820), nor in later trials (Mean +1 SD; B = 0.01761, SE = 0.00961, p = .067). No other interactions reached significance.

3.4 Associations between task performance, subjective measures and ADHD symptoms

3.4.1 Relationship between lapses rate and ADHD symptoms

To better understand the relationship between the lapses rate and the ADHD symptoms, Pearson correlation analysis was performed across control and ADHD participants, and revealed that individuals with higher ASRS scores exhibit higher lapses rate, r(44) = .34, p = .021. lapses rate was associated with the hyperactivity/impulsivity score, r(44) = .40, p = .006; and not with the inattention score, r(44) = .24, p = .104.

3.4.2 Relationship between lapses rate and subjective reports

The lapses rate positively correlated with the difficulty rating, r(44) = .60, p = < .001; but were not significantly related to mind-wandering report, r(44) = .20, p = .189; motivation, r(44) = .01, p = .925; boredom, r(44) = .16, p = .289; or fatigue, r(44) = .24, p = .115.

3.4.3 Relationship between subjective reports

Mind-wandering was negatively associated with motivation, r(44) = -.49, p < .001, and positively associated with boredom, r(44) = .58, p < .001, and fatigue, r(44) = .40, p = .006. Motivation was strongly negatively correlated with boredom, r(44) = -.70, p < .001, and with fatigue, r(44) = -.59, p < .001. Boredom showed a strong positive association with fatigue, r(44) = .77, p < .001. Perceived difficulty was not significantly related to mind-wandering, r(44) = .18, p = .227; motivation, r(44) = -.14, p = .364; boredom, r(44) = .15, p = .330; or fatigue, r(44) = .24, p = .114.

3.4.4 Relationship between subjective reports and ADHD symptoms

Mind-wandering was strongly and positively related to ASRS total scores, r(44) = .56, p < .001, as well as to ASRS inattention, r(44) = .50, p < .001, and ASRS hyperactivity/impulsivity, r(44) = .56, p < .001.

Motivation showed a small negative association with ASRS total scores, r(44) = -.35, p = .018, and with hyperactivity/impulsivity, r(44) = -.39, p = .008, but was not significantly related to inattention, r(44) = -.27, p = .065. Boredom was not associated with ASRS total scores, r(44) = .27, p = .068, nor with inattention, r(44) = .28, p = .064, nor hyperactivity/impulsivity, r(44) = .24, p = .110. Fatigue correlated positively with ASRS total scores, r(44) = .33, p = .026, with inattention, r(44) = .32, p = .033, and with hyperactivity/impulsivity, r(44) = .31, p = .037. Perceived task difficulty correlated positively with ASRS total scores, r(44) = .32, p = .032, and hyperactivity/impulsivity, r(44) = .34, p = .022; but not inattention, r(44) = .26, p = .080.

4. DISCUSSION

The aim of this study was to better characterize the subjective experience associated with outof-the-zone states, indexed by high reaction time variability, in ADHD participants and control participants. Our results revealed a clear dissociation between the two groups. In ADHD, out-of-thezone periods were associated with increased mind-wandering, along with lower motivation, greater boredom, higher fatigue, and increased perceived difficulty. In control participants, the same objective state was instead associated with higher motivation and lower boredom, without increased reports of mind-wandering, indicating a dissociation between objective and subjective attentional fluctuations in control participants. Together, these findings replicate previous results observed in healthy participants and extend them by providing a more precise characterization of the subjective experience accompanying high reaction time variability in ADHD participants.

In the current study, we found converging evidence of sustained attention deficits in ADHD participants. First, ADHD participants reported significantly more mind-wandering than control participants. This effect was observed both in the higher proportion of task-unrelated thoughts during the SART (61% in ADHD participants vs. 35% in control participants) and in Pearson correlation analyses showing a positive association between mind-wandering reports and ASRS scores, for both the inattention and hyperactivity/impulsivity subscales.

These subjective alterations in sustained attention were also associated with objective impairments. ASRS scores were positively correlated with no-go errors, with a particularly strong relationship for the hyperactivity/impulsivity subscale, suggesting a close link between attentional lapses and motor-related symptoms. In addition, ADHD participants showed greater reaction time variability than controls, replicating previous findings (Buzy et al., 2009; Kofler et al., 2013; Moses et al., 2022).

Beyond performance measures, sustained attention deficits in ADHD were accompanied by more negative subjective experiences during the task. Compared to control participants, ADHD participants reported lower motivation, greater fatigue, and higher levels of boredom during the SART.

Taken together, these results indicate a clear difficulty in sustaining attention in ADHD participants.

While these findings largely replicate previous work, the present study extends the literature by characterizing sustained attention deficits through the subjective experience associated with objective fluctuations in attention.

The analysis of the objective and subjective attentional fluctuations showed a successful replication of the capture of these attentional states in our go/nogo task. Indeed, both ADHD and control participants exhibit higher lapses rate (errors on nogo) during out-of-the-zone compared to in-the-zone states, objectively defined as high vs. low RT variability, respectively. Similarly, participants exhibit higher lapses rate during subjectively reported mind-wandering compared to on-task periods, also replicating previous findings (Chidharom, Bonnefond, et al., 2025; Chidharom, Krieg, & Bonnefond, 2021; Chidharom, Krieg, Pham, et al., 2021; Chidharom & Bonnefond, 2023; Esterman et al., 2013).

In control participants, out-of-the-zone states did not overlap with mind-wandering reports.

Specifically, participants did not spend more time in an out-of-the-zone state during mind-wandering than during on-task reports. Consistently, logistic regression analyses showed that time spent out of the zone immediately preceding the probe did not predict mind-wandering. These findings are in line with Chidharom et al. (2025), who used a similar task and showed that time spent out of the zone did not differ between mind-wandering and on-task reports. Converging evidence is also provided by Bertschi et al. (PsyArXiv), who demonstrated a dissociation between subjective and objective attentional states in a bilateral CPT. In that study, mind-wandering was associated with faster reaction times and lower arousal, whereas objective out-of-the-zone states were characterized by slower reaction times and no modulation of arousal (Bertschi et al., 2025).

In ADHD, out-of-the-zone states overlap with mind-wandering reports. Specifically, ADHD participants spent more time in an out-of-the-zone state during mind-wandering reports than during ontask reports. In addition, regression analyses focusing on the period immediately preceding the probe revealed a stronger predictive effect of out-of-the-zone states, indexed by higher reaction time variability, on the report of task-unrelated thoughts. These findings suggest that two impairing attentional states, an objectively defined out-of-the-zone state and a subjectively reported mindwandering state, are more likely to co-occur in ADHD participants, which might result in more frequent disruptions of sustained attention. This cumulative detrimental effect of converging objective and subjective attentional impairments on performance has been reported previously (Chidharom et al., 2025). Specifically, post-error slowing, a mechanism thought to support the reallocation of attention following a lapse, was disrupted only when participants were simultaneously mind-wandering and out of the zone. This pattern could indicate that the alignment of mind-wandering and out-of-the-zone states leads to a greater breakdown of cognitive control mechanisms in ADHD.

The present study therefore shows that out-of-the-zone states predict mind-wandering in ADHD but not in control participants. Importantly, this difference in predictive relationships extends beyond mind-wandering to other types of subjective reports. In ADHD, regression analyses focusing on the period immediately preceding the probe revealed that high RT variability predicted reduced motivation, greater fatigue, increased boredom, and higher perceived difficulty. This pattern suggests that elevated RT variability is systematically associated with negative subjective experiences in ADHD participants.

In control participants, by contrast, out-of-the-zone states unexpectedly predicted higher motivation and lower boredom. While this clearly distinguishes controls from ADHD participants, the direction of this effect is surprising, as motivation is typically associated with improved sustained attention performance. For instance, increasing motivation through monetary reward in CPT paradigms reliably reduces lapses rates compared to unrewarded conditions (Chidharom et al., 2026; Chidharom, Vogel, et al., 2025; Esterman et al., 2017). One possible interpretation is that, in control participants, out-of-the-zone states may reflect a transient re-engagement of cognitive control rather than a sustained attentional failure. Supporting this interpretation, fMRI results have shown increased engagement of cognitive control regions during out-of-the-zone states, particularly prior to correct nogo inhibitions. Thus, in neurotypical individuals, these states may capture moments of attentional reengagement, leading to higher reported motivation and reduced boredom. However, this interpretation remains speculative and should be replicated in future studies.

5. CONCLUSION

To conclude, this study shows a more frequent overlap between objectively and subjectively impaired attentional states in ADHD compared to control participants, which may contribute to sustained attention deficits in ADHD participants. In addition, ADHD participants reported a more negative subjective experience during the SART than controls, highlighting the genuine difficulty they face in maintaining attention during a monotonous task. Although this work advances our understanding of sustained attention fluctuations, further studies are needed to replicate these findings across different sustained attention paradigms. For example, in tasks such as the Metronome Response Task or CPTs with fixed stimulus presentation intervals, reaction time variability appears to be a stronger predictor of mind-wandering (Bertschi et al., 2025; Godwin et al., 2023; Seli et al., 2013). Such paradigms may be particularly informative in ADHD and could help further refine the characterization of sustained attention fluctuations in this population.
