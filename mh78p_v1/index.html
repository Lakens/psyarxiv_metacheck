Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. Abstract

Background: Heritage-language maintenance is shaped not only by family language practices but also by broader social and community contexts. This study examined how participants in community-based talleres (workshops) described the resources they used to support bilingualism and heritage-language maintenance, using Community Cultural Wealth (CCW; Yosso, 2005) as the primary framework.

Methods: Four community-based talleres were held at a public library in a highly multicultural area of San Diego, California. Participants included 23 individuals ranging from families, youth, educators, and community members. Data included facilitator field notes analyzed using a CCWguided qualitative approach alongside inductively identified themes, as well as pre-and postworkshop survey responses summarized descriptively.

Results: Participants described heritage-language maintenance as extending beyond homelanguage use to include literacy, extended-family relationships, cultural activities, educational opportunities, community resources, social networks, and cross-border connections. Linguistic, familial, social, navigational, aspirational, and resistant capital operated as interconnected resources. Participants also described tensions surrounding correction, "proper" Spanish, and linguistic legitimacy. Survey responses were generally favorable at baseline, with shifts in beliefs about school-language difficulties and caregiver expertise.

The talleres functioned as reciprocal spaces in which community knowledge contextualized and sometimes reshaped research-based recommendations. Heritage-language maintenance emerged as an ecological and relational process shaped by family and community resources, opportunities for meaningful language use, and linguistic legitimacy. Community-2 engaged approaches may be most effective when they recognize families, external community networks as knowledge holders and avoid overly prescriptive recommendations.

Introduction

Gloria Anzaldúa (1999) described life in the borderlands as "living on borders and in margins, keeping intact one's shifting and multiple identity and integrity," an experience she compared to "trying to swim in a new element, an 'alien ' element" (p. 19). Rooted in the U.S.-Mexico border, Anzaldúa's borderlands describe spaces where languages, cultures, identities, and systems of power intersect. Bilingual families navigate these intersections across homes, schools, communities, and institutions, where languages serve different functions and carry unequal social value. Heritage-language maintenance must be understood within the broader social conditions that shape language use across contexts and generations. These conditions are relevant in the linguistically diverse United States. In 2025, approximately 74.2 million U.S. residents aged five and older spoke a language other than English at home, including nearly 45 million Spanish speakers (U.S. Census Bureau, 2025). Although home-language use is not equivalent to bilingual proficiency, these estimates underscore the scale of multilingualism in the United States.

Yet demographic diversity does not correspond to equal institutional support for multilingualism. English remains dominant across many educational and public contexts. U.S. bilingual education has historically alternated between additive approaches that support continued bilingual development and transitional approaches that use the home language primarily to facilitate English acquisition. Gándara (2018) describes how bilingual education was often framed as a response to linguistic disadvantage rather than as a means of sustaining bilingualism and biliteracy. Under such models, the home language functioned as a bridge to English rather than as an enduring educational resource.

These tensions are salient in California, where 44.4% of residents age five and older reported speaking a language other than English at home between 2020 and 2024 (U.S. Census Bureau, n.d.). Spanish-English bilingualism is widespread, but bilingual experiences vary by migration history, generation, geography, family language practices, schooling, and opportunities for use. This variation challenges accounts of U.S. Spanish centered on language loss or deviation from monolingual norms and instead points to the heterogeneity of bilingual experience (Amengual et al., 2026).

California's location along the U.S.-Mexico border adds another dimension to this linguistic ecology. Border-region contexts can create opportunities for movement across linguistic, familial, and cultural networks. For example, Brochin Ceballos (2012) found that transnational experiences supported Spanish biliteracy and bicultural identities among bilingual teachers who had limited access to additive bilingual education. These experiences can expand opportunities for heritage-language use, but proximity to the border alone does not ensure maintenance. Continued transmission depends on sustained opportunities to use and develop the heritage language across family, school, and community contexts. The present study takes a community-centered approach to engage directly with families and community members to better understand their experiential knowledge about the linguistic needs of their children and the resources available within their communities in maintaining their heritage languages.

1. Literature Review

>Literature Review

1.1 Heritage Language Maintenance and Shift Across Generations

Heritage languages are minoritized languages learned early through family and community interaction in settings where another language typically dominates schooling and public life (Montrul, 2023; Rothman, 2009). In the United States, this often produces an intergenerational pattern in which immigrant adults primarily use the heritage language (e.g., Spanish), their children become bilingual, and later generations progressively use English more (Alba et al., 2002; Potowski, 2013; Portes & Hao, 1998 , 2002). Language maintenance depends on continued transmission from one generation to the next and on opportunities to use the language beyond early childhood (Pascual y Cabo & Gómez Soler, 2015; Potowski, 2013).

School entry can change these linguistic opportunities. Children who have used Spanish at home may begin spending much of the day learning, reading, and interacting with peers in English. As English use expands across settings, opportunities to hear and produce Spanish may become concentrated within the home. Research with Spanish-English bilinguals shows that changes in the relative amount of input and use in each language are important for Spanish development (Bohman et al., 2010; Hoff & Core, 2013; Montanari et al., 2025). Importantly, English schooling does not necessarily produce immediate Spanish loss: Montanari and colleagues (2025) found continued growth in Spanish during children's first year of English-only preschool in Los Angeles, although outcomes varied with children's experiences and maternal cultural orientation. Sustained instruction in Spanish can provide additional opportunities for development (Cabrera Pérez et al., 2025). Among school-age Spanish-English bilingual children, Montrul and Potowski (2007) found no evidence of increasing language loss with age among students enrolled in a dual-immersion program, suggesting that heritage-language schooling can support continued development.

Opportunities beyond school are also important for heritage-language maintenance.

Heritage-language maintenance is associated with regular use with parents and extended family, access to other speakers, literacy and media experiences, social networks, and institutional support (Albudoor et al., 2025; DeCapua & Wintergerst, 2009; Potowski, 2013). Community context can expand these opportunities. Potowski (2013), for example, reviews evidence that greater concentrations of heritage-language speakers and proximity to regions where the language is widely used are associated with greater maintenance. At the same time, the demographic presence of Spanish does not ensure its transmission: continued immigration may sustain Spanish at the population level even when individual families shift toward English across generations.

These findings also caution against equating bilingual variation with language loss. Heritage speakers differ in when, where, and with whom they use each language, and these differences shape vocabulary, grammar, literacy, and patterns of language choice. Higby et al. (2023) argue that evaluating such outcomes against monolingual norms can position expected bilingual variation as deficiency. The distinction is important for family bilingualism: reduced use of Spanish in some settings may indicate changing language opportunities, but it does not by itself demonstrate that a child has lost the language.

Continued heritage-language use can also sustain communication with parents and extended family and preserve access to broader social networks (DeCapua & Wintergerst, 2009; Gándara, 2018). Such relational functions are especially relevant to the present study because participants frequently described bilingualism in terms of intergenerational communication, family connection, and future transmission. Heritage-language maintenance depends not only on family intentions but also on families' access to, and ability to create or navigate, opportunities for meaningful language use. This distinction shifts attention from individual language choices toward the social and institutional resources that make those choices possible.

1.2 Language Ideologies and Linguistic Legitimacy

Opportunities to use Spanish are only one condition for heritage-language maintenance. Speakers also encounter judgments about what counts as "correct" Spanish and who is considered a competent Spanish speaker. Heritage bilinguals are often evaluated against monolingual or standardized norms that do not reflect their histories of exposure and use (Higby et al., 2023). Similarly, essentialist categories such as native speaker can obscure variation in language experience and position some speakers as more legitimate than others (Namboodiripad et al., 2026). These judgments are not socially neutral. Raciolinguistic research shows that perceptions of language are shaped by broader perceptions of speakers and their racialized identities (Flores & Rosa, 2015; Rosa, 2016; Rosa & Flores, 2017). Flores and Rosa (2015), for example, argue that racialized speakers can continue to be perceived as linguistically deficient even when they approximate standardized forms. Rosa (2016) similarly describes how U.S. Latine speakers may be positioned as deficient in English while also being characterized as speakers of inadequate Spanish. These frameworks help explain why greater conformity to a prescribed standard does not necessarily eliminate linguistic stigma.

For U.S. heritage Spanish speakers, these ideologies often appear through expectations to speak a standardized or "proper" variety. Lynch and Potowski (2014) argue that prescriptive approaches to U.S. Spanish can devalue bilingual practices and undermine efforts to sustain Spanish. More recent research documents similar patterns in educational settings. Johnson et al. (2024) found that Spanish heritage-language students can encounter programs organized around monolingual norms and standard-language ideologies that privilege traditional second-language learners while marginalizing U.S. Spanish varieties. Research with Southern California Spanish-English bilinguals also show awareness of stigma attached to contact-influenced forms and English borrowings associated with local bilingual Spanish (Holguín Mendoza & Higby, 2024).

The label no sabo kid illustrates how these ideologies operate in U.S. heritage-Spanish contexts.

The term has been documented as a deficit-oriented and derogatory label for heritage speakers whose Spanish is perceived as insufficiently proficient, potentially reinforcing linguistic insecurity rather than encouraging continued use (Johnson et al., 2024; McKinnon, 2026a McKinnon, , 2026b)).

The value assigned to bilingualism also depends on who is bilingual and how bilingualism is acquired. Gándara (2018) notes that bilingual programs historically framed as compensatory services for minoritized students have been sought as enrichment opportunities by English-dominant families. Thus, bilingualism may be treated as an asset for some speakers while heritage bilingual practices remain subject to correction or deficit framing. Supporting heritage-language maintenance requires attention not only to how often families use Spanish, but also to whether children and families experience their bilingual practices as legitimate resources.

1.3 Family and Community Knowledge as Resources

Asset-based frameworks shift attention from presumed family deficits toward the knowledge and resources families already mobilize. Funds of Knowledge (Moll et al., 1992) conceptualizes households as sites of historically-accumulated and culturally-developed knowledge. Caregivers and community networks possess practical, cultural, and relational expertise that children draw on across contexts. Applied to bilingualism, these resources may include knowledge of children's language preferences, strategies for encouraging use, literacy practices, media, family relationships, and opportunities for interaction.

Community Cultural Wealth (CCW; Yosso, 2005) extends this orientation by identifying resources that institutions often fail to recognize. Yosso describes six interconnected forms of capital: aspirational, linguistic, familial, social, navigational, and resistant. Linguistic capital encompasses skills developed through multiple languages and communicative styles; familial capital reflects knowledge and support transmitted through kinship networks; social capital involves access to people and community resources; navigational capital concerns movement through institutions; aspirational capital reflects hopes sustained despite barriers; and resistant capital includes knowledge and practices that challenge inequality. Funds of Knowledge and CCW shift attention from what families are presumed to lack toward the knowledge, relationships, linguistic practices, and navigational resources they already mobilize. Applied to heritage-language maintenance, this perspective highlights how families draw on household knowledge, kinship ties, community networks, and institutional navigation while responding to language ideologies and unequal access to resources. This asset-based orientation also changes how community support is conceptualized: if families are knowledge holders, outreach should not be limited to transmitting expert recommendations. Research-based knowledge can instead be placed in dialogue with families' experiential knowledge, goals, and available resources.

Drawing on Yosso's (2005) CCW framework, we propose a conceptual framework (Figure 1) that situates heritage-language maintenance within the broader sociolinguistic ecology of the U.S.-Mexico border region. Rather than viewing language maintenance solely as a product of individual family choices, this framework considers how family language practices are shaped by the interaction of community resources, institutional conditions, and transnational experiences. This framework also acknowledges the barriers that families encounter and the linguistic, familial, social, and other forms of community cultural wealth that are drawn upon to support intergenerational language transmission. In doing so, the framework also provides an asset-based lens for examining how families navigate both the opportunities and constraints of raising bilingual families in a border-region context.

1.4 Community-Engaged Approaches to Family Bilingualism

Community-engaged approaches emphasize local knowledge, reciprocal learning, and collaboration rather than one-way dissemination. Vaughn et al. (2018) argue that community members contribute contextual knowledge, shared experience, cultural understanding, and social connections that can improve the relevance of research and educational initiatives. Communityengaged and dialogic approaches extend this principle by creating opportunities for reciprocal learning and the co-construction of locally relevant knowledge (Charlu et al., 2026). This orientation is relevant to bilingual families because language practices vary across children, schools, communities, and linguistic environments. Research can inform families about bilingual development, but families contribute knowledge about how language is used within specific relationships and contexts. Neither source of knowledge alone captures the full ecology of bilingual development. Community-based talleres (i.e., workshops) can bring these forms of knowledge into dialogue through discussion, case-based scenarios, and opportunities to exchange experiences and strategies. Rather than prescribing a single approach to bilingualism, this model gives participants space to interpret research in relation to their own linguistic practices, goals, and resources. This co-constructive orientation frames the workshops as sites of knowledge exchange rather than one-way instruction and provides the foundation for the present study.

Although research has documented family language practices, heritage-language shift, and the role of community resources, less attention has been given to how families' existing forms of cultural wealth become visible and are exchanged within community-based bilingualism outreach. In particular, relatively little is known about how research-based information and family or community knowledge are negotiated in dialogic spaces where participants bring different linguistic histories, institutional experiences, and resources.

Examining these interactions can extend heritage-language maintenance research beyond descriptions of family practices to consider how knowledge and resources are collectively interpreted within community settings.

1.5 Present Study

The present study examines a series of community-based bilingualism talleres conducted in a multilingual California border-region community. Guided by Community Cultural Wealth (Yosso, 2005), we examine how participants described the resources and strategies they drew on to support bilingualism and heritage-language maintenance, and how the talleres created opportunities for participants' experiential knowledge to interact with research-based information.

The study addressed the following research questions: 1.

How do participants describe the practices, experiences, and resources they use to support bilingualism and heritage-language maintenance? 2. What forms of Community Cultural Wealth are reflected in these practices and experiences?

3. How is knowledge about bilingual development and heritage-language maintenance exchanged and negotiated within the community-based talleres?

1.5 Positionality

We approached this study as multilingual researchers with personal and professional connections to bilingual communities. The first author was born in Cuba, raised in Spain, and moved to the United States at age 16, where he learned English as a second language; his research and community-engaged work focus on bilingual and typical and atypical language development. The second author is Mexican American and a Spanish heritage speaker whose experiences include frequent movement across the U.S.-Mexico border and maintaining family relationships across both countries. Her work with bilingual young adults further informs her understanding of heritage-language use across educational, family, and transnational contexts.

2. Method

>Method

2.1 Study Context and Setting

The study was conducted through a series of four community-based bilingualism talleres at a public library in City Heights, San Diego, California. The talleres took place on four consecutive Saturdays and were conducted mainly in Spanish, providing an accessible community space where families and other community members could discuss bilingual development, heritage-language maintenance, and strategies for supporting multilingualism. City Heights provides a relevant setting for this work because of its linguistic and cultural diversity and its location within the broader San Diego-Tijuana border region. In 2023, City Heights had an estimated population of 67,964, of whom approximately 49% were Hispanic, 20% White, 16% Asian or Pacific Islander, and 11% Black (San Diego Association of Governments [SANDAG], 2023). The public library served as an established community resource where families could access educational materials, multilingual programs for children and adults, and opportunities for interaction outside formal school or clinical settings. This setting was therefore well suited for talleres centered on the exchange of research-based information and participants' lived experiences with bilingualism.

2.2 Participants

A total of 23 participants participated across the four talleres. Participants included parents and caregivers, children, adolescents, young adults, educators, and other community members. Although many participants had connections to Spanish-English bilingualism, the group was linguistically diverse and also included individuals with connections to Vietnamese, Japanese, and other linguistic backgrounds. Participants varied in their experiences with bilingualism: some were raising bilingual children, some were heritage-language speakers themselves, and others were interested in supporting multilingual development within their families or communities. Participants were recruited through community outreach associated with the library and local networks. Participation in the talleres was voluntary, and attendance varied across sessions. The workshops were designed for community participation from individuals of all ages and with different bilingual experiences, hence enrollment was not restricted to a narrowly defined demographic group. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the University of California, Irvine Institutional Review Board (Protocol No. 7583).

A waiver of informed consent was approved for the use of data generated through the community-based talleres.

2.3 Talleres: Design and Procedures

Adapted from the Charlas model described by Perez et al. (2023), the bilingualism series consisted of four community-based talleres, with each session addressing a different aspect of bilingual development and family language use. Topics included: communication in two languages, common misconceptions about bilingualism and language disorders, bilingualism among children with disabilities, and strategies for supporting bilingual development at home and in the community. Although the content varied across sessions, the talleres followed a common structure intended to combine research-based information with participants' experiences and group discussion.

Each taller began with an informal welcome and social period, followed by a brief facilitator-led presentation, and extended participant discussion. Approximately 10 minutes were allocated for introductions and informal conversation, 20 minutes for presentation of the session topic, and at least 30 minutes for discussion, with additional time used for surveys, questions, activities, and sharing resources. Figure 2 summarizes the recurring structure of the talleres, from initial discussion and knowledge elicitation through research-based content, case-based activities, group dialogue, and reflection. content to document participants' knowledge, experiences, and perspectives related to bilingualism. Interactive verdad o mito (truth-or-myth) activities were also used to introduce common beliefs about bilingualism-related topics (e.g., verdad o mito: exposure to multiple languages confuses children or interferes with English acquisition). Participants first considered these statements based on their own knowledge and experiences, after which facilitators discussed relevant research and invited further conversation.

These activities were intended to make participants' existing understandings visible and provide opportunities to discuss areas of agreement, uncertainty, or disagreement.

Case-based scenarios, presented as ejemplos de la vida real (real-life examples), were another central component of the talleres. Scenarios depicted situations that bilingual families might encounter, including a child beginning English-only schooling after primarily using Spanish at home, parents being advised to stop using the home language, a child becoming increasingly reluctant to speak Spanish, bilingual children mixing languages, and families seeking affordable resources for bilingual literacy. Rather than presenting an immediate solution, facilitators invited participants to respond to the situation and share whether they had encountered similar experiences. Participants discussed possible strategies, compared them with their own family experiences, and generated additional considerations before facilitators introduced research-based information and available community resources. We served both as sources of research-based information and as moderators of discussion. We encouraged participants to question, elaborate on, or contextualize the information presented and emphasized that language practices and strategies could differ across families. Participant contributions naturally and frequently redirected or expanded the discussion by introducing examples from their own children, schooling experiences, family relationships, cross-border experiences, and language-learning histories. The talleres were thus structured not simply as educational presentations but as spaces in which research-based information and community knowledge could be discussed together.

2.4 Data Sources and Collection

2.4.1 Fieldnotes

Data consisted of field notes of approximately 6800 words documenting participant discussions and interactions across the four talleres. Following each session, facilitators recorded participants' questions, examples, family language experiences, responses to case-based activities, and strategies shared during group discussion. Field notes also included our observations and reflections about recurring topics, contrasting perspectives, and issues that emerged during each session. Participant comments were analyzed as documented in these notes; verbatim quotations were used only when the wording had been recorded directly.

2.4.2 Surveys

A brief eight-item pre-and post-workshop survey documented participants' perspectives on bilingual development, home-language maintenance, and strategies for supporting bilingual children. Seven statements were administered before and after the taller and addressed homelanguage exposure, continued use of the home language, language-learning practices, interpretation of school-language difficulties, caregiver expertise, classroom support for bilingualism, and home-language reading. An eighth statement, administered only at post-survey, asked whether the taller increased participants' knowledge of bilingualism. Responses used a three-option pictorial scale and were summarized descriptively as favorable, neutral, or unfavorable. Pre-and post-workshop responses were linked using participant identifiers. Written responses were included in the qualitative analysis when relevant to the research questions.

2.5 Analytic Approach

Closed-ended survey responses were summarized using frequencies and percentages.

Pre-post comparisons were based on participants with responses at both time points for each item. We conducted a framework-guided qualitative analysis of the field notes and written responses across the four talleres. Community Cultural Wealth (Yosso, 2005) served as the primary analytic framework. The first stage of analysis involved repeated review of the dataset to identify segments describing family language practices, intergenerational relationships, community resources, educational experiences, aspirations for bilingualism, institutional navigation, and responses to deficit-oriented beliefs about bilingual language use.

We then applied deductive codes corresponding to Yosso's (2005) six forms of capital: linguistic, familial, social, navigational, aspirational, and resistant. Coding was not treated as mutually exclusive; a single account could reflect multiple forms of capital when participants described interconnected resources or strategies. For example, maintaining Spanish through relationships with grandparents could reflect both familial and linguistic capital, whereas seeking a dual-language program could reflect aspirational and navigational capital. Alongside this deductive coding, we attended to patterns that emerged across participants and sessions that were not fully captured by the six categories. These included tensions surrounding language correction and linguistic legitimacy, differences in language practices across children and generations, and instances in which participants expanded, questioned, or contextualized recommendations introduced by facilitators. Codes and interpretations were refined through comparison across the four talleres, with attention to recurring patterns as well as differences across participants and workshops (Saldaña, 2009).

A final cross-session synthesis examined how the different forms of CCW operated together across the workshop series. This analysis also considered moments in which participants connected case scenarios to their own experiences, generated strategies beyond those presented by facilitators, or brought family and community knowledge into dialogue with research-based information. These instances informed our interpretation of the talleres as spaces for knowledge exchange and co-construction.

2.6 Trustworthiness and Reflexivity

To strengthen the credibility of the analysis, we collaboratively reviewed coded excerpts, emerging patterns, and analytic interpretations. Coding decisions were discussed and refined through consensus, and analytic memos were used to document developing interpretations and connections across workshops (Lincoln & Guba, 1985; Saldaña, 2009; Vanover et al., 2021). The analysis also attended to contrasting or discrepant cases rather than limiting interpretation to experiences that supported the most common patterns (Lincoln & Guba, 1985). For example, participants differed in how they approached correcting children's Spanish, responding to language mixing, and creating opportunities for heritage-language use. Reflexivity was maintained throughout the analytic process by considering how our multilingual, migration, cross-border, and professional experiences shaped the questions brought to the data and the interpretation of participants' accounts. Consistent with the study's asset-based orientation, we focused on the knowledge, strategies, relationships, and resources participants mobilized rather than evaluating their practices against a predetermined model of successful bilingualism.

3. Results

Across sessions, participants described bilingualism as something sustained through everyday family routines, relationships, community resources, educational choices, and efforts to create meaningful opportunities for language use. Linguistic, familial, social, navigational, aspirational, and resistant capital frequently operated together rather than as discrete forms of support. Because forms of cultural wealth frequently overlap, the findings are organized around recurring patterns in participants' accounts rather than presented as six discrete CCW categories.

3.1 Family Language Practices as Linguistic and Familial Resources

Participants most often described heritage-language maintenance through ordinary family practices rather than formal instruction alone. Speaking Spanish at home, reading, listening to music, maintaining relationships with relatives, and creating opportunities for children to use the language were described as ways of sustaining bilingualism. These practices reflected both linguistic capital, through continued access to and use of multiple linguistic resources, and familial capital, through the knowledge caregivers developed about how individual family members used language.

Parents emphasized that children within the same family did not necessarily respond to the same language practices in the same way. One mother summarized this expertise directly: "Como padres, somos quienes mejor conocemos las necesidades lingüísticas de nuestros hijos" [As parents, we know our children's language needs best]. This perspective was reflected in how caregivers adjusted their strategies according to children's confidence, preferences, and patterns of language use. During the first taller, for example, one mother contrasted her two children: her younger child continued speaking Spanish even when unsure whether he was saying something "correctly," whereas her older child at times avoided speaking when uncertain. Rather than treating these differences as evidence that one child was more capable than the other, she described adapting how she encouraged each child to participate. She also emphasized maintaining Spanish at home because of its importance for communication with relatives and connection to Mexican culture. Her account illustrates how familial knowledge shaped language practices at the level of the individual child rather than through a single strategy applied uniformly across the household.

Literacy emerged as another family resource. The same mother described how her older son had begun reading Spanish after encountering a book that interested him. He was surprised by how easily he could read and by how quickly his reading improved. In another session, a mother of five described creating her own reading materials when her children were young and working with them regularly. Her approach differed across children because some were initially more willing than others to use Spanish. Rather than applying a single family language strategy, she adapted her practices according to each child's interests, confidence, and willingness to participate. These accounts positioned literacy not only as exposure to Spanish but as a resource families adapted to children's interests and engagement.

Similar patterns extended beyond parent-child interactions. Young adults described intentionally strengthening their Spanish through relationships, workplaces, music, and other forms of repeated language use. These accounts suggest that heritage-language development remained active into adulthood and was supported through the social relationships and environments in which Spanish became meaningful or necessary. Across these accounts, bilingualism was maintained through relationships and repeated use rather than through a single prescribed family language policy. Familial knowledge also shaped how parents modified strategies over time. In one of the sessions, one mother explained that she had emphasized correction and Spanish practice more strongly with her older children but took a different approach with her youngest daughter. When the daughter responded to her in English, the mother continued speaking Spanish without necessarily requiring a Spanish response. She also enrolled her in a dual-language program and ballet folklórico, creating additional opportunities to connect language with cultural participation. Such examples illustrate how families drew simultaneously on linguistic and familial resources while adapting their practices to individual children and changing circumstances. These examples show how linguistic and familial capital operated together as families adjusted their practices in response to individual children, prior experiences, and changing opportunities for language use.

3.2 Community Networks and Navigating Opportunities for Bilingualism

Participants also described language maintenance as dependent on opportunities extending beyond the immediate household. Schools, libraries, community programs, workplaces, extended-family networks, and cross-border relationships created additional contexts in which bilingualism could be developed or sustained. Social and navigational capital were especially visible when participants described how they located, created, or adapted to uneven access to these opportunities.

Cross-border relationships were prominent. One adult participant described traveling frequently between San Diego and Tijuana during childhood to visit family and friends and to use Spanish. He had also attended kindergarten in Tijuana before beginning first grade in the United States. Looking back, he attributed differences between his own Spanish and that of his younger sisters partly to his greater time in Tijuana and more frequent use of Spanish with family. During the discussion, he proposed extended stays in Mexico as one possible way for children with family connections there to experience greater immersion in Spanish. His suggestion emerged from his own history rather than from the facilitators' initial recommendations, illustrating how transnational experience functioned as both a linguistic resource and a form of navigational knowledge that could be shared with other families.

Participants also described navigating institutional constraints within the United States. In the final taller, one African American grandmother wanted her five-year-old granddaughter to learn Spanish through a dual-language program but reported that the child's elementary school did not offer one. The discussion consequently shifted toward other possible resources, including bilingual library programming, Spanish-language books and media, and interactions with Spanish-speaking family and community members. The public library itself functioned as a community resource by connecting families with bilingual materials, programming, and other opportunities for language use. In this way, social capital could help families identify alternatives when preferred educational opportunities were unavailable.

The multilingual composition of the final session further expanded the range of community knowledge represented. A Vietnamese participant brought her own professional and family experiences to a discussion of bilingual development, emphasizing the importance of considering the amount of input and output in each language, children's age and grade, and how much time they spent using each language. A Japanese mother emphasized that teachers should respect students' cultural backgrounds when responding to bilingual families. These contributions broadened the conversation beyond Spanish-English bilingualism and demonstrated that participants were not only seeking resources but also contributing knowledge that expanded the resources available within the group.

Community navigation therefore involved more than finding formal bilingual programs.

Participants described building language opportunities through family travel, cultural activities, workplaces, partners, libraries, media, and community relationships. When formal opportunities were limited or unavailable, participants described combining family, social, and community resources to create alternative contexts for language use. Social and navigational capital were thus closely connected: access to people and community networks often provided the information needed to identify or create opportunities for bilingualism.

3.3 Aspirations for Intergenerational Bilingualism

Aspirational capital was evident in participants' repeated emphasis on maintaining or developing bilingualism for future generations. These aspirations were not limited to parents currently raising bilingual children. Young adults also discussed Spanish in relation to future relationships, families, and children, suggesting that heritage-language maintenance was understood as an intergenerational project.

One young woman who had grown up speaking Spanish described teaching the language to her boyfriend and emphasized that maintaining the home language would be important when they eventually had children. Similarly, a young man who had become more intentional about practicing Spanish as an adult described teaching Spanish to his future children as an eventual goal. His current practices, speaking with a bilingual partner and listening to Mexican music, were therefore connected to a longer-term aspiration for intergenerational transmission.

Parents expressed similar aspirations even when access to resources was uneven. One English-monolingual mother wanted her five-year-old daughter to become bilingual despite neither parent being a Spanish speaker. She described early childhood as an important opportunity for language learning and actively sought immersion and community resources for her daughter. Other parents described bilingualism as important for maintaining relationships with relatives, participating in cultural practices, and ensuring that children retained access to family histories and identities.

These aspirations were often informed by participants' own observations of language shift. During one discussion, an adult participant contrasted his own Spanish with that of his nephew, who used relatively little Spanish beyond words such as "abuelita." He also described how the child's grandmother eventually began responding in English. His account framed language shift not as an abstract generational process but as something visible within family relationships. In this context, participants' efforts to create additional opportunities for Spanish use reflected a desire to interrupt or slow patterns they had observed within their own families.

Aspirational capital therefore intersected with familial and navigational capital. Participants translated these aspirations into efforts to expand opportunities for language use through home practices, educational programs, cultural activities, family relationships, and their own continued Spanish development. Aspirational capital operated not simply as a positive orientation toward bilingualism but as a future-oriented resource that motivated participants to seek, create, and navigate opportunities for intergenerational language use.

3.4 Negotiating "Correct" Spanish

Participants' accounts also revealed tension between maintaining Spanish and concerns about speaking it "correctly." Across sessions, correction was not uniformly rejected or embraced. Instead, families negotiated how to support language growth without discouraging participation. These tensions were visible in discussions of no sabo forms, correction, and language mixing. During the first taller, one mother described her children as sometimes using what others might call "no sabo" Spanish. She rejected ridicule or scolding as a response and instead emphasized encouragement alongside correction. She recalled her younger child saying "no sabo," after which she taught him to say "no sé." For her, correction was part of supporting continued development, but it occurred within a broader commitment to keeping the child engaged in Spanish. Her comparison between her children further illustrated the stakes of this balance: the younger child continued speaking even when unsure, whereas the older child was more likely to remain silent when uncertain.

Similar concerns emerged among young adults. Two young men described sometimes avoiding Spanish because they were unsure of the "right way" to express themselves. Their hesitation suggested that opportunities to use the language were shaped not only by proficiency or exposure but also by confidence in whether their Spanish would be judged as acceptable. At the same time, both described actively trying to create more opportunities to use Spanish, indicating that linguistic insecurity coexisted with a desire to maintain and strengthen the language.

Discussions of language mixing made this tension visible. Participants shared several expressions that reflected contact between Spanish and English. One mother recalled her son saying "Ellos querían sorpresarme" [They wanted to surprise me]. Here, sorpresarme appears to be modeled on the English verb surprise; in standardized Spanish, the expected form would be Although excitado is a legitimate Spanish word, in many Spanish varieties it more often conveys heightened arousal, sometimes with a sexual connotation; emocionado would generally be more idiomatic for expressing positive anticipation or excitement. These examples are consistent with documented English-influenced lexical and semantic patterns in U.S. Spanish (Thomas, 2022).

The participant recalled that people had laughed at some of these expressions without explaining why they were perceived as inappropriate. A mother similarly described correcting her children when they used contact-influenced forms, but she emphasized being careful not to overcorrect because she wanted them to continue speaking Spanish and developing their linguistic resources through use.

We interpreted these responses as a form of resistant capital: participants did not necessarily reject correction or standardized Spanish, but they resisted practices that could turn linguistic feedback into shame, silence, or withdrawal from Spanish. Resistance did not necessarily mean rejecting correction or standardized Spanish. Rather, it appeared in participants' efforts to resist practices that could turn correction into shame, silence, or withdrawal from the language. Participants valued expanding children's Spanish while also recognizing that excessive attention to "proper" language could reduce willingness to speak. This distinction became particularly important because hesitation itself appeared repeatedly across the talleres.

The workshops also revealed that deficit beliefs were not uniformly shared by participants. During the first session, one mother expressed surprise that bilingualism had ever been viewed as confusing for children; she reported being unfamiliar with that negative belief.

Her response complicated the facilitators' assumption that common bilingualism "myths" would necessarily reflect participants' own concerns. This contrasting case reinforced the importance of eliciting participants' perspectives before assuming which beliefs or misconceptions were relevant within a particular community.

3.5 The Talleres as Spaces for Knowledge Exchange

As part of our aims, we examined how the talleres functioned as spaces for coconstruction and knowledge exchange. Across sessions, participant contributions did more than illustrate information presented by facilitators. They introduced new strategies, complicated general recommendations, and at times changed how facilitators framed subsequent discussion.

Case-based activities were useful because participants were asked to respond before facilitators introduced recommendations. During an early scenario, one child participant suggested associating physical objects with words to help learners connect vocabulary across languages. In another session, an adult participant drew on his childhood experiences in Tijuana to suggest periods of immersion in Mexico as a strategy for families who had access to crossborder relationships. These ideas were generated from participants' experiences rather than supplied in advance by the facilitators.

Participants also added contextual factors that complicated generalized recommendations.

During the final session, a Vietnamese participant argued that understanding a bilingual child's language development required considering the amount of English input and output, time spent using each language at home, and the child's age and grade. A Japanese mother emphasized cultural responsiveness from teachers when discussing one of the case scenarios. Such comments redirected discussion from generic bilingualism advice toward the specific relationships and environments in which language development occurred.

Knowledge exchange also took place among participants. Young adults compared strategies for strengthening Spanish through partners, work, music, and intentional language choices. Parents compared how different children responded to correction or encouragement.

Discussions of code-switching prompted participants to contribute their own examples, producing shared recognition of language practices that might otherwise be experienced individually as mistakes or sources of embarrassment. In these moments, the charla format allowed participants to recognize similarities and differences across their experiences rather than positioning the facilitator as the sole source of expertise.

Participant input also shaped our practices. After the first session, field notes documented that some attendees preferred English or used both English and Spanish, leading the facilitators to consider providing materials in both languages and using more flexible bilingual communication. By the second session, discussion itself was conducted bilingually and included code-switching and translanguaging. During the final session, the presence of Vietnamese and Japanese families led us to adapt examples beyond Spanish and to consider how the workshop model could better address multilingual families more broadly. Library staff similarly encouraged greater attention to languages represented in the surrounding community.

Our reflections also changed in response to participant accounts. Following discussions of correction and language mixing, notes explicitly emphasized avoiding prescriptivism and moving away from "one size fits all" recommendations. Participants' descriptions of different children within the same family reinforced the need to frame recommendations as possibilities to be considered in relation to family circumstances rather than universal rules. Thus, coconstruction occurred not only when participants applied research to their own experiences, but also when their experiences altered how facilitators understood and presented that research.

These interactions positioned the talleres as reciprocal spaces rather than one-way educational presentations. Participants contributed experiential and contextual knowledge, while facilitators contributed research evidence, structured prompts, and connections to community resources. Knowledge about bilingual development and heritage-language maintenance was therefore not simply transmitted but interpreted, contextualized, and expanded through dialogue.

3.6 Pre-and Post-Workshop Survey Responses

Pre-workshop survey responses were available from 19 participants, and 17 participants completed the post-workshop survey. Given the small sample, item-level missingness, and high baseline endorsement on several items, survey findings are interpreted descriptively (Table 1).

Responses were generally favorable at baseline, with the largest descriptive shifts observed for beliefs about school-language difficulties and caregiver expertise. Among participants with paired responses, favorable endorsement of the statement that difficulties in the school language do not indicate a learning disorder increased from 47.1% at pre-survey to 70.6% at post-survey.

Favorable endorsement of the statement that caregivers are experts on their children's language development increased from 58.8% to 76.5%. Responses to the remaining items were largely stable because favorable endorsement was already high at baseline. On the post-only item, all 17 respondents reported that the taller increased their knowledge of bilingualism.

4. Discussion

The present study examined how participants in community-based bilingualism talleres described the practices and resources they used to support bilingualism and heritage-language maintenance, the forms of Community Cultural Wealth (CCW) reflected in those practices, and the role of the talleres as spaces for knowledge exchange. Three broader conclusions emerged.

First, heritage-language maintenance was described as a relational and ecological process that extended beyond language use in the home to include extended family, schools, community institutions, cultural activities, workplaces, and transnational connections. Second, CCW illuminated how participants combined linguistic, familial, social, navigational, aspirational, and resistant resources rather than drawing on these forms of capital independently. Third, the talleres functioned as reciprocal spaces in which research-based information was interpreted, questioned, and extended through participants lived experiences. The findings shift attention away from whether families follow a particular language-maintenance strategy and toward the resources, relationships, opportunities, and ideologies that make continued bilingual participation possible.

4.1 Heritage-Language Maintenance as a Family and Community Process

Our findings suggest that heritage-language maintenance is not sustained through exposure or home-language use alone, but through the accumulation of opportunities to use the language across family, community, educational, and social contexts. This pattern is consistent with Potowski's (2013) emphasis on intergenerational transmission while also illustrating how transmission is shaped by the broader environments in which families participate. Rather than relying on a single strategy, participants described maintaining or strengthening Spanish through combinations of home use, literacy, cultural activities, extended-family relationships, schooling, workplaces, community institutions, and social networks. These findings position heritagelanguage maintenance as an ecological process in which family practices remain central but are strengthened or constrained by the opportunities for meaningful use available beyond the home (Inan & Harris, 2025).

The practices participants shared were adaptive rather than fixed. Participants described different language trajectories even among children raised within the same household, suggesting that family language practices cannot be understood as uniform inputs producing uniform outcomes. Caregivers continually adjusted their approaches in response to children's confidence, preferences, opportunities for use, and changing environments. Heritage-language maintenance emerged not simply as the implementation of a family language policy but as an ongoing process of responding to the linguistic ecology of individual family members. This adaptive pattern is consistent with family language policy research emphasizing that language practices are negotiated within families and shaped by changing relationships, ideologies, and circumstances (Iqwan Sanjani et al., 2026; King et al., 2008).

The border-region context further shaped these opportunities. Transnational relationships can expand access to Spanish-speaking family members, schooling, literacy practices, and other contexts for language use (Ceballos, 2012). Participants' experiences similarly suggest that geographic proximity becomes a linguistic resource when families can activate cross-border connections through mobility and relationships. Proximity alone, however, does not ensure maintenance; access to transnational networks, bilingual schooling, community programs, and Spanish-speaking interlocutors remained uneven. The border is therefore better understood as part of the opportunity structure surrounding bilingual families than as an inherently protective factor (Potowski, 2013). Our findings also demonstrate that maintenance and shift can occur simultaneously within the same family and community. Participants recognized changes in Spanish use across generations while also describing deliberate efforts to strengthen their own language use or create opportunities for younger generations. Heritage-language maintenance should not be understood as a binary outcome in which a family either "maintains" or "loses" a language. It is a dynamic process shaped by changing relationships, institutions, identities, and opportunities for participation across the lifespan.

4.2 Community Cultural Wealth and Family Bilingualism

Community Cultural Wealth is a useful framework for identifying resources that might remain invisible within deficit-oriented accounts of heritage bilingualism. A deficit framework could foreground limited access to bilingual education, hesitancy to speak Spanish, contactinfluenced language, or parents' uncertainty about how best to support bilingual development.

Community Cultural Wealth instead directs attention to the knowledge, relationships, strategies, and aspirations families mobilize within those circumstances (Yosso, 2005). Our findings also support Yosso's (2005) emphasis on the interconnectedness of cultural wealth. The forms of capital frequently overlapped: aspirations for intergenerational bilingualism depended on family relationships, linguistic practices, social networks, and the ability to navigate educational and community institutions. This overlap matters because treating CCW categories too rigidly can obscure how families mobilize multiple resources together. Community Cultural Wealth is therefore most informative in this context as an interconnected system of resources rather than a checklist of independent forms of capital.

The CCW framework also foregrounds expertise that institutions may overlook.

Participants demonstrated detailed knowledge of their children's language histories, reactions to strategies, family communication patterns, and the opportunities available within their communities. Young adults similarly drew on knowledge generated through work, relationships, media, and their own histories as bilingual speakers. These forms of experiential knowledge complicate models in which professionals identify a problem, and families are expected simply to implement a recommended solution. An asset-based framework should identify family agency without individualizing responsibility for structural conditions. Families' ability to mobilize cultural wealth depended partly on access to time, transportation, bilingual programs, community institutions, transnational networks, and other resources. Recognizing family agency therefore does not mean shifting responsibility for heritage-language maintenance entirely onto families.

Rather, CCW makes visible both the resources families possess and the institutional conditions that shape whether those resources can be effectively mobilized.

4.3 Linguistic Legitimacy and the Tension Between Correction and Use

Yet access to family and community resources does not by itself ensure continued heritage-language use; the social conditions under which speakers use Spanish also matter. Our findings suggest that efforts to support heritage-language development may discourage participation when linguistic feedback is experienced as a judgment of speaker legitimacy.

Although participants valued helping children expand their Spanish, concerns about speaking "correctly" were also linked to hesitation and reduced participation. This tension suggests that the consequences of correction depend not simply on whether feedback is provided, but on whether it expands a speaker's repertoire while preserving confidence and willingness to use the language. In this sense, heritage-language maintenance involves more than developing linguistic knowledge; it also depends on whether speakers experience their bilingual practices as legitimate forms of participation. This distinction aligns with critiques of deficit frameworks in heritagelanguage research. Higby et al. (2023) argue that bilingual speakers are often evaluated against idealized monolingual norms that do not reflect their histories of language exposure and use.

Heritage-language features associated with language contact, bilingual development, or regional variation may consequently be interpreted as evidence of incomplete or deficient competence rather than as products of distinct linguistic experiences.

Concerns about linguistic legitimacy are relevant in Southern California, where Spanish-English bilinguals regularly navigate code-switching, English-influenced forms, and differing expectations about what counts as "proper" Spanish across family, community, and cross-border contexts. Holguín Mendoza and Higby (2024) show that Southern California bilinguals are aware of the stigma associated with English borrowings, contact forms, and other features of regional Spanish. At the same time, these forms occur within a recognizable Southern California sociolinguistic system. Judgments about "proper" Spanish therefore involve more than grammaticality; they also reflect ideologies about which varieties and speakers are granted legitimacy.

A raciolinguistic perspective clarifies why adopting standardized forms does not necessarily resolve this tension. Flores and Rosa (2015) argue that judgments of linguistic appropriateness are shaped by perceptions of speakers as well as by the forms they produce.

Practices associated with bilingualism can be celebrated in some contexts while stigmatized when produced by racialized heritage speakers. Rosa (2016) similarly demonstrates how U.S.

Latine speakers may be positioned as deficient users of both English and Spanish, producing a condition in which linguistic legitimacy remains difficult to attain even through greater conformity to standardized norms.

Research on linguistic legitimacy and heritage Spanish suggests that supporting heritagelanguage maintenance requires distinguishing between repertoire expansion and linguistic replacement. Speakers can learn additional lexical, grammatical, or register-specific forms without being taught that their existing bilingual practices are illegitimate. Instruction and family feedback can therefore emphasize when and where particular forms may be interpreted differently while preserving bilingual variation as part of speakers' communicative resources.

This orientation is consistent with Lynch and Potowski's (2014) critique of prescriptive approaches to U.S. Spanish that prioritize correction over sustained engagement with the heritage language.

The implications for maintenance are important. Increasing opportunities to use Spanish may be insufficient if speakers anticipate that participation will expose them to ridicule or judgments about authenticity. Linguistic legitimacy consequently becomes part of the ecology of language maintenance: speakers need not only opportunities to use the language, but also interactional spaces in which using it is socially viable. Resistant capital can therefore include challenging deficit interpretations of bilingual practices and sustaining participation despite pressures to conform to narrow definitions of legitimate Spanish.

4.4 Community-Based Talleres as Sites of Co-Construction

The importance of linguistic legitimacy also has implications for how bilingual families are engaged in community-based outreach. Recommendations developed without attention to families lived experiences may reproduce the same prescriptive assumptions that participants were navigating. Our findings instead support models that recognize families and community members as sources of contextual knowledge about language use, relationships, institutional barriers, and available resources. The structure of the talleres created opportunities for that knowledge to shape the discussion. By eliciting participants' interpretations before introducing or finalizing recommendations, research-based information could be considered alongside family and community experience rather than presented as universally applicable guidance. This reciprocal, dialogic orientation is consistent with community-engaged approaches that position community members as knowledge producers rather than solely as recipients of information (Vaughn et al., 2018) and with Funds of Knowledge perspectives that recognize expertise developed through everyday family and community life (Moll et al., 1992). Such reciprocity was important given the linguistic and experiential heterogeneity represented in the workshops. Participants differed in their relationships to Spanish, other languages, schooling, family networks, and the U.S.-Mexico border. A dialogic format allowed general research findings to be interpreted within those different circumstances rather than assuming that a single recommendation would be equally appropriate for all families. Community-based talleres may therefore be most useful when they create structured opportunities for research and community knowledge to inform one another.

4.5 Implications for Practice

The findings have implications for how families, educators, clinicians, and community organizations approach bilingual development and heritage-language maintenance. Across these settings, a central implication is the need to move away from universal prescriptions. Families varied in their goals, resources, children's responses to language practices, and access to bilingual opportunities. Recommendations should therefore provide families with possible strategies and the information needed to evaluate them rather than prescribing a single "best" approach. Supporting bilingualism may involve home-language use, literacy, cultural activities, educational programs, extended-family relationships, or other opportunities for meaningful interaction, but the usefulness of any strategy depends on the circumstances of the individual family.

For educators and speech-language pathologists, this flexibility also requires distinguishing language support from linguistic correction. Professionals can help children expand vocabulary, registers, and context-specific forms without treating bilingual or contactinfluenced practices as evidence of inadequate language. Asking families about children's language histories, communication partners, opportunities for use, and family priorities can provide information that standardized recommendations alone cannot capture. Clinical and educational guidance should build on families' existing knowledge and practices rather than beginning from assumptions about what families are failing to provide.

Community organizations and libraries can play a complementary role by creating accessible spaces in which families encounter both research-based information and one another.

Our experience also suggests that accessibility involves more than selecting a community location (Cabrera Pérez et al., 2026). Although interest during outreach and tabling was substantial, workshop attendance remained relatively small. Community engagement therefore needs to begin before the workshop itself. Developing relationships with library staff, schools, community organizations, and other trusted local partners may help researchers understand when and how families are most likely to participate, which topics are locally relevant, and how information should be communicated.

Trust and relationship building may be important regardless of whether researchers or professionals are themselves members of the communities in which they work. Researchers who share cultural, linguistic, or lived experiences with community members may bring valuable insider perspectives, established relationships, and familiarity with local contexts. However, shared community membership does not automatically ensure trust or eliminate differences in experiences, perspectives, or power. Similarly, researchers working outside their own communities must be intentional about developing relationships, mutual respect, and recognition of community expertise as foundations of effective academic-community partnerships as the pillars for community-engaged research (Khodyakov et al., 2017; Jagosh et al., 2015). Although the present study did not compare different outreach models, our experience suggests that engagement should begin with relationship building rather than recruitment alone. Establishing a sustained presence, learning from people already working within the community, and involving trusted community members in outreach and program development may help make communitybased initiatives more locally responsive.

Language accessibility should likewise be responsive rather than assumed in advance.

Although the talleres centered on bilingualism and were initially designed primarily around Spanish, participants differed in the languages they used with facilitators and in the linguistic backgrounds represented within their families. Future programs could incorporate bilingual materials, flexible use of Spanish and English, and code-switching when appropriate rather than treating one language as the required medium of participation. The participation of individuals from linguistic backgrounds beyond Spanish-English further suggests that community conversations about bilingualism should remain responsive to the broader multilingual ecology of the community.

4.6 Limitations and Future Directions

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings. The study involved a small, self-selected group of participants across four talleres. Individuals who chose to attend workshops focused on bilingualism may have had particular interest in or favorable orientations toward multilingualism that are not representative of the broader community.

Attendance also differed across sessions, and some individuals participated more actively than others. As a result, themes that appeared across the dataset should not be interpreted as reflecting the prevalence of beliefs or practices within City Heights or bilingual families more generally.

Recruitment and participation provide an additional consideration. Informal outreach generated considerable interest, but that interest did not consistently translate into workshop attendance. Because reasons for nonattendance were not systematically collected, the present study cannot determine whether participation was affected by timing, competing responsibilities, outreach methods, topic relevance, or other factors. Future community-partnered work could examine recruitment and participation more systematically and involve local organizations and community members earlier in decisions about scheduling, format, language, and content.

The qualitative dataset relied on our field notes rather than complete audio recordings and verbatim transcripts of workshop discussions. Field notes allowed us to document participant experiences, examples, questions, and facilitator reflections, but they inevitably represent a selective record of interaction. They may not capture the full wording, sequence, or complexity of participants' contributions. Moreover, because we also documented and analyzed the workshop interactions, the field notes and interpretations were necessarily shaped by what we noticed and considered salient during and after each session. Collaborative review, analytic memoing, and attention to discrepant cases were used to strengthen reflexivity and credibility, but these procedures do not eliminate our interpretive role. For this reason, interpretations in the present study focus on recurring patterns across the documented discussions rather than detailed discourse-level analysis. Future studies could combine field notes with audio-recorded and transcribed discussions, participant reflections, or follow-up interviews to preserve more of the interactional context and participants' own language.

The survey findings should also be interpreted cautiously. The survey was brief, several items showed high favorable endorsement at baseline, item-level responses were missing for some participants, and role-specific items resulted in different denominators. As mentioned in an earlier section, the observed pre-post differences are best understood as descriptive patterns in participants' responses rather than as evidence of workshop effectiveness. Future studies could use larger samples, validated measures, and longitudinal designs to examine whether participation in community-based bilingualism workshops is associated with sustained changes in knowledge, beliefs, or practices.

The workshops included participants with heterogeneous linguistic, generational, educational, and family backgrounds. This heterogeneity enriched the discussions and was central to the co-constructive character of the talleres, but the small sample does not permit meaningful comparisons among particular linguistic or demographic groups. Future research could examine how community-based bilingualism initiatives operate across different heritagelanguage communities while retaining attention to local linguistic and cultural conditions.

5. Conclusion

Heritage-language maintenance depends on more than families' desire to raise bilingual children. It is shaped by the opportunities speakers have to use their languages across relationships and settings, their access to educational and community resources, and whether their bilingual practices are treated as legitimate forms of communication. Families in the present study already drew on linguistic, familial, social, navigational, aspirational, and resistant resources to create and sustain such opportunities, often while navigating unequal access to institutional support and competing ideas about what counts as "proper" language.

Community-based spaces can support this work when they recognize those resources rather than positioning families as recipients of expert solutions. The talleres showed how research evidence can be brought into dialogue with family experience, local knowledge, and community resources, producing conversations that are more responsive to the realities of bilingual life. These findings suggest that supporting heritage-language maintenance involves not only sharing research on bilingual development but also creating structures through which families and communities can shape how that knowledge is interpreted and applied.
