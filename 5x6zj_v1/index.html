Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 128 papers with 246 instances of power statements, 203 were correctly detected (true positives), 22 were missed (false negatives) and 21 were incorrectly detected (false positives). Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Marginal Significance works

List all sentences that describe an effect as ‘marginally significant’.

The marginal module searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The list of terms is a subset of those listed in a blog post by Matthew Hankins. The module returns all sentences that match terms describing ‘marginally significant’ results.

Some of the terms identified might not be problematic in some contexts, and there are ways to describe ‘marginal significance’ that are not detected by the module.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. Some differences come from the ground truth: it lacks p-values with a correction name (“p Bonf = .061”), and counts partial eta squared written with its subscript after it (“η 2 p = .05”), “p = .05”, and some table notes and figure legends (“† p < .10, *p < .05”), which this module leaves out, as non-significant p-values. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Effect Sizes in t-tests and F-tests works

The Effect Size module checks if effect sizes are correctly reported in t-tests and F-tests.

The Effect Size check searches for regular expressions that match typical ways in which effect sizes are reported. It subsequently checks different ways in which Cohen’s d, g, ηp2, and ωp2 can be computed against the reported value. If effects are missing, or might be incorrect, you the module provides a warning. The module was validated on APA reported statistical tests, and might miss effect sizes that were reported in other reporting styles. It was validated by the Metacheck team on papers published in Psychological Science.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

Abstract

In the coming years, interpretability research will uncover a vast set of machine constructs: causally efficacious representations and processing mechanisms underlying the use of language and other intelligent behavior in AI systems, including LLMs. Assuming that interpretability will be as successful as can be reasonably hoped for (interpretability in the limit), how can the discovery of machine constructs shape linguistics and cognitive science? First, we provide several axes of analysis that can help scientists conceptualize the internal mechanisms discovered in machines. Even under the assumption that interpretability in the limit guarantees that all constructs are causally implicated in a system's behavior, scientists may want to consider several additional properties, including constructs' theoretical status, domain status, ontological status, and complexity. Second, we argue that scientists should prioritize recurrent constructs, meaning those that surface across a diversity of models converging on behaviors of interest while varying on other axes such as architecture, training data, objective function, language, and modality. Finally, we provide three routes for how machine constructs can inform linguistic and cognitive theories, emphasizing that all of these routes treat machine constructs as hypotheses, where additional theorizing and experimentation by domain experts are required. We conclude with several recommendations that can be implemented now for improving the long-term success of this program.

1 Introduction

We begin this article with a little bit of speculative fiction. Let's suppose, for a minute, that it is the year 2040. Neural network-based language models still remain the core engine of AI systems, and they have become more robust and useful than they were in the late 2020s. Interpretability as a field has also matured in the meantime, leading to a suite of methods for answering the "how" question of AI behavior. These methods provide faithful and satisfactory descriptions of how the internals of a target neural network lead to intelligent behavior. The goal of this paper is to explore how such a situation could benefit linguistics and the cognitive science of language. That is, assuming that interpretability is as successful as one could reasonably hope (interpretability in the limit), in what ways will this deeper understanding of AI help us to understand human linguistic cognition better?

Answering this question will have several benefits for the present moment. First, it will set expectations for how linguistics may be reasonably informed by AI research in the medium term. Second, it will provide high-level directions and suggestions that can serve as a concrete locus of debate. Is this a future we want for linguistics? Given what it could achieve, is this line of work worth the effort and time of the linguistics research community? Finally, by engaging in this speculation, we identify several ways that the goals, methods and common practices of present-day interpretability research could be improved to serve linguistics.

Before we begin, we need to set a few parameters. While we are optimistic about AI impacting cognitive science and science in general on many fronts, we focus here on language-based AI technologies and linguistics. We understand linguistics to be the science of human language. Its goal is to advance human-understandable, explanatory, testable theories for how people represent, learn, and process language. This involves postulating a set of theoretical constructs and descriptions of how these constructs interact with each other to produce observable behaviors (Chapter 2; Frank et al., 2025). While we focus on linguistics, our general framework and takeaways apply broadly to cognitive science and psychology as well.

Interpretability research can be thought of as a branch of "Machine Cognitive Science" (Kim, 2021). The goal of interpretability is to provide human-understandable descriptions and explanations of model behavior. These descriptions and explanations involve multiple model-internal components, which can be understood in terms of (a) representations and (b) computations that operate on these representations, with "circuits" being a common unit of analysis. Together, we refer to (a) and (b) as machine constructs, which we elaborate upon further in Section 2. The goal of interpretability research is to uncover machine constructs.

Right now, interpretability research is at a tipping point. The field is actively discovering new machine constructs (Huben et al., 2024; Marks et al., 2025; Olsson et al., 2022; Soulos et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2023), particularly in an automated manner (Gao et al., 2025; Paulo et al., 2025; Shaham et al., 2024). Novel methods for automatic discovery are poised to surface many unintuitive machine constructs that may not necessarily align with our understanding of human constructs underlying analogous capacities (cf. Hewitt et al. 2025). Our main question, then, is how these new discoveries should influence linguistics. We organize our response around three interrelated questions.

2. Which machine constructs are important? Which ones among the large number of discovered constructs should we pay attention to as linguists, if we are interested in understanding mental constructs in people? While the type of construct is relevant, we argue that construct recurrence can also be highly informative. A recurrent construct is one that is adopted by models diverging in various axes, including architecture or training data properties.

3. What should cognitive scientists do with machine constructs? We identify and discuss three routes for how machine constructs could enter our scientific theories, paying particular attention to methods in linguistics.

Taken together, our responses to these questions provide a sketch for a new type of scientific workflow, one that combines "machine" and "human" cognitive science by attempting to map out the shared mechanisms between these two systems and understand why such mechanisms may be shared in the first place. We do not mean to suggest that such shared mechanisms will provide a complete picture of human (linguistic) intelligence. For example, there are many aspects of human language that stem from egocentric experience, social interactions, or embodied communication, about which machine constructs found in the currently dominant models may have little relevance. Furthermore, we expect interpretability of LLMs to reveal little about why the language network is located in particular areas the brain (Hickok, 2009) or phonological constraints related to the physics of the vocal tract (Moisik et al., 2021). Nor do we think that using LLMs as a source for novel constructs should supplant traditional sources of theoretical innovation, such as creativity, intuition, and domain expertise of human scientists. However, when it comes to integrating LLMs into science, linguistics is in a relatively enviable position due to the fact that these models primarily operate in language space, and language being the capacity that we aim to explain. In other fields such as biochemistry, LLMs can reason about the target processes (e.g., Swanson et al., 2025), but explaining the mechanisms behind this reasoning does not by itself constitute an explanation of how the target processes are implemented in the model. In order to achieve this, a more promising approach would be to interpret domain-specific models designed to capture the target processes (Zhang et al., 2025). On the other hand, explaining how an LLM handles language contributes to understanding a system that exhibits the target capacity, although separate questions remain about the alignment between model and human linguistic behavior to make this explanation relevant for understanding humans. They represent one possible computational strategy for solving the difficult task of processing and producing natural language. In Sections 3, 4 and 5 we discuss our answers to Questions 1, 2, and 3 respectively. Finally, in Section 6, we conclude with forward-looking recommendations for researchers interested in this direction of inquiry.

2 Interpretability in the Limit

For the purpose of the present discussion, we take interpretability to mean the study of the internals of artificial neural network-based AI models with the aim of understanding how the model produces its behavior (the "broad technical definition" of Saphra and Wiegreffe 2024). 1 The behaviors of interest to the linguists might include appropriate handling of complex linguistic utterances, responses to behavioral tests like acceptability judgments, and patterns of error, among others. Interpretability serves to provide a description of the internal representations and computations that underlie such behaviors. Importantly, any such description has to involve some level of abstraction over model-internal components, since a description at the maximal granularity is simply the full specification of the model's architecture and its parameter values. Different interpretability methods yield outputs at different levels of abstraction and granularity: they may be subsets of the actual model components that realize certain functions ("circuits"), algorithmic descriptions or computation graphs, or organizational facts about the models' representations.

After this identification process of faithful machine constructs, what should we do?

3 How should we organize machine constructs?

The machine constructs identified through interpretability can be organized along several dimensions. We discuss a few below, along with their implications for how machine constructs relate to constructs in linguistic theory.

Theoretical status (theoretically accepted vs. theoretically novel). One dimension concerns the status of the identified machine construct within existing linguistic theories. Many machine constructs will align with constructs that have already been posited and widely accepted by linguists. For example, LLMs represent parts-of-speech, syntactic constituency/dependency, and semantic roles (Hewitt & Manning, 2019; Tenney et al., 2019), develop circuits for subject-verb agreement (Ferrando & Costa-jussà, 2024; Marks et al., 2025) and tense/aspect (Klerings et al., 2025), treat a wide array of agreement phenomena as a functional category (Kryvosheieva et al., 2026), and represent grammaticality (J. Li & Kim, 2026). On the other hand, machine constructs may align with theoretical constructs rejected by linguists (e.g., linear position: linear subnetworks that coexist with hierarchical subnetworks (Ahuja et al., 2025); and features corresponding to list position (Templeton et al., 2024)) or ones whose status is actively debated (e.g., to what extent different types of dislocation share underlying representations : Boguraev et al. 2025). Finally, interpretability may also surface novel constructs. These may be known constructs for example, from other areas of psychology whose relevance to linguistic theory has not been established previously, such as a feature that corresponds to lexically-triggered sarcasm (Templeton et al., 2024). Or they are constructs that genuinely have never been proposed. One example of the latter includes recent work showing that an LLM has learned a unique representation for the English BE verb when it appears in the context of want, e.g., She wants to be a violinist (Templeton et al., 2024). While theories of lexical uncertainty have been proposed for functional words, for example to account for various pragmatic phenomena such as scalar implicature (Bergen et al., 2016), as far as we are aware, current linguistic theories do not assume lexical ambiguity in the case of the English BE verb specifically in the context of want. Another illustrative example of a novel construct, although not from the domain of linguistics, are "pizza/clock" algorithms that a Transformer model trained to perform modular addition implements (Nanda et al., 2023; Zhong et al., 2023), which differ from the human-intuitive division/subtraction-based algorithm.

Domain status (linguistic vs. non-linguistic) Machine constructs will be products of many different regularities extractable from the training data, combined with the architectural and algorithmic inductive biases idiosyncratic to each model. Therefore, the constructs that surface do not necessarily have to be linguistic, and may reflect a wide range of statistical dependencies deriving from sources such as world knowledge, corpus statistics, or even data processing decisions. However, because in LLMs even non-linguistic information is often routed through language, determining the domain status of a particular construct may not be straightforward. We propose that methods for doing so could be described as either intrinsic or extrinsic. Intrinsic methods rely on the intuitions and domain expertise of linguists to make a determination. Extrinsic methods seek to quantify the domain status of a construct through external validation. One recent method that may be useful for extrinsic determination involves steering models to produce grammatically coherent but nonsensical and factually incorrect utterances (Winston & Kolter, 2026). Given that such methods preserve linguistic competence while degrading non-linguistic abilities, the constructs that remain after such interventions are good candidates for truly "linguistic" constructs. Ultimately, to what extent traditionally non-linguistic constructs should occupy a place in linguistic theorizing is a scope decision left to the scientists. Different theoretical commitments may draw different boundaries; for example, whether concepts should play a role in formal semantics is contended (Goodale & Mascarenhas, 2026).

Ontological status (primary vs. dependent).

The discovery of machine constructs through interpretability may raise the question of how real these constructs are, for instance whether they are merely epiphenomenal. Our interpretability in the limit assumption settles one aspect of this ontological question: because the outcome of idealized interpretability is a faithful causal description of behavior, any construct that is part of this description is guaranteed causal efficacy (assuming the description is minimal). On the other hand, a construct that does not factor into this description, even if detectable by other means, is causally inert. What the limit assumption does not settle is the primacy of a particular inventory of constructs, which may exist at different levels of abstraction. Because different granularities of abstraction yield different inventories that can be used to construct equally faithful descriptions, a construct that is a single causal variable at one level of analysis may not be one at a different level. One analogous example would be the refinability of non-terminals in context-free grammars to arbitrary granularities, preserving the same structural descriptions (minus the actual labels).

Ultimately, questions of ontological status will likely interact with the other axes of analysis discussed in this section, including domain status and simplicity. In particular, we anticipate that the explananda of a linguistic theory will shape the ontological status of its constructs. Constructs that may be necessary to describe certain behaviors can be eliminated if those behaviors are determined to no longer be within the scope of a theory. At the same time, if competing sets of machine constructs have equal descriptive faithfulness, other factors such as simplicity, discussed below, could be used to adjudicate ontological primacy.

Simplicity (simple vs. complex) In cognitive science and linguistics, simpler theories are often preferred over complex ones of equal descriptive adequacy. Neural networks are not guaranteed to converge on computational solutions that are minimal for a given problem, even with a simplicity bias. For example, in the case of modular arithmetic, Transformer models may implement different algorithms ("Pizza" vs. "Clock"), depending on the contribution of architectural components, and may even implement multiple solutions in parallel (Zhong et al., 2023). As noted in the discussion of ontological status, when weighing multiple valid constructs, simplicity could be one possible means of prioritizing constructs for future research; for instance, it may help us avoid machine constructs that are overfitted to idiosyncrasies of a specific model, and therefore less likely to generalize to human constructs. Thus, when considering a machine construct, especially as a potential basis of scientific theory, it is worth investigating the extent to which the solution is minimal. For circuits, this may mean adopting metrics such as minimum description length, Kolmogorov complexity (M. Li, Vitányi, et al., 2008), or circuit complexity (Arora & Barak, 2009); for features, this may mean determining whether the feature is logically simplex or disjunctive.

What do non-minimal solutions look like in LLMs? We illustrate with two scenarios: The first is when a model has discovered a genuinely non-minimal method for solving a problem, due to contingencies in the initialization, data presentation, or training procedure, or due to some feature of the model architecture itself. The second is when there is functional overlap in mechanisms; the model may have built-in redundancy due to representational constraints, processing demands, or the organization of other constructs in its weight space. Both of these types of complexity can be illustrated by the Indirect Object Identification circuit discussed above (Figure 2). As Wang et al. (2023) find, duplicate token heads and induction heads perform the same role: identify tokens (here, the subject token) that have already appeared in the sentence and signal the duplication. Duplicate token heads achieve this by attending directly to the earlier occurrence of the same token and sig-naling duplication. Induction heads achieve this in two steps through an induction mechanism: the previous token heads copy information about the first occurrence of the subject to the representation of the subsequent token, and then the induction heads signal duplication by matching the current token to this adjacent token. The induction mechanism adds one extra computation step into the circuit; an example of a non-minimal mechanism for duplication signaling. The fact that both of these mechanisms are active at the same time is an illustration of functional overlap since these perform the same functional role. In addition, the circuit has several other redundant components, including eight backup name mover heads. These heads exhibit varied behavior in typical scenarios, but can compensate to function like name mover heads if the originals are suppressed. Wang et al. (2023)hypothesize that these heads arise due to dropout during training, which could reward redundancy. Backup heads may be necessary for to capture the IOI behavior of this specific model, but may not be generally present in IOI-type circuits that emerge in other models, for example ones trained with different dropout hyperparameters.

4 What machine constructs should be prioritized?

Interpretability in the limit will inevitably lead to a very large number of machine constructs. LLMs, while sharing certain core modeling assumptions, are still subject to wide variation in their architecture, training data configuration, and learning algorithms. Critically, even when LLMs converge in behavior, the discovered constructs may diverge across different LLMs (cf. Braun et al. 2025). Which of these constructs are relevant to linguists? We argue, in light of the non-guarantee of convergence of internal structure, that the important machine constructs are those that are discovered across a diverse set of models that behaviorally align with humans on the phenomenon of interest. Such recurrent constructs are ones that likely arise from the demand of realizing the target phenomenon, rather than from idiosyncrasies of the specific system that realizes it or other artifacts. The more diverse the set of behaviorally-aligned models that share certain constructs, the stronger candidates they are for constructs that underlie the analogous capacity in humans. 3 Therefore, the recurrent construct approach, whose inferential power derives from divergence among the models considered, is a fundamentally different mode of discovery that complements computational modeling that relies on linking hypotheses (i.e., convergence assumptions). However, similar to computational modeling, the main downside of this approach lies in its risk: specifically, the risk that fundamental differences between the computational model and the biological system may lead to over-confidence in an ultimately incorrect theory. Thus, LLM-inspired linguistics must be conducted with an eye towards risk mitigation. We view the recurrent construct approach itself as one method for mitigating risk, but this can and should be supplemented by other methods, including those already employed in the field such as pursuing multiple theories simultaneously and emphasizing theory-adjudicating studies in experimental work.

4.1 Where does generalizability come from?

Why do we expect recurrent constructs across diverse models to be more likely to generalize to the human mind? We make arguments from two directions, one from the properties of the task and the other from the properties of the data.

Properties of the task. One idea that is often used as a justifying argument for making inferences from neural network studies is the contravariance principle of Cao and Yamins (2024) (e.g., arguments in Futrell and Mahowald 2025). The contravariance principle supposes that as a task gets harder, the space of viable solutions that implement it shrinks. Therefore, for a sufficiently hard task, independent complex systems are pushed towards discovering similar solutions. For example, the demand for vision led to the evolution of lenses in animals in several independent phylogenetic branches. Nevertheless, the principle does not guarantee convergence to a single solution-humans employ a different solution than lobsters, who use mirrors rather than lenses and therefore reflection rather than refraction to increase visual acuity. Furthermore, it is unclear whether the constraint imposed upon possible solutions via task demands extends to the lower-level implementations of each solution, as can be illustrated from the diverging physical realizations of the lenses of different animals (e.g., humans vs. insects, many of which have compound eyes with thousands of tiny lenses). Therefore, whether behaviors of interest constrain machine constructs via contravariance may depend on the specific level of analysis.

Beyond the contravariance principle, we believe there could be ways to enrich inferences about the mechanism from properties of the task, and one such way borrows from the schema of argument made by Fodor and Pylyshyn (1988). Fodor and Pylyshyn argue that some properties at the functional level, such as systematicity and productivity, both of which are often taken to be properties of human linguistic behavior, require classical/symbolic mechanisms (structured representations and structure-sensitive processing). One broader takeaway from their argument is that there may be mechanistic implications of behavioral properties. Setting aside the particular implicational claim made by Fodor and Pylyshyn (that has been a source of heated debates for decades), we can extrapolate the broader schema of the claim to "Properties XYZ of behavior impose ABC mechanistic constraints on a system". This, if true, would explain why recurrent constructs occur across diverse models with convergent target behaviors, where we take the task to demand the behavioral proper-ties. 4 One possible candidate is same-different judgment behavior on perceptual stimuli. This may require any algorithm that realizes it to have at least two steps: (1) forming category representations for each stimulus based on perceptual features while abstracting away from irrelevant features; and (2) computing same-different determinations over those representations. Indeed, Lepori et al. (2024) have found results consistent with the two stages of computation in Vision Transformers (although of course, this does not yet establish a necessity result). 5 To illustrate this with a different example from biology, the task of path integration (returning to a fixed point after a random walk) may impose mechanistic constraints, including maintenance of a system's direction and velocity. To support this, functionally equivalent systems for computing these two variables have been found in foraging animals, including both insects and mammals (Varga et al., 2017). It is a genuinely open question whether any instantiated form of this argument holds for linguistic phenomena (Cao and Yamins (2024) themselves are skeptical of constraining effects on the sub-functional level in general), although recent work formalizing this line of inquiry in vision may be a promising starting point (e.g., Uselis et al. 2026). We believe recurrent constructs could also help identify candidate phenomena that are subject to these interface constraints.

Properties of the data. The second source of convergence may derive from regularities encoded in the training data itself. A significant portion of the training data of the models we consider will be outputs of the human linguistic system, and therefore the data will reflect certain regularities of the underlying system. Furthermore, we expect important regularities to be extractable from most sufficiently large samples of these outputs (e.g., human children acquiring the same language can do so from different samples). Therefore, convergence could also be reached from the signatures of the data-generating system available from the output of that system, although as discussed in Section 3, whether constructs discovered from data-driven convergence are relevant to linguistics must be adjudicated by theoretical commitments. Of course, this is also not a claim that everything interesting about language can be learned from data alone. Nor is it a claim that every learner can do so from any sufficiently large sample of human-generated linguistic outputs. The scope of the claim is that, if a set of diverse learners converge on certain constructs, one possible source of that convergence is the data property.

4.2 What dimensions should vary?

What dimensions of diversity should we consider when searching for recurrent constructs? Conversely, are there dimensions along which we specifically do not want diversity? The recurrent constructs paradigm we are advocating for imposes a filter condition for models based on their behavioral alignment with humans on the phenomena of interest. Outside of this filter condition, all dimensions of variation count towards diversity: architecture, objective, data, supported input/output modality, languages, and more. As discussed previously, the operating assumption is that the more diverse the set of models, the more likely the recurrent constructs will generalize to human linguistic cognition. Multilinguality is a particularly significant axis that would indicate stronger generalizability of the recurrent constructs compared to ones shared by models trained on a single language.

What this variation looks like in practice will depend on what types of behaviors one wishes to study. If the goal is to investigate end-state linguistic competence, then one could look for recurrent constructs across any model that produces fluent natural language, including models whose training data included non-linguistic data, such as code, or models that have been trained on tens of trillions of training tokens. In this case, one should not restrict diversity on certain axes in an attempt to impose human-likeness or ecological validity, such as only investigating constructs that recur across data-limited models. Doing so would mistakenly privilege one axis of variation (data) over others (architecture, modality, etc.). However, if the scope of the inquiry is, instead, developmental, data constraints on both the size and distribution become a defining part of behavioral alignment, since what now has to be aligned is the full learning trajectory. We argue that for most studies in linguistics, acquisition is a relevant explanandum, and therefore recurrent constructs from humanscale LMs will be of critical interest (Wilcox et al., 2025). However, this is not due to a preference for ecological validity per se; but rather because large pretrained LMs simply would not pass through our behavioral filter when development is concerned.

The behavioral filter, despite being a single constraint, is a powerful one that achieves two effects in the context of the diversity discussion. First, it rules out trivial diversity: for example, models that are trained exclusively on the baa+ language or only on stock photographs are indeed "diverse" in terms of training data, but such models are likely irrelevant to inquiries about natural language. These models will simply be ruled out because they do not exhibit target behaviors. Second, it correctly includes models with different routes to the target capacity. For instance, assuming end-state investigation, adult competence may be reached through various routes: through developmentally realistic training data combined with strong language-specific inductive biases, or through much larger training data with weaker language-specific biases. Because the recurrent constructs paradigm conditions its search on model diversity, this inclusivity is required by design.

We want to emphasize that the recurrent constructs paradigm is not a way for filtering or eliminating constructs as invalid for future study. There is no predetermined threshold a particular construct must reach before it counts as being "recurrent" enough. Rather, the paradigm is a way of ordering constructs and adjudicating which constructs should be prioritized for future investigation. This will necessarily involve balancing the hit-rate of constructs against the diversity of models in which that particular construct has been found. For example, a construct that has been found in 10 textonly monolingual LLMs from different language families may not necessarily be privileged over a competing construct that has been found in only five models, but of differing input modalities and languages. We also want to emphasize that there may be other valid reasons for further investigation of machine constructs besides their recurrence. As mentioned in the discussion of Section 3 on theoretical status, some constructs may be worth investigating due to their relationship to existing theoretical proposals even without high recurrence.

5 How should we incorporate (recurrent) machine constructs into linguistics?

Imagine that we identified candidates for generalization through the recurrent constructs paradigm (Section 4). The constructs' theoretical status (Section 3) then determines whether they are converging/confirmatory evidence for existing linguistic constructs, adjudicating evidence for debated constructs, or evidence for novel, previously undiscussed constructs. However, analysis of neural network constructs so far has had little influence on linguistic theory (Baroni, 2022), and passive acknowledgement of the evidential status of the recurrent machine constructs will have similarly limited impact on linguistics. How can we go beyond observational remarks and actively incorporate them into linguistics?

The bottom line-which we want to emphasize up front-is that the mere presence of a machine construct, or even the presence of a recurrent machine construct that is shared by a maximally diverse set of models does not on its own imply that the corresponding construct underlies the human capacity for language. Therefore, the primary role of (recurrent) machine constructs is feeding hypothesis generation. Taking them as inspiration, linguists will need to conduct further theoretical and empirical work to determine whether, and how, they should be incorporated into our scientific theories. We see three possible routes for this, which we discuss in further detail below.

Corroboration and explanatory hypotheses. When machine constructs align with constructs already posited in linguistic theory, machine constructs contribute converging evidence. Our confidence about those constructs is strengthened, but given their already-established status, corrobo-ration plays only a passive role. Of course, corroboration will be the most theoretically exciting when it supports a debated or controversial theoretical construct. In this case, the recurrence of the construct suggests that this solution may be optimal or easier to learn over and above competing theoretical proposals. Nevertheless, it is unlikely that any machine construct will overlap entirely with existing linguistic constructs. The more likely (and potentially insightful) scenario is partial corroboration, where elements of the discovered causal structure diverge from or are novel to existing theory. In this case, the construct can serve as a jumping-off point for further hypothesis generation, which we discuss later in this section.

Corroboration is necessarily limited in its ability to expand linguistic theory. Still, we envision that corroboratory constructs could deepen explanatory power of linguistic theories by pointing towards the underlying computational or data processing principles that give rise to the construct in the first place. If a construct recurs in many systems, the question of why arises. Then, the sources of convergence that our recurrent constructs paradigm is grounded in become candidate explanatory hypotheses explaining why the construct emerges in the first place. For example, if extant linguistic constructs were found to arise only in autoregressive models, but not bidirectional models, this would point to incrementality of processing as a deeper explanation for the construct.

Of course, since such explanatory hypotheses are hypotheses, they must be followed up by finergrained model studies that control for individual sources of convergence as well as evidence from human studies that verifies the proposed explanatory hypotheses. We have discussed two possible sources of convergence (task and data properties) in Section 4.1, but these are not exhaustive, and future work may posit more sources/hypotheses. Furthermore, because we already have an a priori theoretical interest in the construct, explanatory hypotheses need not be held to such strict diversity standards as we advocated for in the recurrent constructs paradigm. As discussed with the incrementality example above, explanations may arise precisely from observing the limitations under which the construct arises in LLMs.

Hypothesis generation/testing for human verdicts.

What happens when a machine construct is not just corroboratory, but partially or entirely novel vis-à-vis current linguistic theory? Any deeper connection to linguistics requires at least the following steps of hypothesis generation and testing: First, the recurrent machine constructs supply a candidate hypothesis. Second, a linguist refines the candidate hypothesis into a testable hypothesis about the human linguistic capacity. Third, the hypothesis is either confirmed or rejected via methods appropriate to the target level of abstraction (e.g., descriptive adequacy, theoretical analyses, or behavioral or neural experiments). Finally, this human verdict informs modification of existing theories. In this framework, linguists' contribution is indispensable in all steps. This framework is in a similar spirit to the hypothesis generation framework of Misra and Kim (2024), but our distinct contribution is the recurrent constructs paradigm with an emphasis on model internals.

The value of machine constructs is perhaps the most intuitively clear when the construct is entirely novel. In this case, the machine constructs complement the imagination gap, preconceptions, or biases that may be hampering human theorizing. Machine constructs may also be useful when they are partially novel, insofar as they can help flesh out theoretical proposals and make them easier to adjudicate, especially experimentally. Often, theoretical debates hit a stalemate because it is difficult to design clear tiebreaking examples or experiments. Having full access to machine constructs may let us more easily identify where exactly the competing theories diverge, for example through experimental hypothesis generation. Notably, adjudication between theories may also work via recurrent constructs that have been rejected in established theories, or recurrent absence of an established construct. If a diverse range of successful models exhibit the target behavior in such ways that challenge established theories, this may motivate us to actively revisit those assumptions. Taken together, recurrent constructs combined with hypothesis generation/testing can corroborate, extend, and challenge existing linguistic theories. Only this kind of active effort (regardless of adopting the recurrent constructs paradigm or not) will help bridge the disconnect between LLM analysis work and linguistics that Baroni (2022) observed.

Contributions to meta-theoretical debates and novel paradigms/frameworks. The above discussions primarily concerned revisions within existing theories and frameworks. However, we also believe that recurrent machine constructs have the potential to contribute to discussions at a level higher than individual theories. We briefly discuss two examples here: informing meta-theoretical debates and informing the development of novel theoretical paradigms and frameworks. As for contributions to meta-theoretical debates, recurrent constructs can help advance our understanding of how different levels of analysis may constrain each other. The discussion from Section 4.1 about Fodor and Pylyshyn (1988)-type argument schema where functional properties are thought to constrain mechanisms provides a useful illustration: recurrent constructs may provide evidence for or against such constraints.

As for potential paradigmatic contributions, LLMs are raising a puzzle in light of a long-held claim in linguistics, namely that natural language syntax requires formal expressivity beyond contextfree (Joshi, 1985; Shieber, 1985). There is a growing literature on the formal power of Transformers and other neural architectures (Deletang et al., 2023; Hahn, 2020; Hao et al., 2022; Merrill & Sabharwal, 2024; Strobl et al., 2024), and the emerging picture is that Transformers do not neatly line up with the delineations of the Chomsky-Schützenberger hierarchy and fixed-depth Transformers are limited in their ability to recognize arbitrary context-free languages without other means such as a scratchpad (e.g., using Chain-of-Thought). Given that Transformers are the backbones of the most fluent and powerful models of language to date, how these formal limitations relate to their practical capacity is an open question. At the same time, there are also results from the theoretical linguistics side that characterize syntactic dependencies through subregular constraints over structured representations (Graf, 2022a (Graf, , 2022b)). The intersection between these two lines of work could be a productive ground for cross-pollination (e.g., van der Poel et al. 2024). Recurrent constructs may play an interesting novel role in this investigation by connecting formal accounts to learned computations: the structural descriptions and constraints proposed in subregular accounts can guide the search for recurrent mechanisms that may implement those constraints. Such investigations can reveal whether and how the properties of the formal accounts are reflected in the mechanisms. Even if the identified mechanisms do not correspond to the proposed descriptions, they could offer novel insights into how the representations and computations posited by syntactic theory can be implemented.

One additional case comes from the history of Optimality Theory/Harmonic Grammar (Legendre et al., 1990; Prince & Smolensky, 2002). This theoretical approach served as an existence proof that a radically different, influential framework for linguistic theory can emerge from the attempt to reconcile different systems of computation. Regardless of precisely what constructs they learn, it is likely that LLMs achieve behavioral sophistication by combining the power of symbolic and gradient constructs. Understanding how these structures are learned from data, and how (approximately) symbolic computation is performed within a continuous space can help us develop and refine meta-theoretical approaches (e.g., Smolensky 1990) for how people could achieve the same. Long-term, we hope that such theorizing will help refine existing frameworks or even inspire novel ones, with one promising direction being a bridge framework between linguistics and neuroscience that can formalize how language is implemented in the brain.

6 Recommendations

Back to reality: What does all this discussion mean for the present day? Interpretability does not exist in the limit, and the current range of methods is only beginning to elucidate how LLMs' representations causally drive their behavior. Despite this, there are several moves that LLM interpretability researchers, cognitive scientists, and linguists can make now, in anticipation of a future closer to our in the limit scenario. Below, we discuss three recommendations, which will increase the likelihood of successful intellectual exchange between interpretability research and human science in the medium term.

Model diversity.

Following the discussion in Section 4, interpretability research should be expanded to a more diverse range of models. Of course, depending on the phenomenon of interest, obtaining a diverse set of models that pass the behavioral filter condition may not be an easy bar to meet. Some dimensions of diversity are harder than others to sample along with a high-bar behavioral filter, such as fluent conversational abilities. This becomes especially difficult in the data-constrained regimes, which we believe will be most interesting to linguists (Charpentier et al., 2025; Warstadt et al., 2023). Still, cultivating a practice of diversity in model evaluation now will ensure we find truly recurrent machine constructs in the future. Currently, most interpretability research is conducted on text-only, English-centric models. While recent efforts have expanded some analyses to multilingual settings (Resck et al., 2025; Schut et al., 2025), and audio models (Glazer et al., 2026; Zhao et al., 2026), interpretability in these areas lags behind. Interpretability for such models has other benefits, such as increasing the models' controllability and explainability.

Better behavioral evaluations.

As our previous discussion highlighted, recurrent constructs exist only with respect to specific behavioral explananda. Therefore, building tests of theoretically interesting human behaviors is a necessary first step for the research workflow outlined in this article. Left up to their own devices, we believe it is unlikely that AI-focused research labs will develop such benchmarks on their own, largely because competence in fine-grained, theoretically interesting linguistic behaviors no longer aligns with abilities that drive contemporary LLM research. While the field already has widely-used benchmarks (Gauthier et al., 2020; Warstadt et al., 2020, e.g., ), these cover only a small percentage of the linguistic phenomena that our eventual theories should explain. Linguists should, therefore, continue to build rigorous behavioral tests that are constructvalid for the target phenomenon of interest and calibrated against humans. The tests should treat human behavior as the gold standard (idealized or empirically elicited, depending on the research question). Of particular note are phenomena that specifically link experience and behavior, such as linguistic generalization (e.g., compositionality) and language acquisition, where rigorous tests are often challenged by opaque training data (Kim, 2026). Development of such tests should be a field-wide effort. Resources like common data formatting and a centralized data repository can ensure that efforts are reusable and portable across different research groups.

Translating constructs to human verdicts. The most exciting possibility of LLMs is that they will help us to discover unintuitive theoretical constructs. To achieve this, we will have to concentrate efforts on translating insights from LLM interpretability into human verdicts and eventual adjustment of our linguistic theories. Right now, efforts have largely focused on the first part of the hypothesis-testing pipeline discussed in Section 5, namely on generating adequate explanations of LLM behavior. We encourage researchers who bridge these two communities to help identify and publicize machine constructs that could be tested experimentally. Developing methods now, even on non-recurrent constructs, can help establish best practices for when more robust explanations of LLM behavior are available. In this vein, two recent contributions (Boguraev et al., 2025; De-sai & Nair, 2026) have identified how various factors impact LMs' and BabyLMs' representations of English filler-gap dependencies, suggesting that model constructs are sensitive to animacy and construction identity. Following up on these findings and articulating concrete hypotheses to test in human subjects (cf. Misra and Kim 2024) could prove to be an early case study in how LLM interpretation can influence linguistic theory.

7 Conclusion

In the introduction, we laid out three ways that our discussion could prove beneficial in the current moment. Putting aside the third one (recommendations, discussed above), we return to the other two by way of conclusion. First, we hope that our interpretability in the limit paradigm helps to set expectations: We anticipate that the work of incorporating machine constructs into linguistics will be slow and steady, and will require effort from many people who have cognitive science and linguistics backgrounds. Machine constructs may challenge long-standing paradigms in linguistics, but it is up to the scientists themselves whether and how machine constructs move beyond a simple "challenge" to influence linguistic theories. This brings us to our second question-is this a future we want for linguistics? We believe that any scientific discipline is strongest when it approaches core questions through a wide array of methods and theoretical stances. Interpretability (in the limit) offers an opportunity to broaden our empirical practices in ways that may mitigate the downside risk of our own theoretical preconceptions. At the same time, it provides a pathway into linguistics from what is, at the moment, an exploding subfield with lots of technical know-how and young talent. This could be an opportunity for an expansion of our community, as well as an opportunity for our community to engage with and play a significant scientific role in technological discourse that will continuously impact society. On the other hand, the main risks are that linguistics becomes dominated by a single approach, or that the current funding structures and excitement push linguistics towards being only a science of the artificial instead of the human. Our article is an attempt to redirect parts of the current excitement in the artificial back to the human, rather than a call for redirection of already-existing, indispensable efforts for human science within our community towards the line of work outlined here. Ultimately, this second question-is it worth the effort and time?-is not a question two authors can or should answer. We hope that our discussion has provided a vision and vocabulary that can initiate productive discourse around the relationship between AI research and linguistics in the decades to come.
