To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 2 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 128 papers with 246 instances of power statements, 203 were correctly detected (true positives), 22 were missed (false negatives) and 21 were incorrectly detected (false positives). Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Full text

Abstract

People frequently compare themselves to their intimate partners. These partner comparisons are critical for relationship maintenance, with powerful ramifications for intimate partner aggression (IPA) perpetration. However, little is known about the neural correlates of partner comparisons and their relation to IPA perpetration. To address this gap, 96 participants from 49 intimate partner dyads underwent functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) and completed a social comparison task, judging their own negative and positive traits against those of their partner and the average person. Participants then reported whether they had any history of IPA perpetration.

Consistent with previous findings, participants reported having less negative and more positive traits than others. However, participants' tendency to compare themselves favorably to others was attenuated for their partners. Partner (> average person) comparisons were linked to heightened activity in the mentalizing network (DMPFC, precuneus, TPJ) and DLPFC, particularly for negative traits. Such DLPFC/DMPFC activity during partner comparisons related to greater IPA perpetration for negative traits and lesser IPA perpetration for positive traits. These findings suggest that recruiting mentalizing and executive control networks during partner comparisons may be meaningfully linked to IPA perpetration, depending on trait valence.

Introduction

People tend to see themselves and their intimate partners as being better than others, possessing a lesser amount of socially-undesirable (i.e., negative) traits and a greater amount of socially-desirable (i.e., positive) traits than their peers (Zell et al., 2020). However, when pitted against each other, the tendency towards self-enhancement often supersedes partnerenhancement motives, which can undermine relationship functioning (Busby et al., 2009; El-Alayli & Wynne, 2015). Yet it remains unexamined how such self > partner superiority might predict the perpetration of intimate partner aggression (IPA). Further, the neural mechanisms underlying the effect of self-superiority on IPA perpetration remain poorly understood. In what follows, we detail our attempt to fill these two gaps in our understanding.

Self-and Partner-Enhancement

People evaluate their traits, values, opinions, accomplishments, beliefs, and abilities relative to others' (Festinger, 1954; Tesser 1988). These social comparisons occur spontaneously and often subconsciously, indicating that it is an inherent aspect of how people process social information (Biernat, 2003; Dunning & Hayes, 1996; Higgins & Lurie, 1983; Mussweiler & Epstude, 2009). When making these comparisons, people are often motivated by a desire to maintain a positive self-view (Robins & Beer, 2001; Sedikides & Gregg, 2008). This selfenhancing bias is evident in the 'above average effect', in which individuals tend to view themselves as better than the average person on socially-desirable traits (e.g., intelligence, attractiveness; Chambers & Windschitl, 2004; Dunning et al., 1989; Epley & Dunning, 2000; Suls et al., 2002). Susceptibility to such self-enhancement indicates that social comparison judgments are often flawed, particularly when under self-esteem threat and cognitive load (Beer et al., 2013). For example, after recalling a threatening life event, people tend to view their current selves as better than their past selves to protect self-esteem (McFarland & Alvaro, 2000).

People frequently define themselves through their close relationships, integrating their partner into their self-concept (Aron et al., 1992; Branand et al., 2019). Consequently, partnerenhancement-the tendency to view one's partner in an inordinately positive light (Taylor & Brown, 1988; Murray, 1999)-is advantageous. It allows individuals to bolster and enhance their self-esteem by extending a positivity bias to their partner, whose strengths and successes are seen as reflections of the self. Even when partners make mistakes or do not live up to expectations, this bias leads people to dismiss these failures by placing blame on external forces, rather than internal ones such as their disposition or personality (Taylor & Koivumaki, 1976). These selfreflective motives are accompanied by other driving forces behind partner enhancement, such as partners being familiar, emotionally salient, and less abstract than strangers -though the influences of these other variables on partner enhancement are not well investigated.

The partner-enhancement bias to view one's partner favorably compared to others tends to be superseded by a self-enhancement bias, which has been linked to lower relationship quality (Busby et al., 2009; El-Alayli & Wynne, 2015). In these instances of self > partner superiority, enhancement processes that typically promote relational stability may become destabilizing, potentially placing partners at heightened risk for aggression within their intimate partnerships.

Self-Superiority and Intimate Partner Aggression

Intimate partner aggression (IPA) is any attempt to harm an intimate partner who does not wish to be harmed in the perpetrator's intended way or to the perpetrator's intended extent (Allen & Anderson, 2017). The tendency to see oneself as better than others is a well-established predictor of such aggressive acts. For instance, meta-analyses find reliable positive associations between narcissism (i.e., the tendency towards grandiose self-enhancement) and aggression (Kjaervik & Bushman, 2021). This meta-analytic association appears for IPA as well, though its link to physical forms of IPA was non-significant (Oliver et al., 2024). These meta-analyses fit well with the empirically-supported literatures on threatened egotism (Bushman & Baumeister, 1998) and quest for significance (Kruglanski et al., 2023) theories, which have found that individuals who perceive themselves as superior to others are often aggressive because they (A) perceive aggression as an effective means of maintaining their inflated sense of self and (B) experience exacerbated threat and distress due to perceived slights stemming from their elevated and vulnerable sense of self. However, this body of research has relied on self-reported narcissistic traits and has yet to directly measure specific social comparisons against one's partner and relate them to IPA perpetration. Ultimately, it remains unclear whether selfsuperiority over one's partner (i.e., perceiving the self as possessing less negative and more positive traits than one's partner) is linked to such harmful behavior.

The tendency for self-superior individuals to perpetrate IPA is likely to emerge for several key reasons, such that people who feel superior to others feel entitled to special treatment and respond aggressively when they perceive that they are not receiving their desired level of praise, accommodations, or respect (Rasmussen, 2016). This is particularly relevant among IPA perpetrators, who often respond to perceived threats with aggression as a means of reasserting dominance and control within the relationship (Caman et al., 2024). Further, those who perceive themselves as better than others tend to be vengeful and retributive, failing to forgive and tenaciously pursuing revenge against those who have offended them (Brown, 2004). To date, these putative mechanisms have been studied using self-reports and behavioral assessments, which are prone to biases beyond self-enhancement.

The Neuroscience of Self-Enhancement

Self-enhancement during social comparison arises from a complex array of brain activity. Initial functional brain imaging investigations of people making social comparisons-and the self-enhancement that typically co-occurred-focused on the critical role of the medial prefrontal cortex (MPFC; Flagan & Beer, 2013). Activity in the ventral MPFC during social comparisons has been reliably linked to motivated self-enhancement (Hughes & Zaki, 2015), likely due to this region's role in encoding the inherent value of the self (Chester et al., 2016). Additionally, the dorsal aspect of the MPFC (DMPFC), a core hub of the mentalizing network, appears to engage the necessary perspective-taking processes that social comparisons require (Schurz & Perner, 2015; Van Overwalle, 2009). The brain's mentalizing network extends beyond the DMPFC to also include the precuneus and temporoparietal junction (TPJ; Schurz et al., 2014). Further, selfenhancement motives are subserved by biased perceptual and attentional processes arising from the inferior temporal cortex and lateral PFC, respectively (Hughes & Zaki, 2015). These latter two regions coordinate to preferentially perceive, attend to, and encode stimuli in ways that minimize negative and threatening stimuli and maximize positive and favorable stimuli. Lastly, many of these brain regions, though especially mentalizing regions such as the DMPFC, are modulated by social closeness, indicating that this region responds strongly to personallyrelevant individuals (Krienen et al., 2010; Van Overwalle, 2009). As such, the mentalizing network is likely to be critical to understanding the neural mechanisms underlying the effect of self-superiority on IPA perpetration.

The Present Study

To examine how the neural correlates of partner comparisons might correspond to IPA perpetration, we preregistered a series of hypotheses. We first hypothesized that reporting that one has lower amounts of negative traits and greater amounts of positive traits than one's partner would be positively associated with IPA perpetration. We then preregistered that this link between favorable partner comparisons and IPA perpetration would be mediated and suppressed by heightened medial orbitofrontal cortex activity during partner comparisons. Yet we have since abandoned this second hypothesis, without testing it, given the many critiques of cross-sectional mediation (e.g., Cole & Maxwell, 2003) and also due to the realization that our sample size was insufficient to adequately power such a statistical model. We replaced this confirmatory approach with an exploratory one, in which we decided to simply examine the neural correlates of partner comparisons and test how IPA perpetration might relate to partner-comparison-related neural activity. To estimate these potential associations, we used fMRI to assess brain activity while participants engaged in social comparisons with their intimate partner and a control (i.e., the average person) on both negative and positive traits. We then related these neural correlates of partner comparisons to self-reported IPA perpetration.

Methods

This study's preregistration plan can be found at https://osf.io/un2fy, and many elements of that plan were tested and presented in Chester and colleagues (2021) and Rahrig and colleagues (2023), though none of those analyses are redundant with those presented here. The raw data will not be made publicly available due to the extremely sensitive nature of the study, the ability to potentially identify individual participants based on their partner's responses, and the risk to both members of the relationship if the information were to be released. Following the recommendations of Finkel and colleagues (2015), we will provide de-identified data upon request to qualified professionals who contact the corresponding author.

Statistical Power Statement

We were limited to the number of participants recruited based on the availability of funds. Subsequently, we performed a post hoc sensitivity analysis via G*Power (version 3.1.9.7; Faul et al., 2007) to determine the smallest detectable effect size for the 2 (target: Partner versus Average-Person) x 2 (valence: Negative versus Positive) within-participants ANOVA: α = .05, four groups, 60 repeated measurements, nonsphericity correction (ϵ) = 1.0, and r =.50 correlation among repeated measures. At N = 96, we had 90% power to detect effects as small as f = .059, ηp² = .014.

Participants

We originally recruited 102 participants from 51 romantic couples, who were either local community members (n = 73) or undergraduates in a psychology subject pool (n = 29). Couples had to consist of one self-identified man and one self-identified woman, have been in a monogamous relationship for at least six consecutive months and did not meet the following exclusionary MRI safety criteria: ages outside the 18-35 range, body mass index exceeding 30, claustrophobia, color blindness, mental or neural pathology, presence of metallic objects in the body, non-right-hand dominance, history of head trauma, current use of psychoactive medication, and positive results on a pregnancy test. After excluding two participants (from two separate dyads) who failed to meet these criteria during the second, on-site screening process, our initial sample consisted of 100 healthy, right-handed young adults from 51 couples, age: M = 21.61, SD = 3.73, range = 18 -35, 51% women and 49% men, with an average relationship duration of 24.01 months (SD = 14.98, range = 6-84). Specifically, 83% were dating seriously, 5% were engaged, 5% were married, 4% were dating casually, and 3% did not report their relationship status. In terms of ethnicity, 19% of participants identified as Hispanic, 78% as Non-Hispanic, and 3% missing. Regarding race, 7% identified as African-American, 22% as Asian-American, 1% as Native American, 45% as White, 22% as Other, and 3% missing. Participants were compensated with either $100 or university research credit for their participation.

Materials Social Comparison Task (SCT).

To assess participants' perceptions of their own traits in relation to their romantic partner and a prototypical person, we adapted a widely-used, computerized Social Comparison Task (Dunning et al., 1989; Beer & Hughes, 2010; Hughes & Beer, 2012). In this task, participants repeatedly judged the extent to which they possessed less, the same, or more of various negative and positive personality traits than their romantic partner or "the average person". Comparing oneself to "the average person" served as a control condition, as it provided a generalizable reference point that is commonly used in social comparison research to capture normative assessments (Beer et al., 2013; Beer & Hughes, 2010; Chambers & Windschitl, 2004; Hughes & Beer, 2012). This approach allowed us to isolate the psychological and neural processes that underlie trait comparisons against one's romantic partner. We also employed both negative and positive (i.e., socially undesirable and desirable) traits to examine the unique contributions of valence to IPA.

The Social Comparison Task included 60 trials that were structured in a two (comparison target: Partner versus Average-Person) x two (trait valence: Negative versus Positive) withinparticipants factorial design. The 60 trials were presented in 12 blocks of five trials, split evenly by target (i.e., six Partner blocks, six Average-Person blocks). The order of these 12 blocks was randomized, by target, for each participant. The valence of the trait presented on each individual trial was also randomly selected without replacement, such that each participant had their own idiosyncratic sequence of traits. This caused Negative and Positive traits to be intermixed within blocks and created variability in the number of trials assigned to each of the four conditions Each block of five social comparison trials was preceded by a target screen (duration: 5 seconds) indicated who the comparison target would be for the next five trials (i.e., "your partner" or "the average person") and then a fixation cross (randomly jittered duration: 2.5 seconds [75% of trials], 5 seconds [25% of trials]; Figure 1). For each social comparison trial, participants indicated whether they possessed 1 (less), 2 (about same), or 3 (more) of each given trait than the target displayed on the screen (i.e., their partner or an average person; duration: 2.5 seconds). Abuse Within Intimate Relationships Scale (AWIRS).

To assess self-reported history of IPA perpetration, we administered the 26-item AWIRS. The AWIRS quantifies five forms of IPA including: overt violence, emotional abuse, deception, verbal abuse, and restrictive violence (Borjesson et al., 2003). Participants self-reported the frequency with which they perpetrated each form of IPA on a Likert scale from 0 (never) to 7 (more than once a day). To maintain consistency with our prior research (Chester et al., 2021; Rahrig et al., 2023), we focused on the Overt Violence subscale (seven items; e.g., 'I have physically attacked my partner'). Further, we re-coded these scores in a binary fashion such that any endorsement of past overt violence was scored as 1 and no past endorsement of overt violence was scored as 0. We adopted this nonpreregistered scoring approach due to the zero-inflated and positively skewed nature of this measure's scores and to maintain consistency with how we handled these distribution issues in prior research (Chester et al., 2021; Rahrig et al., 2023).

Procedure

Both partners from each couple arrived at the MRI center, where they were separated and consented. Each partner was again screened for MRI safety and then practiced the MRI computer tasks. One partner was randomly selected to enter the MRI scanner first, where they completed initial structural brain scans, followed by functional scans that we acquired while participants completed an aggression task (see Chester et al., 2021), our Social Comparison Task, and then several unrelated functional tasks (see Rahrig et al., 2021). During the scan, the other partner sat in a nearby testing room and completed a battery of behavioral and self-report measures including the AWIRS. Participants then swapped roles, with the scanned participant sitting in a testing room and completing behavioral and self-report measures while the other partner completed the MRI scanning procedures. Participants were led to believe that they were being scanned at the same time as their partner.

We then individually debriefed each participant and provided mental health resources in the surrounding area. After ensuring that both participants were not distressed by our procedures and felt safe and comfortable interacting with their partner, we reunited the couple. They were then collectively debriefed, compensated, and thanked before departing from the laboratory. We obtained a Certificate of Confidentiality (CC-AA-18-025) from the National Institutes of Health to safeguard participant privacy. Further, the research activities described in this manuscript were approved by the institutional review board at Virginia Commonwealth University and conducted in compliance with both institutional and national regulations.

Behavioral Analyses

We conducted a two (comparison target: Partner versus Average-Person) x two (trait valence: Negative versus Positive) repeated-measures ANOVA using JASP (version 0.19.1; JASP Team, 2024) to examine the effects of valence and target on participants' social comparisons. To further explore the interaction between comparison target and trait valence, pairwise contrasts were conducted comparing the four individual conditions to one another. To evaluate how comparisons differed from being 'about the same' as the comparison target (i.e., whether participants judged that they possessed less or more of the traits within each condition), we conducted one-sample t-tests against the midpoint of the response scale (i.e., two). We followed up on these condition comparisons by conducting bivariate correlations between the four SCT scores and binary logistic regression models that examined how each judgment-type related to the likelihood of having ever perpetrated IPA.

All MRI data were obtained using a Philips Ingenia 3.0 Tesla scanner with a 32-channel head coil. Reconstructed functional volumes were corrected for head motion relative to the median functional volume using FSL's MCFLIRT tool. We then aggregated participants' individual contrast maps into a group-level mixed-effects GLM, generating group average maps for each of the seven contrasts. Deviations From Preregistration

We deviated from our preregistration plan in several ways (see the annotated preregistration form in the Supplemental Document for more details). First, we did not test our preregistered mediation hypothesis given that cross-sectional 'mediation' models cannot establish mediation and require more statistical power than we were able to provide (Liu & Wang, 2019; Maxwell & Cole, 2007). Second, we preregistered that we would have 15 trials per condition on the Social Comparison Task, but due to a programming oversight the randomization procedure created the variable number of trials per condition that we detailed in the task description above. Third, we planned to measure IPA perpetration using a laboratory aggression task (see Chester et al., 2021) but instead used self-reported IPA perpetration via the AWIRS. This change was made because the laboratory task measures situational aggression and has not been validated as a trait aggression measure, whereas the AWIRS was specifically designed and validated to capture individual differences in IPA perpetration. The AWIRS thus provided a more appropriate measure of real-world IPA behaviors and allowed us to assess perpetration outside the laboratory context. At the request of a reviewer, we tested whether our key findings replicated using the laboratory IPA task, but this analysis was not conducted in an a priori fashion.

Discussion

The neural mechanisms underlying partner comparisons in the context of intimate partnerships and their contributions to IPA perpetration remain incompletely understood. In this study, we found that greater activity in the central hubs of the mentalizing and executive control networks during positive trait comparisons with intimate partners were linked to having no (versus any) history of IPA perpetration. These potential neural mechanisms shed new light on the psychobiological processes that underlie how self > partner superiority might promote IPA perpetration.

Partner Comparisons

Consistent with prior research on self-enhancement (Zell et al., 2020), we found that participants reported possessing more positive and less negative traits than both their intimate partner and the average person. We also replicated prior work (Fletcher & Kerr, 2010; Morry et al., 2014), in which participants reported that their partner had more positive and less negative traits than the average person. The extent to which participants reported having relatively favorable levels of traits compared to their partner was similar for negative and positive traitsthe symmetry of which deviates from typical social comparison findings in which people preferentially downplay their own negative traits more than they exaggerate their positive traits (Zell et al., 2020). The reasons for this symmetry are uncertain, but it may be that the threat of having negative traits is diminished by the emotion-regulating presence of one's partner (e.g., Enav et al., 2024). We moved beyond these well-replicated effects, being one of the few studies that directly pit participants' self-enhancement and partner-enhancement motives against one another. Consistent with prior research (El-Alayli & Wynne, 2015), participants reported having more positive and less negative traits than their partner. These findings further support that the tendency to compare oneself favorably to others holds even in intimate partnerships.

Self-Superiority and IPA Perpetration

Participants who reported having more positive traits than their partner were more likely to have previously perpetrated IPA, though this exploratory effect was weak and did not replicate when IPA was coded as a continuous variable or in a multilevel framework. Indeed, the p value associated with the original finding was marginal, so we urge caution in placing too much confidence in this result. Future work is needed to ensure that this effect replicates more robustly before we can place much trust in it.

Neural Correlates of Partner Comparisons

Neuroimaging data further revealed increased activation within regions associated with the mentalizing network-TPJ, DLPFC, and precuneus-during trait comparisons with one's partner relative to an average person. Critically, our design examined neural activity during social comparison events and did not directly tie brain activity to the actual judgments that participants selected on each trial. As such, we cannot infer whether the mentalizing activity we observed had direct consequences for the ratings of self-superiority we observed. Future research examining how trial-level brain activity relates to the specific comparative judgments on those trials will be needed to establish these connections between mentalizing network activity and the comparisons people ultimately report.

Given the observed tendency for people to compare themselves less favorably to their partners than to the average person, this neural activity might reflect greater mentalizing effort given to one's partner, suggesting that individuals rely less on their egocentric biases when evaluating their partner and more on attempts to accurately and empathically perceive their partner in a humanizing manner.

This potentially enhanced mentalizing may attenuate biases towards comparing oneself favorably to others, allowing for more generous evaluation of the partner and, by extension, fostering relational harmony (Barrios et al., 2008; Kwan et al., 2007; Nohlen et al., 2019).

Many other alternative reverse inferences could be made regarding the psychological meaning of the neural activity we observed. First, activity in the DLPFC/DMPFC-centered cluster could reflect the greater prior knowledge and intimacy that participants had with their partners (versus the abstract concept of the 'average person'). Though we note that such concrete familiarity is more reliably encoded in the anterior and ventral regions of the MPFC than in the more dorsal portions of this region that we observed in our study (Lieberman et al., 2019; Wagner et al., 2012; Welborn & Lieberman, 2015). Second, the abstract concept of 'the average person' is far less concrete than the identities of our intimate partners. That said, partners are more emotionally salient than average people, who do not evoke strong feeling states. These potential psychological confounds could also have driven participants' distinct pattern of partner (versus average person) comparisons on the Social Comparison Task. Finally, the DLPFC and DMPFC activity could have reflected more extensive, deliberative processing among more thoughtful, less IPA-prone individuals, or dimensions of the traits themselves could be reflected in this heightened activation (see Elder et al., 2023). Future work is needed to isolate selfsuperiority motives from these extraneous variables.

Mentalizing-related activation was especially pronounced when participants evaluated their partner's negative (versus positive) traits. Given the greater evaluative weight attributed to negative information (Norris, 2021), this pattern could reflect greater cognitive effort to regulate social comparison biases. Although our data are unable to isolate whether participants were specifically enhancing their view of their partner and/or attenuating their own self-enhancement, this evidence may reflect motivated partner-enhancement, such that individuals are likely inclined to downplay their partner's shortcomings by engaging more cognitive resources to protect both their partner's image and the relationship. This hypothesis remains untested and future research should examine whether greater perspective-taking is engaged during negative, versus positive, partner comparisons.

Neural Links to IPA Perpetration

Our investigation hinged critically upon whether the brain activity during partner comparisons would be related to IPA perpetration. We found that DLPFC and DMPFC activity corresponding to the target × valence interaction (partner > average person; negative > positive) on the SCT task was related to greater IPA perpetration. This link was robust to whether history of IPA perpetration was scored in a dichotomous or more continuous fashion. Articulating this neural activity in an ANOVA and contrast-based framework allowed us to identify that IPA perpetration was negatively linked to DLPFC/DMPFC activity especially for partner comparisons on positive (versus negative) traits. This result may seem at odds with the behavioral link between Partner-Positive judgments and a greater likelihood of having a history of prior IPA perpetration, yet the marginal p value of this effect (and the fact that it did not replicate when AWIRS scores were treated in a continuous manner) suggest that the inferential heft should be allocated to these neural findings and less upon the logistic regression results.

To the extent that these clusters of activity reflected perspective-taking with one's partner, this finding stands at odds with the notion that the added effortful mentalizing recruitment that partners receive, especially during negative trait judgments, were adaptive and promoted positive relationship outcomes. In light of this most recent finding, it may be that effortful mentalizing during such negatively-valenced partner comparisons may instead reflect a tendency to enhance the partner's negative traits and thus promote aggressive acts against this target. However, these psychological accounts rely on reverse inferences and remain to be supported by psychological investigations.

When we looked more specifically within this interactive effect, we found evidence that activity within this approximate DLPFC/DMPFC cluster had an effect that diverged based on valence. Specifically, we found that the association between activity in this region and IPA was reversed (i.e., negative) when making positive partner comparisons. This suggests that our interaction was driven by the uniqueness of this negative correlation within positively-valenced partner comparisons, which was absent from the other three conditions (i.e., it was not observed to emerge in negatively-valenced comparisons or for positively-valenced comparisons against the average person). As such, our results are consistent with a 'mentalizing boost' interpretation for negatively-valenced partner judgments. For positive partner comparisons, however, activity in the observed regions showed a negative correlation with IPA perpetration, suggesting that recruiting the mentalizing network for partners' positive traits may help people to view them in ways that reduce motivation to harm them. Future work should focus on the uniquely inhibitory role of perspective-taking with partners' positive traits in IPA perpetration.

Limitations

The current study has limitations that warrant consideration. First, the study relied on a self-report measure of IPA perpetration, which may have led to under-reporting of such a consequential and socially-undesirable behavior. However, we took steps to ensure that participants understood the significant efforts we would undertake to ensure their confidentiality (e.g., obtaining a Certificate of Confidentiality) and this may have mitigated these underreporting concerns. We also dichotomized this variable, a scoring decision that shed valuable variability and could have suppressed some associations in doing so. As another consequence of this binary approach our results reflect neural correlates of any reported history of IPA perpetration, rather than neural correlates that scaled with the extent or severity of IPA perpetration. Second, our findings were correlational and cross-sectional, which restricted the ability to draw causal inferences. Though it remains difficult to manipulate brain activity directly, efforts in this domain will be able to replace our correlational approach with a causal one. Further, our sample was limited in terms of minoritized populations of gender identity, sexual orientation, and relationship structure. As such, the findings may not generalize to couples outside of cisgender, heterosexual, or monogamous frameworks. Future studies should aim to include a broader range of relationship identities and configurations to ensure that conclusions are inclusive and representative of the diverse experiences within romantic relationships.

Additionally, the SCT may have been confounded by the abstractness versus concreteness of the two comparison targets (stranger versus partner, respectively). Future work should seek to disentangle these variables. Finally, our participants did not have substantially violent histories.

Recruiting participants with documented IPA perpetration might have strengthened or otherwise altered the study's findings by including those with stronger violent tendencies, yet the inclusion of such individuals would have been an unethical risk to participants who might be at exaggerated risk for violence.

Conclusions

Our findings suggest that activity in mentalizing and executive control brain networks during partner comparisons have meaningful correlations with IPA perpetration, depending on the valence of the trait under evaluation. We hope this context-dependent association will help us further the neuroscience of IPA perpetration, allowing us to develop biologically-informed theories and interventions that will highlight pathways to safer, healthier relationships.
