Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

The study revealed that when participants had no clue about the actor’s intention-whether to grasp the small or the large object-they simply followed the actor’s moving arm with their eyes, as it was the only informative element in the visual scene, until it reached one of the objects.

The participants were 44 male football players divided into two groups based on their skill level.

To provide a margin of safety beyond the calculated minimum of 31 participants, we recruited 44.

Participants (and parents / guardians where applicable) provided written informed consent to take part in the experiment and were free to withdraw at any time.

Participants were invited to the gymnasium where the experiment took place.

Participants were instructed to perform the task as quickly (i.e., minimizing the time between ball reception and the final pass) and as accurately (i.e., passing in the direction required by the running player’s movement) as possible.

First, the participant had to pass the ball to the passing player.

If the run was made to the left or right between the cones, the participant had to pass the ball between the two cones in the direction of the running player.

If the run was made towards the middle, the participant had to pass back to the passing player.

Conversely, the end of the exploratory phase was identified when participants completed their head movement, marked by the return of a clear image in the eyetracker recording.

Thirteen skilled male soccer players (Mage = 19.5 ± 2.1 years, 9 right-footed and 4 left-footed) were recruited for this crossover study; nine of them had also taken part in Experiment 1.

Participants signed informed consent to take part in the experiment and were free to withdraw at any time.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 128 papers with 246 instances of power statements, 203 were correctly detected (true positives), 22 were missed (false negatives) and 21 were incorrectly detected (false positives). Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Skillful performance requires picking up information at the right time to both accurately perform ongoing action and search for subsequent ones. The current experiment aimed to compare the visual exploratory activity (VEA) of skilled (n = 23) and novice (n = 21) players in a football-based dual task scenario that required to manage competing visual demands for ball manipulation and environmental scanning. Skilled players performed the task faster than novices and showed a more proactive VEA: they spent more time looking at cues related to future passing opportunities while novices looked more at cues related to ball reception. In addition, skilled players made longer scanning movements before receiving the ball, but they did not perform significantly more head movements than novices. Under mental fatigue, skilled participants preserved their VEA patterns but required longer visual fixations, suggesting a reduced rate of information processing. These findings suggest that expertise in VEA is characterized by the efficient allocation of visual attention rather than merely its quantity.

Where and When to Look:

How Expertise and Mental Fatigue Shape Visual Exploration in a Football Task

We live in a world full of action opportunities, requiring continuous arbitration between alternative courses of action (Cisek & Kalaska, 2010; Pezzulo & Cisek, 2016). When performing dual taskssuch as receiving an object from one person and passing it to another or driving on a crowded highway while searching for an exit-we must also arbitrate between their competing visual demands, such as looking at the first versus the second person, or at other cars versus traffic signs. Beyond these everyday activities, most skilled behaviors, such as sports, also involve competing visual demands. For example, in soccer and basketball, when receiving the ball from a teammate and launching it to another; or in car and motorcycle racing, when driving while also remaining aware of the position and intentions of other drivers. This prompts the question of what guides visual exploration in such challenging conditions (Hayhoe & Ballard, 2005).

Decades of research have shown that skilled motor performance relies on internal forward models that support the prediction of action consequences and the planning of accurate movements (Friston et al., 2010; Kawato, 1999; Parr et al., 2022; Wolpert et al., 1995). Crucially, the predictions generated by these models become increasingly uncertain over time, requiring calibration through external observations. Various theoretical models propose that it is this uncertainty that drives gaze toward informative locations-gathering observations to reduce the model's uncertainty (Friston et al., 2012; Itti & Baldi, 2009). Conversely, when models generate highly confident predictions, no informationseeking behavior is required, since information that can be predicted with high confidence does not need to be attended to.

Extending this line of thought, it has been proposed that when two internal models are active simultaneously-for example, one predicting the movements of the person handing over an object and the other predicting the movements of the person to whom the object must be passed-the model with greater uncertainty demands more information and thus directs attention to its own most informative location (Rothkopf & Ballard, 2010; Donnarumma et al., 2017). For instance, when the movement of the first person is predictable, gaze can shift early to the second person. In contrast, when the first person's movement is difficult to predict, it must be monitored continuously.

Previous empirical studies have examined the relationship between predictability and gaze dynamics, revealing both anticipatory and information-gain-driven patterns of eye movements. In one experiment, participants observed an actor reaching for one of two objects (a large and a small one), either with or without hand preshaping (Ambrosini et al., 2015). The study revealed that when participants had no clue about the actor's intention-whether to grasp the small or the large object-they simply followed the actor's moving arm with their eyes, as it was the only informative element in the visual scene, until it reached one of the objects. Conversely, when they could infer the actor's intention from the hand preshape, they made anticipatory saccades to the to-be-grasped object, since the moving hand no longer offered additional information once the goal had been inferred. A computational model of the task successfully replicated these findings, based on the assumption that information gain guides eye movements (Donnarumma et al., 2017). Other studies have shown that eye movements during reading and language processing typically land on informative locations while skipping predictable text (Donnarumma et al., 2023; Levy, 2008). In addition, Sullivan et al. (2012) and Johnson et al. (2014) demonstrated that in dual-task paradigms, where multiple tasks are ongoing simultaneously, the allocation of visual attention is dynamically modulated by the growth of uncertainty across tasks.

Specifically, the predictive accuracy of the internal model for one task determines the temporal threshold for attentional disengagement, allowing a shift of resources to another task module. This continuous process enables the minimization of uncertainty across task modules, thereby fulfilling the overarching demands of the dual task. Another study using virtual reality settings to investigate how participants search for task-relevant objects revealed that gaze behavior is systematically shaped by both the structure of the environment and the observer's task goals, supporting the view that visual exploration is actively controlled to reduce uncertainty in a goal-directed manner (Rothkopf et al., 2007). Yet another line of research addressed skilled performance in dynamic tasks and showed that participants often exhibit "justin-time" gaze behavior-fixating on task-relevant elements only at the moment when information is needed to guide ongoing or upcoming actions. For instance, Ballard et al. (1997) demonstrated that, in a block-copying task, participants looked at each block only immediately before they needed to act upon it, rather than memorizing all block positions in advance. Similarly, in more recent work, Keshava et al. (2024) observed that skilled athletes tracking a moving ball timed their gaze to extract critical information just prior to making an interceptive movement, rather than continuously monitoring the ball throughout its flight. Taken together, these studies emphasize the efficiency and adaptiveness of visual attention, showing that gaze is deployed strategically-driven by task demands and the need to minimize uncertainty at key decision points.

Visual search strategies have been studied extensively in sports, too, as they are important for skilled athletes' effective and accurate decisions in performance context (D. Y. Mann et al., 2007). The sports domain is particularly interesting to study the relations between predictability, uncertainty and visual strategies, since athletes are assumed to have superior internal models that generate accurate predictions (Harris et al., 2022; Loffing & Cañal-Bruland, 2017). In keeping, skilled athletes are able to pickup more reliable cues from their surroundings to predict unfolding events, such as when anticipating the side of the kick in a penalty task (Savelsbergh et al., 2002), when predicting the ball trajectory in baseball (Land & McLeod, 2000; D. L. Mann et al., 2013), the success of basketball shots (Aglioti et al., 2008) and the direction of the ball in penalty kicks in soccer (Tomeo et al., 2013). Given these premises, it is expected that athletes' skill level influences the way they visually explore the environment (D. L. Mann et al., 2019).

However, visual search strategies of skilled athletes have long been investigated in laboratory settings, generally involving watching videos of specific sporting situations (Kredel et al., 2017). Such methods, although enabling high experimental control, distort information-movement coupling usually experienced by athletes in performance context (Fajen et al., 2009) and may affect their visual search strategies (Dicks et al., 2010).

A more recent research trend investigates visual search strategies by analysing football players' whole-body behavior during real games, to identify visual exploratory activity (VEA, also called scanning) (Jordet, 2005; Jordet et al., 2013 Jordet et al., , 2020)). Originally grounded in James Gibson 's theory of affordances (J. J. Gibson, 1966), VEA reflects players' activity to search for and specify affordances in their surroundings -or action opportunities, such as, in soccer, passing opportunities to teammates, carrying the ball to a free space of the field, or protecting the ball from the threat of an opponent (Jordet, 2005; Van Andel et al., 2019). The highly dynamic nature of the game of football (and of other team sports) requires players to not limit their search for affordances to the amplitude of the available visual field in front of them, but to also look at what is happening in their back (therefore covering all around them at 360°) -therefore requiring coordination of the eye, head and body movements to pick up information in the environment (Franchak, 2019; E. J. Gibson, 1988; J. J. Gibson, 1966). In keeping, VEA is not limited to eye movements and has been defined as:

A body and/or head movement in which the player's face is actively and temporarily directed away from the ball, seemingly with the intention of looking for teammates, opponents or other environmental objects or events, relevant to perform a subsequent action with the ball. (Jordet et al., 2013, p.2) Besides, VEA is conceived as a means to engage in the preparation and prospective control of future actions, that is "the means by which actors adapt behavior in advance to the constraints and behavioral opportunities in the environment" (Fajen et al., 2009, p. 88).

The original method to investigate VEA consist in annotating players' head movements during the 10-s before ball reception and the actions performed with the ball (Aksum, Pokolm, et al., 2021; Jordet et al., 2013 Jordet et al., , 2020;; McGuckian et al., 2018; Pokolm et al., 2022). Results have shown that head movement frequency was affected by various game constraints such as the player's role, the pressure of the defending opponent, or the location of the player on the pitch (Jordet et al., 2020). VEA was also associated with on-ball performance with higher frequency of high movements being associated with higher pass completion percentage (Jordet et al., 2013). In addition, head movement frequency was higher when action direction was in direction of the opposite goal than sideward of toward player's own goal (Jordet et al., 2013 (Jordet et al., , 2020) ) and when the action type was passing than dribbling or finishing (Jordet et al., 2020). In summary, performing a high frequency of head movement was deemed to be the mark of the most skilled players; therefore, various methodologies aimed at increasing players' VEA, for example through imagery (Jordet, 2005; Pocock et al., 2019).

Beyond the frequency of head movements, various studies investigated the timing of VEA relative to ball reception (McGuckian et al., 2018 (McGuckian et al., , 2019;; Van Andel et al., 2019). In a laboratory task, head movement frequency before gaining possession of the ball was negatively correlated with the response time of the participants, whereas head movement frequency when in ball possession was positively correlated with the response time (McGuckian et al., 2019). A study from Van Andel et al. (2019) proposed that VEA is used for different purposes according to when it occurs relative to action onset. More specifically, they proposed that early VEA would aim at searching for the different available opportunities (Exploration for Orientation) whereas late VEA would aim at specifying the selected affordance ("Exploration for Specification"). These two functions were hypothesized to be associated with different VEA patterns, notably regarding the acceleration profile of the head movements as exploring for orientation would aim for discovering the surrounding environment while exploration for specification would aim to fixate a specific location.

A recent eye-tracking study performed by Aksum, Brotangen et al. (2021) suggested that performing VEA between touches of the ball by teammates or when the ball trajectory could be anticipated would be an optimal strategy. However, this strategy demands athletes to capture clues to anticipate coming events, based on information pickup before initiating VEA. To adjust the balance between the two goals when players are about to receive the ball, it could be suggested that players would optimize their VEA to minimize its cost in terms of effort, that is minimizing the time looking away from the ball to control its reception in case of passing and to track the direction of the game, but looking away for a sufficiently long period of time to assess three types of information: namely, the location of free space to move, the availability of a teammate, or the absence of a relevant opportunity. As emphasized by the coaches interviewed in Eldridge et al. (2023), perceiving such information enables skilled players to make better decisions and manage the rhythm of the game.

Together, this body of work supports the perspective that visual exploration patterns are not merely reactive responses to salient stimuli, but proactive strategies governed by internal models and shaped by the informational requirements of complex, often overlapping tasks. Despite these advancements, some open questions remain. First, it is unclear whether skill level (as a proxy for superior internal models and predictive capabilities) influences the choice of anticipatory versus reactive VEAs in realistic settings, in which multiple tasks pose competing informational demands. Second, it is unclear whether taxing participants' cognitive processing -for example, by inducing mental fatigue (Hockey, 2011; Kool & Botvinick, 2014) -influences their VEAs or only lower levels of perceptual processing.

In this study, we performed two experiments that address the above questions. In the first experiment, skilled and novice soccer players were engaged in a sequential task in which they had to receive the ball from one teammate ("passing player") and then pass it to another teammate in movement ("running player"). This dual task poses competing informational demands, as it requires maintaining information coupling with the ball for controlling its reception, while also visually exploring the movements of the second teammate to prepare passing the ball. Therefore, it presents an interesting trade-off, since scanning the movements of the running player while receiving the ball affords a better preparation of the passing action but at the cost of losing information about the incoming ball. We reasoned that since skilled players have better predictive models compared to novice players, which plausibly afford a better prediction of the ball and of the other players, the two groups should show distinct (more anticipatory versus more reactive) VEAs. Specifically, while receiving the ball, skilled players should look early on at the target necessary to specify the future action (i.e., the running player), whereas novices should look longer at the targets necessary to specify the current receiving action (i.e., the passing player and the ball). Conversely, novices should initially focus on the ball that they need to receive and look for passing opportunities only after receiving it. To further increase the uncertainty of the task and prevent participants from relying on a simple binary elimination strategy between left and right, a third, central run direction was introduced. Runs to the left or right were designed to resemble a teammate's run to offer a passing option in open space. The central run direction, while still performed by the same teammate throughout the task (i.e., the running player), was conceptually inspired by situations in matchplay where an opponent's press restricts forward passing options, prompting a safe return pass to retain possession rather than risk losing the ball (i.e., passing the ball back to the passing player). We therefore expected skilled players, who could anticipate this requirement before ball reception based on their anticipatory VEA, to identify the running player's central trajectory earlier than novices and thus perform the pass in the correct direction more rapidly. Accordingly, we predicted that the performance advantage of skilled over novice players would be even more pronounced in the middle condition than in the left/right conditions.

In the second experiment, skilled players performed the same task under two experimental conditions, with or without mental fatigue. This design allows us to assess whether mental fatigue impairs strategic aspects of participants' visual processing (e.g., when they collect information about the running player) or only low-level aspects of information processing (e.g., the time required to process useful information). We reasoned that if mental fatigue decreases the accuracy of the internal models and predictive strategies used by skilled participants, they should adopt the reactive VEAs similar to those used by novices. Conversely, if mental fatigue only affects low level aspects of participants' information processing, their visual exploration patterns should be similar in the two conditions.

Experiment 1 Method

Transparency and Openness

These studies adhere to the transparency standards outlined in the Journal Article Reporting Standards (JARS; Appelbaum et al., 2018). We report all data exclusions, manipulations, and measures.

Studies were not pre-registered. Sample size for Experiment 1 was determined based on an a priori power analysis using G*Power version 3.1.9.6 (Faul et al., 2007). Data were analyzed using R version 4.3.2 (R Core Team, 2023), with the following statistical packages: dplyr version 1.1.4 (Wickham et al., 2023) for data manipulation, stringr version 1.5.1 (Wickham, 2023b) for string processing, tidyverse version 2.0.0 (Wickham et al., 2019) for integrated data science tools, effectsize version 1.0.0 (Ben-Shachar et al., 2020) for partial eta squared calculation, forcats version 1.0.0 (Wickham, 2023a) for factor-level handling, and ggplot2 version 3.5.0 (Wickham, 2016) for data visualization. Statistical analyses were conducted either using JASP version 0.18.3 (JASP Team, 2024), or R version 4.3.2 (R Core Team, 2023).

Data are available on the project's Open Science Framework (OSF) repository (https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/FKRDS).

Participants

The participants were 44 male football players divided into two groups based on their skill level.

The skilled group (n = 23) was composed of players aged 17-22 years (MSkilled-Age = 17.87 years, SD = 1.58), with more than 10 years of football experience (MSkilled-YearsOfExperience = 11.04 years, SD = 2.98). Among skilled players, 16 were right-footed and 7 were left-footed. All skilled players either competed in the best French youth league or played at a minimum in the 5th French division for senior players. They were volunteers from the elite group of a professional club academy and from the university football team of the University of Rouen Normandy. The novice group (n = 21) included players aged 17-30 years (MNovices-Age = 21.95 years, SD = 3.58), with less than 3 years of football experience (MNovices-YearsOfExperience = 1.00 year, SD = 1.38) and played exclusively in recreational leagues. Among novices, 17 were right-footed and 4 were left-footed.

Novices were volunteer students from the University of Rouen Normandy. Data for Experiment 1 were collected in 2023.

An a priori power analysis was conducted using G*Power 3.1 (Faul et al., 2007) to determine the required sample size. The analysis indicated that a minimum of 31 participants would be required to detect an effect size of f² = 0.35 (α = 0.05, 1 -β = 0.80) in our linear mixed model design with skill level (2 levels) × run direction (3 levels) as fixed effects. This effect size corresponds to Cohen's (2013) conventional benchmark for a large effect, and the choice was informed by the literature and by taskrelated considerations. First, the large difference in skill level between our two groups (professional academy and elite youth players compared to novices) was expected to produce a large effect, consistent with the moderate-to-large effect sizes reported in the meta-analysis by Mann et al. (2007). Second, our task is more sport-specific and closer to the participants' domain of expertise than most of the paradigms included in that meta-analysis, which we expected to further increase the magnitude of the group difference. To provide a margin of safety beyond the calculated minimum of 31 participants, we recruited 44. This final sample size corresponds to a sensitivity of f² = 0.24.

Participants (and parents / guardians where applicable) provided written informed consent to take part in the experiment and were free to withdraw at any time. This research adhered to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the CERSTAPS Research Ethics Committee (Opinion number: IRB00012476-2023-13-01-217).

Protocol

Participants were invited to the gymnasium where the experiment took place. Upon arrival, they completed a questionnaire providing information about their football profile and vision quality.

Instructions and informed consent forms were provided to the participants in advance of the data collection.

The football task involved 3 participants: the "participant", whose behavior and eye movements were recorded and two teammates, which played the roles of "passing player" and "running player", which were not equipped with mobile eye-tracking systems.

The participant was equipped with the Tobii Pro Glasses 3 mobile eye-tracking system (Tobii AB, 2020) and the calibration procedure was performed, by following Tobii's recommendations.

The task was explained and demonstrated to the three participants by the experimenters.

Participants were instructed to perform the task as quickly (i.e., minimizing the time between ball reception and the final pass) and as accurately (i.e., passing in the direction required by the running player's movement) as possible. Then, the participant performed a block of 25 trials. The first 4 were used as warm-up trials and were not analyzed. The recording quality of the eye-tracker and the point of gaze were checked every 5 trials by asking the participant to look at specific targets in the gymnasium. If a trial was judged by the experimenters as poorly executed (for example, if the initial pass required a side movement of the participant), it was noted for removal from the analysis and repeated at the end of the block.

Some individuals who played the role of "participants" also subsequently volunteered to play the role of "teammates" (but we did not allow individuals to be teammates before they were participants, in order to ensure that they had no prior exposure to the task when they were "participants").

Dual Task Design

We developed a dual task that emulated a typical match-play scenario in football and required participants to engage in VEA. The task involved three participants: the participant in the middle, who wore the mobile eye-tracking device and whose behavior was investigated; the passing player, who was positioned in front of the participant at 6 meters; and the running player, who was positioned behind the participant at 12 meters (see Figure 1).

Data Collection

Gaze Behavior. Participants' gaze behavior was recorded with the Tobii Pro Glasses 3 eye-tracker (Tobii AB, 2020). This device is a mobile binocular eye-tracker that tracks visual behavior at a sampling rate of 100 Hz using multiple infrared illuminators (8 per eye) and cameras (2 per eye). It also captures the external scene at 25 Hz using a high-definition camera (1920 x 1080 pixels). Eye-tracking calibration and data acquisition were conducted using the Tobii Glasses 3 software (Tobii AB, 2019).

Before each participant's block (i.e., the block of 25 trials described above), the eye-tracker was calibrated with the participant standing at arm's length from the calibration target. The accuracy of the gaze tracking was then verified by asking the participant to fixate again on the target as it was moved within the scene camera's field of view. If the calibration failed or the point of gaze did not overlap with the target, the procedure was repeated until the gaze location overlapped with the target.

The eye-tracking recordings were subsequently processed using Tobii Pro Lab software (Tobii AB, 2021), applying the "raw" data filter, to perform frame-by-frame mapping of the gaze data.

Performance. To assess task success and measure performance times, we used the recordings captured by the Tobii Pro Glasses 3 system. Task response start and end points were annotated using Dartfish video analysis software (Dartfish, 2024). We defined the task response start point as the first frame showing the participant's foot contacting the ball following the reception of the return pass from the passing player. The task response endpoint was marked by the first frame after the participant's final ball contact during the pass to the running player, constituting the task response.

Data Analysis

Gaze Behavior. The visual exploratory activity of the participants was analyzed by measuring the number of performed exploratory phases (scans), their duration, and the gaze dynamics. In our study, an exploratory phase (scan) was characterized by any head/body movement performed at any point during the trial to collect information about the running player's running direction. These exploratory phases were identified using external scene video recordings from the eye-tracker. The start of an exploratory phase was identified when participants initiated head movement, characterized by motion blur in the eyetracker recording due to head displacement. Conversely, the end of the exploratory phase was identified when participants completed their head movement, marked by the return of a clear image in the eyetracker recording.

Gaze behavior was quantified using two primary metrics: the number of visual fixations per second and the mean fixation duration (ms). Following the recommendations for dynamic visual tasks (Holmqvist et al., 2011), a visual fixation was defined as a period with a minimum duration of 50ms (i.e. 5 consecutive frames), during which the participant's gaze was directed toward one of three areas of interest: (i) the ball, (ii) the passing player, or (iii) the running player. Thus, what we define as a fixation is a period of gaze stability relative to an area of interest (i.e., a period during which the gaze remains within the boundaries of the area of interest), which differs from the definition of fixation as a period of eye stability relative to the head (Hessels et al., 2018 (Hessels et al., , 2024)).

Gaze behavior related to task-relevant AOIs was quantified using dwell time. Six AOIs were defined: (i) the ball, (ii) the pass area, (iii) the passing player, (iv) the running player, (v) the target area, and (vi) other irrelevant areas of the scene. Screen captures from the scene camera of the eye-tracker showing the defined AOIs are available as supplementary materials in the OSF repository.

Using these gaze annotations, we analyzed the visual attention patterns of each participant across all trials. We calculated the average frame-by-frame probability that participants gazed toward cues related to the ball, which included the following AOIs: (i) the ball, (ii) the pass area, and (iii) the passing player. We also measured the probability that participants gazed toward cues related to the run direction, which included: (iv) the running player and (v) the target area. For the run direction category, we additionally included the entirety, from the start until the end, of the scans performed as cues related to the run direction, as these scans reflected participants' intentions to seek information about the running player's movement.

To assess the reliability of the coding method, we randomly selected five participants and reanalyzed all their trials three months after the first coding. Cohen's Kappa showed almost perfect agreement (κ = 0.928) between the two coding sessions (McHugh, 2012).

Performance. Task performance was assessed by measuring the accuracy and the time to perform the task. A trial was considered successful if the participant made a pass in the direction required by the running player's run. The time to perform the task was defined as the duration between the reception of the pass from the passing player and the participant's final ball contact (i.e., the last pass of the trial).

Statistical Analysis

The effects of participants' skill level and of the direction of the run on task success were assessed using two separate Chi-square tests of independence.

Additionally, we employed linear mixed models (LMMs) to examine the influence of skill level and run direction on task performance and gaze behavior. Specifically, the participants skill level and the direction of the run were included as fixed effects variables and the participants' ID as random intercepts in the models. Post-hoc analyses were conducted using Bonferroni-adjusted p-values.

A multivariate analysis of variance (MANOVA) was performed to analyze the dwell time spent in each area of interest, with skill level as the independent variable. The dwell time spent in other irrelevant areas of the scene was excluded from the analysis to avoid the linear dependency arising from dwell time percentages summing to 100%. Some dependency between the remaining AOIs may nevertheless persist, which should be considered a limitation of this analysis.

Effect sizes were reported as Cramer's V for Chi-square tests of independance, partial eta-squared (ηp²) for LMMs, and Wilk's Λ for the MANOVA.

Cohen's d value was calculated for each eye-tracking data point on a period of 2 seconds before and after the reception of the ball to compare the gaze dynamics of skilled players and novices. The threshold value of 0.5 over a period of more than 5 data points (i.e., 50ms) was chosen as a reflection of a medium effect size (Cohen, 2013) to identify the key time periods where the gaze location differed between the two groups of participants.

Data treatments were performed using RStudio software (R Core Team, 2023) and statistical analyses were conducted using JASP software (JASP Team, 2024).

Summary of the Main Results of Experiment 1

Taken together, the results of Experiment 1 show that as expected, skilled participants performed the task faster than novice participants. Despite both groups showing a similar number of scans, skilled participants showed longer durations of their first scan and looked longer at cues regarding the running player and target area, as opposed to participants that looked longer at the ball and the passing player.

Interestingly, our results reveal that skilled and novice participants used distinct VEAs. Skilled participants first made an anticipatory scan at cues related to the running players' movement direction before ball reception, then to the incoming ball. Then, when the running player's direction was to the left or right, skilled participants looked back at the running player just before ball reception, in order to correctly pass the ball to him. They did not look back at the running player when the direction of the run was to the middle, because they had to pass the ball to the passing player instead. Furthermore, skilled participants rarely looked back at the ball after its reception. Conversely, novice participants used a different and more reactive VEA: they focused on the ball almost until its reception and then, when they were about to receive the ball, they looked at the movements of the running player. Finally, they looked back at the ball shortly after its reception, across all conditions.

In sum, this experiment shows that skilled and novice players adopted distinct VEAs -more anticipatory versus more reactive -during the dual task. A possible explanation is that skilled participants can focus rapidly on the second task, because their internal model of ball passing permits predicting the ball trajectory with low uncertainty, whereas novice participants have to look at the incoming ball longer in order to reduce their uncertainty -and can only focus on the second task afterwards.

Experiment 2

Experiment 1 revealed two distinct (anticipatory versus more reactive) VEAs in skilled and novice players engaged in the dual task football paradigm and suggests that the difference might be due to the different accuracy of internal models and predictive strategies across the two participant groups. In Experiment 2, we examine whether and how taxing skilled participants' cognitive processing with mental fatigue affects their VEAs in the dual task. We reasoned that if mental fatigue decreases the accuracy of internal models and predictive strategies used by skilled participants, they should adopt the reactive VEAs that are similar to those used by novices. Rather, if mental fatigue only disrupts low level perceptual processing, for example, the rate at which information is extracted from the sensory signal, skilled participants should use the same anticipatory strategies identified in Experiment 1 -and the mental fatigue effects should only become apparent in factors related to lower-level visual processing, such as longer durations of visual fixations and scanning movements.

To disambiguate between these two hypotheses, two groups of skilled players performed the same dual task paradigm in football presented in Experiment 1, but in two crossover conditions: either after performing a high-load cognitive task -the Time Load Dual Back -to induce mental fatigue (fatigue condition), or after watching an emotionally neutral documentary as a control condition (control condition).

Method

Participants

Thirteen skilled male soccer players (Mage = 19.5 ± 2.1 years, 9 right-footed and 4 left-footed) were recruited for this crossover study; nine of them had also taken part in Experiment 1. Most of the players were recruited from a French professional club academy and played in the top French Under-19 Youth league, while others were from the university football team of University of Rouen Normandy. All players had been playing football for at least 10 years. Participants signed informed consent to take part in the experiment and were free to withdraw at any time. Three more players withdrew between the different days of data collection due to injuries. Data for Experiment 2 were collected in 2024.

Mental Fatigue Induction

To induce a sensation of mental fatigue, we used a cognitive task called Time Load Dual Back (TLoadDBack) developed by Borragán et al. (2017). This task combines a classic working memory updating task of the N-Back type (Kirchner, 1958) with an inference task (parity decision task). Its effectiveness in inducing a high cognitive load has been demonstrated in several studies (Holgado et al., 2023; O'Keeffe et al., 2020).

Eight letters (A, C, T, L, N, E, U, P) and eight digits (1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 7, 8, 9) were presented alternately at the center of a 21.5-inch screen (HP ProOne 600 G6 22 All-in-One PC, HP Inc.; refresh rate: 60 Hz), in Arial font, size 120. Participants were instructed to press the "Space" key with their left hand if the letter presented on the screen was identical to the previous one, and to indicate whether the digits were odd or even by pressing the "1" key (Odd) or the "2" key (Even) on the numeric keypad with their right hand. These keys were covered with distinct colored stickers (White for the "Space" key, Blue for the "1" key, and Green for the "2" key) to avoid any potential conflict between the keyboard key and parity judgments.

The stimuli were presented in a pseudo-random order, continuously, in blocks of 30 letters and 30 digits. The stimulus presentation duration was individualized for each participant based on a pre-test (Borragán et al., 2017).

The pre-test, conducted prior to the main experiment, required participants to perform at their maximum cognitive load level. The goal was to measure the shortest stimulus presentation duration while maintaining an accuracy level above 85% for an entire block. The pre-test included an introduction to help participants understand the task, followed by the pre-test itself. The first block started with a stimulus presentation duration of 1200 milliseconds, with an increment of 100 ms between each block, until the accuracy of a block fell below 85%. Participants took a short break between each block.

The experimental task (TLoadDBack) lasted approximately 16 minutes. In contrast, during the control condition, participants watched an emotionally neutral documentary of equivalent duration.

Subjective Ratings

Subjective ratings of mental fatigue were assessed using a screen-based Visual Analogue Scale (VAS), following established methodologies (O' Keeffe et al., 2020; Smith et al., 2019). Participants were asked the following question: "How are you feeling right now? Please click on the scale to indicate your feeling of fatigue". The scale was anchored at one end on the left with "Not mentally tired at all" and on the other end on the right with "Extremely tired mentally".

The sensation of mental fatigue was measured before and after the pre-test to familiarize participants with the scale, and before and after each condition.

Protocol

Instructions and informed consent forms were provided to the participants in advance of the data collection.

Participants were first invited to the laboratory and asked to complete two questionnaires: the Fatigue Severity Scale (Krupp et al., 1989), the St. Mary's Hospital Sleep Questionnaire (Ellis et al., 1981).

After completing the questionnaires, participants indicated their sensation of mental fatigue on the screen-based VAS.

Participants were then randomly assigned to either perform the Time Load Dual Back task or watch an emotionally neutral documentary for 16 minutes. After this, they again reported their mental fatigue on the VAS. Subsequently, participants were taken to the adjacent gymnasium where the football task was conducted. They were equipped with the Tobii Pro Glasses 3 eye-tracker (Tobii AB, 2020) and the calibration procedure was performed, following Tobii's recommendations. Eye-tracking and video recordings from the gymnasium were then started.

The same football dual task paradigm used in Experiment 1 was explained orally by the experimenters with the same instructions, and accompanied by a demonstration.

The accuracy of the eye-tracker was verified every 5 trials by having participants look at fixed targets in the gymnasium. If the video quality or the point of gaze were not concordant, the calibration was conducted again, and the 5 trials between checks were repeated at the end of the block.

Due to the complexity of scheduling constraints among this category of athletes, participants were invited to return within a period of two to four weeks following their initial session to complete the same procedure, but under the alternative condition to which they had not been assigned during the first data collection day.

Dual Task Design

The football dual task paradigm employed in Experiment 2 was identical to that used in Experiment 1 (see Figure 1), simulating a characteristic match-play scenario in football that necessitates participants to engage in VEA.

Data Collection

Gaze Behavior. Gaze behaviour of participants was recorded using the same mobile binocular eye-tracker device Tobii Pro Glasses 3 (Tobii AB, 2020) as in Experiment 1. Calibration procedure and acquisition were managed with Tobii Glasses 3 software (Tobii AB, 2019).

The calibration procedure was the same as that used in Experiment 1, following Tobii's recommendations.

Eye-tracking recordings were then processed using Tobii Pro Lab software (Tobii AB, 2021), applying the 'raw' data filter to perform frame-by-frame mapping of the gaze data.

Performance. We used the recording system in the gym where the dual task in football was performed to measure the duration and success of task performance. This recording system included three high-definition cameras (1280 x 720 pixels) with a sampling rate of 25 Hz, which allowed the task to be recorded from multiple angles: one camera provided a top-down view, and two cameras provided side views. Task response start and end frames were accurately annotated using Dartfish video analysis software (version 10, Dartfish, 2024).

We defined, as for Experiment 1, the start frame of the task response as the moment when the participant's foot made contact with the ball after receiving the return pass from the passing player. The end of the task response was defined as the first frame when the participant's foot clearly no longer touched the ball while making a pass to the running player in the left and right conditions, or to the passing player in the middle condition.

Data Analysis

To assess the homogeneity of participants' initial levels of fatigue sensation between the different experimental conditions, we used the Fatigue Severity Scale (FSS; Krupp et al., 1989), a validated instrument measuring the impact of fatigue on daily activities over the previous week.

The evolution of the sensation of mental fatigue was evaluated using both subjective and objective measures. Subjective assessment was based on a visual analog scale (VAS) administered before and after each experimental condition, i.e. before and after the Time Load Dual Back (TLDB) task, as well as before and after viewing the emotionally neutral documentary (control condition). This methodology enabled us to verify the similarity of initial mental fatigue states on participants' arrival before each experimental condition, and investigate the evolution of this mental fatigue sensation induced by each condition. Objective evaluation was based on analysis of performance on the TLDB task, measured by the percentage of correct responses per block of stimuli (30 digits and 30 letters).

As in Experiment 1, we use eye tracking to analyze the average number of visual fixations per second and the mean duration of visual fixations. As in Experiment 1, visual fixations were defined as periods of at least 50 ms during which participants' gaze was directed toward one of three AOIs: the ball, the passing player, or the running player.

Participants' performance (accuracy, time to perform the task), visual exploratory activity (number of scans, the duration of scans), and gaze behavior related to areas of interest (dwell time) were assessed using the same methods as definitions as in Experiment 1.

Statistical Analysis

Paired-sample t-tests were first conducted to test whether participants arrived at the same state of fatigue between the two conditions. They were therefore conducted on the number of hours slept and the sleep quality and satisfaction scores for the previous night, measured using items from the St. Mary questionnaire (Ellis et al., 1981), as well as on the impact of fatigue on daily activities during the last week, measured using the Fatigue Severity Scale (Krupp et al., 1989).

These paired samples t-tests were also conducted to control the different manipulations on the participants' feeling of mental fatigue. We conducted a paired samples t-test to compare mental fatigue sensations before each experimental condition (before the cognitive task and before the emotionally neutral documentary). We also performed a paired samples t-test after each experimental condition, as well as on the differences pre-post each experimental condition to verify a greater induction of mental fatigue sensation during the cognitive task, and therefore a higher mental fatigue sensation after this condition.

We employed a linear mixed model (LMM) to measure the evolution of performance per block of stimuli (30 digits and 30 letters) throughout the cognitive task. The position of the block relative to the total duration of the task was included as a fixed factor and participant ID as a random intercept in this model.

To assess the effects of mental fatigue and run direction on performance in the football task, we performed Fisher's exact tests, due to the very low number of missed trials, for task accuracy, and we also performed a LMM for time to perform the task. For this model, the fixed factors included were condition and run direction, and participants' ID was included as a random intercept. Post-hoc analyses were performed using Bonferroni-adjusted p-values.

Other LMMs were also performed to assess the effect of mental fatigue and run direction on variables related to gaze behaviour, i.e. the number of visual fixations per second, the average duration of visual fixations, and the duration of scans. In these models, the fixed factors included were condition and run direction, while participants' ID was included as a random intercept. Post-hoc analyses were performed using Bonferroni-adjusted p-values.

To measure the effects of mental fatigue and run direction on the number of scans performed per trial, we performed a generalized linear mixed model (GLMM) with a Poisson distribution and a logarithmic link. As with LMMs, we included condition and run direction as fixed factors, and participants' IDs as random intercept.

To analyse the time spent in each AOI (Dwell Time), we performed a multivariate analysis of variance (MANOVA), including condition and run direction as independent variables. As in Experiment 1, the dwell time spent in other irrelevant areas of the scene was excluded from the analysis.

To compare the visual patterns between the two conditions, we measured Cohen's d value for each eye-tracking frame, over a period extending from 2000 ms before and after the reception of the ball WHERE AND WHEN TO LOOK 29 during the passing player's return pass. To identify the crucial phases during which the visual patterns differed between the two conditions, we chose to select a period of more than 5 frames (i.e. 50 ms) during which Cohen's d was equal to or higher than 0.5, corresponding to a medium effect size (Cohen, 2013).

Effect sizes were reported as Cohen's d for paired samples t-tests, Pearson's r for Wilcoxon signedrank tests, partial eta-squared (ηp²) for LMMs, and Wilk's Λ for the MANOVA.

Data processing and the various statistical analyses were carried out using RStudio software (R Core Team, 2023).

Summary of the Main Results of Experiment 2

Taken together, the results of Experiment 2 show that skilled participants with or without mental fatigue use similar, anticipatory VEAs to address our dual task: i.e. they look at the running player before they receive the ball. However, participants in the fatigue condition made longer first scans in the fatigue condition. These results suggest that mental fatigue does not significantly affect the quality of the skilled participants' internal models and predictions, but only lower-level aspects of visual information processing, such as the time they require to extract information during visual scans.

General Discussion

Performing dual tasks in embodied contexts, such as receiving the ball from a teammate and subsequently passing it to another teammate in movement, requires arbitrating between alternative actions with competing visual demands; namely, looking at the incoming ball to ensure a good reception versus scanning the environment to infer the running direction of the teammate to which the ball has to be passed.

By analyzing players' performance and gaze under a dual task football scenario, this study aimed to address how people arbitrate between these two competing demands and the effects of their skill level and mental fatigue on the visual exploratory activities (VEAs) that they adopt.

Our results show key differences in VEA patterns between skilled and novice players in the dual task. Skilled players demonstrate a longer exploratory phase (800-ms compared to 600-ms for novices, p < .001; see Figure 5A) and more anticipatory patterns of gaze exploration. Crucially, they shift their gaze towards the running player's direction at an early stage of the task (approximately 1600-ms before they receive the ball), then look at the passing player, then orient themselves in the direction of the player to which they have to pass the ball shortly before they receive it (see Figure 7). We call the VEA of skilled participants "anticipatory" or "prospective", since they appear to prioritize collecting information about their second task (passing the ball) before they complete the first task (receiving the ball).

Novices, by contrast, exhibit longer gaze fixation on ball-related cues related to their first task (Figure 8) and initiate scans towards the running player's direction afterwards (Figure 7). We call the VEA of novice participants "reactive", since they appear to prioritize collecting information about their first task (receiving the ball) until they complete it -and about their second task (passing the ball) only afterwards. A possible explanation of this difference is that novices have poorer internal generative models that generate uncertain predictions about the trajectory and timing of the ball and hence require continuous intake of sensory observations to afford a correct ball reception. An alternative explanation is that novices require longer visual intake to process information and organize the motor response following each step of this football task (Klostermann et al., 2013).

Focusing on the middle condition of our scenario, which required passing the ball to the passing player instead of the running player, underscored additional differences in gaze dynamics and task performance between skilled and novice participants. Skilled players completed the task significantly faster in the middle condition (approximately 450-ms compared to 900-ms in the left and right conditions, p < .001; Figure 2). This improvement in efficiency stemmed from the fact that skilled (but not novice) players already knew that they had to pass the ball to the passing player, before receiving it. It is also worth noting that the exploratory phase of skilled players was approximately 75-ms longer in the middle condition (i.e. about 850-ms compared to 775-ms during the left and right conditions, p < .001; Figure 5A), suggesting that they extended their exploratory activity to ensure the direction of their teammate's movement. Conversely, novices did not exhibit this behavioral flexibility, performing the task in similar duration across all conditions (i.e., about 1200-ms, p > .415, Figure 2), and they maintained a similar duration of the exploratory phase across conditions (i.e., about 590-ms, p > .543, Figure 5A). It should be noted that decision accuracy was extremely high for both groups and across all running direction, suggesting that the task did not discriminate between groups on the basis of decision correctness, but rather on the efficiency with which that decision was performed.

Finally, our results show that skilled players adopted the same anticipatory VEAs even when mentally fatigued. The effects of mental fatigue were most evident on variables such as the duration of visual fixations and the length of the first scan that are plausibly more related to lower-level visual processing, compared to variables more related to strategic aspects, such as the decision to initiate a scan towards the running player before or after receiving the ball. This result strongly suggests that mental fatigue does not significantly affect the quality of the skilled participants' internal models and predictions, but only lower-level aspects of their visual information processing.

These results can be understood within a computational framework in which participants facing dual tasks like the one adopted in this study arbitrate between two internal models, one for the receiving task and one for the passing task (Ballard et al., 2013; Friston et al., 2010 Friston et al., , 2017;; Hayhoe & Ballard, 2014; Rothkopf & Ballard, 2010). Crucially, both models generate uncertain predictions and pose competing demands to visual attention, as they require collecting information about the incoming ball versus the position of the running player, to resolve their uncertainty. The key difference between skilled and novice players is the quality of their internal models. Skilled players can generate confident predictions about the first task (receiving the ball) even with little evidence; hence, they can rapidly turn their attention to the second task, even before the first task is completed. In contrast, novices have weaker generative models and require more visual feedback to successfully complete the first task -hence they can only turn their attention to the second task after they complete the first. The observed differences in VEAs between skilled players and novices therefore reflects the fact that resolving uncertainty about one task licenses allocating resources to the other task in an anticipatory manner, as also observed in other visual exploration studies that manipulated uncertainty levels (Ambrosini et al., 2015; Rothkopf et al., 2007; Rothkopf & Ballard, 2010).

The results of our study add to a large literature aiming to understand cognitive processing and visual exploration during everyday behavior in naturalistic settings, including sports, and the role of uncertainty and expertise in such settings (Fajen et al., 2009; Gordon et al., 2021; Hayhoe & Ballard, 2005; Maselli et al., 2023; McGuckian et al., 2019). Compared to the previous works, our study specifically addresses a dual task in a real, embodied setting (Experiment 1) and additionally it manipulates participants' mental fatigue (Experiment 2). The frequency of head turns observed in our first study was significantly lower than that observed in similar tasks, with most trials (72.61%) involving only a single head turn. This contrasts with McGuckian et al. (2019) study, where all participants (competitive-elite youth players) performed on average about two scans per second. This difference may be attributed to the high time pressure of our dual task paradigm and the lower level of uncertainty of the second task, where participants only had to understand the direction of movement of the running player. In contrast, in the McGuckian et al. (2019) study, participants did not have to control a real ball but had to monitor four screens to identify the available teammate. This distinction underscores the importance of time constraints and task demands in shaping visual exploration patterns.

Summing up, this study proposed a novel decision-making task that effectively enabled to capture the gaze dynamics of football players related to affordance selection. Our findings revealed distinct visual exploratory patterns between skilled players and novices, highlighting how experience supports the optimization of action through efficient gaze allocation. Skilled players performed the task faster than novices, skilled players made longer scanning movements before receiving the ball, and showed a more proactive VEA: they spent more time looking at cues related to future passing opportunities, even before completing the first task. In contrast, novices tended to focus predominantly on ball-related cues rather than looking for what to do next, suggesting a more reactive approach to task execution. Furthermore, our results revealed that when they are mentally fatigued, skilled participants do not change their VEA, but rather require more looking time, as if the rate of information processing was lowered. These findings suggest that skilled VEAs may reside in perceiving optimal moments to shift visual attention between taskrelated cues, and not only on the scanning frequency. Future research could build upon these findings by increasing the time pressure on performers during task completion and manipulating the level of uncertainty in the task modules, potentially further elucidating the efficacy of skilled players' gaze dynamics.
