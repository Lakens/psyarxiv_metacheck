About the personal-information flags. These are pattern matches, not confirmed disclosures, and they are tuned to over-report rather than miss something. Expect false positives:

  • Column names are matched against identifying words in English and other European languages (email, telefoonnummer, geboortedatum, …). A column called phone_number flags whether or not it holds one, and an empty or already-anonymised column flags on its name alone.
  • Values are matched against the shape of an identifier. An IP address pattern also matches a version string or a dotted numeric code; a card-number pattern requires both a real issuer prefix and a checksum, but a long numeric ID can still coincide.
  • Free text is flagged when a column holds long, mostly-distinct prose — the shape open-ended answers take. Most such columns contain no names at all.

A flag means look at this column. Where the data is already de-identified (an ip column reading Anonymized, a name column the authors emptied), no action is needed.

Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Abstract

The prevalence of maternal mental health problems seems to have increased since the start of the 21 st century. Several societal changes may have contributed to this increase, most notably increasing individualism, rising perfectionism and intensification of parenting norms. The current study examines how postpartum mental health and associated factors changed over time by comparing two birth cohorts collected fifteen years apart in the same city. The study found comparable feelings of anxiety and depression during the first six months postpartum among mothers in 2002-2006 and 2017-2021. For both cohorts, lower levels of social support and more perceived infant sleeping problems were associated with poorer postpartum mental health, whereas infant sleep characteristics (e.g. sleep duration) were not. The strength of these associations did not differ between cohorts. Although infant sleep characteristics were similar across cohorts, a higher proportion of mothers in the 2017-2021 cohort perceived their infant's sleep as problematic. This may reflect increasing expectations of parenthood in a changing sociocultural context. Our findings underscore the importance of understanding maternal mental health not as the result of individual vulnerabilities but in relation to the context of how parents experience everyday parenting challenges and the support available to them.

Several studies have examined changes in the prevalence of postpartum mental health problems since the start of the 21 st century. Studies using health records from the United States (U.S.), Sweden and Denmark showed upward trends in the prevalence of postpartum depression and other psychiatric disorders since the early 2000s [4] [5] [6] [7] . However, these trends may partly reflect increased awareness and changes in diagnostic practices, as a U.S. study using self-reported depressive symptoms found no increase between 2007 and 2018 26 . Less is known about whether average levels of postpartum mental health have changed across the broader population of mothers. Second, to our knowledge, no other studies have investigated how societal factors in the context of the postpartum period have changed over time. In particular, little is known about how parents' help-seeking and perceptions of infant behavior have changed across generations, all of which may reflect broader societal developments in individualism, perfectionism and parenting norms. To address these gaps, the current study examines generational differences in postpartum mental health in two large community samples using a dimensional rather than diagnostic approach to mental health. Second, the study analyzes how societal factors related to postpartum mental health have changed over time.

This Study

The study will use data from the Generation R (2002-2006) and Generation R Next Study (2017-2021).

By comparing two prospective birth cohorts collected fifteen years apart in the same city, the study can uniquely show how postpartum mental health, and associated factors have changed over a period of fifteen years. The main aim of the study is, therefore, to compare the postpartum mental health of mothers in the first six months postpartum in 2002-2006 (Generation R Study cohort) to mothers in 2017-2021 (Generation R Next Study cohort). Moreover, the study will examine cohort differences in levels of support, and in reported and perceived infant behavior. Next, we will examine how these factors are associated with postpartum mental health and whether their association with postpartum mental health differs across cohorts.

In line with previous studies, we expect poorer maternal mental health during the first six months postpartum among women who gave birth between 2017 and 2021 compared to those who gave birth between 2002 and 2006. With regard to factors associated with postpartum mental health, we expect similar levels of reported challenging infant behaviors across cohorts, while we expect mothers in the most recent cohort to perceive more difficulties in their infants' behavior. In addition, we expect a stronger association between postpartum mental health and measures of infant behavior (perceived and reported characteristics) in 2017-2021 compared to 2002-2006, in particular for perceived difficulties in infant behavior. Next, we expect that parents report less formal support in 2017-2021 compared to 2002-2006. In addition to these preregistered hypotheses, we explore whether the associations of formal support and perceived social support with postpartum mental health differ across cohorts.

Discussion

The current study found no difference between the postpartum mental health of mothers who gave birth in 2002-2006 versus mothers who gave birth in 2017-2021. Even though we did not find a difference in postpartum mental health over time, we did find elevated depression levels for 8% of women in both cohorts. In both cohorts, perceived social support was moderately associated with postpartum mental health, whereas formal support was weakly associated with postpartum mental health. In addition, a slightly higher proportion of mothers reported using formal support in the most recent cohort. Although reported infant sleep behavior was similar across cohorts, a higher proportion of mothers in the 2017-2021 cohort perceived their infant's sleep as problematic. Perceived infant sleeping problems were weakly to moderately related to postpartum mental health. The study found no evidence that the strength of the associations between postpartum mental health and any of the examined factors differed between cohorts.

We did not find a difference in postpartum feelings of depression and anxiety between 2002-2006 and 2017-2021. This contrasts with our hypothesis, and with earlier reports of increasing postpartum mental health problems during the first decades of the 21 st century [4] [5] [6] [7].

Studies that report an increase in prevalence of mental health problems, mostly assessed mental health through diagnoses (i.e., relevant ICD codes) or use of psychotropic medication.

However, another study reported a stable prevalence of self-reported elevated levels of postpartum depression among U.S. mothers from 2007 to 2018 26 . One possible explanation for these findings lies in how postpartum mental health was assessed. It might be that studies drawing from clinical data, are more indicative of changes in awareness of postpartum mental health problems, help-seeking behavior, or diagnostic and screening practices 5, 27, 28.

Population-based cohort studies using validated self-report measures of mental health (such as the current study) are less likely to be affected by these changes because data collection is not dependent on parents' help-seeking behavior or on national screening practices 29 . These studies may thus be better suited to capture population-level changes in postpartum mental health.

The specific research context might also help explain why we do not find a difference in postpartum mental health between cohorts despite reports of increasing mental health problems in other populations, including the Dutch general population during the same period 57 . In the Netherlands, screening and treatment of mental health problems have become more integrated in standard perinatal care since the 2010s 60, 61 . Additionally, public health campaigns have aimed to improve health literacy among prospective parents, and parental leave for partners has been extended 61, 62 . These changes may have influenced postpartum mental health, although their effects cannot be established from our data. Similar developments in perinatal care and parental support may also have occurred in other countries. In Sweden, for example, perinatal screening of depressive symptoms has expanded over the past decades which might be related to the slower increase in the incidence of perinatal depression compared to other psychiatric disorders in that country 4 . Together, these findings highlight the importance of considering differences in population, measurement, and healthcare context when interpreting trends in postpartum mental health.

Higher levels of perceived social support were associated with fewer feelings of depression and anxiety in both cohorts. We did not find a difference in the strength of this association across the two cohorts. This is in line with previous literature which reports that a lack of social support is consistently associated with a higher risk of postpartum feelings of depression and anxiety [13] [14] [15] . A comparable absence of cohort differences was reported in a study that compared the association between social support and antenatal depression in 2016 to 2021 63 . Thus, although changing parenting norms and expectations may have altered the context in which mothers seek and receive support, our findings do not suggest a corresponding change in the strength of this association. Stability in social support may also have contributed to the patterns observed across cohorts. However, because perceived social support was not measured in a sufficiently comparable way across cohorts, we could not determine whether mothers experienced more or less support over time.

In contrast to perceived social support, we were able to compare the use of formal support across cohorts. Contrary to our hypothesis, a slightly higher proportion of women received formal support in 2017-2021 than in 2002-2006. This might reflect increasing expectations of parenting and infant behavior whereby common infant behaviors, such as crying, are more readily perceived as problematic and reason to seek professional help. For example, parents of unsettled babies seem to seek a medical explanation for this behavior to deal with feelings of guilt and failure, and to regain a sense of control 25 . Furthermore, we found a small positive association between our measure of formal support (i.e., physician visits for the infant), and maternal feelings of depression and anxiety in both cohorts.

However, physician visits may primarily reflect concerns about infant health rather than the formal support mothers actually received, which may in turn be associated with poorer maternal mental health 16.

The potential role of parental perceptions about infant behavior is also reflected in our findings on infant sleep. Consistent with our hypotheses, mothers in 2017-2021 reported more perceived sleeping problems than in 2002-2006, while reported characteristics of infant sleep behavior were similar across cohorts. This distinction between infant sleep behavior and parents' perceptions is consistent with previous studies that emphasize the importance of sociocultural factors and parental expectations in the perception of sleeping problems 64.

Norms around sleep duration, bedtimes, and sleep practices influence how infant sleep is perceived. As these norms vary across cultures and over time, so does the prevalence of perceived infant sleeping problems 36, 37.

The distinction between reported infant sleep behavior and parental perceptions of infant sleep was also present in their associations with maternal mental health. Perceived infant sleeping problems were modestly associated with postpartum feelings of depression and anxiety, whereas reported characteristics of infant sleep were not. In line with previous literature, our findings highlight the importance of distinguishing between perceived infant sleep problems and reported infant sleep behavior when assessing its relation with postpartum mental health 67 . Moreover, the direction of this association is not unequivocal and likely to be bidirectional 68 . For example, infant sleeping difficulties are associated with a shorter maternal sleep duration and lower self-efficacy, which are, in turn, associated with maternal depression 69 . On the other hand, other studies have shown that mothers with more depressive feelings are more likely to perceive their infant's sleep as problematic, independent of infant sleep characteristics 70, 71.

We did not find evidence for our hypothesis that the association between infant sleep and postpartum mental health would be stronger in 2017-2021 than in 2002-2006. Thus, although perceptions of infant sleep differed between cohorts, we found no indication that their association with postpartum mental health had changed. This might imply that sociocultural factors and parental expectations are relevant to how infant sleep is perceived, while the association between these perceptions and postpartum mental health may be similar across cultures. Yet, previous research has found that cultural background moderated the association between infant sleep and maternal mental health 72 . Specifically, bedtime difficulties were associated with maternal mental health only for mothers with a Hispanic and Asian background and not for mothers with a non-Hispanic European background, which may be related to the value placed on interdependency in Asian and Hispanic cultures and the high frequency of co-sleeping. Together, these findings indicate that more insight is needed on the role of sociocultural factors and parental expectations in infant sleep perceptions and postpartum mental health.

The findings of the current study suggest a nuanced picture of change across cohorts.

Whereas mothers' self-reported postpartum mental health and its associated factors were relatively stable across the two cohorts, mothers in the more recent cohort were more likely to perceive their infant's sleep as problematic and reported more physician visits for the child.

Rather than supporting a straightforward narrative of deteriorating maternal mental health, our findings may therefore point to changes in how ordinary infant behavior is perceived and evaluated, possibly reflecting changing societal norms and parental expectations 11, 24 . This raises the question of whether supporting future parents should focus not only on identifying and treating mental health problems, but also on fostering realistic expectations about infant behavior and child-rearing.

The current study has several strengths. We were able to examine two large prospective population-based cohorts from the same geographic region that were collected 15 years apart using the same or very similar instruments at comparable moments during the postpartum period. This design uniquely supports comparisons across time while minimizing differences in geographic context, assessment instruments, and timing of measurement. By using structural equation modeling and examining measurement invariance, we were able to meticulously compare postpartum mental health symptoms across cohorts. Finally, by examining both cohort differences in postpartum mental health and potential change in its associations with infant behavior and support, our study goes beyond documenting temporal trends to investigate whether these relationships have also changed over time.

The study also has several limitations. Although both cohorts have a longitudinal design, insufficiently comparable measures across multiple time points prevented us from fully leveraging this feature. Additional longitudinal analyses could have provided more insight into the temporal ordering of associations between postpartum mental health and its correlates. In addition, limitations in terms of harmonization prevented us from directly comparing levels of social support across cohorts, or including a measures of positive wellbeing and partner well-being 17 . Consequently, our findings primarily concern symptoms of depression and anxiety and cannot address possible cohort differences in broader aspects of parental well-being. Yet, we have aimed to add nuance to the assessment of mental health by approaching it as a continuous construct rather than relying on a clinical cutoff. Next, we cannot exclude the possibility that our results are affected by differences in the study design of the Generation R and Generation R Next Study, as the Generation R Study includes a more ethnically and socioeconomically diverse population. To account for these sociodemographic differences, we applied inverse probability weights and carried out a sensitivity analysis focused on the harmonization of the research design. Still, the relatively high proportion of women with a high socioeconomic status in our analytic samples may limit the generalizability of our findings, particularly to mothers in socioeconomically disadvantaged positions.

In conclusion, the current study found comparable levels of postpartum feelings of anxiety and depression between mothers of infants born in 2002-2006 and 2017-2021. Lower levels of social support were associated with more postpartum mental health problems in both cohorts. Mothers in the most recent cohort reported more physician visits for the child and were more likely to perceive their infant's sleep as problematic. In contrast, reported infant sleep behavior was similar across cohorts. The distinction between reported infant sleep behavior and parental perceptions was also reflected in their associations with maternal mental health. More perceived infant sleeping problems were associated with poorer postpartum mental health, whereas reported infant sleep characteristics were not. Overall, these findings imply changes in how ordinary infant behavior is perceived and evaluated, possibly reflecting changing societal norms and parental expectations. This underscores the importance of understanding maternal mental health not as the result of individual vulnerabilities but also in relation to the context of how parents experience everyday parenting challenges and the support available to them.

Methods

Study Design and Participant Selection

This study has been preregistered on OSF (10.17605/OSF.IO/HJVNT). We followed the preregistered protocol, with a few deviations, specified below. In this study, we compared data on maternal mental health of two large longitudinal population-based prospective birth cohorts from fetal life (Generation R Study) or from preconception onwards (Generation R Next Study). Both cohorts included residents of Rotterdam, the Netherlands and were designed to answer questions on the influence of environmental (e.g., lifestyle or sociodemographic determinants) and genetic factors on the development and health of infants, children, and young adults. For the Generation R Study, pregnant women who were expected to give birth between April 2002 and January 2006 were eligible for inclusion. For the Generation R Next Study, women who had pregnancy intentions and women who were pregnant were included from July 2017 until July 2021 if they were at least 18 years old. The Medical Ethical Committee of the Erasmus Medical Center, Rotterdam, approved the Generation R and Generation R Next Study. The study was performed in line with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. Data collection procedures and sample characteristics of the Generation R Study and Generation R Next Study have been described in detail [27] [28] [29] [30].

For the current analysis, we included mothers who participated in the postpartum phase of the Generation R Study (n = 7,932) and Generation R Next Study (n = 2,905). We selected participants who had a maximum of 25% missing items on one of the outcome scales. If the same mother participated twice in the same study during the postpartum phase, we only included the first enrolment with live birth or the behavioral characteristics of the oldest child in case of twin birth. Lastly, we only selected participants who responded within the desired time window, i.e., around 2-3 months postpartum and around 6 months postpartum. This resulted in a total of 3,588 mothers from the Generation R Study and 1,014 from the Generation R Next Study. The selection process is displayed in Figure 2. Nonresponse analysis showed similar patterns across cohorts (Supplementary Table 1). For both studies, women included in the analytic sample were on average older, less ethnically diverse and had a higher socioeconomic status compared to women who were excluded because of missing outcome data. In the Generation R Study, relatively more women were included prenatally as opposed to postnatally in the analytic sample compared to the excluded sample. In the Generation R Next Study, relatively more women were included preconceptionally versus prenatally in the analytic sample compared to the excluded women.

Measures

We selected validated measures of postpartum mental health in addition to measures that were thought to reflect societal changes. We checked that measures were comparable in terms of content and timing in both cohorts 48 . The measures were reported by the mother at 2-3 months or 6 months postpartum.

Covariates were gathered upon enrolment.

Postpartum anxiety.

Anxiety symptoms were self-reported for both cohorts at 6 months postpartum. We included 5 out of 6 items of the anxiety subscale of the Brief Symptom Inventory (BSI) 49 . The sixth item was not available in the Generation R Next Study. Respondents were asked to what extent they had experienced symptoms in the past week, for example, feeling frightened or tense. In the Generation R Study, a fivepoint scale was used, whereas a four-point scale was used in the Generation R Next Study. To harmonize the scales, the third and fourth response categories ("moderately" and "quite a bit") were merged into one category for the Generation R Study to mirror the "more than half of the days" category in the Generation R Next Study. Higher scores represent a higher severity of anxiety symptoms.

Summed scores could range from 0 to 15. The sum scores were positively skewed in both cohorts.

However, we will report both the mean and median to facilitate comparison with other cohort studies.

The items showed good internal consistency in both cohorts (α = 0.80 Generation R, α = 0.79 Generation R Next).

Postpartum depression.

Depression symptoms were self-reported at 2-3 months postpartum using the Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale (EPDS) 50 . All ten items of the EPDS were reported in both cohorts. Respondents reported on a four-point scale how often they experienced certain symptoms in the past seven days, for example, 'I have felt sad or miserable'. Higher scores indicate worse symptomatology (some items have reversed scoring). The summed score could range from 0 to 30 with a score of 13 or higher indicating elevated depressive symptoms 51 . The EPDS sum scores were positively skewed in both cohorts.

However, we will report both the mean and median to facilitate comparison with other cohort studies.

The EPDS showed good internal consistency in both cohorts (α = 0.85 Generation R, α = 0.86 Generation R Next).

Social support.

Social support was self-reported at 2-3 months postpartum in the Generation R Study through the validated 12-item version of the Social Support List -Interactions (SSL12-I) 52 . The SSL12-I consists of three subscales of four items each, namely everyday emotional support, social support in problem situations, and esteem support. Mothers were asked to rate how much support they received on a fourpoint Likert scale. Example items are: 'Do others sometimes offer help caring for (one of your child(ren)?' or 'Do others sometimes show you affection?'. Higher summed scores indicate more social support. The SSL 12-I showed good internal consistency in the Generation R Study (α = 0.92).

In the Generation R Next Study, social support was assessed during pregnancy using the Multidimensional Scale of Perceived Social Support (MSPSS) 53 . The MSPSS contains twelve items organized into three subscales (four items each) relating to the source of the social support, namely family (e.g., I get the emotional help and support I need from my family'), friends (e.g., 'I can count on my friends when things go wrong') or another significant person (e.g., 'There is a special person who is around when I am in need'). Items are rated on a seven-point Likert scale (very strongly disagree to very strongly agree). A higher summed score indicates more social support. The full scale shows good internal consistency in the Generation R Next study (α = 0.92).

Despite differences in timing and instrument use, the two scales were considered comparable enough in terms of focus, i.e., perception of social support 54 . Additionally, social support has been shown to remain relatively stable from pregnancy until the first months after birth 55 . To harmonize the social support scales, z-scores were created after imputation. We were unable, however, to directly compare levels of perceived social support across generations.

Formal support.

Physician visits for the child were used as a proxy for formal support (yes/no). Respondents reported about the child's contact with medical services at six months postpartum in both cohorts. Mothers were considered to have used formal support when they had visited a general physician or medical specialist for the child, or when the child had been admitted to the hospital.

Infant sleeping difficulties

Mothers in both cohorts reported on infant sleeping characteristics of their child at 2-3 and 6 months postpartum. Questions included average bedtime at night, time of awakening in the morning, frequency of night awakenings, and average amount of sleep during the day. In both cohorts, the questions were derived from the Brief Infant Sleep Questionnaire (BISQ) 56 ; however, different response scales were used across the two studies. In the Generation R Study, mothers reported on the number of night awakenings categorically (e.g., "1-2 times"), whereas in the Generation R Next Study, mothers reported the number of times a child woke up at night. Similarly, average duration of sleep during the day was reported in categories for the Generation R Study (e.g., "2-4 hours"), whereas this was an open question in the Generation R Next Study.

Next, we combined these sleep characteristics into one composite variable to reflect sleeping difficulties (yes/no). We calculated sleep duration at night based on the bedtime and time of awakening in the morning. We added the reported daytime sleep duration to the calculated nighttime sleep duration to compute the total sleep duration during 24 hours. For Generation R, we took the middle of the reported category as average sleep duration during the day. Finally, infants were categorized as having sleeping difficulties (yes/ no) if their sleep duration was less than twelve hours per 24 hours or if they woke up more than four times per night 57 . Notably, few mothers reported more than four night awakenings in combination with a short sleep duration.

Lastly, perceived sleeping difficulties were assessed using one item at 2-3 months postpartum.

Mothers reported whether they considered the sleeping behavior of their child as problematic (yes/no).

Excessive crying

Excessive crying was reported by the mother at 2-3 months postpartum through one item, namely "During the past week, has your child ever cried for more than 3 hours on one day (24 hours)?". If mothers answered yes, this was regarded as "excessive crying" 58, 59.

Covariates

We selected covariates based on previous literature and differences in the selection criteria for the Generation R and Generation R Next Studies 13, 15 . Three differences in the selection criteria may have affected the nature of the study populations. First, because the Generation R Next Study included women preconceptionally, this may have resulted in a smaller group of women with unintended pregnancies compared to the Generation R Study. Having an unintended pregnancy has previously been linked to a higher risk of postpartum depression 60 . Second, previous research has shown that women who were included in the Generation R Next Study preconceptionally have a higher socioeconomic status compared to those who enrolled during pregnancy which may translate into similar differences between the two cohorts as well 47 . Third, data collection for Generation R Next ran partly during the COVID-19 pandemic which may have introduced a healthy population bias.

Women reported on the following covariates upon enrolment: age at childbirth, educational attainment (low (e.g., primary education, prevocational education, or junior years of general secondary education)/ middle (e.g., senior years of general secondary education, secondary vocational education)/ high (e.g., higher vocational education, university)), employment status (working/ not working), ethnicity (Dutch (i.e., the woman and her parents were born in the Netherlands)/ non-Dutch), pregnancy intention (yes/ no), and regular visits to a medical specialist for a pre-existing condition (as proxy for maternal health status; yes/ no), and family income (above median/ below median). For the cohort 2002-2006, below median income constitutes <1800 euros per month. For the cohort 2017-2021, below median income means < 3000 euros per month.

Analysis Plan

All data analyses were conducted in R version 4.5.2. Missing data were handled through multiple imputations by chained equations (MICE) with the mice package 61 . Twenty imputed datasets were generated separately for each cohort using customized predictor matrices. This method is recommended when using ordinal factor models 62 . If available, corresponding measures from other time points were included as auxiliary variables to improve the prediction of missing values.

Next, inverse probability weights were calculated within each imputed dataset using the MatchThem package 63 . The weights were calculated using a generalized linear model with the Average Treatment Effect estimand creating a weighted pseudo-population in which the distributions of measured covariates were comparable between the Generation R and Generation R Next cohorts [63] [64] [65].

Covariate balance before and after weighting is presented in Supplementary Figure S1. Weights above the 99 th percentile were trimmed after which the weights ranged from 0.422-2.668.

To assess whether postpartum mental health of parents could be meaningfully compared between parents in the Generation R Study and the Generation R Next Study, we conducted a measurement invariance analysis using the lavaan (Rosseel, 2012) and semTools (Jorgensen et al., 2012) packages. First, the fit of two predetermined competing measurement models was assessed.

Model fit was evaluated using the chi-square test, Comparative Fit Index (CFI), Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA), and Tucker-Lewis Index (TLI). Standardized factor loadings were examined to assess the strength of the associations between the latent response variables and their respective latent factors. The models were estimated using the weighted least squares means and variance adjusted (WLSMV) estimator to account for the ordinal nature of indicators.

To assess measurement invariance, we successively applied constraints on 1) thresholds, 2) factor loadings, 3) intercepts 66, 67 . Due to the restrictions of having only two factors, we could not separately test first and second order invariance. Deviations from preregistration

Some changes were made from the preregistered protocol regarding the inclusion of covariates in the main analysis. Initially, we intended to include lifestyle and perinatal factors that could have affected generational differences in postpartum mental health in the inverse probability weights for main analysis. However, as we had no reason to expect that these variables varied by design beyond differences in socioeconomic status, we decided not to include these variables to prevent overcorrection 71 . Instead, lifestyle and perinatal factors were intended to be used as exploratory analyses in the case of significant differences between the two cohorts. Next, instead of separately reporting country of birth of the mother and her parents, we reported ethnicity of the mother which is a composite of these variables.

Some changes were made regarding the factors associated with postpartum mental health as well. First, perceived excessive crying at 6 months was not included in the main analysis. The combination of high missingness of this variable in the Generation R Next study and low frequency in both cohorts (1-3%) resulted in unstable multiple imputation models, as well as convergence issues in the structural equation model. Considering that the variable was also measured somewhat differently across cohorts, it was deemed to be too imprecise to be included in the analysis. Second, the definition of sleeping difficulties was changed from "sleeping less than nine hours during the night or more than three night awakenings" to "less than 12 hours during 24 hours and more than four night awakenings".

The number of night awakenings was changed from three to four for harmonization purposes. The sleep duration was changed in order to better align with previous literature on sleeping difficulties 72, 73.

In the preregistration, we proposed to exclude women who gave birth during the COVID-19 pandemic from the Generation R Next sample as sensitivity analysis. However, after reflecting on the purpose of the sensitivity analysis, we concluded that it was more fitting to focus the sensitivity analysis on improving the design harmonization of the two studies. Therefore, we decided to only compare women who were included during pregnancy for both cohorts. Moreover, mental health problems do not seem to have been exacerbated in the Dutch general population during the COVID-19 pandemic 30.

Lastly, we used Wald tests to test moderation instead of comparing nested models with constraints as this test proved to be more efficient and less error prone.
