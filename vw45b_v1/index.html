Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. Some differences come from the ground truth: it lacks p-values with a correction name (“p Bonf = .061”), and counts partial eta squared written with its subscript after it (“η 2 p = .05”), “p = .05”, and some table notes and figure legends (“† p < .10, *p < .05”), which this module leaves out, as non-significant p-values. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Abstract

Emotional dysregulation (ED) is frequently observed among children with ADHD, yet little is known about how it unfolds in everyday life, particularly across cultural contexts. This qualitative study examined ED in primary school-aged children with ADHD in a Western cultural context (Belgium) and an Eastern cultural context (China). We interviewed 20 parents in each country and analyzed the interviews using meta-theme analysis. Across both groups, the interview accounts reflected a recurring sequence captured by five interrelated themes: context-specific triggers, emotional overload, dysregulated emotional expression, relational impact, and perceived effects on children's self-image over time. The groups' accounts differed in some of the situations parents described as triggers and in how parents interpreted emotional episodes and their consequences. These findings suggest that assessment and support for children with ADHD should consider how families in different cultural contexts identify emotional triggers and interpret emotional episodes and their consequences.

Variation in emotion regulation difficulties may contribute to the heterogeneity of everyday experiences and functional outcomes among children with ADHD. For example, in emotionally demanding situations, children with ADHD may need to recruit additional cognitive resources to achieve performance levels comparable to those of their typically developing peers (López-Martín et al., 2015). Socially, ADHD characteristics and ED account for nearly half of the variance in parent-reported social skills, with ED predicting social impairment beyond the effects of co-occurring conditions such as oppositional defiant disorder (ODD) (Bunford et al., 2018; Cleminshaw et al., 2020). Moreover, ED increases vulnerability to a range of psychological conditions, including conduct problems, aggression, anxiety, and depression (Graziano & Garcia, 2016; Steinberg & Drabick, 2015; Yue et al., 2022). Although impairments associated with ED have also been documented in adolescents and adults, childhood represents a particularly important developmental period, as emotion regulation systems are still maturing and patterns of emotional responding are being established (van Stralen, 2016). Emotional difficulties emerging during this stage may consequently have cascading effects on later social, emotional, and functional outcomes (Antony et al., 2024).

Despite increasing recognition of ED as a key feature of ADHD, important gaps remain in how it is currently conceptualized and studied. First, typically, ED is indexed through rating scales that primarily assess observable expressions or trait-like manifestations of emotional difficulties (Freitag et al., 2023). While this body of work has generated important knowledge about the correlates and consequences of ED, it offers limited insight into how ED unfolds in everyday life. This represents a critical gap, given that ED is not merely an individual characteristic but is a dynamic process (Blader et al., 2025), particularly in children (van Stralen, 2016). Specifically, in their theoretical review, Blader et al. (2025) emphasized that ED extends beyond core features describing a difficulty that children experience, as "dysregulation" inherently implies impairment in underlying regulatory processes. Their ED processes framework highlights multiple components, including stimulus processing, response selection and control, emotion generation, feedback-based regulation, and experiential influences. From this perspective, instead of stating "the child became rapidly angry", one examines how the child interpreted the stimulus, experienced escalating arousal, selected responses, and received feedback from the environment. In turn, such feedback may amplify, attenuate, or redirect emotional expressions, thereby shaping both the immediate manifestation of ED and its longer-term relational and functional impact (Blader et al., 2025; van Stralen, 2016). These features underscore the inherently situational and dynamic nature of ED-qualities that are difficult to capture when ED is examined solely as a fixed variable in quantitative studies, and may be more fully explored through qualitative approaches (Falissard et al., 2022; Willig, 2019).

The second gap in ED research is that the expression and consequences of ED are embedded within cultural norms, yet dominant conceptualizations of ED in ADHD rarely account for these cultural influences. A rich body of cross-cultural research indicates that emotional norms regarding appropriateness, intensity, and social consequences vary systematically across cultures (De Leersnyder et al., 2021; Mesquita et al., 2017; Matsumoto et al., 2019; Murata et al., 2013). Specifically, in Western cultures that encourage a more independent self (e.g., Belgium, US), self-expression is emphasized and emotional suppression is less strongly encouraged (Huwaë & Schaafsma, 2018). In contrast, people from relatively interdependent and Eastern cultures (e.g., China, Japan) place greater value on emotional control and composure to maintain interpersonal harmony, reflecting a preference for lowarousal emotional states (Markus & Kitayama, 2010; Schouten et al., 2020; Tsai et al., 2007).

These cultural preferences are already evident in preschool-aged children (Ip et al., 2021).

For children with ADHD, whose emotional reactions are often more intense and unpredictable (Faraone et al., 2019), cultural norms may critically shape whether and how ED is recognized as a difficulty, tolerated as normative variability, or sanctioned as problematic.

Cultural expectations influence not only the visibility and acceptability of emotional expressions, but also their functional implications for children's lives. Despite this, most conceptualizations of ED in ADHD are grounded in Western cultural frameworks, raising the question of whether current definitions capture core features of ED or reflect culturally specific norms. Addressing this gap is essential for advancing theoretical models of ED in ADHD, developing culturally sensitive assessment tools, and guiding interventions that more effectively support children with ADHD and their families.

The current study

Existing quantitative studies have predominantly conceptualized emotion dysregulation as a relatively stable individual characteristic, overlooking its dynamic, situational, and culturally embedded nature. Although recent theoretical developments increasingly conceptualize emotion dysregulation as a dynamic process (Blader et al., 2025), little is known about how this process is experienced by children with ADHD in everyday life or how cultural contexts shape different stages of this process. Thus, the present study adopts a cross-cultural qualitative approach to examine how ED is experienced in daily life across Western and Eastern cultural contexts (i.e., Belgium and China). This study focused on parent-reported ED in primary school-aged children with ADHD. Parents were selected as informants because they serve as primary observers of ED episodes, act as co-regulators within the family context, and integrate information from multiple settings (e.g., schools and public environments). In that case, parents can provide detailed accounts of emotional difficulties in everyday situations. Primary school age is an important developmental period because children face increasing academic, social, and emotional demands while their emotion regulation capacities are still developing (van Stralen, 2016). Given children's limited capacity for self-reflection at this age, parents are included as primary informants.

Methods

Participants

Data collection was cyclical and recursive to ensure data saturation, using social media and personal contacts in both Belgium and China. We recruited a total of 40 parents of children diagnosed with ADHD, including 20 parents from Belgium and 20 parents from China, as Hennink et al. (2017) suggested that conducting between 16 and 24 interviews is sufficient to achieve meaning saturation. The inclusion criteria for parents were as follows: (1) Parents had a child enrolled in primary school; (2) Parents confirmed that their child had received a formal ADHD diagnosis from a clinician. To further ensure diagnostic validity, all parents completed the Swanson, Nolan, and Pelham (SNAP IV) questionnaire (Swanson et al., 2001).

Only parents whose children's SNAP scores met or exceeded the recommended clinical cutoff were included in the final sample; (3) To enhance representativeness in the Belgian sample, only families living in Belgium in which at least one parent held Belgian nationality and spoke Dutch were included, as this was considered indicative of exposure to the dominant Belgian culture. For the Chinese sample, participants who were originally from China but currently lived in other countries (e.g., the USA or Canada) were excluded, due to the potential influence of acculturation in host countries. All participants received a €10 voucher after completing the interview. Eligible parents were also asked to report additional background information about their child, including the age at which the ADHD diagnosis was made, current and past medication use, and parental educational level. Analyses

To examine the cultural similarities and specificity of the lived experience of ED in children with ADHD, a meta-theme analysis framework was employed (Wutich et al., 2021; Xu et al., 2023). This approach builds on established procedures for thematic analysis and has been adapted for cross-cultural qualitative research. As shown in Figure 1, this approach involved two analytic steps, allowing for the identification of site-specific themes (i.e., themes reported by parents within a single country), sub-metathemes (i.e., themes shared across two countries), and metathemes (i.e., broader overarching themes derived from the submetathemes). Site-specific themes grouped under the same sub-metathemes and metathemes were interpreted as reflecting cultural similarities, whereas site-specific themes that emerged exclusively within one country were interpreted as cultural specificities.

In the first phase, to capture the full range of ED-related experiences as reported by parents in each context, site-specific themes were identified separately within each cultural group, by adopting an inductive and a deductive approach, using the framework describing the process nature of ED (Blader et al., 2025). For each group, two coders who were native speakers and had graduate-level training in psychology or educational sciences conducted the analysis. All the coding in this phase was based on their original languages (Dutch for the Belgian group and Mandarin for the Chinese group) to preserve the authenticity and cultural meanings of participants' accounts (Wutich et al., 2021). Prior to coding, to minimize cultural bias during the analysis process, all the coders conducted a cultural reflection to articulate how their cultural backgrounds and training experiences may influence the research process, which is presented in Supplementary Appendix 1. Following the steps suggested by Braun and Clarke (2006), the coders developed code lists for each group through familiarization, initial coding, discussion, and refinement. Notably, to keep the rich information from different perspectives, multiple codes could be applied to the same text segment, and coding prioritized contextual richness to support thick description (Willig, 2019). Codes were formulated, where possible, using participants' original expressions to retain culturally embedded meanings. In this step, code saturation was achieved with 17 interviews in Belgium and 17 interviews in China, as indicated by the identification of more than 95% of the codes (Hennink et al., 2017). The inter-rater reliability was determined through a coding comparison in NVivo 15, where one coder analyzed all interviews, and a second coder reviewed 20% of the interviews to establish an agreement between the two coders (O'Connor et al., 2020). The inter-rater reliability was satisfactory: Belgium (κ = 0.81), China (κ = 0.87). The coders then discussed remaining discrepancies until consensus was reached, and the agreed coding framework was used for subsequent analyses.

In the second phase, a meta-theme analysis examined cross-cultural patterns. Transcripts and site-specific code lists were translated into English to enable systematic comparison across groups. When certain expressions could not be translated appropriately, they were retained in the original language with their meanings explained in English. For example, in the Chinese group, some parents used the phrase "委屈 (Weiqu)" (literally, "feels wronged") to describe children's sadness arising from being treated unfairly by others.

In this stage, coders thoroughly discussed and compared the meanings and conceptual boundaries of codes and themes across the two cultural contexts. All themes were synthesized into broader meta-themes representing cross-cultural patterns of ED. Within each meta-theme, sub metathemes captured phenomena that were judged to be shared across cultural groups. Codes or themes reflecting culturally specific meanings that could not be meaningfully integrated were retained as site-specific themes. Throughout this process, the research team discussed discrepancies until they reached consensus.

Objects or situations that appeared trivial or harmless to adults often carried significant meaning for children and could provoke emotional outbursts, most commonly involving negative emotions such as anger, frustration, or sadness. As one Belgian parent indicated, "These were very small things that were very big for her" (B10, ADHD-only). Chinese parents expressed a similar view, as one noted, "He really does cry very frequently. Even if you just make a joking remark... he can start crying." (C16, ADHD-only). Four broad categories of triggers as sub meta-themes were identified: executive-load triggers, rejection-sensitive triggers, reward-related triggers, and sensory triggers.

Executive-load triggers were mentioned by both Belgian and Chinese parents and included academic demands (e.g., homework), highly structured school environments, and situations requiring sustained attention or compliance. Parents across cultures framed these situations as exceeding their children's regulatory capacity, often preceding rapid emotional escalation.

For example, a Belgian parent described, "The third grade is a stage where things really speed up and the pressure increases. When his attention can't keep up under that pressure, his emotional well-being also starts to suffer." (B10, ADHD-only). Chinese parents similarly emphasized academic demands at certain points as emotionally overwhelming, "When she was little, she did not have emotional problems… after elementary school everything changed, because the education is extremely competitive ("卷 (juan)"), and her abilities are so poor… so only then did emotional problems worsen." (C14, ADHD-only).

Rejection-sensitive triggers involved situations in which children felt criticized, misunderstood, treated unfairly, or excluded by peers or adults. Parents reported that even subtle social cues could precipitate strong emotional reactions. As one Belgian parent noted, "And I do notice that when he keeps getting those remarks all the time, he does find it harder, and then he can sometimes explode more." (B14, ADHD-only). In some seemingly neutral situations, the children may interpret it as a treat and a hint of rejection as well, as one Belgian parent mentioned, "He understood it as her laughing at him. So, it becomes very black-and-white for him, there's no context for him in that moment. Whereas I think she was just teasing him a little, more in a competitive spirit… But she takes it as, 'They're laughing at me." (B03, ADHD + ASD). Chinese parents also described this tendency: "There have been a few occasions when his reactions were somewhat excessive. Once during a PE class, he either didn't perform it well or felt that he hadn't done well. He then saw others laughing and assumed they were mocking him. At that moment, he became very distressed and ran off crying." (C02, ADHD-only). Interestingly, the sense of unfairness also often accompanies ED across cultures; for example, a Belgian parent indicated that "Injustice is a very big issue for us, where her emotions quickly flare up. If she doesn't think something is fair or if she doesn't think someone has been treated fairly, these are things that make it very difficult for her to take a step back and wait a while." (B10, ADHD-only). Same for Chinese parents: "If he's accused of things he clearly didn't do, his reaction tends to be very strong." (C15, ADHDonly).

Reward-related triggers included heightened sensitivity to positive or negative feedback, winning versus losing, right versus wrong. Situations involving delayed gratification or frustration with effortful tasks were also commonly associated with emotional outbursts. As one Belgian parent noted, "But rewards need to be immediate, very much on the spot… Verbal feedback is sometimes already enough for him, because he's very strong that way. But not something spread over three or four weeks, that feels hopeless to him." (B13, ADHDonly). This also holds true for Chinese parents, who reported that when children's sustained efforts do not lead to immediate positive outcomes, the children would exhibit emotional outbursts; for example, "He became frustrated because playing the piano, which is something that, for children of his type, requires repeated practice… you have to keep practicing repeatedly. As a result, he may show a kind of giving-up behavior, saying things like "I don't want to play anymore," or "I'm so stupid, I can't play it well." (C013, ADHD-only).

Sensory triggers referred to overstimulating or uncomfortable environments, such as crowded spaces or excessive noise, which parents described as overwhelming for their children. As one Belgian parent put, "Yes, I find he to be very sensitive, sensitive to stimuli, to tastes, to smells. But also emotionally, yes, very strong emotions indeed." (B13, ADHD-only).

Chinese parent also emphasizes the children's sensitivity to sensory stimuli: "She is very sensitive to all kinds of bodily sensations. When she is focused on doing something and you touch her, or call her loudly, or there are strong lights or other intense sensory stimuli, any of these can give her a shock. She gets startled very easily." (C13, ADHD-only).

Meta-theme 2: Emotional overload Across both cultural contexts, parents described children's emotional reactions as rapidly escalating once triggered, often exceeding the child's capacity for self-regulation. Emotional overload was characterized by rapid arousal and intense affect that was difficult to modulate or contain. As one Belgian parent explained, "…if something goes wrong that she hadn't anticipated or that wasn't her idea, she can become extremely, extremely angry. It's not measured at all." (B01, ADHD + ASD). Similarly, a Chinese parent described the emotions children cannot regulate at all: "He could not regulate it. Once it was triggered, he could not turn back. Like a single touch, and it will explode. He cannot help it." (C10, ADHD-only).

Parents varied in their descriptions of the duration of emotional reactions. Some reported that their children were able to calm down within minutes, whereas others described episodes that lasted for hours. One Belgian parent indicated, "How long did that last? Well, yes, about half an hour or so, I think, before she was calm again." (B04, ADHD + ASD + DCD).

Similarly, a Chinese parent described: "At school, he sometimes shows this kind of crying behavior, and it can even last for about 20 to 40 minutes. Since he is already a first-grade student, this kind of situation is clearly very uncommon among other children." (C13, ADHDonly). By contrast, some parents also mentioned that it does not take a lot of time to calm down; for example, one Belgian parent described the child recovering quickly after being comforted: "I simply sat down on the ground next to him and tried to calm him down, saying, 'Look, it's okay, I. You don't have to. I'm not going to force you. It's okay. Calm down now.' …

And eventually, after a few minutes, he calmed down." (B03, ADHD + ASD). Chinese parents indicated this possibility even without any intervention: "I actually think it's not too bad. We can recover fairly quickly... Her temper just flares up at that moment, but it passes very quickly." (C12, ADHD-only).

Meta-theme 3: Dysregulated emotional expression

When emotional overload occurred, ED was expressed through multiple channels, often simultaneously. Four modes of expression were identified: bodily, facial, verbal, and internalized expressions.

Bodily expressions included clenching fists, banging on tables, kicking doors, throwing objects, pushing peers, running away from the triggering situation, slamming doors, stomping, or becoming physically stiff or frozen. For example, one Belgian parent stated, "He goes through the door and slams it shut" (B06, ADHD + ASD). "She once even threw the Playmobil castle down the stairs" (B16, ADHD-only). A Chinese parent described: "Sometimes he doesn't think about the consequences; he grabs whatever he can and throws it... He throws objects at other children, or chases after them and throws things at them." (C15, ADHD-only). Some parents described escape-oriented behaviors, such as "rolling on the ground" or "fleeing" the environment or the people that triggered their emotions.

Facial expressions commonly included a reddened face, tense facial muscles, angry-looking eyes, or exaggerated facial movements. As one Belgian parent noted, "When he is very sad or angry, he mainly looks angry, you can really tell." (B19, ADHD + ASD). "When it comes to sadness, you can really notice it. Our child, when he becomes sad, his face first turns very red." (B12, ADHD + ASD). A Chinese parent also pointed out: "He would just give you a look, one that makes you feel intimidated, but he wouldn't act out or vent his emotions through actions. It gives the feeling that he really hates you." (C01, ADHD-only).

Verbal expressions involved crying, shouting, cursing, saying hurtful words, or talking back to parents. One Belgian parent mentioned, "He really knows exactly where he can hit you hardest" (B09, ADHD + attachment disorder), and the child defended impulsively, "He then shouts to defend himself. He wants to explain why he did something. Whether that explanation is right or wrong, or whether it's truthful or not, doesn't always matter. He defends himself. He is very verbally strong" (B06, ADHD + ASD). A Chinese parent also noted: "When it is mild, it is small emotions, when she is moderately agitated, she will talk back to you, when she is intensely agitated her volume increases, when it is very intense, she will cry and shout at the same time." (C18, ADHD-only).

In addition to these externalizing behaviors, parents also reported internalized expressions of ED, such as somatic complaints (e.g., stomach pain, tics) and fluctuating mood states. As one Belgian parent noted, "all of a sudden he develops a lot of tics. You can clearly see it. He starts hitting his chest and making repetitive movements." (B05, ADHD-only). This phenomenon was reported by a Chinese parent as well: "She was very anxious. Then she showed bodily reactions; that is, psychological issues appeared, and the teacher noticed that in PE class or going up and down stairs, there were some movements that were uncoordinated." (C06, ADHD-only).

Meta-theme 4: Relational impact of emotional dysregulation.

Parents consistently emphasized that ED had immediate and ongoing consequences for children's relationships, shaping interactions with peers, teachers, and family members.

In peer relationships, parents from both cultures reported that children with ED often experienced insecurity in making friends and were vulnerable to stigmatization. Children were described as being feared, avoided, or labeled as "weird," sometimes leading to peer rejection, exclusion, or bullying. As one Belgian parent noted, "They sometimes find him a bit strange. And I do think that among peers, the fact that he repeats things, that he needs something like that to release tension, means that they sometimes see him as a bit odd."

(B02, ADHD + ASD). Same for a Chinese parent, "Often classmates see him handle emotions poorly, or get upset at unusual triggers. They do not know how to interact with him." (C04, ADHD + ASD).

At the same time, some parents noted peer acceptance, particularly in relationships with neurodivergent peers, where mutual understanding was perceived. As one Belgian parent noted, "And we also notice that she very often connects more with children who are themselves not neurotypical… For example, on holiday she had a little friend who was highly gifted, and another little friend who had a sister with ASD. So, we notice that these are children who are often socially and emotionally a bit more mature." (B10, ADHD-only).

Similarly, a Chinese parent described: "She has at school a group, because in their class there are also maybe three or four with ADHD…. and in such a situation she will play together with those hyperactive girls, so she is very happy at school. … So she is very happy at school, so at school she does not really have emotional problems." (C14, ADHD-only).

In teacher-child relationships, parents across cultures described difficulties in managing children's emotional reactions, which sometimes strained interactions with teachers. One Belgian parent specified the difficulties with the teacher, "He had trouble sitting still in class and would move around, and sometimes he would say something a bit strange during the lesson. And yes, some teachers handle that better than others. That particular teacher was very strict, and with him there's the risk that he feels he's being treated unfairly. Then he starts to resist, and from there it gradually escalates." (B02, ADHD + ASD). Similarly, after a

Chinese parent described a situation where the child pushed the teacher, they mentioned, "the teacher used methods suitable for ordinary children, which produced a different scene with him. It was the first time something like that happened, and the teacher was shocked.

The teacher did not expect a first-grader would dare push a teacher." (C10, ADHD-only).

Nevertheless, some parents emphasized that positive teacher-child relationships could still develop if the teacher showed understanding and acceptance. One Belgian parent indicated, "Now, for example, he has a teacher with whom he does have a bond. He really needs what you might call warm authority. That works best for him." (B02, ADHD + ASD). A similar observation was reported by a Chinese parent: "Some female teachers have training in psychology, and the school also offers psychological or emotional education classes. These teachers appear to be particularly attentive to my child's emotional needs and make an effort to provide additional emotional support." (C06, ADHD-only).

In terms of parent-child relationship, ED was associated with parental emotional distress, including feelings of guilt, worry, frustration, anger, and helplessness. As one Belgian parent observed, "So emotionally, at the moment I'm not the most stable person. When my boys are really struggling, I struggle as well." (B06, ADHD + ASD). Likewise, a Chinese parent explained: "I feel she has no way to communicate well, to the extent that for a period of time, because I did not really understand her situation, and did not know how to get along with her, it made me very depressed, really very depressed, depressed to the point that I began to doubt myself." (C18, ADHD-only).

At the same time, parents from both cultures also described strong emotional bonds with their children, noting expressions of affection, understanding, and closeness. Despite the emotional challenges described above, parents from both cultural groups also emphasized warm and affectionate relationships with their children. Children were often described as expressing love, empathy, and closeness in ways that parents experienced as particularly intense, highlighting that emotion dysregulation coexisted with positive emotional connectedness. For example, one Chinese parent shared, "He brings me flowers every day..." (C01, ADHD-only) and similarly, one Belgian parent noted, "he'll come and hover around me and say, "I love you, Mom. You're the best!" (B09, ADHD + attachment disorder).

Meta-theme 5: Internalization into self-image

Over time, repeated experiences of ED and relational strain were described as shaping children's self-image, which, in turn, appeared to influence how children interpreted subsequent emotional and interpersonal experiences. Parents across cultures reported that children internalized emotional difficulties as feelings of worry, being misunderstood, or not being accepted by others, despite some support around them. As noted by a Belgian parent: "It often comes across to him as if everyone is always angry with him. He notices the negative, but he doesn't really see all the love he receives or how happy everyone actually is because of him." (B04, ADHD + ASD + DCD). And a Chinese parent shared a similar view: "I found that the kids in his class were very nice, very kind and caring… But at that time he could not feel that kindness. He felt others were laughing at him. Looking back now, I think he had a low self-evaluation, which led to those feelings." (C10, ADHD-only).

Negative self-concept was commonly described, including lack of confidence, feelings of worthlessness, and beliefs that the child was "stupid" or a "bad child." Some parents reported that children expressed a desire to be "normal," reflecting awareness of their It is worth noting that, although children displayed potentially high self-esteem, mixed identity patterns could be observed within the same child, combining both self-confidence and self-doubt or insecurity. For example, one Chinese parent described her child as "super confident", yet at the same time noted that the child was very sensitive to social evaluation and tended to interpret the social cues negatively: "Being spoken to about something already makes her feel triggered. Then she takes it one step further and starts thinking, 'Do you not like me? Am I a bad child?' She actually goes straight to that step, and that then leads to expressions of sadness. She moves through these steps very quickly. She reacts extremely fast to that next stage, and she often expresses it verbally, so we know that this is how she is thinking" (C13, ADHD-only).

Culturally specific themes

The culturally specific themes were only observed in the sections of Context-specific triggers of emotional dysregulation, Dysregulated emotional expression, and relational impact of emotional dysregulation.

Meta-theme 1: Context-specific triggers of emotional dysregulation

Although the general categories of triggers were shared, their specific manifestations differed culturally. Chinese parents reported difficulties related to settings or people at school, including prolonged sitting, overly rigid or loosely structured learning environments.

For example, a Chinese parent described, "I have noticed that when the environment is very tightly controlled, or when the level of tolerance is especially high, he will start to show emotional reactions. ... His emotional response takes the form of mischief, like he wants to play pranks on the teacher." (C04, ADHD-only). Belgian parents were, on the other hand, emphasized triggers related to negative social evaluation, such as being watched by others, experiences of exclusion or bullying, and separation from parents (e.g., saying goodbye). As one Belgian parent stated, "Once at school, they were doing group work and he couldn't manage it. He didn't dare ask for help, and the other children just kept working. He felt excluded because he didn't understand it and didn't dare say that he didn't understand. Then he became angry." (B19, ADHD + ASD). Moreover, the emotional and physical states (e.g., pain, anxiety, disappointment, tiredness) were triggered in the Belgian group, but not in the Chinese group. For example, one Belgian parent mentioned "And earlier she had bumped her knee and she just can't let that go." (B16, ADHD-only).

Meta-theme 3: Dysregulated emotional expression

Belgian parents also described bodily distress (e.g., difficulty breathing, hyperventilation), emotionally charged internal states (e.g., anxiety, shame, nervousness), and vocal expressions such as growling, as one Belgian parent indicated, "And they kept confronting him about it, and then he started crying very hard and basically stayed in his room the whole day. He was also very scared. Yes. It was mainly fear at that moment." (B05, ADHD-only).

Chinese parents, on the other hand, emphasized defiant behaviors, especially in the academic context, including refusing to attend school, tearing books or objects, hitting themselves, or talking back to teachers, as one Chinese parent put it, "He was very unhappy about this and loudly questioned the teacher, asking whether the PE teacher had really gone to a meeting." (C16, ADHD-only). Differences were also noted in facial expressions, with Belgian parents mentioning "facial tension" and "wrinkling nose", while Chinese parents described "grinning teeth" or "eye-rolling".

Meta-theme 4: Relational impact of emotional dysregulation.

Cultural differences emerged in how relational difficulties were interpreted. Belgian parents elaborated how children's ED may influence family dynamics. For example, one parent reported that "It has caused a break with family members". (B02, ADHD + ASD) They also noted behaviors related to siblings' adjustment, for example, "Siblings that find it difficult how people deal with the child", and "Siblings try to facilitate situation". (B18, ADHD-only) By contrast, Chinese parents indicated the possibility that parents "become stricter" (C10, ADHD-only) and that children might have "fear of parents". (C07, ADHD-only) Meanwhile, Chinese parents also showed their expectation for children to engage in repair behaviors after conflict with peers, since that "refusal to apologize affects peer relationships". (C01, ADHD-only)

Discussion

Across both cultural contexts, ED unfolds in daily life through a sequence of context-specific triggers, emotional overload, dysregulated expression, relational impact, and internalization into self-image. This process is broadly shared across cultural contexts, while cultural norms shape some interpretation and consequences of specific stages.

Culturally shared themes

Context-specific triggers. A central finding of the present study is that we found that ED can be understood as a dynamic process emerging from the interaction between heightened emotional reactivity and situational demands. Specifically, emotional outbursts were consistently triggered by specific contextual demands that taxed core ADHD-related vulnerabilities, including executive-load demands, rejection-sensitive situations, rewardrelated contingencies, and sensory overload. These triggers highlight ED as a contextdependent manifestation of underlying neurodevelopmental vulnerabilities rather than a constant emotional state. This aligns with the episodic nature identified in previous research, such as irritability being shown as episodic in ADHD rather than chronic expression in ODD or Mood Dysregulation Disorder (Childress & Sallee, 2015; Faraone et al., 2019). These contexts may trigger ED in children with ADHD for several reasons. Many children with ADHD experience difficulties in executive functioning (Kofler et al., 2019). When exposed to high executive-load demands, their regulatory resources may be exceeded, increasing the likelihood of emotional outbursts. Insufficient inhibitory control may also compromise their ability to filter irrelevant sensory input (Blader et al., 2025), making them more vulnerable to sensory overload as a potential trigger. This echoes the idea that emotional reactivity itself includes the process of regulation, including sensory gating, thresholds for stimuli to activate emotion, anticipatory appraisal, and valuation (Blader et al., 2025; Faraone et al., 2019). In addition, for some children with ADHD, heightened sensitivity to rewards or altered motivational processing may contribute to increased emotional reactivity in reward-contingent situations (Sonuga-Barke, 2003), which may increase their reactivity in rewardcontingent situations. In social contexts, because children with ADHD experience more frequent negative feedback and peer rejection than typically developing children, they may gradually learn to interpret such comments in a more negative way at an implicit level (Babinski et al., 2019), which then influences how they regulate their behavior.

Emotional overload. Once triggered, children across cultures were described as exhibiting rapid emotional reactions that varied in intensity and duration, suggesting heterogeneity in both emotional threshold and recovery processes. This mirrors a previous study showing that there are three prototypes of ADHD expressing ED: quick and intense emotional arousal and takes long to regulate the emotion (prototype 1), low emotional impulsivity but ineffective self-regulation (prototype 2), and high emotional impulsivity but efficient emotion regulation, so-called "emotional liability" (prototype 3) (Faraone et al., 2019). This result is important for identifying the fact that ED can be either "difficulties in regulating emotions", or "too quick to regulate emotions". First, Blader et al. (2025) highlighted the temporal dynamics of emotional responses, suggesting that when intense emotions arise rapidly, children may have little time to regulate their responses. Secondly, another potential pathway to emotion dysregulation involves difficulties in inhibitory control. For some children with ADHD, reduced inhibitory control may limit the cognitive resources available to regulate emotional responses in challenging situations. This emotional overload observed across cultures suggests a potential fundamental phenomenon, including emotional impulsiveness and deficient emotional self-regulation among children with ADHD (Faraone et al., 2019), which requires further investigation.

Dysregulated emotional expression.

Emotional overload was followed by diverse forms of dysregulated emotional expression. Across cultures, ED was expressed through bodily agitation, facial tension, verbal outbursts, defiant behavior, and internalizing responses such as somatic complaints or withdrawal, which largely aligns with the expressions found in the existing widely used questionnaires, such as the Emotion Regulation Checklist and the Difficulties in Emotion Regulation Scale (Freitag et al., 2023), particularly in domains of emotional liability and impulse control difficulties. While the questionnaires assess these constructs through generalized items (e.g., "When I'm upset, I lose control over my behaviors"), the qualitative data across cultures provide more detailed behavioral exemplification (e.g., throwing objects, slamming doors, physical aggression), suggesting that the primary expressive channels of ED may be broadly shared across cultural contexts.

Relational impact. Across both cultural contexts, ED was associated with difficulties in peer, teacher-child, and parent-child relationships. Importantly, these impacts were mixed rather than uniformly negative. In peer relationships, ED increased the risk of stigmatization, rejection, and exclusion, consistent with prior research linking ED to social difficulties in ADHD (Bunford et al., 2018; McQuade et al., 2021), and ED further accounts for the association between ADHD and peer victimization (Fogleman et al., 2019). At the same time, some children were described as forming close relationships, particularly with neurodivergent peers, suggesting that children experiencing ED may also facilitate mutual understanding and affiliation with similar others, which is also in line with research showing that neurodivergent people, including those with ADHD, report more same-neurotype friends and describe these as deeply affirming with "compatible weirdness" (Sharman & Seedorf, 2025).

A similar duality was observed in adult-child relationships. On the one hand, consistent with previous studies, ED posed challenges for teachers and contributed to parental emotional distress. For example, teachers often face heightened classroom disruptions from ED, such as outbursts or intolerance of frustration, which strain management efforts and contribute to burnout (Rogers et al., 2015). Similarly, parents across cultural groups report elevated emotional distress due to frequent conflicts, guilt over discipline, and worry about long-term outcomes (Setiawati et al., 2024; Olczyk et al., 2024). Paradoxically, teachers and parents in both cultural groups reported emotional bonds and supportive relationships. In our sample, teachers with greater knowledge of ADHD may help explain this pattern, as increased understanding of ADHD could contribute to higher-quality adult-child relationships (e.g., Krtek et al., 2022). These findings support developmental psychopathology perspectives, which conceptualize risk processes as probabilistic, with outcomes shaped by contextual responses rather than determined solely by core features (Erlandsson et al., 2022).

Internalization into the child's self-image. Over time, repeated experiences of ED and relational feedback were internalized into children's self-image. Across cultures, ED was associated with shame, frustration, and negative self-evaluation. Children were described as perceiving themselves as "bad," "out of control," or different from others, particularly when emotional episodes were met with criticism or misunderstanding. Children with a negative self-concept may be more likely to interpret ambiguous social cues as signs of rejection or criticism. These interpretations may intensify their emotional reactions and reinforce their negative beliefs about themselves. Despite the negative self-image often reported among children with ADHD, some parents in both cultural groups described their children as having a positive self-concept. These positive narratives sometimes coexisted with accounts of negative experiences, suggesting that self-acceptance may be partial and dynamic rather than fixed.

Previous quantitative research indicated mixed findings regarding self-esteem in children with ADHD. On the one hand, these children often reported lower self-esteem than typically developing peers (Mazzone et al., 2013), particularly in the academic domain (Edbom et al., 2008). One possible explanation is that children with ADHD receive more frequent negative feedback (Sonuga-Barke, 2003), which may gradually shape more negative self-evaluations, as summarized in recent reviews (Betancourt et al., 2024). On the other hand, some studies have found no significant impairments in self-esteem, possibly due to the positive illusory bias, a self-protective tendency whereby children overestimate their own competence, observed in children with ADHD (Hoza et al., 2012). Although this bias may help preserve self-esteem in the face of repeated challenges, it may also obscure underlying emotional distress and reduce awareness of difficulties.

Consistent with this literature, our findings highlight the heterogeneity of self-concepts within the ADHD population. Parents described considerable variation in how children understood themselves and responded to emotional difficulties, suggesting that family interactions may play an important role in shaping these experiences, for example, by accepting their child's differences and acknowledging weaknesses while emphasizing strengths. Previous studies have shown that authoritative parenting and permissive parenting contribute to higher self-esteem compared to authoritarian parenting (Kurman et al., 2018), suggesting that perceived parental support and warmth are particularly important for children with ADHD. In line with this, parental acceptance (e.g., warmth and nonrejection) has been found to predict better psychological adjustment and fewer behavioral problems (Ankori et al., 2025). Strengths-based and neurodiversity-oriented approaches similarly emphasize affirming language and the explicit recognition of strengths to help children move from self-criticism toward self-compassion and self-acceptance (Climie et al., 2015).

Culturally specific themes

Cultural context shaped which situations were most likely to be experienced as emotionally taxing (context-specific triggers), how ED was narrated by parents (dysregulated emotional expression), how ED-related relational impacts were interpreted and evaluated by parents (Relational impact).

Context-specific triggers. Belgian parents more often emphasized social-evaluative contexts, such as peer scrutiny or separation, whereas Chinese parents highlighted authority-related demands and expectations for behavioral compliance. These differences suggest that culture influences the contexts in which emotional vulnerabilities are most likely to be activated (Takada & Dan-Glauser, 2024). Specifically, as children develop meaning systems for words and behaviors through socialization with adults, they learn which behaviors are considered appropriate or inappropriate. Over time, their brains become attuned to embedded signals within shared understandings-what we refer to as 'culture'-that are transmitted across generations (Barrett, 2022). In the Belgian sample, participants' accounts highlighted social evaluation as a trigger for emotional distress, particularly when children received negative feedback from peers or felt judged by them. One possible interpretation, drawing on Corapci et al. (2025), is that in contexts that value an independent sense of self, situations in which children express themselves may make them particularly sensitive to how others respond.

On the other hand, Chinese culture encourages a more interdependent self, and following the authorities and rules is relatively valued (Corapci et al., 2025); thus children's resistance to authority-related demands from schools may be more problematic and therefore has greater emotional salience. Moreover, the states (e.g., pain, anxiety, disappointment, tiredness) triggers found in the Belgian group but not in the Chinese group echo the finding in children in the UK (Chung et al., 2022), suggesting that state-triggers may be culturally specific in Western societies where children's inner state is put on emphasis. Raval and Walke (2019) indicated that, when comparing how American and Chinese parents addressed negative events with their children, White American mothers more often mentioned children's mental states (Doan & Wang, 2010) and provided more emotion-focused explanations (Wang, 2001). By contrast, Chinese mothers tended to focus on behaviors (Doan & Wang, 2010).

Dysregulated emotional expression.

Similarly, when discussing the expressions of ED, Belgian parents were more likely to describe internal emotional states (e.g., anxiety, shame, worry) and physiological stress responses, whereas Chinese parents emphasized behavioral manifestations, authority-related conflicts, and recurrent emotional outbursts. In particular, although Chinese parents explicitly referred to the authority-related conflicts (e.g., "talk back to teachers"), suggesting the potential concerns related to ODD, no formally diagnosed ODD comorbidity was reported in the current Chinese sample, making this contrast particularly intriguing. These differences may be understood in light of prior findings on emotion socialization (Corapci et al., 2025). In Western societies, internal distress or problems (e.g., anxiety or depression) may be more readily identified as problematic because it signals threats to personal agency. In contrast, collectivistic contexts prioritize social harmony, making disruptive behaviors or externalizing problems (e.g., aggression) more salient, as they may directly threaten relational cohesion (Gorla et al., 2024). Notably, these findings may reflect differences not only in expression but also in parental attention and interpretative frameworks. This further suggests that cultural norms may shape which emotional signals are noticed, labeled, and problematized, guiding parents' beliefs about which emotions are acceptable or require regulation (Gómez-Carrillo & Kirmayer, 2023), and, in turn, how ED is constructed within family narratives.

Relational impact. Belgian parents reported family destruction and siblings' adjustments, while Chinese parents tended to emphasize parental strictness and expectations that children repair the relationships after conflict. In Western societies, children may be viewed as autonomous agents, and parenting tends to be more child-centered (Corapci et al., 2025).

Accordingly, Belgian parents acknowledged that children's behaviors can affect the family system. By contrast, Chinese families tend to emphasize hierarchical relationships, and parents may place greater emphasis on promoting cooperation and encouraging self-control to guide children to fit into the group. These findings suggest that values and socialization goals may contribute to how ED is framed, either as behavioral misconduct or as emotional vulnerability, and, in turn, impact the relationship and structuring responses to children's difficulties (Corapci et al., 2025).

Theoretical contribution

Overall, our findings support the applicability of a process-based understanding of ED (Blader et al., 2025) to the everyday experiences of children with ADHD, highlighting the importance of situational triggers, rapid emotional overload, and relational feedback in shaping emotional outcomes over time. Meanwhile, the findings also suggest several refinements to existing models. Three refinements appear particularly relevant when applying existing ED process models to children with ADHD. First, parents' accounts highlight the relational nature of ED. Responses from parents, teachers, and peers were described as potentially shaping the escalation, regulation, and resolution of emotional episodes. This suggests that interpersonal responses may be understood not only as broader contextual influences but also as part of the unfolding regulatory process itself. Second, the findings suggest a possible feedback process that extends beyond individual emotional episodes. Repeated experiences of ED and interpersonal difficulties may shape children's self-concept and expectations of others, potentially influencing how they interpret and respond to subsequent emotional situations. This points to the value of considering longer-term, recursive interactions between emotional experiences and developing self-perceptions alongside the withinepisode regulatory processes emphasized in existing frameworks. Third, importantly, culture was not identified as a separate process operating alongside ED. Instead, cultural context appeared to shape multiple stages of the ED process. Cultural norms and expectations influenced which situations were experienced or interpreted as emotionally salient, how emotional expression was understood, how parents and teachers responded to children's emotions, and how repeated emotional and interpersonal experiences were incorporated into children's developing self-concept. Thus, culture may be conceptualized as a contextual layer operating across the ED process rather than as a stand-alone explanatory variable.

Implications

These findings support a cross-cultural, process-oriented conceptualization of ED in which dysregulation emerges from repeated interactions between emotional vulnerability and contextual demands. Methodologically, it underscores the need for assessment approaches that capture process-level dynamics, for example, context-specific triggers, parent-child interaction patterns, and fluctuations in emotional intensity in real life, rather than relying exclusively on global trait measures. Clinically, interventions may benefit from targeting relational responses to dysregulation and modifying environmental demands, particularly in childhood when regulatory systems are still developing. For example, beyond typical interventions that teach children emotional regulation strategies, children with ADHD may also benefit from interventions that focus on predictable structure based on the identified triggers (e.g., removing children from overly stimulating or cognitively demanding environments), promoting emotion labeling (e.g., naming the emotions objectively, such as "the child is overwhelmed" rather than "the child is rebelling"), and reducing shame cycles (e.g., guiding the child to recognize the uniqueness of their qualities). More importantly, these implications should account for cultural norms to ensure more accurate assessment and culturally appropriate interventions.

For example, in contexts where emotional expression is more readily accepted, interventions may instead focus on helping children navigate peer interactions and develop adaptive regulation strategies in socially challenging situations. In contexts where emotional restraint is strongly valued, clinicians may need to work more closely with parents and teachers to differentiate ED from intentional misbehavior and to promote validating responses.

Limitation

Although the present qualitative study captured a process-oriented model of ED across cultures, several limitations should be considered. First, given the documented discrepancies between subjective emotional experience and observed behavior (Faraone et al., 2019), the reliance on parental accounts may not fully reflect children's subjective states. Future studies would benefit from including the children's own perspectives to understand how ED is experienced and internalized. Second, co-occurring conditions and variation in treatment experiences may have influenced how ED was expressed and interpreted. However, given the high rates of comorbidity in the ADHD population (Efron et al. 2016), including children with co-occurring conditions arguably increased ecological validity. Additionally, the Belgian sample included a broader range of formally diagnosed comorbid conditions and more parent ADHD diagnoses, whereas in the Chinese sample fewer diagnoses were reported, although several parents described suspected characteristics. However, within the current study design, it is difficult to determine whether these differences reflect variations in access to clinical services or differences in clinical complexity. This question should be examined more comprehensively in future studies. Finally, the qualitative design does not permit conclusions about the frequency or relative prevalence of the identified behaviors.

Quantitative and mixed-method approaches are needed to examine the distribution of these patterns (e.g., to identify which triggers are more salient within specific cultural groups), and to statistically test the cultural variations suggested by the present findings.

Conclusion

The current study aims to examine ED as a dynamic process in children with ADHD from a cross-cultural parental perspective. The findings show how ED can be triggered and expressed, as well as how it impacts relationships with others and children's self-image. The presence of both shared and culture-specific patterns suggests that ED in ADHD reflects core neurodevelopmental vulnerabilities that manifest across cultures, while their expression, interpretation, and developmental consequences are embedded within culturally shaped norms and relational expectations. Recognizing both ED's cultural similarity and cultural specificity may inform more contextually attuned assessment and intervention approaches.
