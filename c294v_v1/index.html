Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Marginal Significance works

List all sentences that describe an effect as ‘marginally significant’.

The marginal module searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The list of terms is a subset of those listed in a blog post by Matthew Hankins. The module returns all sentences that match terms describing ‘marginally significant’ results.

Some of the terms identified might not be problematic in some contexts, and there are ways to describe ‘marginal significance’ that are not detected by the module.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. Some differences come from the ground truth: it lacks p-values with a correction name (“p Bonf = .061”), and counts partial eta squared written with its subscript after it (“η 2 p = .05”), “p = .05”, and some table notes and figure legends (“† p < .10, *p < .05”), which this module leaves out, as non-significant p-values. The Effect Size module checks if effect sizes are correctly reported in t-tests and F-tests.

The Effect Size check searches for regular expressions that match typical ways in which effect sizes are reported. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Abstract

Newell's challenge remains: experiments often study cognitive mechanisms separately, although real learning requires their coordination. We present CAVL, a functional, multilevel, precomputational architecture of socially embedded vigilant learning. It links working memory, long-term knowledge, expertise, epistemic vigilance, source representation, metacognition, and social context. Structural embedding shapes knowledge, opportunities, and verification costs. Interactional embedding shapes how learners evaluate claims and their sources. CAVL makes three testable claims. First, valid prior knowledge and processing conditions alter the balance between content quality and attributed authority. Second, private acceptance can diverge from public expression when social costs are high. Third, unequal cognitive resources and allocated epistemic credit jointly shape participation and later learning. Six propositions specify critical tests and reasons to revise the architecture. CAVL treats ecological validity as a problem for research, not as a label attached to realistic settings. It proposes controlled and systematically varied experiments that preserve relevant person-task-environment relations. Its purpose is to connect experimentally established mechanisms with socially situated learning, not to replace controlled experiments or existing theories.

1 The challenge of integrating mechanisms in socially embedded learning People learn extensively from others. Teaching, testimony, books, institutions, search engines, and conversational systems provide knowledge that learners cannot reconstruct alone. Yet communicated claims may be wrong, distorted, or socially rewarded despite weak evidence. Accurate claims may also be costly to acknowledge. Learning therefore requires both openness and vigilance (Mercier, 2020; Sperber et al., 2010). Newell (1973) argued that isolated experimental effects cannot, by themselves, yield a cumulative theory of cognition. His later programme sought architectures that coordinate memory, control, learning, and problem solving across tasks (Newell, 1990). The problem remains. Experiments can isolate effects of load, knowledge, fluency, source labels, and accuracy prompts. Yet in a classroom debate or online search these mechanisms operate together, often under conflicting social and epistemic goals. We must explain their interactions, not simply add their separate effects (Anderson & Lebiere, 2003; Muthukrishna & Henrich, 2019).

Several programmes address Newell's challenge. Cognitive architectures integrate memory and reasoning (Hélie & Sun, 2014a , 2014b; Sun, 2012). Brase (2014) proposes cross-level convergence of evidence. Cognitive robotics approaches the problem through embodiment, situatedness, and autonomy (Morse et al., 2011). CAVL builds on these efforts. Its narrower question is how cognitive mechanisms combine with epistemic authority, social stakes, and unequal resources when people learn from others.

A similar fragmentation separates levels of analysis. Sociology explains how institutions, status, identities, and inequalities shape knowledge and authority. Cognitive sociology studies socially organised classifications (Cicourel, 1974; DiMaggio, 1997); habitus concerns dispositions formed through social trajectories (Bourdieu, 1990). Situated and distributed accounts show how artefacts, practices, and environments organise cognitive activity (Hollan et al., 2000; Hutchins, 1995). These insights challenge generalisations from highly stripped-down tasks. They do not require us to abandon cognitive mechanisms CAVL starts from established accounts of cognition. ACT-R distinguishes declarative knowledge and procedural productions (Anderson, 1996; Anderson & Lebiere, 2003). Cognitive Load Theory links limited working memory with schemas in long-term memory (Sweller, 1988; Sweller et al., 2011). Expertise changes task demands through organised chunks and retrieval procedures (Chase & Simon, 1973; Ericsson & Kintsch, 1995). These accounts explain how existing knowledge supports further learning. They leave open how a learner decides whether socially supplied claims deserve integration. Epistemic vigilance addresses that decision. It evaluates communicated content and informants using plausibility, arguments, competence, benevolence, and reputation (Mercier & Sperber, 2017; Sperber et al., 2010). But it is not a complete architecture of learning. CAVL links source evaluation to memory, theory of mind, metacognition, learning procedures, and social goals.

The link involves two forms of social embedding. Structural embedding concerns how education, institutions, and social trajectories shape knowledge, dispositions, cues, and verification costs. Interactional embedding concerns how learners evaluate sources when they cannot directly check claims. Perceived authority may diverge from actual competence. The two forms interact: structural resources shape credibility judgements, while repeated allocations of credit influence whose knowledge is heard and developed. CAVL makes three linked claims. First, valid knowledge and processing conditions change the balance between content quality and attributed authority. When a learner can evaluate diagnostic evidence, quality should matter more; otherwise authority should matter more. Second, social costs can separate private epistemic uptake from public expression. Third, structurally distributed resources and interactionally allocated credit shape one another through participation, feedback, and later learning. These claims, not the individual mechanisms, define CAVL's proposed contribution. Section 8 specifies tests that could challenge them.

Epistemic autonomy does not require constant deliberation or independence from testimony. It requires knowing when to use reliable routines, when to verify, and when to separate epistemic judgement from the social cost of expression. CAVL therefore defines autonomy as regulated dependence.

Section 2 positions CAVL among integration programmes. Sections 3-6 describe its cognitive and social mechanisms. Section 7 connects them. Section 8 proposes discriminating experiments, including tests across environments. Sections 9-11 discuss implications, limits, and formalisation.

2 What kind of architecture is CAVL

2.1 A functional multilevel and precomputational architecture

Cognitive architectures identify functions and relations that constrain performance across tasks (Kotseruba & Tsotsos, 2020; Newell, 1990). Computational architectures also specify executable rules. Surveys of ACT-R, Soar, and CLARION illustrate learning mechanisms and cross-task coordination (Hélie & Sun, 2014a); CLARION models connect memory to reasoning (Hélie & Sun, 2014b; Sun, 2012). CAVL is not yet executable. It is a functional, multilevel, precomputational architecture that specifies components, dependencies, moderators, feedback, and limits. Its status depends on producing discriminating predictions, not on drawing boxes and arrows.

We distinguish systems, mechanisms, and processing regimes. Systems are functions such as memory, metacognition, or control. Mechanisms are operations such as retrieval, source attribution, checking, and updating. Processing regimes describe degrees of elaboration. Epistemic weight is the degree of acceptability assigned to a claim. Perceived competence is an assessment of what a source knows or can do. Epistemic credit is the social recognition granted to a source or its contribution. Epistemic authority is a relatively stable capacity to influence others' judgements and practices. These concepts can diverge. Embedding denotes the social conditions that shape processing. 'Module' means relative functional specialisation, not a separate brain organ.

CAVL allows symbolic and subsymbolic implementations without claiming to be a complete hybrid model. ACT-R combines declarative chunks, productions, activation, and utility (Anderson & Lebiere, 2003). CLARION provides an account of explicit and implicit learning and reasoning (Hélie & Sun, 2014a , 2014b; Sun, 2012). CAVL instead specifies the interfaces linking cognition to communicated claims, attributed authority, and unequal participation. Implementing these interfaces computationally is future work.

2.2 Integration by explanatory transition

CAVL is a problem-driven synthesis, not a systematic review. A construct belongs in the architecture when it explains or constrains a transition in vigilant learning. Three rules guide integration. First, exposure, memory, private uptake, and public expression remain distinct if their causes or effects can differ. Second, structural position affects online processing through specified resources, cues, goals, or opportunities, unless a direct pathway is shown. Third, neural evidence constrains functional theories but does not replace them.

CAVL pursues a bounded version of Newell's programme. It asks whether shared concepts can connect research on memory, persuasion, metacognition, learning, and social epistemology and improve experimental design. This supports calls for stronger theory development in psychology (Greene, 2022). The goal is not to replace controlled experiments. The architecture specifies which variables to manipulate, which outcomes to separate, and which person-task-environment relations may limit generalisation. Ecological adequacy must be tested across settings, not inferred from realistic appearance (Dhami et al., 2004; Miller et al., 2019; Schmuckler, 2001).

Thus, CAVL treats ecological validity as a research question. When moving from simple tasks to multiple sources and interacting learners, experiments should retain, vary, or sample the relations that the theory predicts will matter. Evidence must then show whether the proposed mechanisms generalise. CAVL does not yet establish that they do.

2.3 Relation to adjacent theories

Earlier programmes clarify CAVL's scope. ACT-R, Soar, and CLARION implement learning and reasoning processes (Hélie & Sun, 2014a , 2014b); robotics uses embodiment and autonomy to constrain integration (Morse et al., 2011); Brase (2014) connects evidence across levels. CAVL is less computationally complete. It focuses on how communicated knowledge is evaluated, given weight, and shaped by social structures. It succeeds only if these added interfaces improve prediction.

3 The cognitive learning core

3.1 Declarative and procedural long-term memory

Vigilant learning depends on prior knowledge. Declarative memory contains concepts, causal relations, social roles, and episodes, including source histories (Binder & Desai, 2011; Squire, 2004). Procedural memory supports habits, scripts, search routines, and practiced evaluation. These functions are distinct but work together.

Knowledge affects how a message is understood, compared with evidence, and checked for contradictions. Expertise is organised knowledge, not merely accumulated facts. Schemas and procedures change perception and retrieval, allowing complex elements to be processed as meaningful units (Chi et al., 1988; Ericsson & Kintsch, 1995). Expertise therefore reduces effective processing costs without simply enlarging working memory.

Relevant knowledge supports subsequent learning and direct content evaluation. Yet instruction, practice, access, and recognition distribute expertise unequally. In CAVL, knowledge is both an immediate cognitive resource and a product of social trajectories.

Prior knowledge does not guarantee accuracy. False or identity-protective schemas can make poor evidence seem coherent. Expertise can also cause fixation or overconfidence (Bilalić et al., 2008). CAVL predicts benefits from valid, accessible knowledge, not familiarity or prestige alone.

3.2 Working memory as a constrained coordination space

Working memory coordinates active representations under capacity limits (Baddeley, 2000; Cowan, 2001). Learners may need to hold a claim, a task goal, prior knowledge, source cues, alternatives, and reasons for judgement. Executive control sustains goals, updates information, inhibits responses, and changes strategies (Miller & Cohen, 2001; Miyake & Friedman, 2012; Miyake et al., 2000). These functions rely on distributed networks (D'Esposito & Postle, 2015; Miller & Cohen, 2001). Attentional control also involves interacting attention networks (Petersen & Posner, 2012).

These operations compete for limited resources. A learner may understand a claim without comparing sources, recall a source without assessing its argument, or form a judgement without retaining the evidence. Such dissociations need not imply low motivation.

Cognitive Load Theory predicts difficulty when essential elements cannot be coordinated. Well-organised knowledge and practiced routines reduce that cost. More elaboration helps only when relevant knowledge and diagnostic evidence are available. Otherwise extra effort can produce confusion or rationalisation.

3.3 Learning as schema construction, proceduralisation, and feedback

Learning changes the system that produced it. New information may revise a schema, create an episodic trace, strengthen a source-claim link, or alter a procedure. Practice automates routines; feedback updates both factual knowledge and estimates of source reliability. The resulting knowledge then changes later attention, interpretation, and trust.

Vigilance is not a single gate before memory. It can guide initial selection, monitor comprehension, evaluate arguments, and revise judgements after feedback. Its timing varies with knowledge, goals, load, and context. Section 8 specifies conditions under which that flexibility must produce different outcomes.

3.4 Developmental asymmetry

Children show early sensitivity to informant accuracy, competence, and deception (Clément et al., 2004; Harris et al., 2018; Mascaro & Sperber, 2009). Yet these skills do not supply criteria for evaluating a statistical result or an algorithmic recommendation. Culturally acquired knowledge requires instruction and practice (Geary, 2008; Tricot & Sweller, 2014).

Education must therefore build domain knowledge and usable source-checking procedures, not only encourage a generic critical stance. Early vigilance gains value when combined with taught criteria (Clément, 2010; Clément et al., 2004; Harris et al., 2018). Asking novices to 'check sources' may itself increase load when they cannot identify relevant evidence. 4 Epistemic regulation within the learning architecture

4.1 Theory of mind, social knowledge, and source representation

Theory of mind represents what other agents know, believe, intend, or can access. In CAVL, it is a socialinference function, not an isolated innate module. It draws on early capacities and learned knowledge of people and institutions (Frith & Frith, 2003; Premack & Woodruff, 1978). Not every source requires mental-state inference. Documents, institutions, and AI systems can be assessed through provenance, performance, and source-specific cues without attributing human-like intentions.

Semantic memory supports knowledge of roles, norms, and expertise. Episodic memory records encounters and source histories. Source attribution can be reconstructed and may differ from content memory (Johnson & Raye, 1981; Johnson et al., 1993). Procedural routines help interpret confidence, credentials, and disagreement. Working memory coordinates these resources with the present claim.

The learner must often represent both the claim and the social situation. In a debate, attention may cover the interlocutor's perspective, intentions, status, audience reactions, and the cost of dissent. These demands can compete with attention to factual evidence. Domain expertise may therefore be less useful for judging a claim or identifying a competent informant when social-inference demands are high. This is a hypothesis to test, not an established consequence of debate. Social cognition can also help identify knowledgeable and cooperative informants.

Mentalising recruits distributed regions rather than a unique 'trust centre' (Schurz et al., 2014). CAVL expects tasks requiring inferences about a source's knowledge or intentions to differ from tasks involving simple learned labels. This is a hypothesis, not a claim of one-to-one neural mapping.

4.2 Epistemic vigilance as coordinated content and source evaluation

Epistemic vigilance helps people learn from communication without becoming indiscriminately trusting or suspicious (Sperber et al., 2010). Content evaluation concerns plausibility, evidence, coherence, and arguments. Source evaluation concerns competence, access, honesty, independence, and past reliability.

Arguments connect the two when a learner can assess reasons but not independently verify a conclusion.

Content and source evaluations affect one another. Good arguments can improve a new source's reputation; trusted sources can justify provisional acceptance. Disagreement may prompt checking, reduced confidence, or suspension. It may also be settled too quickly by identity or familiarity. Knowledge, goals, and available cues shape these outcomes.

CAVL treats vigilance as coordination among memory, social inference, monitoring, and control, not as one filter between perception and storage. It can shape attention, interpretation, acceptance, and later revision. Nor should epistemic vigilance be confused with sustained-attention vigilance: attention supports evaluation but does not determine epistemic quality.

4.3 Metacognitive monitoring and control

Metacognition monitors and regulates cognition (Dunlosky & Metcalfe, 2009; Flavell, 1979; Nelson & Narens, 1990). Confidence, felt difficulty, and familiarity guide verification and study decisions. These judgements depend on cues, not direct access to knowledge (Koriat, 1997). Fluency can be mistaken for understanding. Metacognitive judgement involves distributed monitoring systems (Fleming & Dolan, 2012).

The functions have different targets. Metacognition evaluates one's own state; theory of mind represents other agents; epistemic vigilance assesses communicated claims and sources. Recognising ignorance may increase source reliance. Doubting a source may increase checking. One can accurately judge personal ignorance yet misidentify the best informant.

Curiosity, surprise, and confusion may also regulate attention and checking. Such epistemic emotions can arise from gaps or conflicts between knowledge and new information (Nerantzaki et al., 2021). CAVL treats their role as an open question, not a reason to add an untested emotion module.

The educational goal is calibrated confidence and adaptive control, not constant reflection. Learners need to know when quick routines suffice and when further checking is worth the cost.

4.4 Two processing regimes rather than two fixed routes

Dual-process research distinguishes more effortful argument evaluation from greater reliance on peripheral cues when motivation or ability is limited (Chaiken, 1980; Evans, 2008; Petty & Cacioppo, 1986). CAVL treats these as processing regimes, not rigid systems. 'Central', 'systematic', and 'controlled' are not exact synonyms; nor are 'automatic', 'heuristic', and 'peripheral'.

No functional component maps exclusively onto either processing regime. Source evaluation can be highly elaborated, whereas content evaluation can become rapid and automatized through expertise.

A low-cost regime often uses familiarity, fluency, credentials, status, consensus, and other rapid cues. It can work well when cues track competence or direct verification is impossible. It can mislead when prestige or social reward diverges from evidence.

An elaborated regime examines content, evidence, and inference more closely. It can improve discrimination when learners have the motivation, knowledge, capacity, and evidence to use it. Effort alone is no guarantee: reasoning may defend prior commitments or selectively recruit evidence (Ditto & Lopez, 1992; Kunda, 1990).

Regimes can alternate. A source cue may attract attention; a contradiction may trigger checking; an expert routine may then solve the problem quickly. Knowledge reduces the cost of content evaluation. Load and time pressure may favour cheaper cues. Processing costs chiefly affect attention and verification; reputational costs chiefly affect public responses. Social threats may also reduce private scrutiny. Still, silence or conformity does not necessarily imply changed private belief. 5 Effective relevance, epistemic weight, and uptake

5.1 Relevance as effects relative to effort

Relevance Theory relates positive cognitive effects to the effort needed to obtain them (Sperber & Wilson, 1995; Wilson & Sperber, 2004). It does not provide a validated numerical equality. Relevant effects include justified conclusions, revised assumptions, and useful implications. Relevance varies with knowledge, goals, and context. Information may be socially rewarded without accuracy being irrelevant (Altay & Mercier, 2020).

Understanding a claim does not imply accepting it. A rejected claim may still help identify manipulation or revise an estimate of source reliability. Relevance and epistemic assessment interact (Sperber et al., 2010): an uncertain but consequential claim may warrant checking, while an unreliable claim may teach us something about its source.

5.2 Conceptual constraints on relevance, epistemic weight, and uptake

CAVL sets four constraints for later formal models:

First, processing depends on knowledge, goals, capacity, verification costs, and social stakes. Stakes can alter attention without necessarily changing epistemic weight.

Second, epistemic weight depends on content, arguments, sources, corroboration, and valid knowledge. Attributed authority is one input. Relevance concerns whether processing is worth the effort; epistemic weight concerns a claim's acceptability. Credit is recognition granted to the source or contribution, not a synonym for the claim's epistemic weight.

Third, these assessments permit different private outcomes: acceptance, rejection, qualification, suspension, conditional storage, refutation, or source updating.

Fourth, public expression also depends on identity, audience, power, and reputational cost. Endorsement is therefore not a direct measure of belief or durable learning.

These distinctions constrain later measurements and models; they do not yet specify algorithms or parameters.

5.3 From weighting to multiple forms of uptake

Epistemic weighting can lead to acceptance, rejection, suspension, or conditional retention. Learning may change factual knowledge, procedures, or beliefs about a source. Public endorsement is separate: someone may privately recognise strong evidence yet remain silent, or conform without lasting belief change.

Measurement must distinguish exposure, attention, retention, evaluation, uptake, public expression, and delayed learning. Clicks, viewing time, and speaking turns are activity traces, not universal measures of knowledge or belief.

6 Double social embedding

6.1 Interactional embedding: from competence to authority

When learners cannot check content directly, they depend on others' knowledge. Actual competence is what a source can validly know or do; subjective competence is self-assessed knowledge; perceived competence is an observer's attribution. Epistemic weight concerns the acceptability of a claim. Epistemic credit concerns social recognition granted to a source or contribution. Authority is a more stable capacity to influence judgement or action. These quantities can differ: someone may privately accept an argument while withholding public credit from its source.

Competence attributions rely on incomplete evidence: credentials, arguments, past accuracy, institutional status, confidence, fluency, and stereotypes. Source credibility affects persuasion (Hovland & Weiss, 1951). Accurate knowledge of who knows what helps groups (Hollingshead, 2000). Status creates competence expectations that affect influence (Berger et al., 2002; Foschi, 2000). Prestige and dominance are distinct routes to rank, neither identical to competence (Cheng et al., 2013; Henrich & Gil-White, 2001).

CAVL distinguishes four relations: source cues inform perceived competence; competence judgements and arguments shape the epistemic weight of claims; social interaction allocates credit to sources and contributions; and repeated allocations of credit affect influence, opportunities, and subsequent learning. These relations can form feedback loops across episodes, rather than a fixed sequence.

6.2 Structural embedding of expertise and dispositions

Expertise develops through education, practice, language, resources, and access to information. Habitus describes durable dispositions shaped by social trajectories; it is not equivalent to socioeconomic status (Bourdieu, 1990). Cognitive sociology shows how institutional categories and routines become cognitive resources (Cicourel, 1974; DiMaggio, 1997).

Structural effects on an episode often pass through knowledge, habits, goals, cue access, and verification cost. Such mediation does not make structural effects weak. Institutional trust may reflect long experience, whereas a conflict-of-interest notice may change a judgement immediately. Artefacts and social organisation also serve as resources within activity (Hollan et al., 2000; Hutchins, 1995).

The relation is recursive. Social trajectories shape expertise and opportunities to display it. Interactions translate competence and stereotypes into credit. Credit then affects attention, participation, feedback, and later learning. Repetition can reinforce or alter the distribution of knowledge.

6.3 Social goals and the cost of epistemic quality

Cooperation, competition, identity, and status shape epistemic action. Cooperation can support explanations but also conformity. Competition can discourage sharing yet increase error detection.

Research on motivated information processing and sociocognitive conflict shows how relational and epistemic goals combine (Darnon et al., 2006 (Darnon et al., , 2007;; De Dreu et al., 2008).

Truth is not always socially strategic. A well-supported claim may threaten belonging; expressing it may challenge hierarchy; admitting doubt may weaken perceived authority. These costs can divert attention or separate private understanding from public speech. They are part of the phenomenon, not mere noise.

6.4 Epistemic injustice as a sociocognitive process

Testimonial injustice involves an unjustified credibility deficit caused by prejudice; silencing prevents contributions from receiving uptake (Dotson, 2011; Fricker, 2007). Recent work connects these concerns to source-evaluation criteria and multiple-document use (Chinn et al., 2026; Knight et al., 2026). CAVL proposes this possible pathway: unequal structural conditions → differences in expertise, dispositions, exposure, and self-presentation → imperfect attribution of competence → biased epistemic weight and authority → unequal attention, memory, uptake, and influence → reproduction of the original distribution.

Limited resources may make status or familiarity efficient cues even when they lead to inaccurate or unfair outcomes (Dosso et al., 2025). CAVL therefore separates efficiency, accuracy, calibration, discrimination, and justice. Improving one may harm another: credentials can support checking but reinforce hierarchy; added provenance may help experts but overload novices. These trade-offs require empirical study. This is not a fixed sequence. Vigilance may guide initial attention, comprehension, explicit comparison, or later revision. Source information may arrive before content or be reconstructed afterward. Heuristics may trigger elaboration; expertise can make content checks fast. Feedback can reopen a judgement.

CAVL nonetheless predicts conditions. Limited valid knowledge should increase reliance on accessible source cues. Load should strengthen that pattern when cues are cheaper, although expert routines may reduce the cost. Accuracy motivation should help only when useful evidence can be processed. High social costs should increase differences between private and public judgements. These predictions constrain the architecture.

7.2 Functional differentiation and biological constraints

CAVL proposes partly separable functions, not isolated brain modules. Memory, control, mentalising, metacognition, and valuation rely on interacting networks. Epistemic vigilance has no established dedicated network; it describes their coordination when claims and sources are evaluated.

Neural evidence can test whether proposed functions dissociate across tasks, but shared activation neither proves nor refutes a functional distinction. Behavioural and time-resolved measures remain necessary.

7.3 Three indispensable dissociations

CAVL requires three distinctions. First, exposure, comprehension, memory, evaluation, uptake, and durable learning are not interchangeable. Second, knowledge, confidence, perceived expertise, credit, authority, and influence may diverge. Third, structural conditions and within-episode processes occupy different explanatory levels even when they interact. These distinctions guide both measurement and intervention.

8 Testable propositions and decisive evidence

An architecture must make predictions that competing explanations might fail. The individual mechanisms are not unique to CAVL. Its distinctive predictions concern their joint effects, conditional interactions, and feedback across cognitive, interactional, and structural levels. Crucially, effects found in simplified tasks may persist, weaken, or reverse when other mechanisms operate at the same time. CAVL offers six propositions, each with a discriminating test and a reason for major revision.

8.1 The critical benchmark design

The initial benchmark crosses independently manipulated argument quality and attributed authority with a valid measure of domain knowledge. Where possible, training should also vary knowledge. Outcomes should include attention, confidence, private acceptance, source updating, memory or delayed learning, and public endorsement. A single answer cannot distinguish learning from persuasion or conformity.

Ecological testing does not require recreating a whole classroom or debate. It requires preserving or varying the person-task-environment relations expected to change processing. Studies could start with controlled quality-by-authority contrasts, add social stakes and source-checking opportunities, and then vary source reliability, institutional cues, and verification costs. Preregistered comparisons would ask whether effects isolated under simple conditions still hold when cognitive and social mechanisms interact (Dhami et al., 2004; Miller et al., 2019).

CAVL predicts an interaction among quality, authority, and knowledge. Learners with little valid knowledge should rely more on attributed authority. As knowledge grows, diagnostic content should matter more and misleading authority less, provided evidence and capacity are available. However, expertise may not confer the same advantage under high social-inference demands. For example, during debate, attention to another person's intentions, status, or reactions may compete with domain evaluation. A critical test would independently vary knowledge, the demands of social inference, and processing resources, then measure both evidence discrimination and source-competence judgements. Even experts depend on testimony outside their expertise.

Load, accuracy motivation, and social cost should first be tested in focused designs rather than one overlarge factorial study. Load may increase reliance on cheaper cues, although automated expertise can reduce this tendency. Accuracy goals help only with usable evidence. Social cost should chiefly affect the gap between private and public judgement, but may also redirect private attention. These are conditional predictions to test across tasks, not universal effects. Process and delayed measures are needed to distinguish mechanisms.

Ecological generalisation and causal identification are separate goals. Environmental variation tests where effects persist. Random assignment, knowledge assessment, and process measures help identify why they change. Neither naturalistic appearance nor a large sample establishes generalisation. Failed transfers and rival explanations should be reported (Muthukrishna & Henrich, 2019; Schmuckler, 2001).

8.2 What would falsify rather than merely qualify CAVL

CAVL would need revision if knowledge failed to change discrimination or cue use across suitable tasks; if quality and authority could not be experimentally separated; or if confidence, private uptake, memory, and public expression consistently formed one invariant outcome. Testing recursive social embedding also requires repeated episodes or longitudinal evidence. Prespecified tests must examine both whether structural resources shape the allocation of credit and whether this credit changes later access, participation, learning, and subsequent credit. Improved statistical fit alone would not establish mediation or feedback.

A simpler model that explains these patterns equally well would also weaken CAVL. Shared neural activation would not: the architecture claims functional, not anatomical, distinctions.

8.3 Relation to epistemic growth research

SEGL suggests an additional test. Interventions that alter epistemic aims or virtues should affect checking, persistence, confidence, or processing within an episode. Effects should depend on knowledge, load, and provenance. Humility cannot reveal evidence that is unavailable; motivation cannot remove memory limits. Longitudinal work could test whether successful regulation fosters the durable resources described by SEGL (Orona & Greene, 2026).

9 Educational, institutional, and technological implications

9.1 Epistemic autonomy as regulated dependence

CAVL defines epistemic autonomy as regulated dependence. Learners need to judge when they can assess content, when expert testimony is warranted, when to seek independent support, and when to suspend judgement. They must also distinguish private belief from socially strategic expression.

Four capacities support this goal: domain knowledge; links between claims and their sources; calibrated monitoring of effort and confidence; and regulation of social costs around disagreement. Generic checklists cannot replace domain knowledge. Nor should a socially safe response be confused with an epistemically warranted one.

Instruction can make source cues and evaluation routines visible enough for learners to regulate them. Multiple-document teaching offers ways to connect claims, sources, and corroboration (Barzilai & Zohar, 2012; Rouet, 2006). Learners can compare initial judgements with evidence and practise shifting between rapid and elaborated evaluation. Useful automatic routines should be trained, not suppressed.

Expertise can improve discrimination yet also foster fixation or overconfidence. Encouraging alternative explanations and explicit uncertainty can reduce these costs without treating knowledge as suspect.

9.2 Institutions of epistemic vigilance

Institutions regulate learning by shaping access, source cues, costs, incentives, and speaking rights. Peer review, professional certification, classroom norms, and platform provenance can support vigilance. They are social arrangements, not cognitive modules.

Credentials and reputations provide low-cost cues. Evidence displays, structured comparisons, and protected opportunities to revise support elaboration. Repeated use can also build knowledge and regulatory routines.

Such support may backfire. Added provenance can overload novices; credentials may become status shortcuts; public justification may threaten vulnerable participants. Interventions should assess accuracy, calibration, cognitive cost, authority alignment, and justice.

9.3 Digital environments

Digital systems shape exposure and verification costs (Allen et al., 2026; List & Braasch, 2026). Search rankings, metrics, and conversational agents allocate attention and perceived authority. Fluent answers may aid comprehension while hiding uncertainty or source dependence. Effects vary with knowledge and task goals.

Interfaces should preserve provenance, show uncertainty and independent evidence, and make comparisons affordable. They should distinguish understanding, believing, verifying, and sharing rather than infer learning from clicks alone.

10 Boundaries, limitations, and future formalisation CAVL addresses learning from communicated claims, testimony, documents, discussion, and mediated information. A source may be a person, text, platform, or institution. The architecture does not replace theories of basic perception, motor learning, or conditioning, although such processes may contribute to vigilant learning.

CAVL remains precomputational: it specifies functional constraints, not executable rules or parameter estimates. Agent-based models could formalise knowledge, source cues, resource limits, and social relations. But a model matching the diagram would not validate it. Calibration, identifiability, comparison with simpler alternatives, and tests across independent environments would be needed (Dhami et al., 2004; Miller et al., 2019).

A cumulative programme should combine focused experiments, systematically varied tasks, and computational implementations. Ethnographic and sociological work can identify relevant meanings and constraints for experimental designs. Each method contributes different evidence; none alone guarantees causal or ecological validity.

Open issues include argument representation, emotion, language, collective memory, cultural variation, and institutional change. Epistemic emotions interact with metacognition (Nerantzaki et al., 2021), but CAVL does not posit a separate emotion module. Relevance, motivation, and valuation need clearer computational relations. Its two processing regimes are poles of a continuum, not independent systems.

Efficiency, accuracy, and justice may conflict. True beliefs can be costly to express; strategic responses may protect people without reflecting private learning. CAVL describes these tensions but does not derive normative standards from its mechanisms. Justified evidence, calibrated confidence, and fair participation remain important, contested aims.

11 Conclusion

CAVL offers a bounded response to Newell's challenge. It connects separately studied mechanisms where they operate together during social learning. Memory without provenance, authority without cognitive evaluation, and social structure without proximal mechanisms each leave part of the phenomenon unexplained. CAVL joins these processes in one functional account without replacing specialised theories.

Its three core claims concern knowledge-dependent arbitration between content and authority, divergence between private uptake and public expression, and feedback between social resources and epistemic credit. The test of integration is whether component effects remain, change, or reverse when mechanisms co-occur under ecologically informative constraints. Together the claims guide experiments on how mechanisms interact, not merely whether isolated effects exist.

Ecological adequacy is a test, not a property claimed by the diagram. Evidence across tasks and social conditions must show what generalises. Simpler rival explanations, failed predicted dissociations, or unsupported structure-interaction pathways would require revision. CAVL aims to make socially embedded vigilant learning both cognitively specified and experimentally testable.
