Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. Some differences come from the ground truth: it lacks p-values with a correction name (“p Bonf = .061”), and counts partial eta squared written with its subscript after it (“η 2 p = .05”), “p = .05”, and some table notes and figure legends (“† p < .10, *p < .05”), which this module leaves out, as non-significant p-values. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. Abstract

The growing interest in using large language models (LLMs) as human surrogates and as components of generative agent-based models (GABMs) raises a fundamental question for computational social science: what does it mean to model a person? The present paper surveys current work that uses LLMs to simulate human participants, with particular attention to their ability to reproduce findings of social psychology experiments. We document both the recurring challenges and the attempts to mitigate them. We then examine the possibility of endowing LLMs with a synthetic self by focusing on two psychological approaches to selfhood: trait-based personality and narrative identity. Our survey suggests that current LLM agents perform relatively well when selfhood is operationalized by rigidly structured means such as personality questionnaires, but are less reliable when it is induced by an unfolding narrative. We relate this problem to the characterization of LLMs as Decontextualized, Engineered, Anonymized, and Disembodies (DEAD) and consider its implications for GABMs. Drawing on the distinction between makeist and non-makeist takes on simulation, we argue that while GABMs may serve as a powerful tool for exploring how certain assumptions give rise to emergent phenomena, they do not necessarily constitute psychologically valid models of human behavior.

1 Introduction

In agent-based modeling (ABM), computational agents are initialized with a set of rules and allowed to interact with one another and with a simulated environment, producing behavioral patterns that emerge from these interactions (Holland & Miller, 1991; Miller & Page, 2007). A central feature of ABMs is in their focus on dynamics through which individual-level rules and interactions generate population-level patterns of behavior. These rules are typically explicitly specified and tested against empirical observations (Laver & Sergenti, 2011). The emergence of large language models (LLMs) introduces a fundamentally different approach to specifying agent behavior. Rather than relying on explicit rules governing agent behavior, researchers can now leverage linguistic, social, and cultural priors incorporated into, and interpreted by, LLMs. This opens up the possibility for agents to react and adapt more flexibly to the contextual cues, situations, and events. A prominent early work by Park et al. (2023) showed that LLM-powered agents could coordinate and develop social relationships in an artificial society. Agents defined by short natural language descriptions of their identities, occupations, and relationships were capable of independently spreading information about a party, showing up for it, and forming new relationships. Subsequent work extended this approach from individual socializing to the emergence of collective phenomena. For example, a study by Ren et al. (2024) showed that seeding social norms in the descriptions of only three out of ten agents can result in a spread of these norms through the artificial society.

Fully realizing the promise of LLMs in advancing agent-based modeling and computational social science in general requires that LLM-based agents be made psychologically realistic. Here, we focus on one aspect of this challenge, namely, endowing an LLM-based agent with a psychologically credible self. To that end, we offer a brief survey of the interdisciplinary literature relevant to building believable selves for generative agents. We first consider the extent to which LLMs can be made to reproduce the responses of human subjects in behavioral experiments. We then draw on the research into personality and narrative identity, which reveals how human selves are conceptualized and modeled in psychology. By synthesizing insights from those sources, we identify methodological challenges inherent in applying psychology to the construction of LLM-based agents and discuss their implications for generative agent-based modeling.

2 LLMs as Human Surrogates

Following the rise of LLMs, a growing body of research has explored the possibilities of using them as substitutes for human participants, through approaches such as persona-based steering (Dillion et al., 2023). Argyle et al. (2023) coined the term algorithmic fidelity to describe the extent to which LLMs can replicate the ideas, attitudes, and socio-cultural contexts of diverse human subpopulations. In this section, we review attempts to use LLMs as human surrogates, focusing first on the challenges these studies have identified and on subsequent efforts to mitigate them.

2.1 Challenges of Using LLMs as Human Surrogates

One early line of research examined whether LLMs could predict the outcomes of behavioral experiments. Lippert et al. (2024) successfully used LLMs as human surrogates to predict the results of a complex behavioral experiment on emotions, gender, and social perception before data collection. However, the authors acknowledged that these predictions reflected existing knowledge rather than providing new empirical evidence. More recent work showed that LLMs could also predict the results of unpublished social science experiments that were unavailable before the models' training cutoffs, although they tended to overestimate experimental effect sizes (Ashokkumar et al., 2026). Moving beyond prediction, a large-scale replication of top-journal social science experiments using LLMs yielded larger effect sizes and a higher proportions of statistically significant relationships than those reported in the original human studies (Cui et al., 2025). These findings demonstrate that LLMs can reproduce aggregate experimental patterns, but also raise questions about whether such performance reflects genuine behavioral similarity on the individual level or models' ability to reproduce patterns encoded in their training data.

2.1.1 Prompt sensitivity and instability

Prompting presents a major challenge to the use of LLMs as human surrogates. Röttger et al. (2024) found that LLM performance on the Political Compass Test (PCT) varies significantly depending on whether questions are presented in multiple-choice or open-ended prompts. Specifically, LLMs lacked paraphrase robustness and tended to default to neutrality or ambivalence when answering open-ended questions. Similarly, Bisbee et al. (2024) showed that minor changes in persona prompting produce statistically different results. Furthermore temporal instability of LLM prompting is another concern, i.e. running the exact same prompt over a three-month period, led to inconsistent outputs.

Prompting can also introduce representational problems when models are asked to simulate members of sensitive identity groups. Such prompts can produce reductionist or stereotypical representations (Cui et al., 2025; Lee et al., 2024; A. Wang et al., 2025). In addition to biases that are pervasive in training corpora, these problems may stem in part from data contamination and memorization, which can artificially inflate performance metrics (Gao et al., 2025). Even within a single country such as the US, off-the-shelf LLMs flatten fine-grained cultural differences, e.g. by failing to capture regional variations in individualism versus collectivism (Havaldar et al., 2024).

2.1.2 Demographic representation and subgroup diversity

Faithful demographic representation remains an equally problematic challenge. A. Wang et al. (2025) examined LLM outputs across 16 US demographic groups spanning race, age, gender, and disability. They found that models generate stereotypical portrayals and lacked both within-group and between-group diversity. More broadly, a systematic review revealed that research evaluating demographic representativeness focuses overwhelmingly on the United States, while leaving substantial uncertainty about how well LLMs represent populations in other cultural contexts (Sen et al., 2025).

Evidence from studies of public opinion further illustrates these limitations. In the US context, GPT-3 successfully replicated real-world opinion distributions surrounding elections. In contrast, research examining German public opinion showed that LLMs performed better for groups with relatively homogeneous opinions and struggled when within-group diversity was high (Ma et al., 2025). The models also aligned more closely with left-wing and Green supporters. More importantly, adding or removing demographic variables, such as age, education, or party preference, significantly altered opinions generated by the models (Ma et al., 2025).

Notably, Dominguez-Olmedo et al. (2024) caution against interpreting survey-based alignment scores as direct evidence that LLMs accurately represent human values. They argue that these scores often reflect how varied or balanced a human subgroup's responses are. In this sense, alignment may function as a proxy for subgroup diversity rather than a direct measure of representational fidelity.

2.2 Attempts to Mitigate LLM Limitations

Researchers have explored several strategies to address the limitations of LLMs as human surrogates, including fine-tuning, instruction tuning, prompt engineering, and the use of alternative training data.

2.2.1 Instruction tuning and prompt-based approaches

One approach in the literature to combat the biases uses instruction tuning to make model biases explicit and transparent rather than attempting to eliminate them. This allows users to compare outputs across different perspectives, such as political ideology, gender, age, or geographic background (Haller et al., 2024).

Prompt design has also been investigated as a means of reducing representational bias. Lutz et al. (2025) found that interview-style formatting and name-based priming can help reduce stereotyping while improving alignment with human responses. Interestingly, their study also found that smaller language models displayed less stereotyping than larger counterparts, suggesting that increasing model scale does not necessarily improve demographic representativeness.

2.2.2 Fine-tuning on human behavioral data and more

A major effort led to the construction of the Centaur model, which was fine-tuned on over 10 million human choices across 160 psychological experiments with 60,000+ participants (Binz et al., 2025). The goal was to improve the model's decision making by directly training it on fine-grained behavioral data. However, a study using a simple economic game to evaluate strategic reasoning found that Centaur's reasoning (as well as that of other LLMs tested) continued to differ from that of human subjects (Gao et al., 2025). Similar shortcomings appeared when Centaur was presented with survey questions containing minor wording changes or new contextual information (Schröder et al., 2025). These findings suggest that training on a large quantity of domain-specific data does not necessarily guarantee robustness to changes in task formulation or context. However, fine-tuning models on large-scale public opinion survey data has substantially improved their ability to predict opinion distributions (Suh et al., 2025). Compared to prompt-based methods, fine-tuned models match real human responses more closely and generalize better to unseen surveys and demographic subpopulations. Thus, the effectiveness of fine-tuning may depend on the type of human behavior modeled and the nature of the training data.

It is important to note that the attempts to fine-tune the models on social science experimental data are not necessarily free from bias. Social science has been long criticized for its overrepresentation of the opinions of Western, Educated, Industrialized, Rich, Democratic (WEIRD) populations (Henrich et al., 2010). To address flattened outputs and representational harms, one opinion piece advocated training LLMs on historical data. This strategy aims to look beyond WEIRD nations and provide behavioral insights into ancient societies (Varnum et al., 2024). However, the authors acknowledge a critical limitation: benchmark evaluations are impossible for ancient populations.

3 LLMs and Selfhood

There is a tension between, on the one hand, the flexibility of LLMs' responses to prompts and, on the other hand, the relative stability that one expects from a persona or a self. This tension leads to a question: How best to conceptualize the synthetic self of an LLM agent? Unlike traditional rule-based agents, whose behavior is driven by a limited set of explicit rules, LLM agents can draw on the rich priors encoded in their pretrained parameters. These priors can be supplemented by endowing the LLM agent with personality characteristics, memories, and social norms, all textually encoded, as in the work of Park et al. (2023) and Ren et al. (2024). The resulting "personality" of the agent does, however, rely on certain assumptions about what constitutes the self, which, typically, remain tacit. To examine what is gained and what is lost when the the psychological construct of the self is translated into LLM-based agents, this section considers two prominent approaches to selfhood: personality and narrative identity.

3.1 Defining and Measuring Personality

Personality is the dynamic organization of the psychophysical systems that determines the behavior and thoughts within the individual (Allport, 1937). Personality can be conceptualized through several taxonomies that differ in both the dimensions they identify and the way individual differences are represented. One of the most prominent approaches is the Five-Factor Model, or Big Five, which describes personality along five broad dimensions: Openness, Conscientiousness, Extroversion, Agreeableness, and Neuroticism (John & Srivastava, 1999). The model emerged from the lexical hypothesis, which proposes that important and socially relevant personality differences become encoded in natural language and can therefore be identified through the analysis of personalitydescriptive terms (Costa & McCrae, 1992; Goldberg, 1990). In contrast to categorical typologies, the Big Five represents traits as continuous dimensions and can further distinguish more finegrained facets within each dimension (Fang et al., 2023; John & Srivastava, 1999). Another widely known taxonomy is the Myers-Briggs Type Indicator (MBTI), which classifies individuals along four dichotomous dimensions: Extroversion/Introversion, Sensing/Intuition, Thinking/Feeling, and Judging/Perceiving (Briggs Myers et al., 1998). Although MBTI remains popular in applied and nonacademic contexts, the Big Five has a substantially stronger empirical and psychometric foundation and is more widely accepted within personality psychology (Fang et al., 2023). At the same time, the Big Five is not necessarily an exhaustive representation of personality. Research based on lexical studies across multiple languages has identified a sixth dimension, Honesty-Humility, leading to the development of the HEXACO model (Ashton & Lee, 2007). Other taxonomies likewise propose additional or alternative dimensions, illustrating that the structure of personality depends partly on the theoretical and empirical framework used to define it. For a broader review of personality taxonomies and alternatives to the Big Five, see (Feher & Vernon, 2021).

3.1.1 LLMs and personality

Before the emergence of LLMs, machine learning methods were already being used to infer personality traits from people's digital footprints (Azucar et al., 2018; Kosinski et al., 2013), including CVs and short essays for applications such as recruitment. However, the representations used by these methods are not necessarily neutral. For example, word embeddings can encode social stereotypes present in their training data (Bolukbasi et al., 2016). The development of LLMs has therefore not introduced the problem of bias in personality inference as such; rather, it has expanded both the methods available for personality assessment and the questions about what exactly these assessments measure.

Early research examined LLMs as the subjects of personality assessment. Miotto et al. (2022) administered the HEXACO questionnaire to GPT-3 and found personality scores broadly similar to those observed in human samples. However, varying the model's temperature produced different response patterns, raising questions about the stability of these measurements. Other work has examined whether human personality taxonomies can be inferred from natural language. Stajner and Yenikent (2021) found that tweets often contained ambiguous or insufficient linguistic cues for MBTI classification. The Big Five may be more amenable to linguistic inference because its development is closely connected to the lexical hypothesis (Fang et al., 2023). Nevertheless, LLM-based inference of the Big Five has also produced systematic discrepancies. Comparing LLM-inferred personality scores with self-report measures, Peters and Matz (2024) found that GPT tended to overestimate Neuroticism and underestimate Conscientiousness and Agreeableness. Its performance was not better than that of the supervised machine learning models trained specifically for personality inference. The accuracy also varied across demographic groups, with higher accuracy for women and younger participants, potentially reflecting differences in online self-expression. Yang et al. (2023) further explored whether chain-of-thought prompting could improve personality inference from text. Their approach asked the LLM to answer individual questionnaire items before using these responses to infer the overall personality traits. This method outperformed standard prompting and other baseline models, although Neuroticism was again overestimated.

Other studies have questioned whether personality assessment can be applied to language models in the same way as to humans. Pellert et al. (2024) avoided GPT-style generative models because of concerns about stochastic outputs, prompt sensitivity, and limited transparency. Instead, they used BERT-family encoder models and assessed personality and other psychological traits with the Big Five and Short Dark Tetrad inventories. The English-language models produced relatively homogeneous profiles, characterized by high Agreeableness and Extroversion and low Neuroticism. The German models showed greater variation, with two models scoring highly on Openness, Extroversion, and Conscientiousness, while another was high primarily in Conscientiousness. These findings suggest that measured personality profiles can vary across both models and languages. Serapio-García et al. (2025) addressed this issue more directly by applying psychometric validation to LLM personality assessment. They compared the Big Five with a second personality measure (International Personality Item Pool-NEO (Goldberg, 1999)), examined whether manipulating one trait left the other traits relatively stable, and tested whether measured traits were associated with theoretically related variables. They found stronger reliability and validity in larger and instruction-tuned models. This suggests that the interpretability of LLM personality scores depends not only on the personality inventory but also on characteristics of the model itself.

A different line of research treats LLMs not as the subjects of personality assessment, but as tools for assessing personality in other models or in humans. Hu et al. (2024), for example, used the broader linguistic knowledge of LLMs to generate richer personality-related representations and explanations, which were then used to train a smaller language model for personality detection. LLMs can be treated as the subjects of personality inventories, or used as external assessors of personality from text. In particular, questionnaire-style probes capture a structured response to predefined items, whereas open-ended narratives provide a less constrained behavioral trace. The choice of operationalization may therefore affect the resulting personality profile. This issue motivates the next section, which examines the possibility of characterizing personality through narrative rather than by means of standardized and fixed personality inventories.

3.2 The Narrative Self

This alternative approach is rooted in the notion of narrative identity -an internalized and evolving life story that integrates perspectives from developmental, cognitive, personality, and cultural psychology (McAdams, 2001). People continually revise these life stories to maintain an up-to-date view of the self (Greenwald, 1980) and to preserve a sense of continuity over time (Gergen & Gergen, 1997).

The narrative self selects events that are central to defining one's identity and organizes them into a coherent and purposeful life story. In this respect, it differs from autobiographical memory. While autobiographical memory encompasses a broad range of personal experiences, the narrative self selectively draws on these experiences and organizes them temporally and thematically to construct an account of who one is (McAdams, 2001). Although autobiographical memories provide an important building block for narrative identity, research suggests that broader autobiographical structures, such as life chapters or mini-narratives, play a more central role in organizing the life story than individual memories alone (Thomsen, 2009). Further evidence that narrative identity extends beyond autobiographical or episodic memory comes from studies of amnesic patients. Despite being unable to recall specific past events, some amnesic patients can provide relatively accurate self-ratings of their personality, suggesting that aspects of self-knowledge and identity can persist independently of episodic recollection (Klein et al., 1996; Tulving, 1993).

Compared with other aspects of personality, such as traits that can be shared across individuals, narrative identity provides a more distinctive and first-person perspective on the self (McAdams, 2021). It captures the individual's interpretation of their own experiences and the way those experiences are integrated into a meaningful account of who they are. In this sense, narrative identity reflects the "self as a scientist" metaphor in which individuals construct, interpret, and continuously update an internal account of themselves based on their experiences.

3.2.1 Measuring the narrative self

Similar to the trait-disposition approach to personality, there have been attempts to taxonomize this view on the self as well. The most common method of measuring narrative identity is to ask participants to narrate their stories, which researchers then code for content and structure. In the literature, there are four broad categories of narrative variables: motivational themes (protagonists' goals, agentic or communal values), affective themes (emotional shifts, particularly redemption and contamination), integrative meaning (evaluation and meaning making of life experiences), and structural elements (such as biographical, temporal, and causal coherence) (Adler et al., 2016; Baerger & McAdams, 1999; Bauer & McAdams, 2004; Habermas & Bluck, 2000; McAdams et al., 1996 McAdams et al., , 2001;; Pals, 2006). More recently, these dimensions have been organized into a Big Three taxonomy: motivational and affective themes, structural elements capturing temporal coherence and overall narrative architecture, and autobiographical reasoning encompassing self-reflection and thematic coherence (McLean et al., 2020).

There are two main approaches to assessing narrative identity: researcher-coded narratives and self-report measures. In the former, participants generate life stories that researchers then code for narrative features, while in the latter, participants evaluate their own narratives (after narrating) or mental representations (without narrating) of their narratives (Holm & Thomsen, 2018; McAdams, 2018). These approaches are not necessarily interchangeable, as research finds discrepancy between the authority of experience (self-rating) and the authority of experts (researcher coding) (Gehrt et al., 2024; Grysman et al., 2017; Josselson, 2011). This divergence may be explained by the different identity-related processes being captured, such as self ratings capturing conscious ones and researcher-coded narratives capturing less conscious ones while concentrating more on the story (Grysman et al., 2017; McAdams, 2018). Nevertheless, the two methods can complement each other; greater congruence between explicit and implicit measures has been associated with greater personality integration (Brunstein, 2008; Panattoni & McLean, 2018).

A special issue of Journal of Personality examined the narratives of social change agents, seeking a correlation between diverse phenomenological experiences and agentic, change-oriented life trajectories (Adler & Singer, 2023). In particular, studies of Joe Biden, Michele Obama, COVID-19 volunteers, and Cori Bush identified redemptive, motivational, or proactive coping themes in their narratives (McAdams, 2023; Nourkova & Gofman, 2023; Wilson & Hill, 2023; Winston-Proctor, 2023). On a related note, psychotherapy research found that increases in narrative agency, but not in coherence, preceded improvements in mental health (Adler, 2012). These findings align with the social cognitive theory, which conceptualizes agency as the capacity to exercise choice and control through action and reflection (Bandura, 1986 (Bandura, , 2006)). However, agency is also shaped by societal structures in which individuals are embedded, making the internalization of such structures an important consideration in personal narratives (McLean & Syed, 2015). et al. (2024) found that LLM-generated narratives lacked suspense and arousal, showed less diversity, and were less likely to follow negative story arcs. A recent study by Mansfield et al. (2026) similarly compared AI-and human-generated narratives and identified two key findings. First, AI-generated narratives were highly redemptive. The authors attribute this tendency partly to the cultural content of the training data, as American cultural narratives tend to favor redemptive life stories (Syed & McLean, 2022). They further argue that this tendency may be reinforced by the large amount of digital data in which people publicly share positive or redemptive experiences, while less redemptive narratives are more likely to remain in research settings rather than being shared online (Pasupathi et al., 2009). Second, Mansfield et al. (2026) found that people could distinguish not only AI-generated narratives from human-generated narratives, but also AI-generated interpretations of human narratives from human interpretations of those same narratives.

4 Concluding Remarks and Implications for Generative Agent-Based Modeling

In this paper, we reviewed the literature on using LLMs as human proxies, with particular attention to their performance in social science experiments, the problems identified by researchers, and the strategies proposed to mitigate them. We then examined ways to impart a synthetic self to LLM systems from two perspectives: (i) personality dimensions and (ii) narrative identity. Across both literatures, the limitations and biases of training data emerged as a recurring concern. However, from a cognitive science perspective, our brief review also reveals a different and more fundamental problem: the aspects of selfhood that are easiest to operationalize for LLMs may not be the aspects of selfhood that are most important for understanding human beings.

4.1 The DEAD Self

As we saw, personality questionnaires and narratives of the self represent two different approaches to studying selfhood (McAdams & Pals, 2006). Measures such as the HEXACO and Big Five are rooted in the trait-dispositional approach, which conceptualizes personality primarily in terms of relatively stable individual differences. Narrative identity, in contrast, belongs to a social-cognitive approach that emphasizes the construction of the self through the interpretation of experiences and its embeddedness in social and cultural contexts.

This distinction becomes particularly relevant when considering the methodological conditions under which human characteristics are translated into computational systems. Crockett and Messeri (2026) use the acronym DEAD -Decontextualized, Engineered, Anonymized, and Disembodied -to describe the process whereby cognitive and emotional processes are removed from the contexts, bodies, and social environments in which they naturally occur and are instead studied through computerized tasks. The literature reviewed here suggests a similar pattern in research on LLMbased selves. LLMs perform relatively well when selfhood is reduced to decontextualized and structured tasks, such as personality questionnaires or classification problems. Their performance is less consistent when selfhood is examined through narratives, where identity is expressed through temporally organized experiences, cultural meanings, and interpretations of one's own life.

4.2 Implications for Generative Agent-Based Modeling

Our review of the psychology of selves offers several lessons for generative agent-based modeling.foot_1 First, trait-based agent initialization risks implicitly reproducing the systematic homogeneity in LLM behavior. Thus, an agent assigned a low level of a trait such as Agreeableness or Extroversion may behave similarly to an agent assigned a high level of that trait. Consequently, trait-based initialization cannot be assumed to produce psychologically distinct agents. Researchers need to ensure that differences in the assigned traits actually produce distinguishable behavioral patterns.

Second, narrative-based initialization introduces a potential new bias. Because LLMs draw on learned narrative conventions, biographies may systematically favor familiar narratives, such as redemption arcs. If this tendency is present in the agent initialization, GABMs that are trying to model institutional, societal, or individual failures could generate generic, overly optimistic or redemptive trajectories. Therefore, narrative realism should not be conflated with psychological one.

Third, the instability of LLM outputs under different temperature regimes poses a challenge to the reproducibility of GABM findings. Generative agent based models are run at moderate to high temperatures, so as to ensure behavioral diversity. An agent initialized with a particular trait or biography may "wander" behaviorally across multiple runs of the same simulation. This makes it difficult to determine whether the observed population-level differences arise from the modeled characteristics or from variation in the generative process itself. This challenge is particularly important, given the broader reproducibility concerns already documented in the social and behavioral sciences.

Finally, the DEAD critique directly applies to generative agents. GABMs are alienated from lived environments, engineered through prompts and training data, and disembodied from physical and social conditions that shape human behavior. The literature review showing that LLMs perform relatively well on decontextualized personality tasks is of limited relevance to one of the central values agent-based modeling offers: the ability to examine situated, path-dependent behavior as it unfolds through social interaction. The aspect of the selfhood that relates the most to this valuethe unfolding narrative trajectory through which experiences are interpreted and incorporated into continuous identity -is also the regard in which LLM-based representations appear to be the least reliable.

4.3 When Do These Limitations Matter?

An important distinction should be made regarding the implications of these limitations for agentbased modeling. The documented biases, trait homogeneity, model and prompt sensitivity, and stochastic instability constitute threats to GABMs when the simulations are used as an explanans for real-world phenomena (van Rooij et al., 2024). van Rooij et al. (2024) differentiates between makeist and non-makeist uses of simulations. Makeist use treats simulation results as evidence about how the modeled phenomenon works in the real world. Non-makeist use, in contrast, treats simulations as theoretical tools for demonstrating a concept or establishing the in-principle (im)possibility of a phenomenon arising from a specified set of theoretical constructs, without claiming that the simulation dynamics corresponds to the real world dynamics.

This distinction between makeist and non-makeist use of simulation is particularly important for GABMs as they are prone to a form of inferential slippage. The appeal of agent-based simulation lies partly in the characteristics it shares with a social process: individual agents, situated interactions, emergent population-level patterns. This makes it easy to forget that the simulation's resemblance to a social process is representational rather than necessarily explanatory. A simulation can demonstrate that a phenomenon emerges from a particular set of assumptions without, however, demonstrating that those assumptions accurately describe the mechanisms operating in the real world.

To sum it up, it is crucial for GABM researchers to recognize and state which register the simulation is operating in and to restrict their conclusions accordingly. Non-makeist simulations can remain valuable even when their agents are psychologically imperfect. Making makeist claims, on the other hand, inherits all the representational problems documented in this paper: systematic bias, homogeneous trait expression, sensitivity to prompts and models, and instability across runs.

Wang et al. (2024) evaluated role-playing agents by interviewing 32 LLM-based characters with 14 personality measures and assessing whether their responses corresponded to the personality of the characters they portrayed. Most recently,Wright et al. (2026) used seven LLMs to infer Big Five traits from open-ended streams of thought and daily video diary narratives. The estimates produced by individual LLMs differed, while averaging across models produced the strongest agreement with human self-report measures.
