Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. Some differences come from the ground truth: it lacks p-values with a correction name (“p Bonf = .061”), and counts partial eta squared written with its subscript after it (“η 2 p = .05”), “p = .05”, and some table notes and figure legends (“† p < .10, *p < .05”), which this module leaves out, as non-significant p-values. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Full text

Abstract

The Biomarkers and Biological Factors Subcommittee of the Future DSM Strategic Committee has proposed introducing psychiatric diagnostic biomarkers (measurable biological indicators of a psychiatric condition) into the DSM-6. We review this proposal considering the questionable reliability and validity of psychiatric diagnoses and the low sensitivity and specificity of their purported biomarkers. These concerns have underappreciated consequences for epistemic justice, particularly regarding how biomarker information will be privileged or weighted against other sources of information, including patient testimony. We argue that introducing diagnostic biomarkers is premature and potentially harmful, risking further disenfranchisement and minoritisation of already vulnerable and marginalised groups. Future work establishing reliable and valid diagnostic phenotypes may assist in converging on biomarkers with the sensitivity and specificity required for clinical utility. We further argue the need for adequate and equitable representation of diverse groups in the identification and validation of psychiatric biomarkers.

Introduction

The Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition Text Revised (DSM-5-TR) 1 , is the principal framework for characterising, diagnosing, treating, and researching psychiatric conditions 2 . The Future DSM Strategic Committee has established the Biomarkers and Biological Factors Subcommittee to bridge the gap between psychiatry and neuroscience, more overtly grounding psychiatric nosology in biology 3.

Psychiatric diagnostic biomarkers are measurable biological indicators of the presence or process of a psychiatric condition [3] [4] [5] . The promise of diagnostic biomarker research has centred on improving care through enhancing diagnostic accuracy and timely identification or prevention, whilst providing an aetiological explanation of their symptoms or psychological experiences and minimising trial-and-error treatment approaches 5 . Although there are eight biomarker types recognised by The Biomarkers and Biological Factors Subcommittee, diagnostic biomarkers are amongst the most common investigated in the literature.

Despite the proposal to include a dedicated biomarkers chapter in the DSM-6, there are a myriad of issues regarding their validity due to the methodologies used to identify them. As we show, psychiatric biomarkers have not achieved the level of sensitivity and specificity required for clinical utility. Moreover, there has been comparatively little discussion regarding the potential social, ethical, and legal implications and problems of biomarker application for psychiatric contexts. While it is well-understood that the strict application of DSM-5 diagnoses can lead to structural inequality and injustice when criteria are interpreted without sufficient attention to cultural context, social determinants of health, structural discrimination, and the phenomenology of those presenting for care (i.e., something acknowledged by the Future DSM Strategic Committee in their Structure and Dimensions Subcommittee 6, 7 ), professionals lack direction regarding how to include biological data during assessment whilst mitigating these kinds of risks. Furthermore, even whilst considering complex psychosocial contextual factors, diagnosis is inherently complicated due to the questionable reliability and validity associated with mainstream nosology from which candidate biomarkers are commonly derived.

In what follows, we examine issues with incorporating biomarkers into the DSM-6 with respect to disorder validity and reliability, biomarker sensitivity and specificity, and the associated concerns we hold for epistemic justice. Epistemic justice concerns who is believed, whose knowledge is allowed to shape shared understanding, and who decides how much authority and weight particular sources of knowledge carry within a field 8, 9 . Epistemic injustice occurs in healthcare settings when prejudices or healthcare structures erode the credibility of patients as knowers of their own experiences, or when a group is denied the resources to make sense of their experiences 10, 11.

Despite biomarkers historically ranking lower in reliability and validity compared to other clinical knowledge sources in psychiatric decision making 4 , investment in biomarker discovery efforts has been substantial. Psychiatry has an ethical duty to support individuals and their communities 12 by providing evidence-based avenues for identifying, preventing and providing relief from psychological suffering. We argue that given the current state of evidence, embedding biomarkers within psychiatric nosology is premature and potentially harmful, going against this aim. We offer suggestions to progress biomarker discovery while centring an empirically grounded nosology and reprioritising individuals seeking healthcare and their communities.

The Move towards Bioreductionism in Psychiatry and

The Lived Experience Revolution Since its inception in 1952 13 , the DSM has undergone successive revisions reflecting shifts in psychiatric philosophy, advances in research methods, priorities in clinical need, and directions in clinical practice. Nevertheless, the core purposes of psychiatric diagnostic systems have been to 1) provide an aetiological explanation for the source of psychiatric symptoms; 2) inform treatment and guide clinical-decision making; and 3) indicate prognosis; 4) provide a foundation for research; 5) assist administrative and legal functions; and 6) assist social and systems level functions (e.g., resource allocation; estimation of burden of disease) 14, 15 . Diagnosis itself also entails potential therapeutic value for the client by providing a shared vocabulary to explain symptoms and provide psychological meaning 16.

Efforts to simplify the complexity of psychiatric presentations through accounts of deviations in genetics, neurotransmitters, neurons and networks 2, 17 , as supported by the rapid expansion of psychopharmacology 18 , for example, has been argued to add credibility to psychiatry as a field of medicine. Indeed, the ongoing push for psychiatric biomarkers aligns with the ontological positions adopted in other areas of medicine, which prioritises biology and data above what is experienced or observed. Though biomarkers were first proposed to feature in the DSM in its fifth edition, this was ultimately abandoned given the state of scientific evidence at the time 2, 4, 19 . Moves to include biomarkers in the DSM have resurfaced 3, 20 despite the absence of any significant advancements regarding psychiatry biomarker identification.

Here, we emphasise an important distinction between 'biological correlates' (e.g. risk factors) and biomarkers. The former terms describe biological characteristics that statistically confer greater probabilities towards some outcome 21 , whereas biomarkers constitute a specific class of correlate of biological origin, measured with high reproducibility 22 , and are in this context a corollary of bioreductionist views of mental illness 5, 23 . While a substantial body of literature has indeed identified numerous biological correlates, none so far have appeared to demonstrate the clinical utility required to meet biomarker status.

That this push persists despite the absence of clinically useful biomarkers has prompted concerns regarding the rise of genetic (or biological) essentialism: a set of cognitive biases reflecting beliefs that genetic/biological variation is more relevant to a behavior, condition, or social group than is warranted 24 . Implicit in essentialist thinking are assumptions that disorders are immutable, aetiologically specific, and homogeneous; that they represent 'natural' categories; and that conditions with a biological anchor are more morally legitimate than those without 24 . Essentialist framings have proven appealing to the public and policy-makers, offering seemingly tangible and actionable explanations for mental suffering where previous accounts have failed 25 , and converge with neoliberal values concerning self-improvement and personal responsibility for one's suffering 26 . By locating pathology within the individual, such framings may underweight or ignore the influence of social harms such as racism, misogyny, anti-LGBTQ+ attitudes, ableism, and other system-level forces, shifting responsibility for care away from costly public health initiatives that might mitigate these forces onto individuals themselves.

To accommodate the absence of validated biomarkers, the DSM-6 committee opts for the qualifier 'candidate biomarkers' to acknowledge that evidence for biomarkers has not yet accumulated to reach the point of being clinically meaningful. However, the move to enshrine biomarkers in the DSM this way arguably signifies an already existing ambition and eagerness to centre bioreductionist accounts of mental suffering. We argue that the qualifier 'candidate' does little against the implicit, a priori ideological position communicated to DSM-6 readership that psychiatric conditions have intrinsic biological signature worth including in official diagnostic manuals.

Concurrent to bioreductionist shifts in psychiatric philosophy, there has also been growing recognition for the value of lived-experience testimony in informing psychiatric practice 27 . For example, both the psychiatric survivors 28 and the neurodiversity movements 29 have advocated for the value of lived experience perspectives whilst collectively seeking to depathologise and reframe diverse psychological traits as natural aspects of human variation 29, 30.

In line with Fricker's seminal work on epistemic injustice 9 , these movements have sought to address two main types of injustice in psychiatric care 8 . The first, testimonial justice, concerns whether a person is dignified in their account of their own experiences 9 . The second is hermeneutical justice, which concerns whether a person has equitable access to language and knowledge to describe their experiences 31 . Hermeneutical injustice occurs when individuals with lived experience are not equipped with the vernacular or mental health literacy to adequately communicate their first-hand experiences in a way that ensures their perspectives are given sufficient wright in clinical decision-making 31 . This occurs when professionals erode the value of perspectives of individuals with lived experience and their communities due to prejudice (i.e., as in the case of BPD overdiagnosis in queer individuals 32 ) and/or due to professional overconfidence 33.

As we discuss below, given the current state of the literature, we hold concerns that the introduction of diagnostic biomarkers into the clinical decision-making process potentially threatens to exacerbate issues of epistemic injustice in psychiatric care, privileging biological essentialism over first-hand lived-experience. How best to balance aims to centre those with lived experiences while pursuing biomarker discovery has received scant attention in the literature. Furthermore, diagnosis itself relies upon the assumption that psychiatric diagnoses are reliable, valid, homogeneous, and that the instruments used to indicate disorder presence, absence and prognosis are empirically supported. However, psychiatric disorders as they are traditionally defined have attracted significant controversy.

1. Ontological and Statistical Concerns Regarding Existing Psychiatric Nosology

The DSM-5-TR or ICD-11 are the gold-standard in psychiatric practice, informing much of the extant biopsychiatry literature investigating the genetic, molecular, neuroanatomical, and functional neuroanatomical correlates of mental illness 34 . However, evidence consistently indicate that DSM categorical diagnoses do not 'carve nature at its joints' 35 ; that the prevailing ontology does not meet basic scientific standards concerning construct validity and reliability. Criticisms of psychiatric classification systems centre on six key issues.

1. Low Reliability

Two clinicians independently evaluating the same patient may disagree on diagnosis far more often than they agree, raising fundamental questions about the validity of any biomarker anchored to such an unreliable criterion. For example, the inter-rater reliability of major depressive disorder (MDD) in the DSM is as low as kappa (κ) = .28 (poor); the κ of generalised anxiety disorder (GAD) is as low as .20 36.

Standardised clinical interviews may be of limited help in this regard, themselves demonstrating poor inter-rater reliability 37 . Most DSM-5-TR diagnoses are not reliably ascertained and are therefore of dubious value, both from realist and pragmatic perspectives 38.

Despite poor reliability, historically, professional perspectives have taken precedence, such that the subjective reports of those seeking psychiatric support are systematically assumed to lack credibility 9 . Thus, low reliability is an issue of testimonial (epistemic) justice as much as it is an issue of diagnostic precision.

Furthermore, diagnostic biomarkers anchored to phenotypes with low reliability are necessarily at least as unreliable as the disorders they are claimed to index.

2. Misalignment Between Clinical and Symptom Profiles.

There is a fundamental misalignment between symptoms reported by patients and those that form the criterion sets of the diagnostic categories utilised by psychiatrists and mental health professions. Rather, patients tend to exhibit mixed features of multiple disorders 39 . Thus, imposing a diagnosis on an individual effectively ignores and deprioritises some symptoms to meet a Procrustean concept of a predefined psychiatric disorder. At a more fundamental level, this misalignment indicates that diagnostic entities do not represent 'natural kinds', contrary to bioessentialist assumptions. Therefore, diagnostic biomarkers from which such criterion are derived are similarly unlikely to hold validity.

3. Variable and Person-Centred Heterogeneity

Statistical investigations reveal tendencies for supposedly singular disorder categories to constitute multiple disparate statistical dimensions (multidimensionality; also, 'variable-centred heterogeneity) 40, 41 . Major depressive disorder (MDD), for example (one of the most common mental health conditions 42 ) has been found to comprise up to 5 dissociable dimensions related to depressivity 43, 44 , each with its own unique genetic risk factors 45 . Furthermore, DSM diagnostic categories are polythetic, meaning that diagnostic classification is based on meeting any combination of a minimum number of signs and symptoms from a set of diagnostic features. The result is that any two people with the same disorder may have very little symptom overlap ( high person-centered heterogeneity for the same disorder) 46, 47 . For example, 636,120 distinct symptom combinations satisfy diagnostic criteria for post-traumatic stress disorder, and 16,400 different symptom combinations satisfy diagnostic criteria for MDD 47, 48.

Person and variable-centred heterogeneity complicates hermeneutic justice.

When linguistic tools (i.e. prescribed diagnostic categories) are incongruent with lived experience, patients are stripped of the linguistic specificity needed to make sense of their own distress. Furthermore, heterogenous categories obfuscate the search for homogeneous biomarkers with strong etiological signal [49] [50] [51].

Crucially, the essentialist assumption of homogeneity may obscure variability in first-hand accounts of a condition, whilst implying a 'one-treatment-fits-all' approach, including for biopsychiatric intervention.

4. Comorbidity and Construct Differentiation

The 'comorbidity problem' refers to the well-documented tendency for individuals meeting criteria for one psychiatric disorder to meet criteria for many others 52, 53 . For example, anxiety and mood disorders frequently co-occur 54 , and also overlap substantially in their clinical features 55 ; their diagnostic separation thus appears arbitrary rather than natural 56 . Co-morbidity is a problem frequently misattributed to individuals presenting for clinical care and rarely seen as a problem with disorder conceptualisation and categorisation, such is the entrenchment of traditional nosology 57.

Yet, at a biological level, comorbidity has been found to reflect shared genetic etiology across putatively distinct disorders 58, 59 . Notably, shared cross-disorder genetic aetiology directly contradicts bioessentialist assumptions regarding aetiological specificity.

5. Arbitrary disorder thresholding

Further concerns surround the artificial dichotomisation of what are inherently continuous spectra. Apart from a few disorders, diagnosis represents the extreme end of a continuum of liability 60, 61 . Generally, psychiatric disorders are highly polygenic, with the additive contribution of thousands of common genetic variants of very small effect, thus manifesting as quantitative traits in the population 62 . While these traits tend to aggregate into spectra 63 spanning non-clinical through to clinical diagnosis, their aetiology reflect a combination of genetic, environmental, and environment x genetic correlations factors 64 . Thus, any biological threshold demarcating disorder from nondisorder is essentially arbitrary 65, 66.

Importantly, people who don't reach an arbitrary diagnostic threshold may experience hermeneutical isolation, where their suffering or distress is considered "normal" or non-existent. Clinically significant distress and functional impairment provides some basis for lumping individuals into 'meets criteria' vs. not, but even these indicators offer no natural decision point: for continua such as schizotypy, for example, symptom severity scales increase linearly with functional impairment 67 , meaning that decisions about when to intervene remain inherently subjective rather than empirically determined. We note that a significant proportion of individuals are already turned away from services despite clinically significant impairment, owing to the arbitrary cut-offs common in polythetic diagnostic systems 68 . Even still, many individuals with subthreshold illness go on to develop diagnosable mental health conditions 69.

Unsurprisingly, dichotomous outcome variables (i.e. 'case' vs 'healthy control') are generally associated with reduced statistical power and attenuated effect sizes in psychiatric risk factor research 70 -including for biomarker discovery efforts. Furthermore, categorical diagnoses are less likely to yield valid diagnostic psychiatric biomarkers compared with continuous dimensions 71.

6. Diagnostic Bias

Diagnostic bias has been identified as an issue at least in part attributable to how DSM constructs are operationalised and applied by clinicians. Borderline Personality Disorder (BPD), for example, is disproportionately diagnosed in females 72 and queer individuals 73 ; despite 'inappropriate and intense anger' featuring more often in men than in women, clinicians appear to judge this symptom as more problematic when displayed by women 72, 74 . Similarly, sexual minorities are more likely to receive a BPD diagnosis independent of their actual psychopathological symptoms 32 , and BPD symptomatology has been found to index global functioning differentially between sexes 75 . More generally, recent research indicates that psychiatric diagnosis could simply be making distinctions between individuals along sociodemographic lines 76 . This is particularly pertinent to biomarker identification given findings that socioeconomic status is systematically linked with both psychopathology and brain-wide alterations in structure and function 76, 77.

Problematically, some diagnoses predispose people to bias, prejudice, and discrimination. For example, a diagnosis of BPD is associated with negative clinician attitudes, reduced empathy/therapeutic engagement, and barriers to accessing or receiving care 78 . Testimonial quieting occurs when a person diagnosed has their testimony prematurely dismissed; ultimately, testimonial smothering occurs when the patient modifies or withholds their personal account in anticipation it may be misunderstood or discounted 79 . Hermeneutic injustice can occur in BPD because the symptoms comprising a diagnosis reflect a complex interplay of, among other factors, trauma, attachment relationships, socioeconomic adversity, and environmental stressors 80, 81 . Without adequate language to express these challenges people may struggle to make sense of and express their own experience leading to reduced epistemic agency, and the withdrawal from epistemic participation. The biases inherent in how psychiatric conditions are diagnosed further undermine the validity of these diagnoses in research settings; biomarkers validated against such criteria will inevitably perform suboptimally in clinical practice.

Given these concerns, many in the field have come to acknowledge that diagnostic categories as they are traditionally defined fail to meet basic standards of reliability and validity.

Issues Regarding Psychiatric Biomarkers

Low Sensitivity, Specificity, and Small Effects Sizes

We acknowledge that, even whilst considering issues with the prevailing psychiatric ontology, there has been some success in the identification of biological correlates of disorder.

For example, the neurobiology of fear and phobias is well-articulated 82 , with phobic reactions reliably characterised by differences of high effect sizes in the amplitudes and latencies of delineated event related potentials (ERPs) between those with and without these concerns 83 , thus serving as a potential diagnostic biomarker. Modification of these ERP components could additionally serve as response biomarkers for specific phobias 84 . However, this is not the case for psychiatric disorders more generally. Psychiatric biomarkers have yet to reach sufficient sensitivity or specificity to be clinically meaningful. Sensitivity refers to a test's ability to correctly identify those with a disorder, whilst specificity refers to a test's ability to correctly identify those without a disorder 85 . For a test to be of clinical value, it should demonstrate both high sensitivity and high specificity to limit the rates at which people presenting for care go incorrectly undiagnosed or misdiagnosed.

Of the few biomarkers proposed for inclusion in the DSM-6, C-reactive protein (CRP) for depression is among the most prominent 3 , yet only 27% of those diagnosed show elevations in this proposed biomarker 86 , a sensitivity that compares poorly with established self-report measures let alone the clinical utility expected of a diagnostic biomarker in any other fields of medicine. We also note that C-reactive protein elevations are a highly non-specific bodily inflammation indicator utilised across medicine, having been similarly implicated in cancer risk 87 , bacterial infection 88 , and general gastrointestinal issues 89 , for example, further complicating use in psychiatric settings (i.e. poor specificity). Pharmacogenomics for psychiatry has also attracted considerable interest, with the promise of assisting psychopharmacological decision making given established drug x gene interaction studies. Research efforts in this context have largely focused on a subset of conditions such as MDD.

However, 20% of those with MDD at most feature genetic markers relevant to guiding psychopharmacological decision making, leaving the majority of those seeking care without psycho-pharmacogenomic guidance 90 . Incorporating pharmacogenomics in psychiatry has not been found to impact hospitalisation rates in matched controls 91.

We note that various isolated studies have otherwise reported strong results. For example, Koutsouleris et al. (2015) reported sensitivity and specificity of .80 -.90 for distinguishing between those with and without a high risk of psychosis based on neuroanatomical pattern classification in some validation samples 92 . However, performance declined when testing was extended out-of-sample to different sites and more heterogeneous samples 93 , suggesting that the classifier was not generalisable. This study also ignored strong evidence that psychosis risk is an extended phenotype in the general population 94 . The best replicated psychiatric biomarkers tend to have ~70 -85% sensitivity and specificity, or Area Under the Curve (AUC) = .75 -.85, such that they would misclassify up to 30% of individuals; even higher misclassification for lower prevalence disorders. However, such studies are typically confined to case-control comparisons or comparisons between 'pure' disorder pairs. Furthermore, it is not typical for such studies to investigate specificity and sensitivity variations across disorder cycles (e.g. mood phases featuring in bipolar disorder). Ideally, for clinical purposes, classifiers should be adept not only at separating clinical cohorts from non-clinical cohorts, but also at separating clinical cohorts based on their various presenting concerns (i.e., those with schizophrenia from those with bipolar disorder from those with obsessive compulsive disorder, etc), ideally regardless of disorder phase. The experimental designs used at present are unable to demonstrate classifier utility in this regard.

Polygenic risk scores (PGRS), reflecting the sum of common risk alleles carried with respect to a reference sample of individuals diagnosed with a psychiatric condition, also represents a possible avenue for psychiatric biomarker discovery efforts [95] [96] [97] . The clinical utility of PGRS has been most strongly demonstrated in relation to schizophrenia-spectrum disorders [98] [99] [100] . Even still, PGRS for schizophrenia tends to explain a maximum of 7-10% of liability variance in independent samples, while common variants (selective nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs)/genes carried by greater than 1% of the population) collectively account for ~20 -25% of variance in liability 101 . PGRS are thus not yet ready for clinical use 102.

Furthermore, PGRS merely describe the number of common alleles uniquely shared with a defined reference cohort and are not a genetic predisposition for a psychiatric condition 103 . The reference cohort is, in some ways, arbitrarily defined with respect to DSM criteria and are therefore subject to the same criticisms levelled above, as well as being dependent on sociodemographic composition, and methodological practices that often do not represent ethnic and cultural minorities 104.

Overdiagnosis remains a significant concern in psychiatry 105 . We contend that biomarker-based diagnosis has the potential to compound this further wherever specificity is low, i.e. biomarker-based diagnoses may compound overdiagnosis for those without true disorder (i.e., due to a lack of specificity), thus inflating the number of people inappropriately treated. Meanwhile, low sensitivity risks underdiagnosis, denying the treatment, accommodations, or recognition that follows for individuals who do have a true disorder.

Issues regarding Biomarker Interpretation

Biomarker discovery research to date has largely focused on basic correlational studies rather than pursuing a mechanistic understanding of the biology of mental illness, creating interpretational issues regarding what the presence or absence of a biomarker signifies.

Nevertheless, the role of biomarkers in the pursuit of objective psychiatric diagnosis is not phenomenologically neutral. For example, those who identify as "anxious people" experience greater functional impairment even after controlling for anxiety symptom severity, demonstrating that psychiatric labels can carry weight independent of psychiatric symptoms 106 . Furthermore, some biomarkers may signify compensatory processes for some individuals, and pathogenic processes in others.

Adverse childhood experiences (ACEs), for example, remain by far the strongest predictor of psychopathology, accounting for over 22% (16 -28%, 95%CI) of all cases of mental disorder 107 . In the psychotraumatology literature, neural differences observed in trauma survivors have long been presumed to index pathogenic risk; however, such changes may reflect adaptive mechanisms that promote survival at the cost of quality of life for some people, rather than pathogenic processes per se 108, 109 . Accordingly, framing biological difference as pathogenic and constitutive of potential diagnostic biomarker status here could be argued erroneous; an unnecessary pathologisation of deviancy rather than an appreciation for human difference and adaptation. While biomarkers may ostensibly relate to protective or pathogenic risk per the position of the DSM-6 Committee, our concerns lie with the relative absence of discussions surrounding the clarification of these possibilities, and their implications for patient diagnosis and care.

Medication use also represents a pervasive and underappreciated confound in biomarker discovery research 110 . Though routinely acknowledged in individual studies, the implications rarely surface in broader discussions of psychiatric biology, including for recent proposals to incorporate biomarkers into the DSM-6 3 . Candidate diagnostic biomarkers identified in the extant literature may index, at least in part, chronic pharmacological effects rather than disorder-relevant pathophysiology 110 . Interpreting these pharmacological effects as intrinsic diagnostic biomarkers may inadvertently lead to spurious conclusions whilst reifying the prevailing nosology. We further raise the possibility that some people presenting for care may carry biomarkers, and for all intents and purposes a latent propensity for future disorder, and yet not feature or even develop psychopathology due to the presence of well-established psychosocial protective factors 111 , consistent with a biopsychosocial model of mental illness etiology 112.

The Need to Validate Biomarkers Across Diverse Groups

We are concerned that the lack of systematic representation and validation across diverse groups within training and classification datasets during biomarker discovery efforts will continue to perpetuate a dark history of disenfranchisement against marginalised groups 113.

It is well understood that pharmacological drug response can vary based on the allelic differences across different ethnic populations; pharmacogenomic sensitivity and specificity is likely to vary considerably across ethnically diverse groups 114 . Yet, the populations represented in current candidate biomarker research follow historical biases i.e. White, heterosexual, cisgender, non-disabled, European cohorts. We are concerned that findings will be applied to more diverse groups without judicious consideration. Indeed, we have seen historical misattribution or over-representation of specific psychiatric diagnoses (e.g. anxiety, schizophrenia, borderline personality disorder, intellectual disability) among women, Black, Indigenous, Queer, and disabled people, for example 32, 115 . It is entirely possible that a push to involve non-generaliseable biomarkers in clinical settings will continue this trend. For example 'Drapetomania,' the term coined to pathologise enslaved peoples who attempted to flee their captors, was legitimised on the basis of colonial superiority 116 ; phrenology, which involved attempting to diagnose one's character and mental capabilities via skull shape, continued this legacy, having been used to legitimise sentiments that non-White people lacked intellect and dignity 117 . 'Protest psychosis' concerns the documented conflation of Black political protest and anger with symptoms of schizophrenia, inflating psychosis diagnosis rates in African American people during Black civil rights movement periods 115, 118.

We also note the historical pathologisation of queerness 119, 120 , justified via older assertions of testicular deviance 121 to more recent efforts to locate the 'gay gene' 122 . While the underrepresentation of people of non-European ancestry in Genome-Wide Association Studies (GWAS) from which PGRS are constructed is a focus of recent enquiry and effort 123 , it remains an emerging science. Differences in structural neurobiological indices associated with social inequality and stratification are only now beginning to be addressed 124, 125 . Such findings now indicate that established psychiatric biomarkers may instead delineate along socioeconomic and ethnic lines. Critically, a clinically meaningful biomarker must not only be sensitive and specific enough for general purpose, but also rigorously tested across subpopulations. We note that no candidate biomarker to date satisfies these criteria.

The Ecological Fallacy and its Relation to Epistemic (In)Justice

In assessment, there will always be those for whom disease indicators are not present despite symptoms, and those for whom disease indicators are present despite a complete absence of issues. Accordingly, translating population-level biological signals into individual diagnostic decisions risks the ecological fallacy: conflating what is statistically true of a group with what can be assumed of any given member 126 . This phenomenon is especially problematic if sensitivity and specificity rates are low and vary systematically across diverse groups, as is the case for psychiatric biomarkers.

There is a robust literature concerning the historical positioning of epistemic superiority upheld by psychiatric professionals over those seeking support, particularly for marginalised groups 127 . These errors have taken decades for the field to acknowledge and have done considerable damage to the field's reputation. More recently, concerns have been raised given the potential for digital phenotyping to be leveraged to disavow the experiences of individuals seeking psychiatric care 11 . We hold concerns that the injudicious use of biological assays in psychiatry, in conjunction with increasing bioreductionist tendencies in the field, risks similarly re-enacting such injustices, prioritising biological factors above psychosocial factors and nonscience knowledge (e.g., Indigenous knowledges 128 ).

The ability to comprehend, analyse, and interpret scientific knowledge is an integral asset, particularly in supporting and advocating for one's psychological wellbeing 129, 130.

However, people seeking psychiatric care often lack the shared language needed to engage with, and potentially challenge, professionals and institutions regarding diagnosis and treatment based on nuanced biomarker assays. This difficulty is compounded by the vulnerability associated with mental illness and the epistemic authority required to contest a clinician's assessment when it conflicts with one's lived experience. If the clinical utility of biomarkers remains dubious, then how individuals interpret their own distress and the ways in which they seek care will necessarily be impacted in detrimental ways 131.

Political and Economic Concerns

Given our concerns regarding validity, specificity, and sensitivity alone, involving psychiatric biomarkers alongside standard clinical protocols is difficult to justify. However, at present, biomarker assays in psychiatry are also financially prohibitive. A basic MRI sequence to quantify regional grey matter volume, for instance, currently costs hundreds to thousands of U.S. dollars. Government subsidisation of procedures with insufficient evidence or clinical utility would be hard to defend, and psychiatrically vulnerable populations already bear disproportionate socioeconomic burden 132 ; encouraging them to pay out-of-pocket for such data would be unethical and clinically unjustifiable.

We note risks that biomarkers in the DSM-6 would nevertheless legitimise lobbying for costly biomarker assessments. Pharmaceutical companies in many parts of the world already conduct direct outreach to psychiatrists to promote their products 133 , and the same dynamic is emerging among commercial entities offering biosignature-based psychiatric assessment.

Commercial clinics offering neuroimaging-based diagnostic services have already appeared despite an evidence base that remains, at best, preliminary 134, 135 . This risks perpetuating mistrust in psychiatric care whilst building on already significant levels of epistemic vigilance in this context 136 . At this stage of the science, introducing a dedicated biomarkers chapter in the DSM is likely to benefit companies and professionals more than the people they claim to assist, favouring business-model solutions over evidence-based care.

If biomarkers are upheld as objective indicators of truth, we hold concerns regarding the institutionalisation of genetic and neurobiological results at the level of government, legal, healthcare, employment, and education systems, which, without adequate empirical backing, can lead to issues of privacy and systemic discrimination. Examples of this have already occurred within the misuse of biometrics and biomarkers for people seeking asylum. For example, DNA-methylation assays were recently used by a German regional asylum authority in attempts to verify the claimed age of an asylum seeker presenting as an unaccompanied minor, attempts which were widely condemned given the state of the science at the time 137 . The prioritisation of biometric data in determining who can access borders and state resources and care risks rights to just process. Government legislation (such as that recently passed in Australia 138 ) is needed to protect the rights of individuals from abuses and the misappropriation of diagnostic biomarkers; something that is currently foregone in the current (and premature) move to enshrine candidate biomarkers in the DSM-6.

The Future of Biomarker Discovery

We are not wholly opposed to future biomarker implementation efforts, but if biomarkers are to be included in official psychiatric nosology, there must be a rigorous empirical basis to justify their use. Official manuals must also include detailed directions concerning how biomarkers should be weighted and interpreted alongside other clinical indicators, psychosocial factors, and patient testimony. We argue that overhauling psychiatric ontology given empirical evidence and lived-experience informed understandings concerning what disorders actually are, how to measure them, and how to distinguish them from one another 139 would offer researchers the best chance of achieving this.

Two alternative frameworks have been proposed to address the empirical limitations of the DSM-5: the Hierarchical Taxonomy of Psychopathology (HiTOP) 140 and the Research Domain Criteria (RDoC) 141, 142 . The HiTOP model is an alternative, empirically based nosology that involves the clustering of individual signs, symptoms and maladaptive behaviours into hierarchically organised dimensions ranging from narrow specificity at its based, to broad generality at the apex, with constructs representing increasingly shared underlying vulnerabilities to related (i.e., comorbid) psychological problems 143 . Critically, the HiTOP model has demonstrated invariance across the sexes and ethnic groups in the populations studied to date, suggesting equivalence in its structure and scoring, and an absence of bias along these lines of individual and group differences 144, 145 . Conversely, the RDoC is a classification framework for guiding biological research in psychopathology, but critically advocates for a dimensional approach 141 . It takes a 'biology first' approach, such that constellations of biological data are correlated with psychiatric data to converge on candidate pathophysiological processes 142 . These disparate paradigms have convergent aims to characterise the nature of mental illness and its aetiology using an empirical rather than consensus-based approach. They are complementary in that the former provides the phenotypic targets for mapping onto the biological targets uncovered by the latter 146.

Though precision-phenotyping approaches such as HiTOP and RDoC are hoped to assist in converging on biological risk factors to the level of scientific credibility required to be clinically useful 71 , so far, these approaches have only incrementally improved biomarker discovery efforts. The ontological frameworks themselves require further validation (particularly across development), and the role of those with lived experience in informing such models has yet to be clarified. In principle, constructs derived from phenotypic-level modelling may have entirely disparate underlying neurobiological and genetic processes contributing to their expression 147 , and RDoC-derived biological correlates may similarly remain lacking in the precision required for practical utility. We acknowledge that future efforts combining multi-omics approaches 148 and finer parsing of sample heterogeneity 149 may yet yield biological correlates of greater precision, and thus improve sensitivity and specificity rates (and thus reduce the potential for epistemic injustice). However, we caution that such biomarkers must be rigorously validated across diverse groups to avoid systematic disenfranchisement, and that laws should be introduced to protect individuals from the institutionalisation of genetic and neurobiological results.

We also suggest, as have others, that certain disorder types may be more biologically based than others; and, conversely, that some may be better accounted for by psychosocial determinants than biological ones. In other words, for many forms of psychopathology, psychosocial determinants may be of primary importance, rendering biological correlates noisy downstream derivatives of non-biological processes perpetuating disorder 150 . The weight of evidence increasingly suggests this is possible, though the degree to which this holds likely varies across disorders. It is also possible that the nature of psychiatric illness and the noise inherent to biological data will together place fundamental limitations on biomarker discovery efforts too difficult to overcome, at least for some disorder types. While we do not necessarily adopt a constructivist view of mental disorder, we note that psychiatric criterion is operationalised through language, clinical judgement and self-report. Importantly, a predictor's validity is capped by the reliability of the criterion it is validated against 71 . This is particularly pertinent for biomarker discovery given the characteristically weak effect sizes observed in this literature and the noise and complexity inherent to biological data 135 . Accordingly, it is possible that for some disorder types, noisy biologic data may be unable to supplant existing clinical protocols any more than psychosocial risk factors associated with far greater robustness and reproducibility have. The implicit bioreductionist stance of much contemporary psychiatric research risks foreclosing these possibilities.

Conclusions

The case for embedding biomarkers within official psychiatric nosology rests on an evidentiary foundation that, as we have shown, remains substantially underdeveloped. No degree of biological precision can compensate for the poor reliability and validity of traditionally defined mental health disorders when these are used as the criterion against which biomarkers are validated. Candidate biomarkers to date have exhibited poor sensitivity and specificity, and are therefore not, at present, ready for clinical use. Further pursuing their integration into clinical practice despite these concerns is likely to entail systematic misclassification, marginalisation, and disenfranchisement of those seeking care, particularly given the lack of validation across diverse groups.

We stress that we are not opposed to the eventual introduction and utilisation of clinically meaningful biomarkers. Rather, we contend that before biological correlates can meaningfully anchor diagnostic practice, psychiatry must first resolve the more fundamental question of what its diagnostic constructs are meant to capture, and how they might be measured with greater reliability and validity, as well as be better informed by those with lived experience of mental illness. Foregone in discussions on enshrining biomarkers within psychiatric nosology is an acknowledgement that patients are at the heart of what we do. With respect to hermeneutical justice, we must not forget the power and weight candidate biomarkers carry in society in how we define, understand, and interpret psychological experiences.

Formalising biomarkers without regard for these concerns will institutionalise a hierarchy of knowledge in biological measures with uncertain validity, compounding testimonial injustice for groups whose credibility is already diminished within healthcare settings. It also risks perpetuation of hermeneutical injustice via a narrowing of concepts available to people for making sense of their own psychiatric experiences. Frameworks such as HiTOP and RDoC represent meaningful, if incomplete, steps in this direction, and multi-omics approaches may also help isolate biological signatures worthy of future consideration. However, maturing biomarker science in psychiatry will require resolving what biological correlates represent (e.g. including compensatory mechanisms, latent predispositions, or medication effects) while remaining open to the likelihood that biological mechanisms will not equally explain all disorder types. Regardless, the rights of individuals must be upheld, and legal protections are needed against systematic harms such as risk profiling. Until such foundational work matures, embedding biomarkers within the DSM risks lending premature legitimacy to noisy, poorly validated correlates, at potential cost to the epistemic standing, service access, and trust of the very people psychiatry exists to serve.
