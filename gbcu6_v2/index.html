To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 4 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). Statcheck might still miss one-sided tests that are described in another sentence, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Strategies such as retrieval practice, spaced repetition, and elaboration can strongly influence how efficiently learners acquire and retain knowledge and skills. However, students often underuse the most effective strategies, perceiving them as ineffective or overly timeconsuming to implement. Instructors can nonetheless shape students' strategy use through explicit recommendations and course design. Accordingly, we surveyed 599 Norwegian higher education instructors regarding which strategies they (1) believe are effective, (2) recommend to students, and (3) facilitate through course design. We also examined whether mandatory university pedagogy training was associated with more evidence-consistent responses. Overall, instructors ranked elaboration as most effective, while retrieval practice and spaced repetition received considerably lower rankings. Vignette judgments suggested most instructors believe open-book summarizing is superior to retrieval practice, even at a 7-day retention interval. Group work was the most frequently recommended strategy, although recommendations rarely made retrieval or spacing demands explicit. Facilitation practices oriented toward explaining and elaborating were considerably more common than those involving retrieval or spacing. University pedagogy training showed little association with evidence-consistent beliefs or practices. Our findings suggest that instructors favor elaborative encoding and undervalue repeated retrieval practice. Faculty development programs may benefit by moving toward a more mechanism-oriented understanding of learning.

Introduction

A cornerstone of any education is the development of students' knowledge and skills. Because nearly all educational programs are time-bound, instructional time must often be used in ways that efficiently develop learners' comprehension, long-term retention, and ability to transfer learning across contexts. This is especially evident in professional education programs that prepare students for high-stakes practice (e.g., health care, aviation, engineering), where inadequate learning may have substantial practical consequences. For example, nursing students receive three years of training, only half of which is formal on-campus instruction (European Parliament & Council of the European Union, 2005 , 2013). Within this constrained timeframe, students must acquire complex and safety-critical skills, such as calculating drug dosages, where even minor errors can have serious consequences for patients' health (Stolic et al., 2022). This raises a central question for educational practice: which learning and instructional strategies are most efficient for developing knowledge and skills that are both retained over the long-term and transferable across contexts?

In recent decades, memory and learning researchers have made substantial progress in addressing this question. Using both laboratory experiments and studies conducted in representative educational contexts, researchers have identified the underlying mechanisms of effective learning (Yan et al., 2024) and demonstrated how these can be translated into classroom practice (Agarwal et al., 2021; Mawson & Kang, 2025). One early effort to synthesize this body of research was an Institute of Education Sciences report on effective instructional strategies (Pashler et al., 2007). Based on the prevailing evidence at the time, the authors identified seven strategies with moderate to strong empirical support, including retrieving information from memory, spacing learning over time, and learning from concrete examples. Some years later, Dunlosky et al. (2013) reviewed ten learning strategies commonly used by students and categorized them into three levels of utility-high (retrieval practice, spaced repetition), moderate (e.g., elaboration), and low (highlighting, rereading, summarizing). These classifications were based on the extent to which strategies promote comprehension, long-term retention, and transfer, as well as their generalizability across materials, learner characteristics, and educational contexts.

More recently, the conclusions from both of these early reviews have been supported by subsequent and more targeted systematic reviews and meta-analyses (e.g., Agarwal et al., 2021; Carpenter et al., 2022). Taken together, this body of research indicates that a core set of strategies (e.g., spaced repetition, retrieval practice, elaboration, concrete examples) are consistently identified as most effective and efficient, both when implemented by instructors to support students' learning and when employed by students themselves.

The practical significance of these strategies is evident in numerous applied studies.

As an example, Breckwoldt et al. (2016) compared two versions of an undergraduate emergency medicine course in which identical content was delivered either over 4.5 days (spaced) or 3.0 days (crammed). Students in the spaced condition performed moderately better on procedural knowledge and clinical decision-making (Cohen's d = 0.56). Importantly, this was achieved without increasing total instructional time, illustrating how small changes in instructional design can improve learning efficiency for important skills.

While many interventions focus directly on helping students understand and use effective strategies, instructors play a crucial role because they shape the learning environment. Through course design and explicit recommendations, they can influence students' default behaviors and steer them towards more efficient strategies (Masui et al., 2014; Wang et al., 2023). However, instructors' experience and knowledge about teaching and learning vary widely, which may influence what they believe to be effective, recommend to students, and facilitate in their courses. Because university pedagogy training has recently become mandatory for Norwegian faculty (Ministry of Education and Research, 2018), the Norwegian context provides an opportunity to examine how such training relates to instructors' beliefs about learning strategies, and to their recommendation and facilitation practices. Notably, the national guidelines for university pedagogy training specify that instructors should be able to "choose, justify, and develop appropriate learning activities and assessment methods" [authors' translation] (Universitets-og høgskolerådet, 2022), but these guidelines are formulated at a general level and do not reference specific evidence-based learning mechanisms.

Several previous studies have investigated pre-service and in-service teachers' and instructors' knowledge and beliefs about learning strategies (e.g., Firth, 2021; Granström et al., 2023; Halamish, 2018; Morehead et al., 2016; Surma et al., 2022). Many of these studies assess accuracy by asking participants to judge the effectiveness of different strategies in hypothetical learning scenarios. Findings generally show that instructors perform somewhat better than students in recognizing empirically supported strategies such as retrieval practice and spaced repetition (Morehead et al., 2016). However, their judgments remain far from fully accurate. Instructors often struggle with less intuitive strategies such as interleaving (Halamish, 2018), and misconceptions are common-particularly when teachers are asked to explain or justify their evaluations (Granström et al., 2023). Moreover, when teachers are asked open-ended questions about learning strategies, empirically supported strategies are often mentioned infrequently or not at all (Surma et al., 2022).

While this line of research offers useful insights into teachers' theoretical understanding of learning strategies, and to some extent that of instructors, it provides limited evidence about how such knowledge translates into pedagogical practice. Even when instructors endorse empirically supported strategies, they often appear to lack a clear understanding of how these strategies operate or how they can be implemented effectively in instructional settings (Firth, 2018; Granström et al., 2023). Critically, few studies have examined whether higher education instructors actively recommend these strategies to students or deliberately facilitate their use through course design. To our knowledge, no prior study has simultaneously examined instructors' beliefs, recommendations, and instructional practices, nor their association with formal pedagogical training. Addressing this gap is important because overestimating the effectiveness of low-utility strategies-or failing to incorporate high-utility strategies into instruction-may lead to less efficient learning conditions for students.

The Current Study

In the present study, we extend prior work on instructors' knowledge of learning strategies by investigating (1) which strategies instructors believe are effective (via a forcedranking task and mechanism-focused vignettes), (2) which strategies they explicitly recommend to students (via an open-ended question), and (3) which strategies they most often facilitate students' use of within a specific self-selected course. This design allows us to assess both how well-established strategies are understood and whether they are translated into classroom practice in higher education.

To examine beliefs in greater depth, the vignettes were specifically designed to probe instructors' sensitivity to three core mechanisms of effective learning: spacing, retrieval, and interleaving (Yan et al., 2024). The scenarios test, for example, whether instructors' judgments about strategy effectiveness shift appropriately when the retention interval increases, and whether "active" restudy in the form of writing a summary with an open book is distinguished from retrieval-based learning.

Given that university pedagogy training is now a formal requirement for Norwegian faculty (Ministry of Education and Research, 2018), we also examine (4) whether participation in such training is associated with instructors' beliefs, recommendations, and facilitation practices. Taken together, this study examines how instructors' conceptions of effective learning relate to their recommendations and course design, and addresses the following research questions: • RQ1: What are instructors' beliefs about the relative effectiveness of various learning strategies, and to what extent do these align with the current empirical evidence?

• RQ2: Which learning strategies do instructors most frequently recommend to their students? • RQ3: To what extent do instructors facilitate the use of various learning strategies through their course design and teaching practices?

• RQ4: Does participation in university pedagogy courses relate to the degree of instructors' alignment with evidence-based practices across beliefs, recommendations, and facilitation?

Methods

Sampling Procedure

In early 2022, we distributed an online survey (Qualtrics) to all permanent and temporary academic staff at two large Norwegian higher education institutions: one public university and one private college. Invitations were sent to 3,552 institutional email addresses (3,206 public; 346 private), with the aim of collecting more than 300 responses, a commonly recommended minimum for stable parameter estimation in factor analysis (Tabachnick & Fidell, 2013, p. 666). Participation was voluntary and anonymous. No incentives were offered, and respondents could withdraw at any time. Respondents gave electronic written informed consent through a consent item on the first page of the survey, before any questions were shown. Because the survey was anonymous and no personal data were processed, it was exempt from notification to Sikt -Norwegian Agency for Shared Services in Education and Research (2026). Formal approval from an ethics committee was also not required, as Norwegian regulations mandate such approval only for medical and health research (The Norwegian National Research Ethics Committees, 2020).

Several blocks used randomized item order to reduce sequencing effects. To limit attrition, respondents could end the survey before the final block on effectiveness beliefs; most (n = 441, 73.6%) answered at least one item in it. In total, 599 participants completed at least 10% of the survey and were retained as the analytical sample (response rate ≈ 17%). Unless otherwise specified, percentages reflect valid responses and may not sum to 100 due to rounding.

Respondents' mean age was 47.7 years (SD = 11.6; median = 48.5; range: 22-86; 10.5% missing). Compared to national statistics, our sample modestly underrepresented ages 30-39 and overrepresented ages 50-59 (Norwegian Directorate for Higher Education and Skills, 2025). The distribution of both gender and academic positions did however closely match national statistics for academic staff in Norway (Haugen et al., 2025). Measures

Beliefs

We investigated instructors' beliefs about the relative effectiveness of different learning strategies using a forced-ranking task and a set of five contrasting vignettes.

Effectiveness ranking. Instructors completed a drag-and-drop ranking task in which they ordered seven well-known learning strategies from least (=1) to most effective (=7). Effectiveness was defined as the extent to which a strategy efficiently helps typical students understand and retain course material over the long term. Six of these seven strategies were drawn from Dunlosky et al. (2013). The seventh (i.e., crammed repetition) was added to distinguish between instructors' beliefs about crammed versus spaced repetitions. Likewise, to avoid conflating different types of rereading (e.g., returning to a text after a delay, reread difficult passages), we explicitly worded the item as "read the same text multiple times in a row" (see Kuhbandner & Emmerdinger, 2019 for details about wording). Items are detailed in Table 2. Crammed repetition a Study important information by repeating it multiple times distributed over a short period of time (minutes, hours).

Note. The majority of respondents completed the survey in Norwegian. Item wordings here are therefore the authors' translations of the Norwegian items which differ slightly from the English survey items reported in the supplementary materials. a In Norwegian, the term used for cramming (pugge) does not have a direct English equivalent, but refers to an activity that falls somewhere between rote learning and cramming.

Vignettes. Inspired by previous vignette studies (i.e., McCabe, 2011; Morehead et al., 2016), we developed five short vignettes that focused on three cognitive processes that are recognized as central to effective learning: interleaving, spacing, and retrieval (Weinstein et al., 2018; Yan et al., 2024). Each vignette described two groups (Group A and Group B) studying the same material for the same amount of time but using different strategies.

Instructors were asked to predict which group would perform better on a later test using a 5-point scale (1 = "Very likely B",2 = "Somewhat likely B",3 = "Likely equal",4 = "Somewhat likely A",5 = "Very likely A"). The full vignette texts are provided in the supplementary materials (https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/A8QXB).

Interleaving. The first vignette contrasted interleaving (mixing examples or categories during practice) versus blocking (practicing one category at a time) in a scenario involving classification of different cell types. As prior research strongly supports the benefits of interleaving in this context (Chen et al., 2021; Firth et al., 2021; Ge et al., 2021), we coded responses as either consistent (favoring interleaving) or inconsistent (neutral or favoring blocking) with evidence-based expectations.

Spacing.

To assess beliefs about the spacing effect, we constructed two matched vignettes. In both vignettes, one group distributed their practice across ten days (spacing) while the other group concentrated the same practice in two consecutive days (crammed). Crucially, however, one vignette described a final test 10 days after the last study session (long delay), while the other placed the test just 1 day later (short delay). This design served two purposes. First, it allowed us to examine whether instructors generally believed spacing to be more effective than cramming. Second, it enabled us to assess whether instructors' judgments shifted toward more strongly favoring spacing when the retention interval was longer. This distinction matters because, although spacing typically enhances long-term learning outcomes, it may offer no advantage-or even a disadvantage-when tests follow soon after learning (e.g., Latimier et al., 2021; Rohrer & Taylor, 2006; Soderstrom & Bjork, 2015).

We designed the vignette pair with this in mind because we anticipated that many instructors held generally negative views of cramming, which could lead them to favor spacing regardless of delay. However, a broad "cramming is bad" belief does not constitute evidence of conceptions consistent with the spacing effect. We therefore treated the shift in judgments from the short-to long-delay vignette as the more diagnostic indicator, rather than overall endorsement of spacing. Given mixed empirical findings at short delays, we did not specify an empirical expectation for the short-delay vignette. For the long-delay vignette, we treated spacing-favoring responses as evidence-consistent, and responses favoring cramming or judging both strategies equally effective as inconsistent.

Retrieval. To examine instructors' beliefs about retrieval practice, we developed two vignettes: one contrasting retrieval with passive restudy (rereading), and the other contrasting retrieval with active restudy (open-book summarizing). Because it was made explicit that the summarizing group had access to the materials while summarizing, retrieval was the only condition that necessarily required recall from memory.

Prior evidence indicates that retrieval is typically superior to both rereading and summarizing for comprehension, retention, and transfer outcomes (e.g., Dobson & Linderholm, 2015; Karpicke & Blunt, 2011; Lechuga et al., 2015; O'Day & Karpicke, 2021). We therefore treated retrieval-favoring judgments as evidence-consistent in both vignettes. However, a recent meta-analysis by Gonçalves et al. (2025) compared elaborative encoding activities (including summarizing) with retrieval practice and found that retrieval does not consistently outperform elaborative encoding activities when retention intervals are short (< 6 days), feedback is absent for the retrieval group, or the task is cued rather than free recall. Our vignettes involved free recall and a seven-day retention interval but did not specify whether students could consult the text afterwards. To be conservative, we therefore also treated equal performance in the retrieval-versus-summarization vignette as empirically expected.

To characterize response patterns across the two vignettes, we combined judgments using a simple rule in which absolute deviations of ≥1 were treated as meaningful preferences, classifying responses into five profiles (see Table 5). Model-based analyses of the paired comparisons are described in the Analysis section.

Recommendations

Instructors' recommendations were assessed with two items. First, instructors were asked: "Do you recommend specific learning strategies/study techniques to your students? (Choose all that apply)." Response options included "No, I do not recommend any…" and "Yes," followed by one or more contexts in which recommendations were provided (i.e., "in class", "during supervision or conversations with students", "by sharing resources in our learning management systems (LMS)", and "other [please specify]"). A follow-up openended item then asked instructors to describe which learning strategies or study techniques they most often recommend. A total of 378 instructors (63.1%) responded.

Open-ended responses were analyzed using quantitative content analysis, suited for categorizing and quantifying textual data (Krippendorff, 2019; Züll, 2016). We developed an initial categorization scheme with 11 strategy categories and operational definitions based on prior research on learning and instructional strategies (i.e., Dunlosky et al., 2013; Pashler et al., 2007), then piloted this codebook on approximately 100 responses and iteratively added and refined categories to resolve ambiguities. The finalized codebook, available in the https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/A8QXB contained 26 specific strategy categories and a category for uncodable responses.

Due to the low ambiguity of responses, single coding by the first author was considered adequate. Responses were assigned multiple codes when they contained more than one distinct recommendation (e.g., "Mindmap, read the curriculum both before and after the lectures, collaborate" [authors' translation] was coded as mind mapping, read before/after class, and group work). Percentages reported in the results reflect the proportion of respondents who mentioned each category at least once. Notably, 32 responses (8.5%) contained advice that did not fit any recognizable strategy (e.g., "Use Vygotsky Zones") and were classified as uncodable.

Facilitation

To anchor responses in a consistent context, instructors were told to answer all facilitation questions with a single, specific course in mind. We expected the relevance of strategies to vary by course characteristics-especially exam type and course level. We therefore collected several characteristics of the selected courses, including level (e.g., first year, PhD-level), final assessment format (e.g., in-person versus take-home exams), and whether the course was mainly practical (e.g., laboratory practice).

Instructors then rated, on a 5-point scale (1 = Very little/Not at all,5 = Very much), the extent to which they structured the selected course to facilitate students' use of specific learning strategies. Our list included nine strategies based on prior research synthesis (Dunlosky et al., 2013; Pashler et al., 2007), with modifications intended to support more valid interpretation of responses. For example, interleaving was not included because its effectiveness depends heavily on how it is applied to specific types of material (Firth et al., 2021; Ge et al., 2021), making it difficult to capture as a single survey item.

Similarly, there is substantial heterogeneity in how "summarizing" can be implemented (Dunlosky et al., 2013). We therefore avoided treating summarizing as a single, well-defined activity and instead focused on a form that was unlikely to involve substantial generation or retrieval from memory, labelled "compilation-style summarizing." This item was worded as "Compile excerpts of texts from the syllabus into a summary." We included this lower-generativity form specifically to provide a contrast with more effortful strategies in the facilitation set. To complement the nine learning strategies, we included two general instructional practices: establishing clear learning goals (Schneider & Preckel, 2017) and clearly communicating what students need to do to prepare for class, bringing the total to 11 items (see Figure 1). Full item wording is available in the supplementary materials (https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/A8QXB).

While the set of facilitation items showed acceptable internal consistency (ω = .70, 95% CI [.66, .73]; α = .70), we also conducted an exploratory factor analysis (EFA) to facilitate subsequent analyses while limiting multiple comparisons. The data were suitable for factor analysis (KMO = .77). Factors were extracted using minimum residual estimation and rotated using an oblique (oblimin) rotation to allow correlated dimensions.

Analysis

We processed and analyzed the data using R (4.5.2) in RStudio (2026.01.0+392) (Posit Team, 2026; R Core Team, 2025). We used the haven and tidyverse packages to preprocess data and report results (Wickham et al., 2019; Wickham et al., 2025). Missing data were minimal for the facilitation section (1-9 nonresponses per item) and highest for the ranking task. Of the 441 respondents who answered at least one item in the final block, 37 (8.4%) skipped the ranking task. The remaining 404 all provided complete rankings. Given the low level of missingness, we analyzed available cases without imputing data.

We analyzed the ranking task and retrieval vignette pairs using a Plackett-Luce model (Luce, 1959; Plackett, 1975), implemented with the PlackettLuce package (Turner et al., 2025; Turner et al., 2020). This approach is well suited to ranking data because responses are interdependent within respondents, such that placing one strategy higher necessarily constrains the ranks available to the others. For paired comparisons in the retrieval vignettes, the model reduces to a Bradley-Terry-type model (Bradley & Terry, 1952), with an extension that accommodates ties (Turner et al., 2020). Worth estimates were scaled to sum to 1 and can be interpreted as the model-implied probability that a strategy would be ranked first (ranking task) or selected as more effective (paired comparisons), based on the set of compared strategies. To test whether spacing judgments shifted toward favoring spacing at the longer retention interval, we fitted an ordinal mixed-effects model to the 5point responses using the ordinal package (Christensen, 2025).

To facilitate sub-group comparisons for RQ4, we also computed an evidence consistency index from the vignette responses. Scores were -1 (evidence-inconsistent), 0 (neutral/equal), or +1 (evidence-consistent). For the retrieval versus open-book summarizing vignette, responses favoring retrieval or indicating equal performance were scored as evidence-consistent (+1), whereas favoring open-book summarizing was scored as evidenceinconsistent (-1). The short-delay spacing vignette was not scored for evidence-consistency due to mixed empirical expectations. Instead, a spacing-sensitivity score captured whether respondents shifted their judgment toward spacing (+1), showed no categorical shift (0), or shifted toward cramming (-1) when the retention interval increased from short to long delay. The overall evidence-consistency index was computed as the mean of the five vignette scores (range -1 to +1), with higher values indicating greater alignment with empirical expectations.

Results

Research Question 1: Instructors' Beliefs About Effectiveness

Effectiveness ranking

Instructors' effectiveness rankings showed a clear hierarchy (Table 3). Elaboration was rated most effective by a wide margin. Summarizing and retrieval practice formed a second tier with comparable ranks, followed by spaced repetition. Highlighting, crammed repetition, and repeated rereading were consistently ranked least effective and received minimal worth.

Vignette judgments

Table 4 summarizes instructors' judgments across the five vignette comparisons. For the interleaving vignette, most instructors made judgments that were not evidenceconsistent, with only a minority favoring interleaving. Instructors generally expected the spacing group to outperform the cramming group in both vignettes. This judgment pattern was evidence-consistent for the long-delay vignette, but for the short-delay vignette, where empirical expectations are less clear, responses still tended to favor spacing. It should also be noted that about 10% of instructors favored cramming in the long-delay vignette, which strongly deviates from empirical expectations. Additionally, as detailed in the analysis section, we computed an evidence-consistency index ranging from -1 to +1. Scores were modestly positive on average (n = 435; M = 0.160, SD = 0.356; median = 0.200; range = -0.800 to 1.000).

To assess whether instructors were sensitive to the retention interval, we analyzed the shift in judgments from the short-delay to the long-delay vignette. On average, responses shifted modestly toward spacing (mean shift = 0.46, SD = 1.29), and an ordinal mixed-effects model with a fixed effect of delay and a random intercept for instructor confirmed this shift (OR = 2.43, 95% CI [1.86, 3.18], p < .001). The average masked considerable heterogeneity. Across the full sample, 40% of instructors shifted toward spacing, 43% showed no change, and 17% shifted in the opposite direction. The no-change group partly reflects a ceiling effect, because 63 of its 181 instructors (35%) had already selected the most pro-spacing option at the short delay and could not shift further. Among instructors whose short-delay response left room to move (n = 330), 51% shifted toward spacing, 36% showed no change, and 13% shifted toward cramming. Notably, 19% of instructors never expected the spaced group to outperform the crammed group at either delay.

Retrieval vignette response profiles

We categorized instructors' responses to characterize different response profiles based on the two retrieval vignettes (Table 5). The most common profile favored open-book summarizing over retrieval, while also favoring retrieval over rereading. A smaller group favored retrieval over both rereading and summarizing, which is the pattern most consistent with empirical expectations given the vignette conditions. Notably, about 13% of instructors responded either opposite to empirical expectations (i.e., did not favor retrieval in either vignette) or judged all strategies to produce equal outcomes.

A sensitivity test using a stricter preference threshold (absolute deviations ≥ 2, with deviations of 1 treated as partial preferences rather than ties) increased the proportion classified as mixed or partial to 74%, indicating that strong and consistent differentiations across the two vignettes were less common.

The Plackett-Luce model worth estimates converged with the simpler rule-based categorization. Research Question 2: Instructors' Recommendations

Most instructors (78%) reported recommending learning strategies to students, whereas 22% reported that they did not. Recommendations were most commonly provided during teaching (56%) and/or in supervision or individual conversations (43%), while 32% also reported sharing resources in the learning management system.

The open-ended responses typically contained multiple recommendations (M = 2.04, Mdn = 2, range = 0-9). A subset of the open-ended responses (5.8%) was coded as "other" because they were more generally oriented, such as recommending students to listen to podcasts about a topic or instructors explaining that they shared links to learning strategy resources.

Among the open-ended recommendations (n = 378), the most frequently mentioned strategies were group work (39.2%) and completing practice assignments or coursework (20.4%). Using concrete examples or studying cases was recommended by 14%, often framed as a way to connect theory and practice. Teaching or explaining to others was also somewhat common (13%), as were recommendations to read or review the provided course materials (12.4%) and to specifically prepare by reading before and/or after class (11.9%). General recommendations to just practice a lot (e.g., "practice, practice, practice") were mentioned by 9.3%. Explicit recommendations to use retrieval practice (e.g., self-testing, practice exams) were less common (7.9%), and spaced repetition (1.6%), flashcards (1.6%), and studying worked examples (0.5%) were rare. Research Question 3: Facilitation of Students' Use of Learning Strategies

Course context

Responses to the facilitation questions were anchored to a single course that each instructor supervised and/or taught. Most respondents selected a course for which they were both course coordinator and instructor (71%), while 25% selected a course they taught at least three hours in without course-coordinator responsibility. Of the selected courses, 22% were described as mainly practical. The courses were most commonly second-or thirdyear (40%), followed by first-year (25%) and fourth-or fifth-year courses (21%). Fewer respondents referenced sixth-year (3%) or PhD-level courses (2%), and 8% indicated "other" level. The final assessment format (exam type) was most commonly an in-person, supervised school exam (48%), followed by an unsupervised take-home exam (31%). Around 13% reported using a mixed format (in-person + take-home exams) and 8% selected "other [specify]" and described formats such as artistic performances.

Facilitation of learning strategies and activities

As shown in Figure 1, instructors most strongly facilitated activities such as using concrete examples and cases, preparing for class, and elaboration through why/how questions. Facilitation of revisiting concepts over time (spaced repetition) and activities involving explaining or teaching to others were also relatively common. In contrast, instructors less often reported facilitating the use of worked examples, and strategies that more directly emphasize retrieval demands, such as retrieval practice and pretesting. For compilation-style summarizing, a small minority of instructors reported high facilitation (13% endorsing 4-5).

Research Question 4: University Pedagogy Training and Evidence Consistency

First, we tested the association between university pedagogy course status (UniPed status) and the evidence-consistency index derived from the vignette scores. A one-way ANOVA indicated that the groups did not differ, F(4, 429) = 0.52, p = .724, ηp² = .005. This conclusion did not change when teaching experience was added as a covariate.

Next, we tested whether instructors' strategy rankings differed based on UniPed status. As the UniPed status group "signed up but not started" was very small (n = 9 in the ranking sample), it was excluded from subgroup comparisons. We compared the rankings using pairwise likelihood-ratio tests on the worth estimates from the Plackett-Luce model (i.e., the model-implied probability that a strategy would be ranked first given the set of alternatives). For each pair of UniPed groups, we contrasted a model in which the two groups shared a common worth profile with a model that allowed group-specific worth profiles. P values were Holm-adjusted across the pairwise tests.

Only one pair differed reliably; the "completed < 5 years ago" versus "completed > 5 years ago" groups, χ²(6) = 26.81, p < .001. Both groups placed elaboration first. The primary difference between these two groups was the relative placement of summarizing and retrieval practice. Instructors who completed pedagogy training < 5 years ago ranked retrieval practice second (worth = 17.6%) and summarizing third (worth = 14.4%), whereas those who completed training > 5 years ago ranked summarizing second (worth = 21.4%) and retrieval practice third (worth = 15.8%). The remaining strategies followed the same ordering in both groups with highlighting, crammed repetition, and repeated rereading ranked lowest.

We then examined whether UniPed status was related to the facilitation composites derived from the EFA. For the Explain-Elaborate composite, facilitation differed by UniPed status, F(4, 590) = 3.04, p = .017, ηp² = .020. Tukey HSD post-hoc comparisons indicated higher scores among instructors currently attending UniPed compared with those reporting no UniPed (mean difference = 0.29, p = .044). For the Retrieval-Spacing composite, facilitation also differed by UniPed status, F(4, 590) = 2.63, p = .033, ηp² = .018. Post-hoc comparisons indicated slightly higher scores among instructors who had completed UniPed within the past five years compared with those who have not taken the course (mean difference = 0.23, p = .037).

Finally, we examined whether learning strategy recommendations differed by UniPed status. We excluded recommendation types with very low prevalence (< 5%) to reduce unstable comparisons, leaving 16 of 26 recommendation types for analysis. Because recommendations were coded as binary (present vs. not present), we conducted Pearson chi-square tests for each recommendation type. To account for multiple testing, we used a Bonferroni-adjusted threshold of .003 (α = .05/16). No test met this threshold (smallest p = .18), indicating no evidence of differences in recommendation types based on UniPed status in our sample.

Discussion

We surveyed higher education instructors about their beliefs, recommendations, and facilitation of various learning and instructional strategies, and whether university pedagogy training (UniPed) was associated with more evidence-consistent responses. Overall, our findings paint a complex picture which suggests that instructors' beliefs and practices are partly consistent and partly inconsistent with current empirical understandings. For instance, instructors correctly assigned low ranks to repeated rereading and highlighting but they ranked elaboration well above retrieval practice and spaced repetition, and ranked summarizing similarly to retrieval practice. A parallel pattern emerged for facilitation. Instructors often reported facilitating beneficial strategies such as concrete examples and elaboration, but much less frequently facilitated the use of retrieval practice, pretesting, and worked examples, all of which have strong empirical support. Finally, UniPed status showed no association with instructors' evidence-consistency.

Regarding strategy effectiveness beliefs (RQ1), the vignette responses provided the most fine-grained insight into instructors' understanding of the mechanisms underlying effective learning. Most instructors did not recognize the advantage of interleaving over blocking, consistent with previous work identifying interleaving as one of the least understood evidence-based strategies (Halamish, 2018; Morehead et al., 2016). For retrieval practice, the dominant response profile (57%) favored retrieval over rereading but simultaneously judged open-book summarizing as superior to retrieval, and only 19% of instructors consistently favored retrieval across both comparisons. Because summarizing with open materials reduces the need to recall from memory, this pattern suggests that many instructors view effective learning as primarily dependent on elaborative encoding of study materials, without fully recognizing that retrieving information from memory is a particularly potent learning mechanism. For spacing, instructors generally favored spaced over crammed practice, but a substantial minority did not adjust their judgments to the retention interval. Among instructors whose short-delay response left room for change, 51% shifted toward spacing at the longer interval, while 36% did not change and 13% shifted toward cramming. In addition, 19% never expected the spaced group to outperform the crammed group at either delay. This pattern suggests that many instructors may not fully consider how spacing interacts with retention intervals when evaluating learning strategies, which could influence how courses and assessments are designed. Taken together, the moderate evidence-consistency across vignettes suggests that many instructors do not clearly distinguish between strategies that improve short-term performance and those that more reliably support durable and transferable learning (Soderstrom & Bjork, 2015).

Instructors' recommendations (RQ2) largely mirrored the pattern observed in their beliefs. Although most instructors (78%) reported recommending learning strategies to students, these strategies were not necessarily those with the strongest empirical support, a pattern consistent with findings that such strategies are rarely mentioned by teachers (Surma et al., 2022). Group work was the most frequently recommended strategy. One possible explanation is that instructors value its social and motivational benefits, which are well documented and important (Kroeper et al., 2025; Zengilowski et al., 2023). However, the extent to which group work benefits students' learning depends heavily on how it is implemented (e.g., Mende et al., 2021; Song et al., 2025), and may be conditional on students' working memory capacity and prior knowledge (Andrews & Rapp, 2015; Hood et al., 2023). Hence, non-contingent recommendations to work in groups are unlikely to produce reliable learning benefits.

Notably, explicit recommendations to use retrieval practice were much less common, and spaced repetition was seldom mentioned. Some frequently recommended activitiessuch as completing practice assignments-may implicitly involve retrieval, but the low frequency of explicit mention suggests that instructors do not consistently frame these activities in ways that make the underlying learning mechanisms apparent. This may be consequential because students already misinterpret the mental effort associated with effective strategies as a sign of poor learning (Kirk-Johnson et al., 2019) and perceive more passive and fluent instruction as more effective, even when it is not (Deslauriers et al., 2019). It is also well documented that mental effort is generally aversive (David et al., 2024), suggesting that people will gravitate towards lower-effort strategies unless they understand the necessity or value of engaging in a more effortful activity. Without explicit framing from instructors, students may complete practice assignments in ways that reduce retrieval demands-for instance, by relying on open-book resources or AI tools rather than attempting recall from memory (Blasiman et al., 2017; Pan et al., 2025). Recent national data indicate that 72% of Norwegian students sometimes or often use AI tools to study, most commonly for explanations or summaries of course material (Norwegian Directorate for Higher Education and Skills, 2026). While this may support elaborative encoding, it does not require retrieval or facilitate spaced repetition. Given that students may be particularly receptive to concrete strategy recommendations from their instructors (Carpenter & Sanchez, 2025), this represents a missed opportunity to shape students' self-regulated learning.

Instructors' facilitation practices (RQ3) clustered into two broad dimensions: an Explain-Elaborate composite and a Retrieval-Spacing composite, with the former being facilitated considerably more often than the latter. Although this pattern may partly reflect how the items were formulated, at least two additional, non-mutually exclusive interpretations should be considered. First, instructors may recognize the value of repeated retrieval but regard it primarily as the students' responsibility. From this interpretation, instructors focus on explaining the material and supporting students' understanding, whereas practicing and retaining that knowledge is implicitly left to students' independent study. Second, and perhaps more consistent with the vignette findings, instructors may not fully appreciate the value of repeated retrieval because they assume that elaborative encoding-through activities such as summarizing, elaboration, or group discussion-is sufficient to produce durable learning. This assumption is not unreasonable. Elaborative and generative activities do support learning, and can complement retrieval practice (McDaniel, 2023). Such activities may even outperform retrieval practice under some conditions (Fiorella, 2023; Gonçalves et al., 2025), and it is well established that making material meaningful and linking new information to prior knowledge improves comprehension and subsequent memory (Bransford & Johnson, 1972; van Kesteren et al., 2018).

However, although more elaborative encoding can improve both immediate and delayed performance, emerging evidence suggests that it raises the level of initial learning without substantially reducing the rate at which information is subsequently forgotten (Peng et al., 2025; Rivera-Lares et al., 2022). If so, the practical implication is that even wellunderstood material remains vulnerable to forgetting over typical educational time intervals. Moreover, knowledge and skills that appear well established when assessed within a few days may show substantial additional forgetting across longer intervals (Radvansky et al., 2022), and the fluent performance produced by elaborative activities in the short term can easily be misread as evidence of durable learning (Soderstrom & Bjork, 2015). If instructors assume that strong initial understanding is largely sufficient for long-term retention, they may systematically underestimate how much students forget-even after high-quality instruction.

This underestimation may itself have a cognitive basis. Research on metacognition suggests that people are often poor at anticipating their own forgetting. Koriat et al. (2004) found that individuals who had recently studied material expected to remember it nearly as well after a week as after a day, and Kornell and Bjork (2009) found that learners substantially underestimated how much additional practice would improve later retention. If a similar stability illusion applies to instructors, it could help explain why elaborations, explanations, and concrete examples are facilitated more often than repeated retrieval, despite substantial evidence that repeated retrieval practice is more efficient.

Lastly, our results indicate only a few minor associations between UniPed status and more evidence-consistent responses (RQ4). Instructors who completed UniPed less than five years ago assigned slightly higher worth estimates to retrieval practice and slightly lower estimates to summarizing, but only relative to those who completed the course more than five years ago. There were no differences in instructors' recommendations. For facilitation, those currently attending UniPed scored slightly higher on the Explain-Elaborate composite and those who completed UniPed less than five years ago scored slightly higher on the Retrieval-Spacing composite, although both effects were small. These largely null results suggest that university pedagogy courses at the two institutions we investigated have not substantially shaped instructors' understanding of central learning mechanisms, or at least not sufficiently to be detected with a cross-sectional survey of this kind. This likely reflects the content and emphasis of past courses rather than the potential of such programs.

Practical implications

The clearest practical implication of our findings relates to faculty development, perhaps most immediately through university pedagogy courses. While there have been significant efforts in recent years to promote active learning in higher education, our findings suggest that instructors are not adequately familiar with the mechanisms that make a learning activity more or less effective. Specifically, instructors promote activities such as elaboration, summarizing, and group discussion, which may be seen as active, without recognizing that their effectiveness ultimately depends on whether they engage core mechanisms underlying effective learning, such as retrieval from memory. Faculty development and university pedagogy programs could therefore benefit from moving beyond broad dichotomies such as active versus passive or deep versus surface, towards a mechanism-oriented understanding grounded in the cognitive processes that underlie effective acquisition, retention, and transfer of learning. Moreover, across nearly all measures, a small but meaningful minority of instructors (approximately 10-30%) responded in ways that were opposite to evidence-based expectations. This suggests that faculty development efforts need to reach not only the majority who may benefit from a more nuanced understanding, but also instructors whose current conceptions may actively undermine effective instruction.

Retrieval and spacing stand out as two particularly important target mechanisms. Most instructors in our study correctly recognized retrieval practice as superior to rereading, which is encouraging. Many also recommended doing practice assignments. However, the majority did not make retrieval demands explicit, and favored open-book summarizing over retrieval practice. If instructors gain a more accurate understanding of when and how retrieval practice supports learning, they may design and recommend activities that better support durable and transferable learning.

Regarding spacing, it is important that instructors understand how the timing and distribution of repetitions affects short-term performance versus long-term retention and transfer. This is clear in professional education, where teaching is sometimes organized in intensive blocks. In nursing programs, for instance, high failure rates on drug dosage calculation exams have been a persistent challenge (Alteren & Nerdal, 2015; McMullan et al., 2010). A common response has been to deliver intensive calculation courses, often in close proximity to a final exam (Stolic, 2014). Several published studies also use immediate post-measurements as indicators of success (Grugnetti et al., 2014; Simonsen et al., 2014). From a memory perspective, this is problematic. Crammed practice can reduce immediate failure rates and produce confident learners even though it can compromise long-term retention and students' ability to transfer what they have learnt (Rohrer, 2015; Soderstrom & Bjork, 2015). Studies also indicate that learners can become "grossly overconfident" after crammed practice compared with spaced practice (Emeny et al., 2021; Kornell & Bjork, 2007; Logan et al., 2012), meaning that instructors who optimize for short-term outcomes may foster learners that are both overconfident and underprepared.

Strengths, Limitations, and Future Research

A central strength of this study is that we examined instructors' understanding and application of learning strategies across three related but distinct measures: beliefs about strategy effectiveness, open-ended recommendations to students, and self-reported facilitation of strategies in teaching. Additionally, to better distinguish evidence-consistent from evidence-inconsistent implementations (e.g., "reading the same text multiple times in a row"), we took particular care to formulate items that captured meaningful differences in how strategies were implemented. This was motivated by studies demonstrating that item wording can substantially affect conclusions. For example, Malain and Hartwig (2026) showed that previously observed null associations between self-reported spaced study and academic performance were likely driven by how "spaced study" had been operationalized. Similarly, Kuhbandner and Emmerdinger (2019) found that items intended to measure "repeated rereading" may instead be interpreted as rereading not-understood parts, which reflects a more productive practice.

However, the important limitation that follows is that our questionnaire has not been previously validated. Although validated instruments exist for assessing both students' learning strategies (Pintrich et al., 1993) and instructors' teaching activities (Williams et al., 2015), these did not offer the precision needed to distinguish between effective and ineffective implementations. Our approach therefore trades established psychometric properties for increased conceptual precision. Furthermore, self-report measures do not necessarily reflect real-world behavior (Podsakoff et al., 2003). This concern may be particularly relevant for the facilitation items. For instance, although rigorous implementations of spaced repetition appear uncommon (Rohrer, 2015), instructors in our sample reported facilitating spacing relatively often, which could reflect minimal practices such as mentioning last week's content, or including an end-of-semester recap lecture rather than more systematic implementations. Finally, our sample was drawn from only two institutions, with the majority of respondents from one university. The observed associations-or lack thereof-between university pedagogy training and instructors' beliefs and practices therefore reflect the characteristics of these specific courses, and not university pedagogy courses in general.

Future research should address these limitations in at least two ways. First, the survey items developed here could be subjected to systematic validation work, including assessment of reliability across different contexts and languages. Second, self-report measures could be complemented with more behavior-proximal indicators, such as analysis of course materials and assignments or structured observation of classroom practices. Regarding the vignettes specifically, future versions should include a rereading-versussummarizing comparison, specify the presence or absence of feedback for retrieval practice, and clarify that the final test format includes both factual recall and comprehension, so that instructors do not vary their responses based on assumptions about what the test measures.

Conclusion

We examined how higher education instructors understand, recommend, and facilitate various learning strategies. To their credit, instructors correctly identified lowutility strategies such as rereading and highlighting, and most recognized that retrieval practice is superior to restudy. However, instructors less often supported strategies such as interleaving, pretesting, spaced repetition, and worked examples, and the majority favored open-book summarizing over retrieval practice. Retrieval and spacing were also rarely mentioned in recommendations, and elaboration-oriented activities were facilitated considerably more than retrieval-based practices. University pedagogy training showed little association with more evidence-consistent beliefs or practices. Together, these findings suggest that many instructors hold an incomplete understanding of the mechanisms that support durable and transferable learning-emphasizing elaborative encoding and undervaluing repeated retrieval practice. Bridging this gap will require faculty development that moves beyond abstractions such as active versus passive learning toward a more mechanism-oriented understanding of how effective learning works.
