How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Youth First improved several adolescent outcomes during exposure and showed some sustained or delayed effects when delivered by government teachers through school systems in participating rural schools in Bihar.

Abstract Introduction

Adolescents face intersecting challenges that increase their risk of poor mental health, particularly in low-and middle-income countries, where 90% of adolescents live.

School-based wellbeing programs offer a promising, cost-effective prevention strategy, but evidence from large-scale, government-delivered interventions with long-term follow-up is limited. We evaluated Youth First, an in-school wellbeing program delivered by teachers in rural government schools in two districts of Bihar, India.

Methods

We conducted a secondary analysis of a cluster-randomized controlled trial involving 70 schools. Sixty-nine schools provided baseline data from 5,445 Grade 7-8 students and were randomized to Youth First or standard education. Data were collected at baseline, 8 months, and 20 months. Primary outcomes were physical wellbeing, psychological wellbeing, and social and peer support; secondary outcomes included psychosocial and behavioral measures. Modified intention-to-treat analyses used mixed-effects difference-indifferences models with school and student random intercepts. Primary-outcome p-values were adjusted using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure; secondary analyses were exploratory and unadjusted for multiplicity.

Results

Youth First significantly improved social and peer support across multiple time points and cohorts after adjustment, with an additional effect on psychological wellbeing.

Exploratory analyses found nominally significant effects on self-efficacy, empowerment, internalizing symptoms, and self-reported tobacco use, varying by grade and follow-up.

Several effects persisted or emerged in the baseline Grade 8 cohort one year after program completion.

Introduction

Globally, adolescents face challenges including climate change, social media pressures, rising violence and crime, poverty, discrimination, migration, and displacement (Kałwak et al., 2024; World Federation of Public Health Associations, 2025; World Health Organization, 2025). These crises are amplified for adolescents living in Low and Middle-Income Countries (LMICs; Alegría et al., 2018; Sánchez-Castro et al., 2024), which are also home to 90% of the world's adolescents (United Nations, Department of Economic and Social Affairs, Population Division, 2019). Mental health challenges exist in a vicious cycle with each of these other crises: mental health concerns are both exacerbated by and can worsen such challenges (Alegría et al., 2018; Mental Health and Poverty Project, 2008; Sánchez-Castro et al., 2024). Given that 75% of mental disorders emerge by age 24 (Kessler et al., 2005) and 1 in 7 adolescents worldwide experiences a mental disorder (World Health Organization, 2025), it is crucial to find better and more feasible ways to prevent mental health problems and promote mental wellbeing among adolescents.

One promising direction is to integrate universal wellbeing programs into schools. These programs could be particularly beneficial and cost-effective in LMICs, where a recent review showed that school-based mental health programs can avert a disability-adjusted life year (DALY) for $1.59 and return $346 per $1 invested (Stelmach et al., 2022, Supplemental Material File 4, p. 5). Such programs are gaining attention and commitment from LMIC governments (Barry et al., 2013; Harte & Barry, 2024), with multiple state and national governments integrating attention to wellbeing programming at policy levels (UNICEF, 2023; Ministry of Human Resource Development, 2020) and multilateral agencies releasing guidelines in support (World Health Organization and United Nations Educational, Scientific and Cultural Organization, 2021). However, despite increasing interest, there are few scalable programs with adequate evidence that have been conducted in LMICs (Barry et al., 2013; Gimba et al., 2020; Bradshaw et al., 2021).

There are three areas in which more research is needed to support full-scale, sustainable government adoption of wellbeing programming among adolescents in LMICs: (1) real-world effectiveness evidence on scalable programs; (2) clarity surrounding highpriority government concerns; and (3) longer-term follow-ups.

First, although there are multiple efficacy trials of such programs (e.g., interventions delivered by trained counselors or at smaller scale), evidence supporting effectiveness under "real-world" conditions (including delivery by intended at-scale delivery personnel and at larger scale) is relatively rare.

Second, governments often prioritize youth wellbeing when evidence clearly shows that such programs lead to improvements on existing policy mandates. Yet, for several priority areas, including substance use (e.g., tobacco), mental health conditions (e.g., anxiety and depression), and concrete indicators of gender empowerment (e.g., freedom of movement), evidence from school-based wellbeing programs remains limited. Although prior studies have reported positive effects (Barry et al., 2013; Harte & Barry, 2024; Lekagme et al., 2025), findings have lacked the specificity and consistency required to support large-scale adoption. For example, substance use has often been examined in aggregate rather than for specific substances (e.g., Leventhal et al., 2016). Additionally, much of the strongest evidence for these programs has included positive psychological constructs like wellbeing, resilience, and coping, with less attention to mental health symptoms that feature more prominently in government priorities. Further, evaluations of wellbeing programs have more often examined gender attitudes and norms than observable behaviors (Beckwith et al., 2023; Melendez-Torres et al., 2024).

Finally, follow-ups are rare among wellbeing program evaluations in LMICs (Harte & Barry, 2024). This gap is primarily due to resource constraints; indeed, follow-ups are less common in LMICs than in High Income Countries (HICs) (Franzen et al., 2017; Grande et al., 2023; McGregor et al., 2014). However, in order to fully understand these programs' benefits, it is crucial to evaluate past post-test as far as possible. As adolescence is a key period of transition, and supporting wellbeing among adolescents can support positive trajectories into adulthood (e.g., Patton et al., 2016), longitudinal follow-up is essential.

Youth First is an in-school wellbeing program for adolescents that has served 3.3 million individuals to date through teacher training and/or classroom sessions in India, Kenya, and Rwanda since its inception in 2009. It is generally delivered by trained schoolteachers during the school day via weekly sessions across 1-2 years. Youth First is among the most extensively evaluated school-based wellbeing programs in any LMIC. A recent systematic review of school-based wellbeing interventions found that only 3 out of 34 programs had been evaluated more than once through controlled or quasi-experimental designs (Ahmed et al., 2023), whereas Youth First has undergone five controlled or quasiexperimental evaluations. Findings across studies indicate multi-domain improvements, including psychosocial assets and wellbeing (e.g., peer relationships, psychological wellbeing, resilience, social-emotional assets, and self-efficacy), gender attitudes, health-related behaviors and access (e.g., nutrition, handwashing practices, ability to obtain care when needed, and lifetime substance use), and education indicators (e.g., grade progression, time spent on homework, student-teacher relationships (S. Leventhal & Andrew, 2020; Cooper, 2025).

Youth First is evidence-based, culturally-specific, and designed for scale and government adoption: government schoolteachers receive training that is both rigorous yet feasible to integrate within government systems (K. S. Leventhal et al., 2018) and provided with easy-to-follow session manuals (WorldBeing, 2018). Youth First uses a "just right" dosage: enough to be effective but not overwhelming for teachers to complete along with other responsibilities (Goodwin et al., 2021). Youth First, and practices based on its learning and evidence, is in process of being integrated into multiple state and national government systems across India, Kenya, and Rwanda. It is therefore a good candidate for exploring the three evidence gaps described above.

To address these evidence gaps, we conducted a secondary analysis from a clusterrandomized controlled trial (cRCT) of Youth First delivered by trained government teachers in participating rural government schools in Bihar, India. Outcomes were assessed, during intervention delivery for a Grade 7 and a Grade 8 cohort, and, for the Grade 8 cohort, approximately one year after completion. We hypothesized that students receiving Youth First would show greater improvements in physical, psychological, and social wellbeing (primary outcomes), as well as in other psychosocial outcomes, including self-efficacy, resilience, internalizing symptoms, gender equality attitudes, empowerment, and tobacco use (secondary outcomes), compared with students at control schools.

Methods

Design

This study involved secondary analysis of data gathered during a cluster-randomized controlled trial (cRCT) conducted in Bihar, India, from 2018-2020. Schools served as the unit of randomization rather than individual students because Youth First training and delivery operated at the school and classroom levels. A total of 70 schools naïve to the Youth First Study staff visited potentially eligible schools to assess remaining eligibility criteria and ascertain willingness to participate. Eligible schools were required to have a studentteacher ratio of 50:1 or lower; an appropriate space for program sessions; no delayed start to the 2017-18 school year or extensive flooding during that year; no concurrent emotionalresilience or life-skills program; and accessibility within three hours of the nearest urban center. Principals and teachers also had to indicate their willingness to support teacher training and weekly program delivery. Of the schools assessed, 72 met all eligibility requirements and agreed to participate. Seventy of these schools were randomly selected for inclusion in the trial.

The 70 selected schools were randomly assigned to study arms using a computergenerated sequence. School eligibility and agreement to participate were established before random selection and allocation. An external evaluator who was not involved in school recruitment or program implementation randomly selected 70 of the 72 eligible schools, generated the allocation sequence, and assigned the schools to study arms; consequently, school assignment was not known during school recruitment. Randomization was not stratified or matched by district, school size, or other school characteristics; eligible schools from Darbhanga and Patna districts were pooled before allocation. No balance restrictions or rerandomization criteria were applied. No formal cluster-level balance assessment was conducted before intervention delivery, and the allocation was not subsequently modified on the basis of school characteristics. Available baseline school-level characteristics by randomized arm are reported descriptively in Supplementary Table I. Data collectors were not informed of schools' study-arm assignments and were not involved in intervention delivery, although the success of blinding was not formally assessed.

Data were collected at three timepoints: baseline (prior to the start of the intervention); first follow-up (approximately 8 months after intervention initiation); and second follow-up (approximately 20 months after intervention initiation). These timepoints were selected to capture both short-term (8-month) and longer-term (20-month) intervention effects.

The duration of exposure to Youth First varied by student grade. Within intervention schools, students enrolled in Grade 8 at baseline received one year of intervention, while students enrolled in Grade 7 at baseline received two consecutive years of intervention.

Outcomes were assessed for both cohorts at all three timepoints, allowing for examination of intervention effects over time across differing exposure levels.

Schools assigned to the control arm continued with their usual curriculum and school activities. As a condition of eligibility, participating schools were not implementing a similar emotional-resilience or life-skills program at the middle-school level at study entry. Controlschool teachers did not receive Youth First training, manuals, mentoring, or implementation support, and Youth First sessions were not delivered in control schools during the study period. Routine educational practices and other activities in control schools were not documented through a formal process or qualitative evaluation.

Participants

Participants were students enrolled in Grades 7 and 8 at baseline in government schools in two districts of Bihar, India: Dharbanga and Patna. Students were recruited into the study after schools had been assigned; however, all students enrolled in the eligible grades at participating schools were invited to participate. Students entered the longitudinal analytic cohort if parental consent and student assent were obtained and they participated in baseline data collection; students who were absent during baseline data collection or did not provide the required consent or assent were therefore not included.. Of the 70 randomized schools, one control school withdrew following a request from parents during baseline data collection and contributed no student-level data at any study wave. Because no baseline outcomes or covariate data were available for this school, it could not be included in outcome analyses. The achieved baseline analytic sample therefore comprised 69 schools (40 intervention and 29 control schools) and 5,445 students. Among the randomized schools, 24 intervention schools and 20 control schools were located in Darbanga, while 16 intervention schools and 10 control schools were located in Patna (Supplementary Table I). Data were collected at baseline, first follow-up (8 months) and second follow-up (20 months), with retention rates of 92% and 88% at the respective followups.

Written parental consent and student assent were obtained prior to participation. All study procedures were approved by Sangath Institutional Review Board.

Sample Size and Power Calculation

The sample size was determined a priori based on the primary outcomes of student physical, psychological, and social wellbeing over the follow-up period. Assuming a standardized effect size of 0.18, an intra-cluster correlation coefficient (ICC) of 0.05, a twosided alpha of 0.05, and 80% power, we estimated that 70 schools (40 intervention and 30 control) with an average of 45 students per school would be required to detect a statistically significant difference between study arms. These calculations accounted for the clusterrandomized design and anticipated levels of attrition.

The achieved baseline sample included 69 schools and 5,445 students, corresponding to an average of 78.9 students per school, compared with the planned average of 45. The observed baseline ICCs for the primary outcomes were 0.04 for physical wellbeing and 0.03 for both psychological wellbeing and social and peer support, all below the value of 0.05 assumed in the power calculation.

Procedure

Baseline data were collected prior to initiating the intervention. Student surveys were administered at each timepoint during regular school hours using paper-based questionnaires in Hindi. Data collectors received training on study protocols, ethical research among adolescents, and procedures for responding to and referring students in distress as needed. During intervention delivery, potential adverse or unintended effects were monitored through routine session observations and implementation-support contacts, including signs or reports of student distress, discomfort with sensitive content, stigma, peer conflict, or other adverse changes in classroom or peer dynamics. This monitoring was programmatic rather than based on a standardized adverse-event instrument, and the study did not formally assess whether adverse or unintended effects varied by gender or baseline vulnerability.

Measures

Outcome domains were selected to align with Youth First's ecological resilience framework and theory of change, which hypothesized effects on proximal psychosocial assets and skills, including resilience, self-efficacy, social connection, and gender attitudes, as well as physical, psychological, and social wellbeing, empowerment, and health-related behaviors (Benard, 1991; Brodsky & Cattaneo, L. B., 2013; Leventhal et al., 2016https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?UmGTNN ). Among available instruments, preference was given to established measures suitable for adolescents, applicable in low-resource settings or previously used in India, and feasible for repeated group administration.

Primary Outcomes

Primary outcomes assessed adolescents' physical and mental health. These outcomes were measured using scales from the KIDSCREEN-52 questionnaire, a screening instrument for the quality of life of children and adolescents (Ravens-Sieberer, et al., 2014). To assess physical health, the 5-item KIDSCREEN Physical Wellbeing Subscale was used. To assess mental health, the 6-item KIDSCREEN Psychological Wellbeing Subscale and the 6-item KIDSCREEN Social and Peer Support Subscale were used. Across all three scales, items were scored from 1-5, with higher scores indicating higher physical, psychological, or social wellbeing. For each scale, scores were computed as the sum of item responses.

All three KIDSCREEN scales have demonstrated good reliability and validity among adolescents in prior studies and similar settings (Power et al., 2019; Ravens-Sieberer, et al., 2014; Tzavara et al., 2012). In the current sample, internal consistency was acceptable across waves (see Supplementary Table 2).

Secondary Outcomes

Secondary outcomes included psychosocial assets and skills, gender equality attitudes, empowerment, and substance use.

Assets and skills.

Assets and skills were measured using three validated scales: Schwarzer's Generalized Self-Efficacy Scale to assess self-efficacy (Schwarzer & Jerusalem, 1995); the Connor-Davidson Resilience Scale-10 to assess emotional resilience (Campbell-Sills & Stein, 2007), and the Internalizing Factors Subscale of the Strength and Difficulties Questionnaire to assess internalizing symptoms, including anxiety, depression, and social withdrawal (Goodman, 1997).

Gender equality attitudes.

Gender equality attitudes were measured using the Gender Roles and Gender-Based Violence Subscales of the International Center for Research on Women's adaptation of the Gender-Equitable Men Scale (Achyut et al., 2017).

Empowerment. Empowerment was measured using the Empowerment Scale from the Global Early Adolescent Study, measuring freedom of decision-making, movement, and voice (Zimmerman et al., 2019).

Substance use. Substance use was assessed using a single item asking respondents to self-report the number of times they had smoked tobacco in the previous 30 days. This measure was converted to a binary variable for analysis (smoked one or more times in the past 30 days vs. did not smoke).

Internal consistency estimates for secondary outcome measures ranged from Cronbach's α = .63-.84 across scales and waves, with the lowest internal consistency for internalizing factors and gender-based violence (see Supplementary Table 2).

Covariates

Pre-specified covariates included student age, sex, marital status, and continuous school enrollment at baseline. Age and sex were included as basic developmental and demographic characteristics that the evaluation protocol identified as potential sources of variation in intervention effects. Marital status and school enrollment reflected early marriage and school discontinuation, which were identified as salient contextual vulnerabilities in Youth First's resilience framework (Benard, 1991). Covariates were selected before outcome modeling and not on the basis of observed differences or statistical significance.

Measure Adaptation and Translation

All measures were translated and back-translated into Hindi by bilingual English-Hindi speakers. Cognitive interviews and pilot testing were conducted to ensure comprehension and cultural relevance. Minor adaptations were made to item wording without altering underlying constructs. Formal measurement invariance across waves or study arms was not evaluated.

Analysis

Analyses followed a modified intention-to-treat framework comprising the 69 randomized schools that contributed baseline data. All students in the baseline analytic cohort were analyzed according to their school's randomized assignment, regardless of intervention attendance, exposure, or adherence. The control school that withdrew without contributing baseline data was not included in imputation or analysis. Students who entered participating classes after baseline, and students who were enrolled at baseline but did not contribute baseline data, were not added to the longitudinal analytic cohort. No schools or baseline participants were subsequently excluded on the basis of intervention adherence or observed outcomes.

Student outcomes were analyzed using a difference-in-differences (DiD) framework. This approach was selected because the target estimate was the between-arm difference in change from baseline at each follow-up and because it permitted all three study waves to be modeled within a common longitudinal framework. The parallel trends assumption of difference-in-differences was considered reasonable a priori given the study's randomized design. Baseline measurements were included as outcome observations rather than as covariates. The treatment main effect estimated any baseline difference between randomized arms, while the treatment-by-time interactions estimated differences between intervention and control schools in change from baseline to each follow-up. The DiD specification was retained consistently across outcomes, cohorts, and follow-up comparisons rather than selecting models based on the statistical significance of individual results.

To account for the cluster-randomized design and the longitudinal structure of the data, analyses employed mixed-effects regression models with observations nested within students and students nested within schools. Models included random intercepts for schools to account for clustering of students within schools and random intercepts for students to account for repeated observations within students over time. Teacher-level random effects were not included because students could not be uniquely or consistently linked to a single teacher across implementation years. For inference on the fixed effects, we used school-level cluster-robust standard errors with the bias-reduced CR2 small-sample correction, with school treated as independent clusters. Statistical tests and confidence intervals used Satterthwaite-adjusted degrees of freedom. Standard errors were clustered at the school level, the unit of randomization, using the bias-reduced CR2 cluster-robust variance estimator. Confidence intervals and statistical tests incorporated finite-sample degrees of freedom, and estimates across the 12 imputed datasets were combined using Rubin's rules. Time was modeled categorically, with baseline serving as the reference. The treatment-by-time interaction provided the DiD estimate of intervention effects at the relevant follow-up. For continuous outcomes, linear mixed-effects regression models were used, with results reported as adjusted mean differences and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Tobacco use, measured as a binary outcome, was analyzed using mixed-effects logistic regression, with results reported as adjusted odds ratios (aORs) and 95% CIs. No minimally importantdifference thresholds were prespecified or applied. Statistical significance was therefore not treated as establishing practical importance; effect magnitude was interpreted using adjusted estimates, 95% confidence intervals, and standardized effect sizes.

Models adjusted for pre-specified covariates. The combined-sample, grade-stratified, and follow-up-specific analyses were specified a priori in the statistical analysis plan. Gradestratified analyses were planned because the baseline grade cohorts differed in intervention exposure: baseline Grade 8 students received one year of Youth First, whereas baseline Grade 7 students received two years. Analyses at first follow-up examined the combined sample and prespecified grade-specific estimates after approximately eight months. At second follow-up, analyses were conducted separately by baseline grade to distinguish two-year intervention effects among Grade 7 students from one-year post-intervention effects among Grade 8 students. The outcomes, covariates, and mixed-effects difference-in-differences model specifications reported here followed the original analysis plan.

The three primary outcomes (physical wellbeing, psychological wellbeing, and social and peer support) were treated as co-primary outcomes. Within each prespecified combined-sample or grade-specific follow-up comparison, the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure was applied across the three primary-outcome tests to control the false discovery rate. Statistical significance for the primary outcomes was assessed using Benjamini-Hochberg-adjusted p-values at a two-sided threshold of .05. No hierarchical testing sequence, global multivariate test, or composite primary outcome was used because the three primary outcomes represent distinct constructs. No multiplicity adjustment was applied to the secondary outcomes; secondary-outcome p-values are nominal and these analyses are interpreted as exploratory.

Missing follow-up data among students in the baseline analytic cohort were addressed using multiple imputation by chained equations under the assumption that data were missing at random (Rubin, 1987). Twelve imputed datasets were generated using an imputation model that included 356 variables, including all outcome measures, baseline covariates, and auxiliary variables associated with missingness. Multiple imputation was not specified in the original statistical analysis plan and was added during the present analysis to reduce potential bias and loss of precision associated with missing longitudinal observations.

The originally planned analyses were retained as a sensitivity analysis.

Item responses were imputed, and outcome scale scores were reconstructed within each completed dataset. For each pre-specified treatment-by-time estimate from the pooled continuous-outcome models, we calculated the fraction of missing information (FMI) and the relative efficiency of 12 imputations compared with an infinite number of imputations.

Among the 15 co-primary estimates, FMI ranged from 2% to 9% and relative efficiency ranged from 99.3% to 99.8%. Among the 30 secondary continuous-outcome estimates, FMI ranged from 2% to 17% and relative efficiency ranged from 98.6% to 99.8%.

We conducted complete-case sensitivity analyses for each continuous outcome and follow-up comparison. Complete cases were defined separately for each analysis as participants with observed outcome values at baseline and the relevant follow-up and complete data for treatment group, gender, age, baseline continuous school enrollment, and baseline marital status. We applied the same linear mixed-effects model used in the multiply-imputed analyses, including fixed effects for treatment group, time, and their interaction and random intercepts for students and schools. We used maximum-likelihood estimation with standard errors clustered by school.

All statistical tests were two-sided. Analyses were conducted using R version 4.5.1.

Analysts had access to study-arm identifiers and were not blinded during the analyses.

Results

Results are presented regarding one-year effects for both grades, two-year intervention effects among Grade 7 students, and one-year post-intervention effects among Grade 8 students.

Sample Characteristics

Of the 70 randomized schools, one control school withdrew during baseline data collection without contributing student-level data. The modified intention-to-treat analytic cohort therefore comprised 5,445 students from 69 schools: 3,054 intervention students and 2,391 control students. Fifty-one percent of students were in Grade 7 and 49% were in Grade 8 at baseline. The mean participant age was 12.74 years (SD = 1.20), and 56% identified as female. The study flow diagram is presented in Figure 1.

At first follow-up, data were available for 5,010 students (92% retention). Retention at first follow-up was significantly lower in the intervention arm (89%) than in the control arm (95%, p = .000). At second follow-up (approximately 20 months post-intervention initiation), data were available for 4,777 students (88% retention). Retention rates at second follow-up did not differ significantly by study arm.

Student-level baseline characteristics were generally similar between intervention and control groups (Table 1). Continuous school enrollment at baseline was significantly higher in the control compared to the intervention arm (p = .041). This variable was included as a covariate in subsequent analysis. No statistically significant differences were observed across arms for the remaining demographic characteristics. Implementation Fidelity

Youth First was implemented in the 40 intervention schools using manualized training and session support procedures. A WorldBeing implementation report documented 119 observed sessions in the intervention schools. Mean implementation fidelity was 2.5 and mean implementation quality was 2.3 on a scale from 1-3, where 1 indicated a need for significant improvement, 2 indicated a need for some improvement, and 3 indicated excellent implementation. The report also indicates that 93% of targeted classes within the intervention schools completed all 25 sessions. In total, 167 teachers delivered Youth First to 3,581 students in Grades 7 and 8. This delivery population included students who entered participating classes after baseline or did not participate in baseline data collection, and was therefore larger than the intervention arm's baseline analytic sample. Although the archived report documented program-specific teacher training, manuals, mentoring, and session support, it did not provide sufficiently detailed resource-use or cost data to quantify teachertraining time, implementation staffing, material costs, opportunity costs, or per-school or perstudent delivery costs.

The available fidelity information consisted of aggregate summaries. Observations were not conducted in uniform numbers across schools, and the report did not provide school-specific observation counts or fidelity estimates. Student-level attendance and session exposure were not linked to outcome data. No intervention-related adverse or unintended effects were reported or identified through the monitoring procedures.

Sensitivity Analysis

Because follow-up missingness was associated with study arm at first follow-up, we compared the multiply-imputed estimates with complete-case estimates. The estimates were generally similar in direction and magnitude, and conclusions for the primary outcomes were unchanged (Supplementary Tables 3 4 5 6 7). Complete-case sample sizes represented approximately 66%-84% of the corresponding multiply-imputed analytic samples. The direction of the estimated intervention effect was the same in 42 of 45 comparisons. The three exceptions involved KIDSCREEN Psychological Wellbeing at the first follow-up, for which both the multiply-imputed and complete-case confidence intervals included zero.

Complete-case confidence intervals were consistently wider, and fewer excluded zero than in the multiply-imputed analyses.

Discussion

This study, based on secondary data analysis from a longitudinal cRCT of Youth First among Bihar, India adolescents, represents one of the largest RCTs to date of an in-school wellbeing intervention for middle school students in an LMIC. Compared to 34 LMIC studies identified in a recent systematic review of school-based wellbeing interventions, the current study is larger than 85% of the studies for all age groups and 100% of middle-school focused studies (Ahmed et al., 2023). Youth First produced improvements relative to control across several outcomes, including social and peer support, psychological wellbeing, self-efficacy, emotional resilience, internalizing factors, gender attitudes, empowerment, and self-reported tobacco use, although patterns varied by grade and follow-up. Several effects remained evident or emerged one year after intervention completion. Additionally, this study contributes insights into three priority areas: (1) real-world effectiveness under supported government-delivery conditions; (2) outcomes in key priority areas; and (3) longer-termfollow-up.

Effectiveness under supported government-delivery conditions

Previous studies of wellbeing programs in schools have often evaluated interventions delivered by those outside of the intended at-scale system (e.g., counselors) rather than by the intended at-scale providers (e.g., teachers; Barry et al., 2013; Bradshaw et al., 2021). Many studies have also been on much smaller samples. This evidence gap exists in part because very few programs are scalable and fully prepared for integration into government systems in LMICs (Gimba et al., 2020; Harte & Barry, 2024). Additionally, resources required for conducting and evaluating larger, government-integrated interventions are scarce. By contrast, this evaluation focused on 5,445 students in 69 participating rural government schools. Youth First was delivered during the school day by government teachers who received program-specific training, manuals, mentoring, and session support. The findings therefore provide evidence of effectiveness under these supported implementation conditions.

In other studies of scaled-up health interventions, effects have attenuated vs. effects prior to scale-up by approximately 40% (Lane et al., 2021). Shifting delivery personnel can also have unintended consequences: for example, a large-scale effectiveness trial in India of an adolescent health and wellbeing program, the SEHER trial, showed little effect from a teacher-delivered intervention in schools, yet showed effects when counselor-delivered (Shinde et al., 2020). Unfortunately, counselors are not yet reliably present across schools in India, impeding scalability of that model via existing systems. It is therefore highly encouraging that Youth First benefited students across a host of outcomes even when conducted at larger scale and utilizing existing government delivery personnel (teachers) in participating rural schools.

The trial estimated the overall effect of Youth First as a multicomponent intervention and did not include mediation or component analysis. It therefore cannot determine whether its teacher-training model, program intensity, "inside-out" concept progression, or changes in psychosocial assets accounted for the observed effects. However, research suggests that training teachers to deliver wellbeing programming is crucial, particularly in LMICs, as the pedagogical and facilitation techniques required are often distinct from what teachers are used to and have been trained for previously (Schonert-Reichl, 2017; Murano et al., 2019; Mastercard Foundation, 2020; Bremner et al., 2023). Accordingly, the content and format of teacher training may influence implementation and warrant direct evaluation.

Youth First comprises not only student sessions but also rigorous, manualized training for teachers that has been honed over the course of 15 years to be delivered over only a few days of in-person time (K. S. Leventhal et al., 2018). In other studies of Youth First, the intervention's specific teacher training approach has been identified as one of the main implementation drivers of success (Cooper, 2025).

Youth First is also designed to balance sufficient exposure to evidence-based activities with feasibility within a crowded school curriculum and timetable. As others have pointed out (Goodwin et al., 2021), having enough time in the school day, particularly given the other demands on teachers' time in LMICs, is a major barrier to integrating wellbeing programming into schools. Youth First is designed to be delivered across approximately 15-25 sessions per school year, allowing coverage of multiple concepts while accommodating holidays, examinations, and other scheduling constraints. Although the present study did not compare alternative program dose or test whether program intensity contributed to the observed outcomes, this is one hypothesis behind the observed program effects.

Finally, Youth First adheres carefully to an "inside-out" concept progression in which students focus first on intrapersonal assets and skills, such as self-concept and emotion regulation, and then move on to focus on interpersonal assets and skills, such as conflict resolution and assertive communication. The present trial did not compare alternative sequences or test sequencing as a mediator, but evidence in higher income settings suggests that this concept sequencing may influence the success of school-based wellbeing programs (Cipriano et al., 2023).

Youth First's design features should be considered candidate implementation factors rather than mechanisms demonstrated by this study. Future studies should investigate them through prespecified component, moderator, and mediation analyses. Clarifying whether and under what conditions particular design and implementation features contribute to program effects will be important for guiding government adoption. Key Priority Areas: Mental Health Symptoms, Substance Use, and Gender Empowerment In India, some of the greatest priorities for state and national governments, including Ministries of Education and Health, include gender empowerment, prevention of mental disorders, and substance use prevention (particularly tobacco; e.g., Ministry of Women and Child Development, 2015; Ministry of Health and Family Welfare, 2016; Ministry of Education, 2021). Unfortunately, there is limited evidence supporting scalable programs that are effective for these outcomes, leaving ministries with little clear direction. For example, one intervention among adolescents in Haryana, India, showed improvements in gender attitudes and behavior vs. control (Dhar et al., 2022), but was an intensive, multi-year intervention, making government integration more difficult.

By contrast, the current study shows that it is possible to significantly improve adolescent mental disorder symptoms, gender empowerment behaviors, and self-reported tobacco use via an intervention that can be delivered by teachers within the school day, requiring less than one week of in-person training for teachers. The findings from this study suggest that a single intervention may benefit multiple policy-relevant domains.

Longer-term evidence

Approximately one year after the intervention ended, the Grade 8 cohort assigned to Youth First showed more favorable changes than controls across several outcomes, including psychological wellbeing, social and peer support, self-efficacy, emotional resilience, internalizing symptoms, empowerment, and self-reported tobacco use. Because the difference-in-differences analysis compares changes between the two arms, it accounts for developmental or maturation trends shared by both groups. Given random assignment at the school level, we had no reason to expect maturation to differ systematically between the intervention and control arms.

Follow-up assessments are uncommon in evaluations of school-based wellbeing programs. A large meta-analysis of 252 interventions across 53 countries found that only 11% included follow-up beyond four months (Cipriano et al., 2023). Longitudinal follow-up is particularly rare in studies involving adolescents in LMICs due to resource and implementation constraints (Barry et al., 2013; Tol et al., 2011). Thus, the favorable changes observed among the Grade 8 cohort one year after intervention completion is encouraging.

The Grade 8 findings contribute to an emerging body of research suggesting that the effects of some wellbeing interventions may persist or become more apparent over time (Brunwasser & Garber, 2016; Taylor et al., 2017; Cipriano et al., 2023). This possibility is also supported via theories, such as Fredrickson's "broaden-and-build" theory, in which supporting positive outcomes leads to more positive outcomes in an upward spiral (Fredrickson, 2004). Although this study's findings support the value of longer-term followup, it did not test mediation and cannot determine whether earlier changes in self-efficacy, empowerment, or other psychosocial assets contributed to later changes in wellbeing.

Future studies should examine these potential pathways using prespecified mediation analyses and temporally ordered measures.

Limitations

The original cRCT was designed to include four data collection waves: baseline, first follow-up (~8 months), second follow-up (~20 months), and third follow-up (~32 months). The third follow-up was intended to assess longer-term post-intervention effects, including oneyear post-intervention outcomes for the Grade 7 cohort and two-year post-intervention outcomes for the Grade 8 cohort. However, prolonged school closures resulting from the COVID-19 pandemic necessitated cancellation of the third follow-up, limiting the ability to examine longer-term effects.

A second limitation concerns missing data. Although complete-case analyses produced broadly similar estimates, they were less precise than the multiply-imputed analyses. Multiple imputation relies on assumptions about the missing-data mechanism, while complete-case estimates may be biased if data completeness is related to outcomes.

Residual bias therefore cannot be excluded, and individual outcome comparisons should be interpreted cautiously.

A third limitation concerns interpretation across outcomes, follow-ups, and grade cohorts. Observed effects varied across these comparisons, and because no mediation or component analyses were conducted, the study cannot identify the mechanisms underlying this variation. Measurement limitations also warrant caution. Tobacco use was self-reported without biochemical validation or indirect questioning, so differential underreporting following exposure to health-behavior messaging cannot be excluded. Measurement invariance of the psychosocial scales across waves and study arms was not formally evaluated. Although using the same measures in both randomized arms and comparing changes between them reduces concern about shifts in interpretation shared by both groups, intervention-related differential shifts remain possible. Longitudinal estimates should therefore be interpreted as between-arm differences in observed scale scores rather than definitive changes in fully invariant constructs (Widaman et al., 2010; Fokkema et al., 2013).

Finally, although Benjamini-Hochberg adjustment was applied to the three co-primary outcomes, secondary-outcome p-values were unadjusted and should be considered exploratory.

A fourth limitation concerns the representativeness of the participating schools.

Eligibility was restricted to rural government schools in two districts that met specified enrollment, accessibility, student-teacher ratio, infrastructure, and implementation-readiness criteria. Requiring principals and teachers to indicate willingness to participate may also have selected schools with stronger leadership or greater capacity to implement the program.

Although these criteria allowed Youth First to be evaluated under realistic governmentdelivery conditions, they may limit generalizability to urban, remote, flood-affected, lowercapacity, or less implementation-ready schools. The findings should therefore be generalized primarily to rural government schools with similar characteristics and support structures.

A fifth limitation is that potential contamination between intervention and control schools was not formally assessed. Randomization at the school level and restriction of teacher training, program materials, and program delivery to intervention schools reduced opportunities for contamination, consistent with the use of cluster randomization to limit diffusion between study arms (Chaplin & Dwan, 2023). However, geographic distances and cross-school interactions were not systematically recorded; consequently, spillover cannot be excluded, and a distance-based sensitivity analysis could not be conducted. Additionally, Hawthorne-type or other awareness effects resulting from study participation and repeated data collection were not assessed. Such effects may have influenced behavior or selfreported outcomes in either study arm, though it is worth noting that spillover would most plausibly have attenuated the estimated between-arm effects If control students were indirectly exposed to intervention content (Torgerson, 2001).

Finally, implementation fidelity and dose were available only as aggregate summaries.

Student attendance and session exposure were not linked to outcome data, and session observations were not conducted in uniform numbers across schools; the implementation report did not provide school-specific observation counts or fidelity estimates. Consequently, the study could not assess individual-or school-level dose-response patterns or determine whether effects were broadly distributed across intervention schools or concentrated among schools with higher implementation fidelity. Any school-level fidelity-outcome association would also have been exploratory and vulnerable to confounding because fidelity is a postrandomization implementation characteristic. Detailed cost and resource-use data were also unavailable, and implementation burden was not compared with existing government programming. Consequently, the study cannot establish the affordability, cost-effectiveness, or resource requirements of broader government implementation; these questions require prospective implementation and economic evaluation.

Conclusion

In addressing mental health needs of adolescents worldwide, school-based interventions remain promising. This study provides evidence that Youth First, a universal school-based mental health promotion program designed for scale, can improve mental health, empowerment, and substance use outcomes for adolescents when delivered by government teachers in participating rural schools in two districts of Bihar, India. Several effects remained evident or emerged at longer-term follow-up, although patterns varied across outcomes, grade cohorts, and time points. These findings are of critical importance to governments with priority areas such as gender empowerment, substance use prevention, and mental health among adolescents, as well as to practitioners, school administrators, and academics seeking to find better, more scalable ways to support adolescent wellbeing in the longer term. Further evaluation across a broader and more representative range of schools and government support contexts is warranted.
