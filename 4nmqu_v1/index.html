Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

Abstract

People with aphantasia seem unable to create visual imagery, yet they perform mental manipulation of objects in the mind. Different frameworks have different views on how this is possible. Mental rotation is a particularly useful test case for comparing these frameworks, as people with aphantasia show systematic differences and similarities on this task compared to people without aphantasia. Here, we propose a computational model of mental rotation based on the 'broken rendering' account of aphantasia (Balaban & Ullman, 2025), which separates physical/spatial representations from graphical, picture-like ones. Our model proposes that both people with and without aphantasia are similar in their identification and rotation of objects, but differ in the comparison stage: People with and without aphantasia can use spatial/physical similarity to compare rotated objects, but people with typical imagery can also render a rotated object back into a 2D, pixel-like representation, and carry out a fast-but-less-reliable comparison between such visual depictions. Both sources of comparison (physical and visual) provide evidence to a single drift-diffusion decision process. We used our model to generate simulated experimental data, which we compared to the detailed findings of Kay et al., 2024. We found that our model captures the qualitative and quantitative patterns of behavior in a mental rotation task for people with and without aphantasia, including the canonical increase in response time (and decrease in accuracy) with angle growth, the slower but more accurate responses in aphantasia, and the opposite within-group correlations. This lends support to the broader framework of 'broken rendering' over 'different strategies' and 'lack of meta-access' as approaches to aphantasia. The findings hold across a wide set of parameter settings, and the model makes novel and testable predictions for further study in mental rotation.

Introduction

We are not passive audience members in the theater of the mind, we are directors. We can manipulate the mental images that come before us, moving them about and changing them. One of the most well-studied mental manipulations of this sort is mental rotation: people seem able to take an imagined item and turn it around in various ways (Shepard & Metzler, 1971). A compelling feature of mental rotation is the linear relationship between the time it takes people to mentally rotate an object, and the real angular distance that same object would travel, were it to exist in reality (Cooper & Shepard, 1973; Shepard & Cooper, 1982 /1986; Shepard & Metzler, 1971). This linear relationship is often taken as evidence that the format of mental imagery resembles that of perception (Kosslyn et al., 2006), and that the manipulation of mental images proceeds through mental simulation (Shepard & Cooper, 1982 /1986). The mental rotation task has been studied in many ways over the decades, including neurally (Tomasino & Gremese, 2016; Zacks, 2008), behaviorally (Just & Carpenter, 1976; Shepard & Cooper, 1982 /1986), developmentally (Frick et al., 2014), and computationally (Khazoum et al., 2026; Provost & Heathcote, 2015). The surrounding claims regarding the format of mental imagery have also been hotly debated (Just & Carpenter, 1976; Kosslyn et al., 2006; Pylyshyn, 2002). The main question for us here is: how can a director manipulate something that they cannot see?

When we imagine things, many of us seem to see something. But, it seems that some of us cannot see things in the mind's eye at all. Over the past decade, there has been a tremendous amount of research and interest in aphantasia, a condition in which people cannot voluntarily create visual mental images (Kay et al., 2022; Keogh & Pearson, 2018; Milton et al., 2021; Wicken et al., 2021; A. Zeman et al., 2015). For people with aphantasia, the stage before the mind's eye seems dark or non-existent. And yet, people with aphantasia are apparently able to direct the goings-on in the mental stage: many studies suggest that people with aphantasia are able to carry out a wide range of imagery tasks (Bainbridge et al., 2021; Milton et al., 2021; Pounder et al., 2022; A. Z. Zeman et al., 2010; Zhao et al., 2022). How can this be?

The views on how people with aphantasia carry out mental imagery tasks can be roughly sorted into 3 camps: (1) Different strategies. In this view, people with aphantasia use non-imagery based strategies to carry out mental imagery tasks (Kay et al., 2024; Reeder et al., 2024). If this were true, it would not be surprising. People use various mental strategies to carry out the same operations. But, such a view needs to account for the cases in which people with and without aphantasia seem to be broadly similar. While it is not unreasonable to suggest that different strategies converge in their final output, it does seem like a suspicious coincidence that different strategies would just-so-happen to produce similar behavioral profiles on the same task (including in mental rotation), and a person who holds this view would need to spell out the specific strategies people with aphantasia supposedly rely on for this to be a testable alternative; (2) Same format, but lack of metaaccess. In this view, people with and without aphantasia both create mental images in the same visual-perceptual format, and operate on them in similar ways. It is just that people with aphantasia lack meta-access to these perceptual images (Michel et al., 2024; Nanay, Figure 1 Schematic of proposed computational model of mental rotation in people with and without aphantasia. Computational Model

Theoretical Framework

Following Shepard and Cooper, 1982/1986, we focus on three stages of a mental rotation task: stimulus identification, mental rotation, and comparison. Continuing the broken-rendering account (Balaban & Ullman, 2025), we assume the first two stages are the same in people with and without aphantasia. For stimulus identification, this assumption is backed up by evidence that shows similar visual working memory capacity for people with and without aphantasia (Keogh et al., 2021). For the mental rotation stage, the assumption of similarity between people with and without aphantasia is a hypothesis, based on the separation between "physics" and "graphics" and the suggestion that physical scene representation is intact in aphantasia. For the stimulus comparison stage, we propose that people with and without aphantasia can both make similarity judgements on the basis of physical object representations (the same simplified physical/spatial representations that are being rotated in the second stage). However, in addition to this, people without aphantasia can "render" physical/spatial representations in 2D graphical representations, and use those as an additional source of evidence for similarity judgements.

We model the actual rotation stage of the mental rotation task by aligning the probe figure and the reference figure to the degree that best minimizes the least-square distances in the three-dimensional space. The alignment is only about the vertical axis, as that is the only axis of rotation in the stimulus set (Ganis & Kievit, 2015). We also introduce noise to account for imprecision in people's representations or mental operations. Specifically, we add a small random error φ ∼ N (0, s 2 jit ) about the same vertical axis of rotation. We considered four discrete levels of s jit , s jit ∈ {20, 25, 30, 35} •.

Turning to the comparison stage: The two sources of evidence (physical/spatial and imagistic/graphical) feed into a single drift diffusion model (Ratcliff & McKoon, 2008).

In what follows, we use the subscript S to denote the spatial/physical similarity route, and the subscript I to denote imagistic/graphical similarity route. We denote the "spatial dissimilarity" function as f S , and "graphical" dissimilarity function as f I . There are various (dis)similarity functions that can be used for either f S or f I , and our proposal is not strictly tied to one particular function. Here, specifically, we used: • f S : The input is the 3D representation of the objects shown in the stimuli (post rotation and alignment, if relevant).

The output is the spatial/physical dissimilarity between the two objects. To make this concrete, we used the root-mean-square distance between the two arrays of cube centers, but other spatial/physical similarity metrics can be considered.

• f I : The input is the 2D, pixel-based representation of the objects shown in the stimuli (post rotation, and re-rendering, if relevant). The output is the visual/graphical dissimilarity between the two objects. To make this concrete, we used the root-meansquare difference of pixel intensity between the two pixel images, but other visual similarity metrics can be considered.

To reiterate, we assume people with and without aphantasia make decisions of 'same' or 'different' in mental rotation based on the spatial route through f S , and that in addition to this people without aphantasia have an additional source of information through f I . Since the input to f S and f I rely on the same rotated object, their evidence is correlated, but it is not identical. We weight the additional information of f I by α j ≥ 0, which is specific a person's reliance on visual imagery. The aphantasia group has α j = 0.

In summary, the architecture of our model includes two routes of evidence that feed into a single decision process, modeled via drift-diffusion.

A hierarchy of populations, people, and trial-by-trial differences Our computational model describes the populations of people performing mental rotation in different trials. It has three levels: • Population.

We assume there are two sub-populations/groups, people with and without aphantasia. People in the aphantasia group have zero reliance on the visual route described above, but share all the other population-level distributions.

• Person. Each simulated participant has individual-level characteristics describing their degree of reliance and proficiency with spatial and visual comparison (Appendix A). In line with standard drift-diffusion models for decision making, participants also have individual differences when it comes to non-decision time, and overall speed.

• Trial. Each trial presents a participant with a new pair of objects in a mental rotation task. The model draws read-outs for this pair, and then runs a simulation of a driftdiffusion decision with this participant's specific characteristics.

The available data from Kay et al., 2024 include per-participant summaries, mean correct RT, and accuracy for each angle × {mirror versus same} combination. Given this, we simulate the whole experiment, and compute these same summaries for each simulated participant. We then compare the summary statistics from our model with the experimental data.

Relationship to previous models. We note that ours is certainly not the first model of mental rotation. As mentioned, our overall framework builds on and modifies the model of Shepard and Cooper, 1982/1986. That model did not provide a process-level account connecting to a decision-making process, with differences between the comparison functions available to people with and without aphantasia in a way that can account for the data we consider. A more recent process-level model of mental rotation by Provost and Heathcote, 2015 fit the rate of evidence accumulation from behavioral data, but does not specify how the evidence is collected, nor relate to aphantasia. An even more recent model, by Khazoum et al., 2026, proposed a deep-learning model of mental rotation, which showed behavioral results comparable to those of humans. However, this model too did not consider varying similarity functions that split between graphics and physics, nor try to account for mental rotation in aphantasia.

Experiment: Simulating mental rotation in aphantasia Stimuli

We use the custom rendition of three-dimensional cube objects made by Ganis and Kievit, 2015, to match the same stimulus set used in Kay et al., 2024. An example is shown in Figure 3. The objects were built in Python for our purpose of obtaining their 3D structure (cube centers), while the rendering choices, such as lighting and view point, remain as close to Ganis and Kievit, 2015 as possible. To err on the side of caution, we excluded 6 stimuli pairs from the original 48 due to them being either achiral, or not mirrors of each other. This leaves us with a set of 42 image pairs. We computed 1000 same and 1000 mirror trials for each pair of figures, and at each level of imprecision, s jit ∈ {20, 25, 30, 35} • . These samples are the model's "readout bank", and all following simulated experiments draw from them.

The two routes already differ in their discriminability before setting any parameters (Figure A1). At s jit = 25 • , physical/spatial comparison separates same and mirror pairs better than imagistic/graphical comparison for all 42 figures, with a median discriminability of 2.90 against 1.06 (Figure B1; Appendix B). Method

We simulated 1000 experiments at a representative parameter setting, chosen from a fixed rule (Appendix E). Each simulated experiment reuses the original experimental design of Kay et al., 2024, including 91 participants with aphantasia, and 107 controls. A simulated participant in either group was tested on 12 randomly drawn figures out of the set of 42. Each of the figures was presented with a same pair and a mirror pair, crossed with four angle disparity values, θ ∈ {0, 50, 100, 150} • , which totals 96 trials per participant. In each trial, a readout pair is sampled from the bank of the figure at the predetermined imprecision level, s jit = 25 •.

We analyzed our simulated experiments following the same procedure as in Kay et al., 2024. The accuracy of a participant at each angle is the proportion of their correct responses. A participant's RT is counted for analysis when the response is correct, consistent with Kay et al., 2024 , though we note Shepard and Cooper, 1982 /1986 further excluded the mirror trials. The between-subject correlations are calculated on each participant's means in the 100 • and 150 • conditions. Following Kay et al., 2024, we use Spearman's correlation to measure the correlation between RT and accuracy in the aphantasia group, and Pearson's correlation for the control group, with both tested at α = 0.0125.

We relied on the data in Kay et al., 2024 to inform our model in two ways. First, the shared rotation stage used the group-level summary statistics from Kay et al., 2024's data (including the mean RT, accuracy at each angle, and the spread of RT across participants, see Appendix A). We assume that this stage is the same for the two groups, and it can only attenuate the within-group correlations but not alter the specific difference between the two groups. Second, our choice of representative parameter settings favored parameter settings with similar size in effect to Kay et al., 2024. We emphasize however that the resulting patterns do not come simply from taking the original data. The main findings we describe below (increase of RT with angle, decrease of accuracy with angle, aphantasia group is slower but more accurate, and the opposite signs in within-group correlations) hold in 96.9% of a broad region of parameter settings (Appendix D).

The first analysis examined where the empirical data fell in the distribution of our 1000 simulated experiments (Palminteri et al., 2017). People with aphantasia are slower but more accurate. Our model showed the same pattern that both groups are slower and less accurate at larger angles, which is from the built-in shared rotation stage. Kay et al., 2024 found that people with aphantasia were slower than controls by 351 ms, and more accurate by 8.7%, across all angles. Our model shows a slowdown of 549 ms ([374, 711]) and increase in accuracy of 10.9% ([8.8%, 12.9%]). We note that this accuracy difference between the two groups is ever-so-slightly larger than observed (p = 0.036), but we do not take this to be meaningful.

We note a main difference in RT between our model and people, at small angles (0 • and 50 • ). Specifically, our model predicts an RT difference between people with and without aphantasia throughout, whereas the empirical findings show an increase in the RT difference, due to a very small (or possibly non-existent) RT difference at small angles. Such a difference is easily accounted for in principle: For small angles (or even no rotation, 0 • ), mental-rotation may not be necessary. Since both images are still available to the participants perceptually, it is possible in principle for people with aphantasia to make use of graphical similarity functions to compare the two images. Our model is more similar to a situation in which one (or both) of the original images are removed from perception. We can easily amend our model to account for this, but preferred to keep things simpler in the main analysis. We discuss this further in the Discussion.

Opposite speed-accuracy correlations between the two groups.

With betweenparticipant speed spread matching the data, the aphantasia group's RT-accuracy Spearman's correlation is -0.25, with 95% of experiments showing a correlation going from -0.43 to -0.06. This is comparable to the empirical data correlation of -0.33 (p = 0.45). The Pearson's correlation for the simulated control group is 0.20, with 95% of experiments showing a correlation that ranges from 0.00 to 0.38. This is comparable to the empirical data correlation of 0.36 (p = 0.078). Kay et al., 2024's data is around the upper limit of the model's range.

From the comparison stage alone, without the added speed spread from the shared rotation stage, the correlations are stronger than observed, -0.72 for the aphantasia group ([-0.80, -0.60]) and +0.53 for the control group ([0.37, 0.65]). The model reproduces b in aphantasia (-201 ms for the model, vs. -198 ms for people; p = 0.95), but only half of it in the control group (163 ms for the model, vs. 307 for people, p = 0.054).

Applying Kay's statistical tests on our simulated data matches most of their conclusions. We run the same statistical tests on each of our simulated experiments, to see whether the conclusions agree (Table 2). Overall, the model achieved 10 of 14 conclusions in Kay et al., 2024's results regarding group and angle in at least 96% of the experiments. The four non-matched conclusions all concern small angles, which can be explained as discussed above. For RT, all of the simulated experiments replicate the main effects of group and angle, but not their interaction, where only 5% of the simulated experiments are significant. Multiple comparisons show larger RT for aphantasia at larger angles, 100 • and 150 • , for almost all experiments. For accuracy, all of the simulated experiments replicate the main effects of groups, that of angles, their interaction, and multiple comparison except at 0 • (only 2% of the simulated experiments are non-significant at 0 • ).

For the within-group correlations, 47% of the simulated experiments with Kay et al., 2024's sample size find significance in the aphantasia group, 34% in the control group, and both 16% (Type I error rate = 0.0125). A bootstrap analysis using Kay et al., 2024's original participants with replacement finds 74% for aphantasia, 92% for controls, and 68% for both, but this could be over-optimistic. At conventional Type I error = 0.05, our simulated experiments give 70%, 55%, and 39%, against 86%, 98%, and 84% in bootstrap sampling.

Discussion

Mental rotation is just one example of a mental operation that people can carry out on objects of the mind (Shepard & Cooper, 1982 /1986). But it is a particularly important one, and one that has been studied, turned over, and examined from many angles. It is also a particularly useful case-study for comparing different frameworks that try to account for the phenomena of aphantasia. What framework can account for the fact that people with aphantasia show systematic similarities and differences in mental rotation, when compared to people who can create visual imagery? Here, we proposed a process-level account of mental rotation that can account for how it is carried out in aphantasia. Our model was based on the more general proposal of 'Broken rendering' (Balaban & Ullman, 2025), which itself is based on a basic separation in the mind between "physics" and "graphics". Our model suggests that people with and without aphantasia share many of the same operations of mental rotation: both types of people recognize and de-render the stimuli into physical/spatial representations, both rotate it using physical-based operations, and both can compare the results using physics/spatialbased similarity functions. However, in addition to this, people without aphantasia can 'render' the rotated object in a 2D graphical representation, which allows for an additional comparison route via similarity functions that expect pixel-like, 2D visual input. We further operationalized these two routes (physics and graphics) as sources of evidence for a driftdiffusion decision model.

We found that our model reproduced the main behavioral characteristics shown by people with and without aphantasia when doing a mental rotation task (Kay et al., 2024), including both the similarities and the differences. The model, like people, shows increased overall RT and lower accuracy with rotation angle, a higher RT and higher accuracy for aphantasia, and a speed-accuracy tradeoff for control, but not aphantasia. This suggests that the conclusions are based on the overall model structure, rather than a hyper-specific choice of parameters.

We take the ability of our model to capture diverse findings in mental rotation to be more generally supportive of the 'broken rendering' account. The general notion is that people with and without aphantasia can carry out spatial/physical operations supported by mental simulation, akin to how a game/physics engine can be used to run a simulated world forward without necessarily rendering it graphically (Balaban & Ullman, 2025; Ullman et al., 2017). How does this 'broken rendering' account fare against the other two main alternatives, roughly categorized as 'lack of meta-access' and 'different strategies'? The 'lack of meta-access' account can explain the similarities in performance between people with and without aphantasia, but we are hard-pressed to see how it accounts for the specific differences. The 'different strategies' account could perhaps come up with dual strategies that can account for the similarities and differences, but if so it would be less parsimonious: our view does not require an entirely different strategy, but merely one reasonable difference in an additional available representation. Further, we ourselves could not come up with a truly alternative, reasonable 'dual strategy' model that just-so-happens to fit the similarities and differences in the empirical data. This does not mean such models/strategies don't exist, but we believe the onus is now on supporters of that view to come up with them and put them to a rigorous comparison with the data and our model.

While our model is generally in line with the findings of Kay et al., 2024, we do recognize that it diverges from it for small angles, and most especially for cases where there is no angle-difference between the two objects that are shown for comparison. For example, our model predicts that people with aphantasia will be slower to respond when the difference between the two objects is 0 • , but empirically it appears that there is no RT difference in such cases. However, this difference can be easily accounted for: When the two stimuli images do not differ in angle, the information is already in 2D visual format and is available to people with and without aphantasia, without the need for a 'rendering' process. In such a case, people with aphantasia could use the same similarity functions as people without aphantasia, and we should expect little to no difference between them. This is in line with other studies, which show that for perceptual tasks people with and without aphantasia perform equally well (Bainbridge et al., 2021). Our model in its current form assumes that objects are de-rendered into a physical representation and then (for people without aphantasia) rendered back. This is more akin to an experimental set-up in which the two objects are not presented simultaneously, and one has to be kept in memory. We could simply augment our model with the additional assumption that for small angles no re-rendering is necessary for either group, but opted to keep the model specification cleaner and simpler for our present purposes.

Beyond broadly matching the available data, our model makes specific predictions that can be examined with further behavioral studies. A first such prediction continues the point above: if stimuli were presented to the participants such that the first object was removed from direct perception, followed by a delay prior to the presentation of the second object, we would expect a bigger RT difference between the aphantasia and control groups even in small angles. A different prediction relates to the fact that our model suggests that the difference in RT between people with and without aphantasia is due to the comparison stage, rather than the recognition or rotation stages. The current data clumps all of these stages together, but it is possible to design experiments that tease these stages apart. Indeed, Shepard and Cooper, 1982/1986 introduced such studies. We would propose then to run equivalent studies that separate the different stages of mental rotation, and the response of people with and without aphantasia for each stage. We expect to find that much of the RT difference between the groups is due to the comparison stage rather than the rotation stage.

While the bulk of our studies here have focused on the comparison between aphan-tasia and control groups, we note that our model is also a model of mental rotation in people without aphantasia, and is interesting to consider on its own as such. In particular, while our current model is set up to 'render' an object at the end of the rotation, it is interesting to consider whether such a description matches people's reports and behavior, and how rotation can be modified to render objects at intermediate outputs.

To explain what we mean by this, consider first that it is possible for a game/physics-engine to rotate a body stepby-step, but either with or without rendering what the body looks like visually at different sub-steps. Rendering at different sub-steps is possible, but slows the process down (we note as an aside that we did not account for the processing time of rendering in the model here, which could introduce an added constant on top of the results). Now, consider the visual imagery associated with mental rotation in people. Consider for example an image with a high level of internal visual detail, such as a zebra or a birthday cake with many colored candles. Next, try to mentally rotate that image by an unconventional angle (e.g. 60 degrees, or 150 degrees). Based on anecdotal examination, it seems even many people without aphantasia might agree with the suggestion that they do not 'see' the internal details of the object being rotated but rather have only the impression of the bulk shape, outline, or axis rotating, and it is only once the rotation is finished that they picture the individual features. Such an impression is in line with our model here. One could then go further and have people try to render the image at sub-steps of the rotation, which we then expect to lead to specific, predictable slowdowns, akin to those of a physics/game-engine tasked in a similar way. Our model has focused on mental rotation and aphantasia. It accounts for both the similarities and differences between people with and without aphantasia in this task, and suggests novel studies to test this framework further. Because the model was based on the 'broken rendering' proposal, we also see it applying more broadly. The broader suggestion is that people with and without aphantasia create and manipulate objects of the mind in a similar, hierarchical way, with much of the work being done by spatial/physical operations. It is a separate operation to then 'render' what a scene or object looks like from a particular perspective. This operation may not be available to all of us, but also may not be terribly crucial. When we wonder how a director can move around the scenery on the mind's stage without seeing it, we should remember that even for those of us who can see the set, it was we who set it up before the curtains lifted.

As stated above, the rotation stage is shared for everyone, so it does not drive the group difference. However, the rotation stage attenuates the correlation between RT and accuracy, because the speed factor ψ j injects differences in speed that are independent of performance. The observed correlation is therefore smaller than the comparison stage alone. The error factor k(θ ) only slightly changes the correlations as it weights 100 • and 150 • a little differently. This change is about 0.004 on average and at most 0.02, with no sign change in 1000 simulated experiments.

Although it has been suggested that there is trial-to-trial within-participant variability (Provost & Heathcote, 2015), this is inconsequential to our between-participant level behavioral pattern. Representative parameter setting selection This section describes how we chose the parameter setting used for the detailed comparison with the behavioral results of Kay et al. (2024).

Magnitude targets

We set four magnitude targets from Kay et al. (2024)'s effect sizes, measured over 100 • -150 • , to achieve better quantitative mapping: 1. the RT difference between the two groups, 513 ms from Kay et al. (2024); 2. the accuracy difference between the two groups, 0.107; 3-4. b, how many milliseconds a group's RT changes per one standard deviation difference in accuracy (b = r × SD(RT ) = Cov(RT,accuracy) SD( accuracy)).

This quantifies the portion of RT spread that scales with accuracy. While r is attenuated when the shared rotation stage is added on top, b stays the same: -198 ms for aphantasia and 307 ms for controls.

A setting reaches a target if it lies within two standard errors of Kay et al. (2024)'s estimate (for accuracy difference, ±0.05, about 2.5 standard errors).

Selection

The selection is done in two stages, separately at each imprecision level.

In stage 1, we tested 8192 parameter settings from a scrambled Sobol sequence (with a different random seed from the parameter space partitioning above), with 30 simulated participants per group, at each imprecision level s jit . Among the settings for which the four sign criteria hold, we keep the 50 parameter settings per imprecision level that best match the magnitude targets. For each of these 50 settings, we run 100 simulated experiments at the sample sizes of Kay et al. (2024), and one with 1500 participants per group. The 100 simulated experiments give the probability that a single experiment of the sample size shows all four sign criteria. The n = 1500 check verifies that all four sign criteria hold.

In stage 2, we choose one of the 50 with a scoring rule that weighs two items: (1) with weight 0.6, the probability that a single experiment of Kay et al. (2024)'s sample size shows both within-group correlations as significant, which we estimate for each parameter setting from its b divided by SD(RT ) in Kay et al. (2024)'s data; and (2) with weight 0.4, how close the comparison stage gets to the magnitude targets. We report the parameter setting at s jit = 25 • , while s jit = 30 • gives similar results.

Our current model uses vertical-axis perturbations. If the perturbation is about a random axis, at matched d S , the results are essentially the same. At matched difficulty (the median d ′ of the vertical axis at s jit = 25 • ), the four sign criteria hold in 97.0% of the parameter space with a random axis, against 96.9% with the vertical axis; at s jit = 30 • , the figures are 96.1% and 96.3%. The drift form

The critical assumption of the model is the mapping from evidence to drift (Equation A3), that the more cleanly a route separates trial types, the smaller the drift. To check how much the characteristics depend on this assumption, we replaced it with a family of mappings: d re f is a constant that is the same for every route, figure, and participant.

The spatial route's evidence includes the same ability gain, z S + (g j -1) sign(z S )/2, as in Equation A4. The exponent k determines how drift depends on discriminability. When k > 1, the dependence is steeper: at k = 2, drift scales with 1/ d2 r . At k = 1, the mapping is exactly v r = z r / dr . At k = 0, drift does not depend on discriminability at all. At k = -1, drift is proportional to discriminability. This is the usual case for drift-diffusion models (Ratcliff & McKoon, 2008). At k = -2, drift is proportional to the log-likelihood ratio of the route. For normally distributed readouts with equal variances, it would be d2 r z r , and the combination would be optimal if the routes were independent (Drugowitsch et al., 2014).

k changes how drift differs between routes (also figures and participants), therefore how much the two routes contribute relative to each other. A figure's image route contributes dS / dI times as much drift as its spatial route at k = 1, the same amount at k = 0, and ( dI /d S ) 2 times as much at k = -2. We fix the constant d re f = 2.90, the median discriminability of the spatial route at s jit = 25 • . This choice is arbitrary, but it should not matter, as it is the same for all forms (the constant effectively acts as the caution level in the drift-diffusion model).

With the setting described in the notes of Table F1, we found that the qualitative pattern holds as long as drift does not grow with discriminability. For k ≥ 0, all four sign criteria were satisfied in at least 90% of the parameter settings. The pattern fails at k = -1 and k = -2, satisfying only 3.9% and 0%, respectively.

The model's pattern, therefore, does not depend on the exact form of the mapping. It only requires that the less reliable route does not lose its influence. The inverse mapping is one way of doing so.

Procedure

We explore the qualitative patterns produced by the model in the whole region of plausible parameter values by partitioning parameter space (Pitt et al., 2006), to see whether the characteristics are produced by the structural commitment of the model. We sampled 1024 parameter settings from a scrambled Sobol sequence over the parameter box in Table D1. We simulate 500 participants for both groups in each setting. The parameter range is as follows: • each individual difference has a standard deviation from 0 to 5; • state ability ranges from 0.5, below neutral, to 6 ( ḡ = 1 is neutral, below 1 weakens the signal evidence); • mean reliance on the visual comparison ( ᾱ) from 0 to 3 times the spatial comparison weight; • mean visual comparison unreliability ( κ) from 1 (none) to 10 times the discriminability of the algorithmic outputs.
