Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. Abstract

Free will is one of the most enduring problems in philosophy and cognitive science, yet what, exactly, requires explanation remains unclear. The most tractable target is the experience of free will -the vivid sense of self-authorship, control, and a felt capacity to have done otherwise. As with the scientific study of mystical experiences, this phenomenology can be investigated without resolving the metaphysical claims implied by its content. Here, I argue that free will is an "easy problem" of consciousness: a phenomenon that can be measured, manipulated, predicted, and mechanistically explained. Taking causal determinism as a working postulate, I consider whether prediction and reinforcement could generate the experience of controlling one's own actions through causal misattribution and superstitious conditioning, without granting consciousness independent causal power. If the experience can be explained under deterministic constraints, the core scientific explanandum of the free will phenomenon has been addressed. Free will can then be distinguished from agency, which remains a causally consequential, graded capacity for goal-directed, adaptive, and self-regulating action across living systems, machines, and hybrids thereof.

Introduction

Free will remains one of the most enduring topics in philosophy and cognitive science. Discussions of action, choice, desire, intention, fate, karma, spontaneity, and liberation appear independently across ancient Greek, Indian, and Chinese philosophies (Bobzien, 2021; Dasti & Bryant, 2013; Marchal & Wenzel, 2017). However, modern debates have centered on the implications of determinism and indeterminism, compatibilist accounts of control and responsibility, and neuroscientific studies of volition, decision-making, and the sense of agency -or the experience of free will (Moore, 2016; Nestor, 2019). Although some authors distinguish the experience of free will from the sense of agency (Synofzik et al., 2015), here I use Haggard's (2017) definition for both: "the feeling of controlling one's own actions and, through them, events in the external world". I further include the felt availability of alternative possible actions as part of the broader experience of free will.

Much of the modern conversation surrounding this felt state has focused on whether the experience of control corresponds to actual causal power (Dominik et al., 2024; Mudrik et al., 2022) or whether decisions and actions could have been otherwise given the same preconditions (van Inwagen, 1975). And while the experience of free will is privately compelling, several authors have suggested that it is an illusion born of a perceived causal relationship between thoughts and actions where only correlations exist (Wegner & Wheatley, 1999; Wegner, 2002). Indeed, Wegner's (2002) theory of apparent mental causation proposed that actions are experienced as consciously willed when thoughts about an action precede it, are consistent with its outcome, and other plausible causes are not apparent. On this account, the resulting experience of self-authorship is merely a misperception and causally uncoupled from action. Related work by Synofzik and colleagues (2008, 2015) similarly treats the experience of free will as a reconstructive process in which motor predictions, sensory consequences, contextual information, and higher-order beliefs are integrated to infer self-authored causation.

Building on these accounts, we recently proposed a candidate learning mechanism by which the experience of free will might arise through causal misattribution (Kansala et al., 2026). Under a predictive processing framework, neural states that anticipate forthcoming actions are repeatedly followed by their realization through movement and environmental feedback. We suggested that these prediction-realization pairings could be noncontingently reinforced, such that predictive states conventionally labelled as intentions, plans, and decisions come to be superstitiously experienced as causes of later actions. If this or any related mechanism consistent with Wegner's theory of apparent mental causation can generate the experience of free will without that experience exerting causal control over action, what else is there to be explained?

Here, I propose that free will, as a phenomenon and object of investigation, constitutes an "easy problem" of consciousness (Chalmers, 1995). Drawing on analogies from the study of mystical experiences, which often bracketed metaphysical truth claims in favour of studying phenomenology (Barrett & Griffiths, 2018; James, 1902; Persinger, 1983), I argue that the primary scientific explanandum of free will is the experience itself, not the source of actions or an agent's causal relationship with its environment. Once an experience containing illusory content can be reliably predicted and controlled, its central scientific explanandum has been addressed. As I will discuss, any remaining questions about the truth value or metaphysical implications of that content fall outside the explanatory burden of the core explanandum and should be treated separately. This approach requires conceptually uncoupling free will as an experience from agency as a capacity to pursue goals, self-regulate, adapt, and act upon an environment (Mudrik et al., 2022). When the phenomenon of free will is stripped of its agential dimension, what we are left with is a conscious experience that can be explained, predicted, and controlled with existing theory under a deterministic framework.

Free will as a conscious experience

There is something that it is like to be a human agent. However, this does not imply that all agents are conscious, that consciousness causes action, or that agency requires consciousness. Indeed, the dependency may run in the opposite direction -agency may exist without consciousness, while consciousness may depend on the capacities of an embodied system to act and self-regulate (Thompson & Varela, 2001). These questions should be kept conceptually separate from the experience of free will itself.

The conscious experiences of deliberating, planning, intending, deciding, and trying feel self-authored with an internal locus of control (Haggard, 2017; Synofzik et al., 2008). And choosing among alternatives carries the impression that more than one course of action was available and that, under the same preconditions, one could have done otherwise (Synofzik et al., 2015). Feelings of regret, shame, and pride stem from these counterfactuals and their valenced outcomes (Byrne, 2016; Niedenthal et al., 1994; Tracy & Robins, 2004) . By contrast, desires, urges, reflexes, compulsions, rumination, and obsession are often experienced as less than fully self-authored, even automatic, impulsive, witnessed, or inevitable. In some psychiatric conditions, thoughts and actions may even be experienced as "intrusive", "forced", or externally controlled (Synofzik et al., 2008; Voss et al., 2010). Between these extremes lie experiences related to hesitation, habits, rehearsed skills, and flow states, whose perceived authorship varies with context. Together, these phenomena comprise a spectrum of experienced authorship and control, ranging from boundless freedom at one end to the sense of being "driven" at the other (Watts, 2011; Watts, 1977). As meditation practitioners and contemplatives have keenly observed, the experience of breathing represents one of a few examples of actions that appear to come under transient and reversible degrees of control, oscillating between voluntary and autonomic states (Brown & Gerbarg, 2009). And this sense of control can be manipulated with visual feedback from a virtual body (the "embreathment" illusion), enhancing a sense of body ownership and agency (Monti et al., 2020).

If the experience of free will, causally uncoupled from agency, is a problem for consciousness research, what kind of problem is it? Chalmers distinguished the "hard problem" of explaining why physical processes are accompanied by experience at all from the comparatively "easy problems" of explaining the cognitive and neural mechanisms underlying particular perceptual capacities and reports (Chalmers, 1995). I claim that the experience of free will belongs to the latter category. Indeed, people report a characteristic experience of self-authorship and alternative possibility, and the scientific task is to identify the mechanisms that predict, modulate, and abolish that experience. In principle, the scientific study of free will as an experience should resemble the longstanding practice of relating perceptions such as colour and pitch to regional activations of brain tissues and then asking how lesions, stimulation, pharmacology, learning, or context alter those experiences.

The experience of free will is already experimentally tractable in this way. In a classic experiment, Brasil-Neto and colleagues (1992) showed that subthreshold transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) over motor cortex biased participants toward selecting the index finger contralateral to the stimulated hemisphere in a forced-choice task. The effect was driven primarily by very rapid responses (<200 ms), yet participants remained unaware that stimulation had systematically influenced their choices. The authors concluded that movement preparation could be externally influenced without disrupting the conscious perception of volition. Treated as an experience of action, free will becomes a mechanistic problem for consciousness science rather than a metaphysical conundrum.

What about the experience of free will requires an explanation?

As an object of investigation, free will is a many-layered phenomenon with metaphysical features and normative implications. Therefore, on first inspection, its core explanandum is not obvious. Because the phenomenology of free will is enmeshed with perceived mental and bodily action, it is perhaps understandable that so much of the literature has been dominated by discussion of causality and whether actions align with a particular metaphysical framework (Delnatte et al., 2023). However, it is worth considering the possibility that other layers of the free will phenomenon are equally or more salient; focusing on the conscious experience of free will over the correlated actions of subjectagents may reveal new opportunities for progress toward a more complete understanding of the phenomenon. One useful precedent for a study of conscious experience that privileges phenomenology over the metaphysical implications of experiential content comes from the scientific study of mystical experiences.

In The Varieties of Religious Experience, William James (1902) outlined a scientific approach to the study of private mystical, spiritual, and religious experiences. Rather than focusing on the implied truth claims of individual mystical experiences, he undertook a comparative psychology approach, taxonomizing common phenomenological features across reports from individuals with different cultural backgrounds and personal histories. Of mystical experiences, he noted they were typically ineffable, noetic, transient, and passive -which is to say, out of the subject's control. Although James remained interested in their philosophical and metaphysical implications, he treated these as separable from the psychological task of describing and explaining the experiences themselves. In doing so, he helped establish scientifically tractable questions about conscious experiences that, to the subjects reporting them, were of profound and life-altering importance.

Building on his seminal line of inquiry, we now know that mystical experiences can be evoked in the laboratory with psychotropic drugs (Griffiths et al., 2006 (Griffiths et al., , 2011) ) and brain stimulation (Persinger, 2001; Persinger et al., 2010). In naturalistic settings, they are more easily generated under conditions of sensory deprivation, hypoxia, during periods of fasting, and when partaking in repetitive, ritualistic behaviour (Demmrich et al., 2023; Perry et al., 2021; Vaitl et al., 2005). Mystical experiences are also closely tied to temporal lobe activity (Persinger, 1983), correlate with lesions in these regions (Cristofori et al., 2016), and are commonly reported among individuals with temporal lobe epilepsy (Dewhurst & Beard, 1970; Kasper et al., 2010; McCrae & Elliott, 2012; Trimble & Freeman, 2006). While many questions remain unresolved, we know the proximate causes of mystical experiences and continue to treat them as important aspects of human psychology. In addition to understanding their neural correlates, we have methodshowever crude -of predicting and controlling them. This, I claim, is a success story for a philosophy and science of mind; mystical experiences, reported sincerely and taken seriously, were investigated and analyzed with rigor and explained without relying on the mystical or supernatural. Implied truth claims about the nature of the universe, inherent to the content of the experiences, did not need to be explained to understand this type of conscious experience.

In hindsight, what was there to explain about mystical experiences? It was not whether the objects of those phenomena really existed, whether a sensed presence was truly divine, or whether the universe was in fact suffused with the unity, meaning, or transcendence that subjects reported. Those questions may be philosophically interesting and valid lines of inquiry, but they concern the personal content and interpretation of mystical experiences rather than the mechanisms underlying the experiences themselves. Treating the veridical status of mystical experience as the primary explanandum conflates what an experience represents with why or how the experience occurs. The scientific explanandum -the phenomenon investigators beginning with James set out to understand -was why the mystical experience arises under some conditions and not others, how it is generated by the brain, why it varies between individuals, and how it influences subsequent cognition and behaviour. Treating mystical experiences as "easy problems" of consciousness rather than revealed truth claims about the nature of reality enabled significant scientific progress (James, 1902; Griffiths et al., 2006; Cristofori et al., 2016). I suggest that explanation of free will should be approached in a similar manner. Humans report a vivid and ordinarily compelling experience of being able to choose among alternative possible actions, of initiating actions for reasons, and having been able to do otherwise (Haggard, 2017; Synofzik et al., 2015). What about the conscious experience of free will requires an explanation? Of course, we can ask whether this experience accurately reflects the cause-effect structure of motor events, but we need not answer that question before studying how or why the experience occurs. Just as a scientific account of mystical experiences does not require first determining whether angels exist, a scientific account of the experience of free will does not require a study of causality in physical or metaphysical terms. Borrowing from James' playbook, we can instead ask how the brain generates the experience of a freedom to choose, act, and do otherwise; we can also describe the contingencies that determine how those experiences can be strengthened, weakened, or lost (James, 1902).

Superstitious conditioning and the misattribution of causality

We (Kansala et al., 2026) previously approached free will by first postulating determinism and then attempting to explain how the conscious experiences of intending, planning, choosing, and acting could be formed without the existence of a causal relationship between experience and action. We proposed that the experience of free will can be understood as a misattribution of causality shaped by superstitious conditioning -a type of associative learning that occurs when feedback reliably follows a behaviour even though the behaviour did not cause it (Ono, 1987). Citing B.F. Skinner's (1948) seminal experiments with pigeons, we explained that because temporal proximity provides a powerful basis for causal inference, non-contingent reinforcement can produce superstition consistent with the false view that one event caused another. We suggested that a similar process operates within brain circuits associated with perception-action loops. Pre-motor activity within the cerebral cortices predicts forthcoming motor events, while the subsequent realization of those predictions through movement and environmental feedback repeatedly pairs an internal anticipatory state with the action that follows. If the subject becomes aware of information predictive of an impending action and then observes that action occur, the reliable sequence of prediction-realization pairings would be experienced as conscious control. On our account of free will, which is consistent with the theory of apparent mental causation, predicting an impending action is experienced as choosing the action because of the temporal structure of the perception-action loop.

Several observations were presented as consistent with this account. Dopaminergic and fronto-striatal systems implicated in reinforcement learning and predictive processing also underlie bodily movement, motivation, addiction (i.e., dysfunctions of will, desire), and superstitious belief (Freeman et al., 2009; Rao et al., 2014). Sham neurofeedback can induce a false sense of conscious control while engaging regions of the brain canonically associated with "decision-making", including the basal ganglia, ventral striatum, supplementary motor area, and prefrontal regions (Grössinger et al., 2021). When motor outputs are reliably determined by brain stimulation in an experimental setting, subjects erroneously describe their own actions as self-authored (Brasil-Neto et al., 1992). Interestingly, disorders involving dopaminergic, basal gangliar, and fronto-striatal systems (e.g., Parkinson's Disease, Schizophrenia) are often accompanied by experiences of compulsive, forced, or externally controlled thought and action (Mameniškienė et al., 2024; Shiah et al., 2014; Voss et al., 2010). When these same dopaminergic pathways are normalized, the experience of free will is restored and superstitious beliefs are diminished. We therefore suggested that the experience of willing is neither fixed nor allor-none, but can be strengthened, weakened, or reorganized by learning, disease, pharmacology, and altered action-outcome contingencies (Kansala et al., 2026).

Our broader claim is that, under causal determinism, both agential behaviour and the experience of free will can be explained without granting consciousness any causal power. The experience of free will is real, and so is agency, but the two are conceptually distinct and can be causally disconnected. On our account, anticipated actions are experienced as consciously caused because of the temporal contiguity of prediction and realization -a view that aligns with Wegner's theory of apparent mental causation. Once prediction and action are paired, the causal misattribution reinforces a broader superstitious belief in free will that is projected onto others and incorporated into language, morality, law, and concepts such as guilt, regret, pride, and responsibility. Of course, we acknowledged that the kind of free will we describe overlaps significantly with the compatibilist view; however, we arrive at this position without assuming any causal role of experience upstream of action.

Shattering the teapot

If the experience is what there is to explain about free will under deterministic constraints, and its nature is a misattribution of causality, what can be said of actions and their causes? Human agents exist in a continuous and reciprocal cause-effect state with their environments, including other agents. And as I will explain in a later section, agency is an undeniable capacity of diverse systems, including the human organism, with real causal power that can be quantified by independent observers (Barandiaran et al., 2009; Butlin, 2024; Dung, 2025). However, because the experience of free will does not require any causal power to exist exactly as it is reported in a deterministic world, we should revisit some specific features of the misattribution with a new perspective. How do we interpret a subject's claim that they possess intentions and decisions that hold causal power? Is there, in fact, a there there? Bertrand Russell noted that the inability to disprove the existence of an undetectable teapot orbiting the Sun does not shift the burden of evidence away from the person asserting its existence (Russell, 1952 (Russell, /1997)). The same principle applies to the contents of conscious experience. A mystical experience of a divine presence is evidence that the experience of a divine presence occurred; it is not, by itself, evidence that a divine being was present, which would require evidence beyond the private contents of conscious experience. This is consistent with Daniel Dennett's heterophenomenology, which is a third-person approach to the study of consciousness that treats first-person reports as valid qualitative data while remaining neutral about the reality of their reported contents (Dennett, 2003). Likewise, the experience that one could have chosen otherwise establishes the existence of that experience, but not the existence of an actual freedom to choose otherwise under identical conditions -which, incidentally, is unfalsifiable, since time cannot be reversed and preconditions cannot be reset. The Russellian teapots that subjects conjure from their experiences must be shattered; we must stop treating the phenomenological content of the free will experience as evidence of the causal powers it appears to imply. Once the experience itself can be explained, the burden of proof about the existence of conscious control shifts to the subject.

Hoffman has presented evidence to suggest that conscious experience evolved to maximize fitness outcomes, not to mirror veridical reality (Hoffman et al., 2015; Prakash et al., 2021). On our account of free will, the experiences of choosing from possible alternatives or being able to do otherwise are illusory in the same way that vision, hearing, and other perceptions likely fail to capture an underlying reality. Instead, all perceptions comprise a kind of user interface -a translational space between perception and an external world. Here, I claim that human agents have causal impact in the world, including the ability to change their own experience upon receiving feedback, but their sense of agential freedom that suggests the existence of unrealized but possible alternatives is impotent.

Championing a philosophy and science of agency

Stripping free will down to its experiential dimension and uncoupling it from agential behaviour does not diminish the scientific or philosophical importance of agency. Quite the opposite -agency presents a richer set of tractable questions about what systems actually do, including how they pursue goals, self-regulate, respond to feedback, and alter their environments. Contemporary accounts increasingly treat agency as graded and multidimensional rather than binary, with capacities such as goal-directedness, autonomy, and efficacy varying independently across systems (Barandiaran et al., 2009; Dung, 2025). A useful philosophy of action can still engage with the felt sense of agency; however, it should also move away from treating those experiential correlates as causes. As embodied artificial and biological intelligences challenge conventional accounts of agency, there is an urgent need to confront fundamental questions related to its minimal conditions and boundaries (Butlin, 2024; Dung, 2025; Rouleau et al., 2021; Rouleau & Levin, 2024). Championing a philosophy and science of agency to ask how systems acquire goals, adapt to dynamic environments, and self-regulate under myriad constraints does not require the assumption that an agent experiences free will or any conscious state (Mudrik et al., 2022; Butlin, 2024). However, as we have previously argued, agency may serve a deeper functional prerequisite for evolved consciousness, imbuing systems with the goals and interests that only embodied and embedded systems display (Roskies & Rouleau, 2026).

Conclusion

What, then, is there to explain about free will? As a conscious experience, causally downstream from agency, investigators should seek to explain its neural mechanisms and the many factors that predict, shape, and abolish the felt sense of authorship, control, and alternative possibility. The experience of free will can be studied as an "easy problem" of consciousness, through mechanisms that can be identified and manipulated without ever settling the metaphysics of causation. Agency, on the other hand, concerns what systems do -how they pursue goals, self-regulate, adapt, and cause their environments to change. Separating these questions preserves agency as a highly tractable, systemagnostic property of ethical consequence without treating correlated experiential content as evidence of causal power.
