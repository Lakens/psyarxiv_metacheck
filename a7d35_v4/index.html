Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. Full text

Abstract

Depression is a debilitating condition that is highly prevalent among adolescents. However, depression is often understood through a nomothetic lens, meaning that there is a tendency to measure general patterns of symptoms. This risks overgeneralisation that largely ignores the individual, lived experiences of depressed youth. We develop and demonstrate a mixedmethods assessment approach in which participants can identify and elaborate on what lived experiences are important to them in therapy. Participants were 21 university students (M = 20.5, SD = 1.3) who had attended some form of psychotherapy in the past 2 years.

Participants first performed a statement-sorting task, in which they rate the subjective importance of youth-identified experiences of depression and psychotherapy. This task is followed by an interview on their reasons for prioritising statements. Descriptives of the statement ratings indicated that there was a high variability in how the statements were rated.

Thematic analysis was conducted to examine this heterogeneity, and to explain why participants had different priorities. The generated themes concerned: 1) different rationales for prioritisation of problems when navigating core problems versus "minor" problems, 2) differences in the effect of trust in the therapist on the course of therapy, 3) the degree to which one's mental health concerns are part of one's identity, and 4) if and how individuals chose to accept social support. These findings underscore the variety in psychotherapy experiences, and suggest the need to prioritise different things in psychotherapy assessment through a tailored approach.

Introduction

Adolescence is recognised as a transitional period involving a broad range of physical, cognitive, emotional, and social developmental phases. These developmental changes coincide with an increase in psychopathology (Rapee et al., 2019), with one of the most common examples being the increased prevalence of depressive complaints (Thapar et al., 2022; World Health Organisation, 2018).

Depression During Adolescence and its Current Treatment

Major Depressive Disorder is a debilitating condition associated with anhedonia, sleeplessness, significant change in appetite or weight, feelings of worthlessness, and suicidal ideation amongst other characteristics (American Psychiatric Association, 2013). Clinical diagnosis of Major Depressive Disorder is informed by criteria such as the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM-5; American Psychiatric Association, 2013).

According to the DSM-5 diagnostic criteria, a patient must show at least five symptoms of depression (e.g., depressed mood most of the day, insomnia or hypersomnia nearly every day, decreased concentration nearly every day) all within a two week period. These diagnostic criteria classify disorders with a nomothetic understanding of symptoms (Köhne, 2020), which gives little room to acknowledge the subjective experiences in disorders (Midgley et al., 2015).

Indeed, we see that many depressive symptoms in adolescents are left undetected and undiagnosed, in spite of their clinical impact (Lachal et al., 2012; Shorey et al., 2022). Failing to acknowledge their critical perspectives has potential negative implications for adolescents and their treatment for depression (Thapar et al., 2022), and suggest that current psychotherapies are not optimally effective for adolescents. In fact, analyses of temporal trends in youth psychotherapy suggest a significant decrease in efficacy of treatment for depression (Weisz et al., 2019), with relapse rates ranging from 47% to 67% within a period of 6 to 24 months for youths with Major Depressive Disorder (Bockting et al., 2015).

In the current study, we therefore argue for a youth-tailored assessment method which systematically addresses the subjective, adolescent-specific experiences of depression. Such an assessment method is necessary to turn the focus of assessment to what adolescents themselves indicate they need, potentially increasing the efficacy of treatment through more tailored care.

What are Adolescent-Specific Experiences of Depression?

Indeed, there are several adolescent-specific factors that have been identified, which nomothetic frameworks of depressive symptoms do not account for. Firstly, adolescents' depressive symptoms can be misattributed to regular stress or normative adolescent experiences, and are therefore not recognised as signs of depression (World Health Organisation, 2018). Secondly, from previous studies it seems that the DSM-5 criteria for depression are not always in line with the characteristics reported by adolescents. For example, experiences of social isolation and anger (Viduani et al., 2024), and irritability (Stringaris et al., 2013) were frequently reported among depressed adolescents, despite not being part of the DSM-5 criteria. The other way around, there are criteria in the DSM-5 that seem less relevant for adolescents. Adolescents for example do not report "a change in appetite or weight" to be a core experience (Lachal et al., 2012), and the criteria of loss of interest/pleasure and concentration problems are also regarded as more relevant to adults, rather than adolescents (Rice et al., 2019). Thirdly, there may be aetiological differences in depression across age groups (Rice et al., 2019) that are unaccounted for in nomothetic classification systems. Current theories in the aetiology of depression are primarily based on models of adult psychopathology, and tend to neglect that adolescence as developmental stage can influence the course of depression differently than during adulthood (Bernaras et al., 2019). This has led to the criticism that current theories and diagnostic criteria have long treated adolescents as "miniature adults" with depression (Midgley et al., 2015), thereby potentially ignoring the influence of adolescent-specific experiences in depression.

An Idiographic Approach to Assessing Depression in Adolescence

To examine the adolescent-specific experiences of depressive complaints, a turn towards an idiographic approach has been suggested (see De Smet et al. 2024; Viduani et al. 2024). In contrast to a nomothetic approach that aims at generalizability, idiographic research aims to understand the particular, which is subject-and context-specific (Syed, 2024).

Qualitative research methods are ideal for such an approach, considering that it allows us to examine experiences of depression and psychotherapy from the perspectives of adolescents (Syed, 2024; De Smet et al. 2024). Such an approach can help provide conceptual clarity on what depression means for adolescents, and how this should be meaningfully assessed (Bringmann et al., 2022; Köhne, 2020), thereby potentially informing current developments in personalised treatment (De Smet et al., 2024).

The Current Study

In this study we use a sorting task to identify relevant domains of depressive complaints and psychotherapeutic intervention from the perspectives of adolescents themselves. Ultimately, this could be employed for a more tailored approach to the counselling of depressive complaints in youths. We ask the main research question: "What are important experienced complaints and experiences of change in psychotherapy for adolescents with depression?", divided into two sub-questions: (1) What patterns in youth-identified complaints and experienced changes in psychotherapy can we identify?

(2) How and why do youth prioritise certain complaints/needs in psychotherapy?

We depart from existing qualitative research on adolescents' lived experiences with depression and psychotherapy to create a statement-sorting task. We then conduct post-task interviews with the participants to capture the reasoning behind the statement's ranking, as well as the subjective context of each individual's therapeutic trajectory. With this idiographic method, we conceptualise complaints and change within psychotherapy from the individual's experiences, potentially revealing important information about individual needs in counselling and psychotherapy.

Materials and Methods

Design

A mixed-methods sequential design was used through several phases, as depicted in Figure 1. In a first phase we synthesised qualitative literature on experiences of depression and psychotherapy of adolescents to create the statements for the sorting task. In a second phase, the participants performed the quantitative sorting task. In a next phase, in-depth interviews were conducted based on the sorting task results. During the analysis, the descriptive results were used to inform what patterns/domains the in-depth qualitative analysis could focus on.

Procedure

Statement selection

For this study's materials, existing statements were collected to create a set of statements on adolescents' lived experiences with depression and psychotherapy. First we collected statements from an existing pilot study on adolescents in therapy for mood problems ([link to OSF page removed for peer review]). Second, statements were collected from existing literature, which included studies implementing statement-sorting tasks and systematic reviews of qualitative studies that examined depression in adolescence (Calderon et al., 2017; Krause et al., 2021; Twivy et al., 2023; Viduani et al., 2024). This resulted in 90 initial statements, which were then analysed for their similarity and clarity by the first author (see OSF page for details; [link removed for peer review]). The similar statements were either combined or the copies were removed. The merging and deletion of statements was discussed with the third author for consensus. Afterwards, the statements were assessed by an expert on psychopathology in adolescents, who reviewed the statement content and their suitability and clarity for young participants. The expert suggested two statements to be added, resulting in a final set of 47 statements. Post-sorting task interview

The in-depth interview was conducted directly after the statement-sorting task such that the interviews could focus on what the participants found most relevant and meaningful to discuss. The interviews were intended to gain insight into the interpretation of the statements and the rationale of the card placement on the sorting grid (Gallagher & Porock, 2010). This semistructured interview was partially based on existing questions that uncover the underlying beliefs that lead to a particular card sort (Gallagher & Porock, 2010). In addition, participants were asked if they feel as if there were statements relevant to their experiences that were missing. This final interview guide and sorting cards are available on Open Science Framework ([link removed for peer review]).

Pilot testing

A pilot study was conducted with the first participants that signed up for the study (n = 8). This pilot phase was used to examine the clarity of the initial statements and the interview guide/task instructions. Based on the results, all of the statements were clearly understood and were left unchanged. Slight changes were however made to the interview guide as result of the pilot phase. Namely, some of the follow-up questions that emerged during the pilot phase were included in the general interview guide.

Analytic plan

Thematic analysis

Inductive thematic analysis was conducted in Atlas.ti 25.0.1 (Atlas.ti Scientific Software Development GmbH, 2024). The analysis was conducted with a contextualistic perspective. Through this theoretical orientation, it is acknowledged that there is a certain objective reality of adolescent depression, and that individual representations (i.e., lived experiences) are influenced by the broader social context (Braun & Clarke, 2006).

Thematic analysis was performed with the six-phase recommendations from Braun and Clarke (2006), an overview of our process can be found in Figure 1. The analysis was conducted by the first author, who served as the main coder, and repeatedly discussed with the third author in all stages of the analyses until consensus was reached on the final themes.

All interview transcripts were checked and read through by the first author, and preliminary insights were noted to gain familiarity with the data (phase 1). This was followed by multiple iterations of coding the interviews for parts relevant to the research question (phase 2).

In the first iteration of coding, each interview in the dataset was coded for its semantic content. In the second iteration of coding, more in-depth coding was performed with guidance from the quantitative findings (e.g., specific answer patterns or domains). Codes that seemed less relevant to the research question were organised into a "rest" category. In the third iteration, the codes were collated into higher-order codes. These higher-order codes were organised into preliminary themes and reviewed through constant cross-examination with other codes (phase 3 & 4). The preliminary themes were then refined and named (phase 5). Before finalising and reporting the themes (phase 6), the codes in the "rest" category were analysed to certify that important findings have not been overlooked.

Research team and positionality

This study's research team consisted of three members who come from different epistemological backgrounds, and are at different stages within their academic trajectory. The first author is a PhD student with a main focus on lived experiences of psychopathology in young (student) populations. She is situated in the contextualist paradigm. The first author primarily conducted the interviews, and at the time of interviewing, was 23 years old. This was close in age to the participants, which may have implications for the interaction during the interviews. Potentially, this could have fostered a more open and comfortable setting, but it could also have made the participants more uncomfortable to share certain peer-related experiences. The second author is a full professor in computational methods in psychology, working from a primarily post-positivist paradigm. The third author is an assistant professor in qualitative methods for youth psychotherapy, and has a constructivist epistemological stance. This study's analysis was performed mostly through a contextualistic lens.

Throughout the study's procedure however, we have taken a pragmatic position where we used our diversity in perspectives to triangulate and interpret the data.

Results

We first present the quantitative findings from the statement-sorting, followed by the qualitative findings from the interviews. The quantitative findings were used to answer the sub-question: "What patterns in youth-identified complaints and experienced changes can we identify?". The descriptive statistics indicated that there was no clear pattern in how participants sorted the statements. Their differences in ratings signal a sample of diverse perspectives, which were used as starting point to explore the sub-question: "How and why do youth prioritise certain complaints/needs in psychotherapy?" through inductive thematic analysis.

Descriptive statistics

An overview of each statement's mean ratings, standard deviations, and ranges is presented in Table 3, and an overview of the frequency of each statement's rankings (i.e., placement from -5 to +5) is presented in Appendix C, Table C1. On average, the experience of "knowing how to cope with or challenge negative thoughts and emotions" received the highest mean rating (M = 2.86, SD = 2 .31) suggesting that on average, this is regarded as an important experience within this sample. The statement "not recognising and relating my experiences to depression (e.g., having to be told by others that I am depressed)" on the other hand has the lowest mean rating (M = -3.10, SD = 2.26), indicating that this experience, on average, was the least important across the participants. However, the violin plot (Figure 3) shows a large spread of statement ratings across participants and the descriptive statistics show high standard deviations of ratings (see Table 3). This suggests that there was a high heterogeneity in how participants sorted the statements, and that there is no general pattern in what domains are consistently important within the psychotherapeutic content. This is also seen in the statement-by-statement descriptive statistics. The aforementioned statement "knowing how to cope with or challenge negative thoughts and emotions" (M = 2.86, SD = 2.31) for example has frequently been ranked as "+5" (n = 7), but has also been ranked "-4" by one individual (see Table C2).

Another specific example is the statement: "keeping others at a distance and not wanting to burden them" (M = 0.24, SD = 3.46), which has been ranked around the middle, and has been ranked as "-5" (n = 4) almost the same as "+5" (n = 3). Central themes

The thematic analysis resulted in four themes that capture the participants' lived experiences and how they help understand the prioritisation of certain complaints/needs in psychotherapy (see Figure 4 for an overview). Based on the heterogeneous findings in the sorting task, we aimed to construe each theme as a concept that captures both the similarities as well as the divergent perspectives of said complaints and changes. Overview of themes on experiences of complaints and change in psychotherapy for adolescents Theme 1. Different rationales for internal prioritisation of problems: Recognising and navigating core problems versus "minor" problems in therapy

The first theme captures how the participants navigated their therapeutic priorities differently, and how this difference is reflected in their sorting task responses. Most participants recognised the "+5" category as the core of therapy. The category was described by participants as "the core complaints" (Paula), "the root of all problems" (Luke), and the "essentials" of therapy (Sara). Statements that were ranked below "+5" were often recognised as more secondary, or "minor" problems. As the participants stated, these statements were more "general everyday problems" (Lisa) or "one-off issues, not something that escalates" (Michelle). From here on, participants diverged in their preferences/strategies for addressing these core issues versus minor issues in therapy. We further explore this through two subthemes.

Subtheme 1A. Addressing the "core" problems first, and "minor" problems second. Participants expressed that they regarded the core problems as a priority, which they wanted to solve first before moving on to more minor problems. Visualising this sorting strategy, participants would work their way from the outside (+5) towards the middle.

Michelle for example described that for her, the experience of loneliness (rated +4) was more of a background problem that she did not "actively deal with" before having worked on her issues with anxiety (rated +5). Kylie has also stated to prioritise the resolution of the more important issues first, as the changes in primary complaints could, by association, change some of the other complaints.

Subtheme 1B. Addressing "minor" problems first, and building up to the "core" problems. On the other hand, there were participants that preferred to address the minor problems as a "stepping stone" (Nelly) towards the bigger complaints. In terms of the sorting task, they would first address the statements ranked in the middle, before moving outwards to the core statements. Beginning with these minor issues was a way for participants to build up to their primary goals, considering that the "less important" statements do influence the more important statements (e.g., for Luke). In terms of change throughout therapy, for Valeria it was helpful to do "the little things every session" and then getting to the biggest problems.

Theme 2. The importance of trust in the therapist, and how it affects the course of therapy

This theme captures the variety of therapy experiences the participants had and the level of trust they had put in their therapist. Participants reported both positive experiences and negative experiences during their therapeutic trajectory, which they stated had impacted their trust in the therapist. Further experiences with trust in the therapist are described using three subthemes.

Subtheme 2A. The professional role of the therapist. Within the first subtheme, participants describe the effect of the therapist's professional role on their trust in the therapist and the state of the client-therapist relationship. Professionality was defined through language, with Lisa reporting that the therapist's use of excessively formal language made them feel distant. Another participant reported that the therapist's professional degree was the basis for his initial trust in therapy, though this initial trust can waver (Dennis). This wavering trust was also noticeable from Evelin's experiences. Her previous psychologist had shared confidential information with her parents, resulting in her being "closed off" in subsequent therapy sessions, and making it important for her to be able to trust her future therapists.

Subtheme 2B. Actively hiding things from the therapist versus seeing no point in discussing things. The second subtheme emphasises that participants can actively avoid mentioning certain topics in therapy, because they feel uncomfortable addressing it (e.g., Viv) or because they do not want to dedicate time to it in their sessions (Valeria). However, participants can also avoid talking about certain topics due to a sense of anticipated failure. The same participants for example have stated to avoid discussing their physical health due to a lack of motivation for the anticipated advice (Valeria), or to avoid discussing topics because previous advice had not worked and discussing it further seemed pointless (Viv).

Subtheme 2C. Therapist's working style and setting. The third subtheme describes the influence of the therapist's characteristics on the participant's trust in the therapist.

Regarding the therapist's working style, it was noted that open communication and a positive attitude was favoured (e.g., Valeria). It was not favourable when a therapist worked inflexibly with a set step-by-step plan, especially if the plan's exercises were not helping. Paula noted that because of this inflexibility, she did not feel as if the therapist was there to "help me with my complaints". Participants also spoke favourably of therapy in an online setting, as this enabled them to still attend therapy with a trusted therapist when they were abroad.

Theme 3. Recognising the boundary between 'me as myself' and 'me in therapy'

For many of the participants, the domain of "identity and existence" was not regarded as something critically important to discuss during therapy. This third theme focuses on the key reason behind this, which to our understanding is that participants draw a clear distinction between their actual self ('me as myself') and their mental health concerns ('me in therapy').

Participants recognise that mental health concerns do not have to stand in the way of personal achievements and daily life. Maya acknowledged that whilst she had been attending therapy since childhood, she never felt directionless and always had an ambition to study psychology. Similarly, Evelin recognised her own ambition to become a psychologist: "so I wouldn't let depression or like my mental problems get in the way of achieving the things that I want". Participants have also noted that mental health concerns are something momentary and do not need to influence your identity: "it's just some moments of our life we experience these things, but it doesn't mean it's a part of me. […] it's really unhealthy to become your mental health issue, like make it a part of your life" (Melanie). Other participants recognise that struggles with self-image and questioning identity were normative experiences. For example for Maya, questioning her identity had been important during high school, but not anymore for the time being. In another instance, Kelsey did acknowledge to struggle with her identity, however, she still saw little importance in discussing identity-related topics during therapy, as they are experiences that everyone struggles with: "who am I? I don't know, but who does know?".

Some participants did indicate to find the domain of identity and existence important to discuss in therapy, though in this sample they were in the minority. Charlie indicated they had always struggled with experiencing a loss in identity, and that these were mental health concerns that "actually come in the way of my life quite often". On another note, Selena stated that she would want to resolve her negative self-image in her therapeutic trajectory, though it would be difficult to change and thus also difficult to discuss in therapy.

Theme 4. Knowing where and when social support is available.

Many of the participants in this sample had rated the statements from the "social relationships" domain as low priority to work on or change through therapy. The participants indicated that they knew they had a good social support system available around them (e.g., family, friends). We understood that it was not a question of if support was available, but rather where and when it was available. We further expand on this distinction in three subthemes.

Subtheme 4A. Finding alternative sources of support. Some participants were situated in contexts that were either less supportive or lacked support. To name some experiences, Luke felt isolated from their roommates, Viv had difficulties with forming friendships outside of online contexts, and Maya felt stigmatised for their mental health concerns in their collectivistic home country. Despite these circumstances, the participants were able to fulfil their needs for social support due to a supportive romantic partner (Luke), meaningful friendships made online (Viv), or due to supportive family members (Maya).

Subtheme 4B. Not wanting to burden others.

There seemed to be a difference between knowing that social support was available versus actually asking for social support when needed. Participants overall acknowledged that they had supportive social relationships, and therefore did not prioritise the improvement of social relationships during therapy.

However, in some instances the participants did not reach out to others in fear of burdening them (e.g., Lisa), or in fear of opening up (e.g., Maisie), despite the support being available.

In another case, Luke felt that he did continuously have support, but he felt guilty when their support was not helpful.

Subtheme 4C. Brushing off social needs during therapy.

In some cases, participants indicated to consciously disregard the social aspects during therapy because they did not wish to dedicate time of their therapy to social needs. Reasons for social needs being less of an improvement point included: social needs being a "basic thing that people want"

(Gabriella) and it therefore being a lower priority; the notion that therapy should focus on the individual ("I think you're the most important person during therapy and it's not about the others"; Sara); and that in general one is not concerned with social aspects during the therapeutic trajectory (Luke).

Additional experiences important to adolescents

Towards the end of the interview, participants were asked if any relevant experiences were missing from the set of statements that have been discussed. Most participants indicated that the current statements were an accurate representation of their experiences, but almost everyone also did have additional subjective experiences they would want to add as statements. Discussion

In this study we developed a statement-sorting task and demonstrated its use in a research context. We sought to identify relevant domains of complaints and change in psychotherapy from the perspective of youth by combining the youth-driven sorting task with in-depth qualitative interviews.

Heterogeneity in youth-identified complaints and experienced changes

In response to the first sub-question on what patterns of complaints and change in psychotherapy can be identified, the sorting task demonstrated high heterogeneity in this sample's statement ratings. Some statements were ranked similarly by some participants, but for the most part, each participant had a rather distinct statement ranking with no consensus on one specific domain being the most important. As such, we did not identify patterns at group level but rather observed individualised patterns of prioritisation. This heterogeneity suggests that there was no uniform experience of depression and change in psychotherapy, instead, there was a range of unique contexts behind the prioritisations of each individual.

The lack of consensus on these priorities is in line with previous research, which indicated that young people often report a vast range of depressive symptom experiences (Bear et al., 2021) and the developmental trajectories of such symptom experiences (Chaiton et al., 2013). Other related work had implemented a similar statement-sorting design to examine treatment outcomes of adolescent depression (Krause et al., 2021). In this study, participants' responses were clustered as treatment outcome profiles, yet, their analysis indicated that not all participants belonged to an outcome profile. Similar to our verdict, clustering participants by their sorting profiles may not be "exhaustive and representative" (Krause et al., 2021, p. 314) of all viewpoints in a given sample.

Adapting to individual contexts and needs in psychotherapy

Through thematic analysis, we generated four themes that contextualise the participants' sorting behaviour and their prioritisation of domains of experienced complaints and changes in psychotherapy: (1) Different rationales for internal prioritisation of problems:

Recognising and navigating core problems versus "minor" problems in therapy; (2) the importance of trust in the therapist, and how this trust affects the course of therapy; (3) recognising the boundary between 'me as myself' and 'me in therapy'; and (4) knowing where and when social support is available.

These thematic findings indicated that whilst participants differed in their priorities, there were overarching patterns in how subjective experiences have helped individuals to sort their priorities.

Assessing which complaints should be prioritised first

The first theme indicated that participants were able to distinguish and acknowledge which complaints were priorities and which ones were relatively minor. The participants' view on the start and progression of their therapeutic trajectories diverged. While some preferred to address the major problems first, others preferred to start with smaller problems and then progress to what they perceive as being the larger problems. This may partially be due to differences in illness representation, which include perceptions of cause and feasibility of control of a condition (i.e., perceived chance of recovering or controlling an illness; Bear et al., 2021). Through these representations, individuals may also create their own perceptions of how feasible treatment and adherence is (Hagger & Orbell, 2022). These findings, in turn, suggest an agency on part of the participants in prioritising complaints, which highlights the practical utility of the sorting task as therapeutic tool in clinical settings.

Our participants indicated that such a sorting-task could be used during the intake session to point out problem areas, or throughout therapy as a way to track progress.

Assessing the context around different priorities

The second through fourth theme highlight that participants can find similar domains important, due to different subjective experiences. To illustrate, within the second theme "trust in the therapist" one individual found trust an important need because her therapist had broken the trust, whereas another individual found it important because she had experienced a positive setting with her therapist. Recognising the different reasons participants have for prioritising trust in their therapist is important towards improving the therapeutic alliance between patient and therapist. The therapist could for example shift their focus to how they are delivering the therapy (rather than the actual content) in cases of patients that start therapy with high mistrust (Li et al., 2022).

In contrast to trust in the therapist, the domain of "problems and changes in identity" was regarded as less of a priority within psychotherapy, leading to the third theme: "Recognising the boundary between 'me as myself' and 'me in therapy'".

Many participants were inclined to see their mental health problems as something separate from their self, so that their condition did not have to define their identity, life, and goals. A large part of participants were in late stages of adolescence at the time of their therapeutic trajectory. For them, identity formations arguably were processes that had already occurred before the start of therapy, and therefore, remained relatively stable during the therapeutic trajectory itself. In fact, some of the participants themselves acknowledged that identity was a more important domain for them back in high school. This could be a reason for the lack of importance assigned to identity within this study. It would also corroborate existing research stating that identity formation processes can certainly be associated with psychopathology (Klimstra & Denissen, 2017), but that they are more central to adolescent development (Erikson, 1950).

On a similar note, the fourth theme, knowing where and when social support is available, also indicated that a lack of social support was not regarded as a priority during therapy. Existing theories indicate that depressive symptoms may serve as backdrop for interpersonal problems and vice versa (Rudolph et al., 2017). Current findings however suggested that depressive complaints do not necessarily implicate strained interpersonal relationships, and likewise, having a supportive social context does not exclude individuals from experiencing depressive complaints. Interestingly, our findings do indicate that individuals struggled with accepting social support, for example in the fear of burdening others, despite social support not being a key complaint. The disparity between availability of social support and accepting the support may be an important contextual factor to consider for social adjustment (Elsina and Martinsone, 2020). For some individuals in therapy it may therefore be more appropriate to focus on accepting social support, rather than solely focusing on a lack of social support as experienced complaint.

Methodological decisions and future directions

This study details an approach to assess depressive complaints and important changes in psychotherapy, with the perspectives of adolescents as starting point. In this section we want to address two methodological decisions. The first decision is our sample selection.

Many of our participants had either already finished their therapeutic trajectory, or at the point of interviewing, had been in therapy for years. Therefore they potentially were better equipped to reflect and provide rich narratives on their treatment and recovery (Henriksen, 2014). Yet, it could have also been a limitation to rely on the participants' reflections only, as it could overlook important insights held during therapy. Future research could therefore examine adolescents' experiences of depression prospectively or during the therapeutic trajectory. Using this study's sorting task to map their experiences could deliver interesting complementary results to the current findings.

The second decision to discuss is the use of a mixed-methods approach. The mixedmethods approach allowed for a novel structured and in-depth examination of experienced complaints and changes in psychotherapy, which would have not been possible through a quantitative or qualitative approach only. We note that such an assessment can be quite resource-intensive for the researcher and/or practitioner to utilise, especially in combination with other professional activities (Gürsoy & Vatansever, 2025). Despite the associated time and labour intensity, it remains important to consider such qualitative designs as the groundwork for idiographic assessment. Qualitative components are key to uncovering new perspectives (Gürsoy & Vatansever, 2025), and they are especially complementary to more quantitative methods when examining complex mental health problems (Demkowicz et al., 2025).

This specific assessment method is an accessible task to administer to adolescents, it can for example help adolescents visualise and identify the experiences and complaints they struggle with. For researchers, such a sorting task can be used to provide a justification for what should be measured, and why (Bringmann et al., 2022). The sorting task plus interview could help towards improving design choices by justifying the domains and constructs of interest. One timely application being Experience Sampling Methodology. For practitioners, it may be fruitful to include such a sorting task throughout the therapeutic process for shared decision-making on the content of therapy sessions, or to decide which topics should be emphasised/which topics are less relevant. Such interaction may foster patient-therapist collaborations and co-creation in psychotherapy.

Conclusion

Our study provides a means to map heterogeneity in, and subjective experiences of depression and psychotherapy during adolescence. These findings highlight the importance of taking subjectivity of experiences into account during interventions, as subjective experiences can reveal a lot of important information about person-specific needs. Utilising such an assessment method can therefore inform more tailored care of depressive complaints.
