Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

If you want to extend the package to be able to download files from additional data repositories reach out to the Metacheck development team.

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. Abstract

Psychology entered the twenty-first century with an exceptionally large empirical base and a conspicuously thin theoretical infrastructure. The expansion of cognitive neuroscience, the institutionalization of evidence-based practice, and mounting evidence of a replication crisis exposed foundational assumptions to scrutiny of a kind the discipline had not faced since the paradigm debates of the mid-twentieth century. This article offers a structured critical overview of the five most consequential theoretical problems that shaped psychological science between 2000 and 2010 and traces their trajectory through 2026. The problems are ranked according to three explicit criteria-logical priority, breadth of disciplinary impact, and urgency for scientific and applied practice-and the ranking itself is defended as an argument, with alternative orderings considered and rejected. The resulting order is: (1) fragmentation of psychological theory and the absence of a unifying metatheory; (2) the crisis of construct validity and operationalism; (3) the mind-brain identity problem and the reductionism debate; (4) the cultural challenge to universalist assumptions; and (5) the methodological-epistemological tension between positivist and interpretivist traditions. Each problem is analyzed with respect to its antecedents, its specific manifestations during the 2000s, and the partial responses advanced in the subsequent decade and a half. The central claim is that the five problems form an interlocking system whose keystone is metatheoretical fragmentation; because the terms in which the remaining four problems are stated presuppose unsettled metatheoretical commitments, piecemeal reform cannot produce durable resolution. The concluding section outlines a research agenda centered on integrative theoretical reconstruction and epistemologically grounded methodological pluralism.

[div-01]

By the opening of the twenty-first century, psychology had accumulated an empirical literature of extraordinary volume. Tens of thousands of articles appeared annually, the discipline had become one of the most popular undergraduate majors in North America and Europe, and psychological constructs had migrated into medicine, law, education, management, and public policy. Yet the theoretical architecture supporting this output remained fragile. Consensus on foundational questions-the ontological status of psychological constructs, the explanatory relationship between mental and neural levels of description, and the scope conditions of psychological generalizations-remained elusive. The discipline could demonstrate thousands of statistically reliable effects while remaining unable to say, in any agreed vocabulary, what those effects were effects of.

This disjunction between empirical productivity and theoretical poverty is not a purely academic concern. Clinical diagnosis, educational policy, forensic assessment, and organizational practice all depend on constructs whose theoretical foundations frequently go unexamined. When a court admits expert testimony about "psychopathy," when a school district allocates resources on the basis of "executive function" scores, or when an insurer reimburses treatment for "major depressive disorder," each decision presupposes that the named construct refers to something determinate and that the instruments used to measure it track that referent. During the 2000s, each of these presuppositions came under sustained attack from within the discipline itself.

The practical deficit became visible across multiple domains. The DSM-IV-TR (American Psychiatric Association, 2000) was increasingly criticized for its atheoretical, symptom-based structure and its lack of correspondence with neurobiological or developmental mechanisms, a critique that culminated in Hyman's (2010) analysis of diagnostic reification and in the launch of the Research Domain Criteria initiative as an explicit alternative (Insel et al., 2010). Evidence-based practice mandates required operationally defined interventions whose mechanisms of change were poorly specified, so that treatments could be certified as "empirically supported" without anyone being able to state why they worked. Crosscultural researchers demonstrated that findings from Western, Educated, Industrialized, Rich, and Democratic (WEIRD) samples could not be automatically generalized to the human population (Henrich et al., 2010), undermining the implicit universalism on which exported psychological technologies-from intelligence tests to parenting programs-had long relied.

These convergent pressures assigned to theoretical psychology a set of problems with immediate scientific and social significance. The present article identifies the five most consequential of these problems, defends a particular ordering of them, and evaluates the adequacy of the responses that the discipline has offered in the period from 2000 through 2026. The emphasis on ranking is deliberate. Lists of disciplinary problems are common; arguments about which problems are prior to which, and why, are rare. Without such an argument, the discipline lacks any principled basis for allocating its finite theoretical attention, and reform efforts risk expending resources on symptoms while the underlying condition persists.

Relevant Research and Publications

The 2000s produced a substantial metatheoretical literature diagnosing foundational tensions, much of it building on critiques first articulated decades earlier. Koch (1959 Koch ( , 1981)), reflecting on the multivolume Psychology: A Study of a Science that he had edited, concluded that psychology had never been a coherent discipline and was unlikely to become one; Gergen (1973) had argued that social-psychological regularities are historically situated and therefore resist the kind of timeless generalization that the naturalscience model presupposes; Meehl (1978) had shown that the discipline's dominant inferential practices permit theories to survive indefinitely without ever being seriously tested. These earlier diagnoses gained renewed force in the 2000s. Smedslund (2009) argued that a large share of psychological propositions are analytically true-entailed by the meanings of the ordinary-language concepts they employ-and therefore untestable by conventional experimental means. Machery (2009) questioned whether canonical cognitive constructs such as "concept" pick out natural kinds at all. Slife and Williams (1995), whose analysis of hidden assumptions in behavioral science acquired fresh currency, demonstrated that methodological training systematically suppresses metatheoretical reflection by presenting paradigmatic commitments as technical defaults. Toomela and Valsiner (2010) characterized the preceding six decades of methodological development as a sustained departure from the questions that had originally motivated the discipline.

On the mind-brain interface, Bennett and Hacker (2003) identified a systematic mereological fallacy in neuroscientific discourse, the attribution to the brain of predicates that apply only to whole persons. Kim (2005) demonstrated that non-reductive physicalism, the default position of most cognitive scientists, faces an unresolved causal exclusion problem. Uttal (2001) and Coltheart (2006) argued, from different starting points, that neuroimaging cannot adjudicate between competing cognitive theories without independent behavioral evidence. After 2010, Poldrack (2018) synthesized the inferential limitations of neuroimaging for a general scientific audience, while Thompson (2007), Shapiro (2019), and Gallagher and Zahavi (2021) advanced embodied, enactive, and neurophenomenological alternatives to straightforward reduction.

In construct validity, Borsboom et al. (2004) demonstrated that the dominant epistemic conception of validity inherited from Cronbach and Meehl (1955) is internally incoherent and proposed a causal-realist alternative. This program was extended by Borsboom and Cramer (2013) through network theory, by Fried and Nesse (2015) through empirical demonstration of diagnostic heterogeneity, and by Flake and Fried (2020), who documented pervasive measurement slippage across the published literature. The replication crisis, anticipated by Ioannidis (2005), was confirmed at scale by the Open Science Collaboration (2015) and analyzed further by Camerer et al. (2018) and Nosek et al. (2022). Muthukrishna and Henrich (2019), Oberauer and Lewandowsky (2019), and Eronen and Bringmann (2021) converged on the claim that replication failure is at bottom a theory crisis. In cross-cultural psychology, Nisbett et al. (2001), Henrich et al. (2010), Kitayama and Uskul (2011), Rad et al. (2018), and Apicella et al. (2020) Despite this substantial critical literature, interconnected lacunae persist through 2026. No comprehensive metatheoretical framework has achieved disciplinary acceptance; pluralism remains descriptive and has not become integrative. Realist construct validity theory has generated philosophical clarity without commensurate change in measurement practice. Reductionist neuroscience and phenomenological psychology continue to operate with limited mutual engagement despite partial bridging proposals. The cultural challenge has produced extensive deconstruction without a positive alternative framework of comparable scope. The positivism-interpretivism divide has been managed through mixedmethods conventions without genuine epistemological integration at the level of theoretical inference (Tashakkori et al., 2021). Most importantly, the existing literature treats these problems largely in isolation. Each has its own specialist community, its own journals, and its own reform agenda; few authors ask how the problems relate to one another or which must be addressed first. These unresolved tensions, and the absence of a structural account of their interdependence, motivate the present synthesis.

The article pursues four objectives: (a) to identify and characterize the five most pressing theoretical problems confronting psychology during the 2000s; (b) to argue for their relative ranking on explicitly stated criteria, including consideration of the strongest alternative orderings; (c) to evaluate the adequacy of responses advanced from 2000 through 2026; and (d) to delineate a prospective research agenda directed toward integrative theoretical reconstruction. The contribution is metatheoretical: the article offers a structured critical map of a complex intellectual terrain and an argument about its topology; it does not present original empirical data.

Top-5 Ranking

Ranking Criteria and the Logic of Ordering

Any ranking of theoretical problems requires criteria that are stated in advance and applied consistently. Three criteria are employed here. The first is logical priority: Problem A is prior to Problem B if the formulation of B, or the evaluation of candidate solutions to B, presupposes a resolution of A. This criterion is the most demanding and is given the greatest weight, because a problem that is logically prior cannot be bypassed; any apparent solution to a downstream problem will inherit the unresolved status of the upstream one. The second criterion is breadth of impact: the number of subdisciplines, research programs, and applied domains in which the problem produces identifiable distortions. The third is practical urgency: the degree to which the problem generates concrete harms-misdiagnosis, misallocated resources, misleading policy advice-in the period under review.

The criteria are not always aligned, and the ranking must therefore be defended where they diverge. Three features of the argument should be made explicit at the outset. First, logical priority is treated as lexically dominant: where one problem is prior to another, it is ranked higher regardless of comparative breadth or urgency. The justification is that breadth and urgency measure the present cost of a problem, whereas logical priority measures whether the problem can be solved at all without solving another first. Second, breadth serves as the tiebreaker among problems not related by priority. Third, urgency is used only to order problems that are tied on both prior criteria, and it is the criterion most sensitive to historical contingency; an ordering that rested primarily on urgency would need to be revised with each new crisis.

Applying these criteria yields the following structure. Problem 1, metatheoretical fragmentation, is logically prior to all four others, because the standards by which construct validity is assessed, the terms in which levels of explanation are related, the scope conditions that would specify where cultural variation is expected, and the ontological commitments that distinguish positivist from interpretivist research are all metatheoretical commitments. Problem 2, construct validity, is prior to Problems 3 and 4 in a weaker sense: disputes about whether a psychological construct reduces to a neural one, or whether it generalizes across cultures, presuppose that the construct has been identified with sufficient precision for such questions to have determinate answers. Problems 3, 4, and 5 are not related to one another by priority and are ordered by breadth. The mind-brain problem affects every subdiscipline that invokes neural evidence-which, by the 2000s, was nearly all of them-and so ranks above the cultural challenge, whose immediate reach is largest in social, developmental, and personality psychology. The positivism-interpretivism tension is ranked last not because it is unimportant but because, under the criteria adopted here, it is best understood as the methodological expression of Problem 1, and ranking it independently would double-count the same underlying disagreement.

Two alternative orderings deserve brief consideration because each has defenders in the literature. The first would place the replication crisis-and therefore construct validity and measurement-at the top, on the grounds that it is the most urgent and most publicly visible problem of the period (Open Science Collaboration, 2015; Nosek et al., 2022). This ordering is rejected because it privileges urgency over priority. As Muthukrishna and Henrich (2019) and Eronen and Bringmann (2021) argue, replication failure is a symptom; treating it as the root problem leads to methodological reforms that improve the reliability of findings without clarifying what those findings are about. The second alternative would place the mindbrain problem first, on the grounds that the rise of neuroscience was the dominant institutional fact of the decade and that the fate of psychology as an autonomous discipline depends on how the levels question is resolved. This ordering is rejected because the levels question cannot be posed coherently without a prior account of what psychological constructs are; the mind-brain problem is downstream of both metatheory and construct ontology. The ordering defended here is therefore not the only possible one, but it is the one that follows from criteria that give priority to what must be settled first.

Rank 1: Fragmentation of Psychological Theory and Absence of a Unifying Metatheory

Ranked first as the problem foundationally prior to all others, this is what Koch (1959 Koch ( , 1981) ) termed constitutive fragmentation and Staats (1999) called "disunity." The diagnosis is not that psychology contains many theories; every mature science does. It is that psychology possesses no shared framework for adjudicating among them, no agreed account of what would count as one theory superseding another, and no common vocabulary in which theories from different subdisciplines could be compared. By the 2000s, the discipline comprised dozens of semi-autonomous subdisciplines-cognitive, social, developmental, clinical, personality, biological, cultural, industrial-organizational, and others-each with its own theoretical vocabulary, ontological commitments, methodological conventions, flagship journals, and training pipelines. Communication across these boundaries occurred, but adjudication did not. Sternberg and Grigorenko (2001) illustrated the condition by documenting irreconcilable theoretical accounts of a single construct, intelligence, across subdisciplinary boundaries: psychometric, cognitive, developmental, biological, and cultural researchers each operated with a different conception of what intelligence is, used different methods to study it, and published in venues the others rarely read. Their proposed remedy-a "unified psychology" organized around phenomena instead of methods or subdisciplines-was widely cited and rarely implemented. Two more ambitious proposals met the same fate. Henriques's (2003) Tree of Knowledge system attempted to locate psychology within a general account of emergent levels of complexity (Matter, Life, Mind, Culture), defining the discipline's object as the behavior of animals with nervous systems and distinguishing it from the symbolic-cultural level studied by the human sciences. Buss (2004) argued that evolutionary theory already provides the metatheory psychology lacks, because every psychological mechanism is a product of selection and can be analyzed in terms of the adaptive problem it solved. Neither proposal achieved broad acceptance: the first was judged too abstract to guide research, the second too narrow to accommodate the cultural and developmental phenomena it was meant to subsume.

The failure of these proposals is itself instructive. Each was rejected on grounds that were themselves metatheoretical-too abstract, too narrow, too reductive-yet the discipline had no agreed standards for evaluating such objections. A proposal could be dismissed by one community for reasons that another community regarded as irrelevant. This is precisely the condition that Koch had described: not disagreement within a shared framework, but the absence of any framework within which disagreement could be productive.

After 2010, the problem was reframed in light of the replication crisis. Muthukrishna and Henrich (2019)argued that the proximate causes of replication failure-low power, flexible analysis, publication bias-are themselves consequences of the absence of an overarching theoretical framework: without such a framework, researchers have no principled way to decide which hypotheses are worth testing or which failures to replicate are theoretically informative. Oberauer and Lewandowsky (2019) made a complementary argument, showing that the discipline's preferred form of hypothesis testing provides only weak evidence for or against theories that are loosely specified. Eronen and Bringmann (2021) went further, contending that construct ontology-the question of what psychological constructs are and how they relate-must be addressed before methodological reform can achieve lasting effect, and that this question is unanswerable without metatheoretical commitments the discipline has not made. Borsboom et al. (2021) responded with a practical "theory construction methodology," an attempt to teach formal theory building as a skill, which represents the most constructive recent development but which presupposes, without settling, the question of which phenomena a theory should be built to explain.

The case for ranking this problem first rests on logical priority, and the argument can be stated compactly. To assess whether a measure is valid, one needs a criterion of validity; the candidate criteria (correlational, causal-realist, pragmatic) are metatheoretical options. To decide whether a psychological explanation reduces to a neural one, one needs an account of inter-level explanation; the candidate accounts (identity, supervenience, mechanism, emergence) are metatheoretical options. To determine whether a finding should generalize across cultures, one needs scope conditions derived from a theory of what the construct is; such theories are metatheoretical commitments. To decide whether a phenomenon requires nomothetic explanation or idiographic interpretation, one needs an ontology of psychological kinds; that ontology is a metatheoretical commitment. In each case the downstream question cannot be answered, or even clearly posed, without an upstream decision that remains open. No integrative metatheory commanding consensus has emerged by 2026, and the four problems that follow remain, in consequence, structurally unsolvable in their present form.

Rank 2: The Crisis of Construct Validity and Operationalism

Operationalism-the practice of equating a construct with the operations used to measure it-entered psychology from early twentieth-century physics and was formally superseded by the construct validity framework of Cronbach and Meehl (1955). In practice, it never left. Through the 2000s, the typical empirical article defined its constructs by naming the instruments used to assess them, treated reliability coefficients as evidence of validity, and assumed that a construct existed because a scale bearing its name produced interpretable variance. Borsboom et al. (2004) subjected this state of affairs to a critique that has not been answered. They argued that the dominant epistemic view of validity, which locates validity in the pattern of correlations a measure exhibits, is internally incoherent: it makes validity a property of the inferences drawn from scores instead of a property of the measurement relation itself, and it therefore cannot distinguish a measure that tracks its intended attribute from one that merely correlates with other measures of unclear status. Their causal-realist alternative holds that a measure is valid if and only if the target attribute exists and causally produces variation in the observed scores. On this view, validity is an ontological question before it is a statistical one.

The consequences of adopting the realist criterion are severe. Machery (2009) applied a parallel argument to the cognitive vocabulary, concluding that the term "concept" as used in cognitive psychology does not pick out a natural kind and that the field should abandon it as a theoretical term. Hyman (2010) showed that DSM categories, whatever their reliability, lack validity in precisely the realist sense: there is no evidence that "major depressive disorder" names a single causal attribute that produces the symptom profile the manual lists, and considerable evidence that it does not. Kendler (2005) argued that psychiatry required an explicitly pluralistic philosophical structure to accommodate the multiple, partially independent causal pathways that converge on any given diagnostic category. In each case, constructs that had organized decades of research turned out, under realist scrutiny, to be of uncertain reference.

Post-2010 work extended the critique and began to offer alternatives. Borsboom and Cramer (2013) proposed network models as an alternative construct ontology, on which a disorder is not a latent common cause of its symptoms but a self-sustaining pattern of causal relations among them. Fried and Nesse (2015) demonstrated empirically that patients meeting criteria for major depression in a large clinical trial exhibited over a thousand distinct symptom profiles, with the most common profile accounting for a tiny fraction of cases. Flake and Fried (2020) documented the prevalence of questionable measurement practicesunvalidated ad hoc scales, undisclosed modifications to established instruments, and reliance on singleitem measures-across the published literature, and Fried (2020) argued that neither factor-analytic nor network approaches can resolve construct ontology without the theory building that both have neglected.

The argument for ranking this problem second has two parts. It is ranked below Problem 1 because its resolution presupposes metatheoretical commitments that remain unsettled. The realist criterion of validity is not a neutral technical proposal; it commits the discipline to an ontology of psychological attributes as real causal entities, a commitment that interpretivist, constructionist, and pragmatist traditions reject. Whether to adopt it is therefore a question that belongs to Problem 1. At the same time, Problem 2 is ranked above Problems 3 through 5 because it is prior to them in the weaker sense already noted: questions about reduction and generalization presuppose that the constructs in question have determinate reference. One cannot ask whether "working memory" reduces to prefrontal activity, or whether it generalizes to non-literate populations, until one has some account of what working memory is. The construct validity crisis is also the problem that most directly produced the replication crisis, the most visible disciplinary event of the period, which gives it the highest practical urgency of any problem on the list. Were urgency the dominant criterion, it would rank first; under the criteria adopted here, it ranks second, and the difference between those two positions is the difference between treating symptoms and treating causes.

Rank 3: The Mind-Brain Identity Problem and the Reductionism Debate

The decade of the 2000s was, institutionally, the decade of the brain. Functional magnetic resonance imaging became widely available, funding agencies prioritized neuroscience, and psychology departments renamed themselves, reorganized their curricula, and recruited faculty on the strength of imaging expertise. The accompanying rhetoric frequently implied that psychological explanation was provisional and that neural explanation was its destination. This implication was rarely argued for, and when subjected to philosophical scrutiny it proved difficult to defend. Bennett and Hacker (2003) argued that cognitive neuroscience systematically commits what they termed the mereological fallacy: it ascribes to the brain-a part of a person-psychological predicates such as "believes," "decides," "represents," and "interprets" that are intelligibly ascribed only to the whole person. The brain does not perceive; the person perceives, using the brain. On their account, much neuroscientific theorizing is not false but conceptually confused, and its apparent explanatory successes rest on an equivocation between the literal and metaphorical uses of psychological vocabulary. Kim (2005), approaching from analytic metaphysics, demonstrated that non-reductive physicalism-the position most psychologists implicitly hold, on which mental properties are distinct from but dependent upon physical properties-faces a causal exclusion problem: if every physical event has a sufficient physical cause, mental properties have no causal work to do, and non-reductive physicalism collapses either into reductionism or into epiphenomenalism. Neither horn is comfortable for a discipline whose constructs are supposed to explain behavior.

Within psychology itself, Uttal (2001) and Coltheart (2006) established epistemological limits on what neuroscientific data can contribute to psychological theory. Uttal argued that localization of cognitive functions to brain regions presupposes a prior decomposition of cognition into functions, and that this decomposition is supplied by psychological theory, not discovered in the imaging data. Coltheart, in a widely discussed challenge, invited proponents of imaging to identify a single case in which neuroimaging had adjudicated between competing cognitive theories, and argued that the proffered cases all relied on behavioral evidence to do the adjudicating. The point was not that imaging is useless but that its evidential bearing on psychological theory is mediated by theoretical assumptions that imaging cannot itself supply.

Post-2010, Poldrack (2018) systematized the inferential limitations of neuroimaging, including the problem of reverse inference-reasoning from activation in a region to the engagement of a psychological process-which is valid only to the extent that the region is selectively engaged by that process, a condition rarely met. Constructive alternatives to reductionism also matured. Thompson (2007) developed an enactive account on which mind is continuous with life and cognition is a property of embodied, environmentally embedded organisms; Shapiro (2019) surveyed the embodied cognition literature and distinguished its defensible from its overstated claims; Gallagher and Zahavi (2021) advanced neurophenomenology, following Varela (1996), as a methodology in which first-person phenomenological reports and thirdperson neural data mutually constrain one another without either being reduced to the other.

Despite these contributions, naive reductionism retains institutional dominance. The argument for the third-rank position runs as follows. The problem is ranked below Problems 1 and 2 because the question of how psychological and neural descriptions relate cannot be answered until one has an account of what psychological descriptions describe; the levels question is downstream of both metatheory and construct ontology. It is ranked above Problem 4 on the breadth criterion: by the 2000s, neural evidence was invoked in nearly every subdiscipline, from developmental to social to clinical psychology, whereas the cultural challenge, however profound, bore most directly on a subset of fields. It is ranked above Problem 5 because the reductionism debate concerns the discipline's object, whereas the positivism-interpretivism tension concerns its method, and object is prior to method. A discipline that cannot say whether its constructs are autonomous or merely placeholders for future neuroscience cannot say whether it should exist.

Rank 4: The Cultural and Cross-Cultural Challenge to Universalist Assumptions

Scientific psychology inherited from its nineteenth-century founders the assumption that its laws, like those of physiology, hold for the species. Cultural variation was acknowledged but treated as surface noise over a universal substrate, and the research practices of the discipline-convenience sampling from undergraduate subject pools, publication in a small number of Anglophone journals, theory construction by researchers trained in a handful of countries-reflected that assumption. During the 2000s it became empirically untenable. Nisbett et al. (2001) documented systematic differences between East Asian and Western participants in attention, categorization, causal attribution, and tolerance of contradiction, and argued that these differences reflect culturally organized cognitive habits-holistic versus analytic modes of thought-and not differences in competence. The implication was not that cognition varies at the margins but that processes previously treated as basic, including the allocation of attention to objects versus contexts, are themselves shaped by cultural practice. Markus and Kitayama's (1991) distinction between independent and interdependent construals of the self, elaborated extensively through the 2000s, showed that the individuated self presupposed by mainstream theories of motivation, emotion, and cognition is a cultural product, so that theories built on it may describe a culturally specific configuration while claiming specieswide scope. Henrich et al. (2010) synthesized the comparative database and reached a conclusion that reframed the problem. Standard behavioral-science samples-drawn from WEIRD populations and, within those, disproportionately from American undergraduates-are not merely unrepresentative; they are outliers on virtually every dimension for which comparative data exist, including visual perception, fairness norms, spatial reasoning, moral judgment, and self-concept. The discipline had been generalizing to the species from the population least typical of it. Subsequent work by Kitayama and Uskul (2011), linking cultural variation to neural and physiological measures; by Rad et al. (2018), showing that sampling practices had barely changed in the decade following the WEIRD critique; and by Apicella et al. (2020), reviewing a decade of comparative research, confirmed and extended these findings across cognitive, motivational, and economic domains.

What remains absent by 2026 is a positive theoretical framework specifying when and why cultural modulation occurs. The critique has been overwhelmingly successful as deconstruction: no serious researcher now claims that findings from undergraduate samples generalize without qualification. But the discipline lacks a theory of scope conditions-an account of which psychological processes are expected to be invariant, which are expected to vary, and what features of cultural environments produce the variation. Without such a theory, the response to the cultural challenge has consisted largely of replicating Western findings in additional populations and reporting whether they hold, an enterprise that accumulates data without producing explanation.

The fourth-rank position is defended as follows. The problem is ranked below Problems 1 and 2 because scope conditions are derived from theories of what a construct is, and such theories are what Problems 1 and 2 concern; the cultural challenge inherits their unresolved status. It is ranked below Problem 3 on the breadth criterion, though the margin is narrow and the ordering is the most contestable in the list. One could argue that the cultural challenge is broader than the reductionism debate, since it bears on every generalization the discipline makes, whereas the levels question bears only on generalizations that invoke neural evidence. The response is that the reductionism debate concerns whether psychological constructs are the right kind of thing to figure in explanations at all, whereas the cultural challenge concerns the range over which given constructs apply; the former is a question about the discipline's existence, the latter about its reach. The cultural challenge is ranked above Problem 5 because it identifies a substantive empirical failure-a pattern of unwarranted generalization-whereas Problem 5 identifies a disagreement about method whose empirical consequences are indirect.

Rank 5: The Methodological-Epistemological Tension Between Positivist and Interpretivist Traditions

The "paradigm wars" of the 1980s and 1990s, in which quantitative and qualitative researchers contested the legitimacy of one another's methods, did not end in the 2000s; they were institutionally formalized. Distinct journals, training programs, professional societies, and funding streams aligned with one epistemological tradition or the other, so that the two communities could coexist indefinitely without the question dividing them ever being addressed. Danziger (1997) had established that the dominance of experimental method in psychology was a historically contingent outcome of institutional competition and not an epistemologically necessary feature of the discipline; the categories through which psychology describes the mind were themselves products of particular investigative practices. Benton and Craib (2001) taxonomized the epistemological commitments underlying the divide-realism, empiricism, interpretivism, critical theory-and showed that the methodological dispute was a surface expression of disagreement about the nature of social-scientific knowledge.

The principal institutional response was the mixed-methods movement. Creswell and Plano Clark (2007) codified designs in which quantitative and qualitative data are collected and analyzed in sequence or in parallel, and the approach was widely adopted in applied fields. Its limitation is that it produced pragmatic combinations of data types without genuine epistemological integration: a study could report regression coefficients and interview themes side by side without any account of how the two kinds of evidence bear on a single theoretical claim. Post-2010, the open science movement strengthened the positivist pole by institutionalizing preregistration, replication, and transparency norms that presuppose a hypothetico-deductive model of inquiry (Nosek et al., 2022), while Tashakkori et al. (2021) offered the most theoretically serious attempt to date at integration at the level of inference, proposing that quantitative and qualitative strands be united not by data combination but by shared inferential goals.

The fundamental question-whether psychological phenomena require nomothetic explanation, idiographic interpretation, or both under specifiable conditions-remains without consensus resolution. The fifth-rank position is defended on the grounds given in the discussion of criteria: under the present analysis, the positivism-interpretivism tension is the methodological expression of the ontological disagreements that constitute Problem 1. To ask whether a phenomenon requires explanation or interpretation is to ask what kind of thing it is, and that question is metatheoretical. Ranking the tension independently and higher would therefore double-count the same underlying disagreement. This placement should not be read as a judgment that the problem is minor. It is the problem that most directly shapes graduate training, and training is the mechanism by which all the other problems are reproduced across generations. But its resolution will follow from, and cannot precede, the resolution of the ontological questions on which it depends.

Synthesis: An Interlocking System of Theoretical Tensions

The five problems constitute an interlocking system, not an independent list, and the dependencies among them can be stated precisely. Metatheoretical fragmentation (P1) precludes principled resolution of construct validity (P2) because competing metatheories imply incompatible validity criteria: a realist metatheory demands that constructs name causal attributes, a pragmatist one asks only that they support useful prediction, an interpretivist one denies that the question of validity is well posed-a connection made explicit by Eronen and Bringmann (2021). The mind-brain problem (P3) is partly constituted by the same metatheoretical deficit: without agreed standards for cross-level explanation, neural and psychological descriptions cannot be related systematically, and each community is free to treat the other's vocabulary as provisional. The cultural challenge (P4) undermines constructs whose validity (P2) was established on nonrepresentative samples, and it does so with particular force because the absence of metatheoretical scope conditions (P1) leaves the discipline unable to say in advance which findings should have been expected to travel. The positivism-interpretivism tension (P5) enacts at the methodological level the ontological disagreements that drive fragmentation (P1), and it is sustained by training practices that transmit those disagreements as technical defaults (Slife & Williams, 1995).

The replication crisis is the observable symptom of this systemic incoherence. As Muthukrishna and Henrich (2019)argued, a discipline without a theoretical framework cannot distinguish informative from uninformative failures to replicate; as Oberauer and Lewandowsky (2019) showed, loosely specified theories cannot be strongly tested; and as Flake and Fried (2020) documented, constructs of uncertain reference are measured with instruments of uncertain validity. Each of these proximate causes traces back to an upstream problem on the list. The practical implication is that piecemeal solutions will be absorbed and neutralized unless the underlying metatheoretical deficit is directly addressed. Preregistration improves the reliability of findings about constructs whose reference remains uncertain; network models offer a new ontology for constructs whose relation to neural mechanisms remains unspecified; cross-cultural replication tests the reach of constructs whose scope conditions remain untheorized. Reform at any single level produces local improvement and systemic stasis.

This structural account also explains a puzzling feature of the period: why a discipline that produced so much penetrating self-criticism changed so little. Each critique was received within the subcommunity to which it was addressed, generated a reform agenda appropriate to that subcommunity, and left the others untouched. The construct validity literature reformed measurement practice; the neuroscience critiques produced methodological caution about reverse inference; the WEIRD critique produced calls for diverse sampling; the mixed-methods literature produced design templates. None of these reforms required, or produced, a change in the metatheoretical commitments that made the original problems possible, and so none could be more than partial.

Conclusions

This article has identified, described, and ranked the five most consequential theoretical problems in psychology originating in the 2000s and has evaluated their trajectory through 2026. The rankingmetatheoretical fragmentation first, construct validity crisis second, mind-brain problem third, cultural challenge fourth, positivism-interpretivism tension fifth-follows from three explicit criteria applied in a fixed order of precedence: logical priority, breadth of impact, and practical urgency. The decisive consideration is that Problem 1 is foundationally prior. The terms in which all other problems are formulated, and the standards by which candidate solutions to them would be evaluated, presuppose metatheoretical commitments that the discipline has not consensually adopted. Alternative orderings that would place construct validity or the mind-brain problem first have been considered and rejected on the grounds that each privileges a criterion-urgency in the first case, institutional salience in the second-that cannot override logical priority without producing reforms that treat symptoms in place of causes.

None of the five problems has been systematically resolved across the period under review. Open science reforms have improved methodological integrity without substituting for theoretical work. Realist and network approaches to construct validity have clarified what the problem is without producing agreement on what the solution should be. Embodied and neurophenomenological programs have demonstrated that alternatives to reduction are coherent without displacing reduction as the institutional default. The cultural critique has won the argument against universalism without supplying a theory of scope. Mixed-methods conventions have permitted peaceful coexistence without integration. What the record from 2000 to 2026 demonstrates is that empirical productivity, when it is not organized by adequate theoretical frameworks, generates an expanding but incoherent knowledge base-prone to replication failure, resistant to cumulative synthesis, and susceptible to conceptual annexation by neighboring disciplines that possess the metatheoretical coherence psychology lacks.

Five corresponding research directions follow, and their order mirrors the ranking. First, development of genuinely integrative metatheoretical frameworks that provide principled criteria for theoretical evaluation across subdisciplinary divisions, building on the theory-construction methodologies that have begun to appear since 2020 while extending them to the ontological questions those methodologies presuppose. Second, elaboration of realist construct validity theory as a sustained research program, uniting network, process, and developmental approaches under an explicit account of what psychological attributes are. Third, development of non-reductive mind-brain accounts that are philosophically defensible and empirically applicable, advancing embodied, enactive, and neurophenomenological proposals from programmatic statement to working methodology. Fourth, construction of culturally sensitive theoretical frameworks through cross-cultural metatheoretical collaboration that specifies universals, cultural particulars, and the theoretical conditions distinguishing them. Fifth, design of epistemologically grounded research protocols that integrate positivist and interpretivist approaches at the level of theoretical inference, so that different kinds of evidence bear on shared claims instead of being merely juxtaposed.

These directions constitute an agenda whose urgency the record of the past quarter century plainly confirms. They also constitute an argument about sequence. A discipline that pursues the fifth direction without the first will produce better-integrated studies of constructs it cannot define; one that pursues the third without the second will relate neural mechanisms to psychological entities of uncertain reference. The ranking defended here is, in the end, a claim about where the discipline's scarce theoretical attention should go first. Theoretical psychology has spent the period since 2000 diagnosing its condition with increasing precision. The task of the coming period is to act on the diagnosis in the order the diagnosis requires.
