The sample size was determined based on previous studies utilizing the “Look-do” Stroop task to explore the CAE (Chen, Li, et al., 2024; Tang et al., 2013). These studies employed a 2 × 2 × 2 factorial design, manipulating Instructions (Look-do, Do-do), Previous congruency (“Look”: congruent, incongruent) and Current congruency (“Do”: Congruent, Incongruent). Given our primary focus on how prior emotional experience influences current conflict processing, we calculated the required sample size using G*power based on the reported interaction effect between Previous congruency and Current congruency in the “Look-do” condition (ŋ𝑝2 = 0.231). To achieve a statistical power (1 -β) of at least 0.8, a minimum of 38 participants would be required. Forty-eight participants (5 men, 20.50±2.08) from the Sichuan Normal University participated in Experiment 1. Nine participants were excluded: eight due to a low accuracy (< 60%) on the “Do” phase or excessive undesirable responses (> 100 times) during the “Experience” phase in the “Experience-do” blocks (see below), and one for failing to complete the experiment. The final analysis included 39 participants (4 men; age: 20.44±1.78).

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Conflict processing typically elicits control adjustments, as expressed by the Conflict Adaptation Effect (CAE), which can be mediated by negative affect. Recent findings further suggest that the affective experience of conflict, even without responding, improves subsequent performance on both conflict and non-conflict trials, suggesting a broader enhancement of cognitive control. To clarify the mechanisms underlying this general benefit resulting from the affective experience of conflict, the present study conducted three behavioral experiments using a confound-minimized Stroop task.

Experiment 1 compared a standard "Do-do" condition with an "Experience-do" condition, where participants experienced conflict at the affective level in a non-response phase first, before carrying out the Stroop task during a second one. Experiment 2 replaced "Experience-do" with a "Look-do" condition, where participants merely observed the Stroop stimulus first (without the need to experience it at the affective level), before completing the Stroop task. Experiment 3 introduced a "Judgementdo" condition, directing attention to a conflict-unrelated feature. We found that conflict elicited negative affect and a reliable CAE in all "Do-do" conditions. Crucially, both affective experience (Experiment 1) and mere observation (Experiment 2) of conflict produced general performance improvements extending from incongruent to congruent trials (i.e., a General Adaptation Effect -GAE), with a larger benefit in Experiment 1 than 2. This improvement disappeared when attention was directed to a conflictunrelated feature (Experiment 3), suggesting that it is tied to conflict-related processing. These findings suggest that affectively experiencing or simply observing conflict is sufficient to enhance proactively a general form of cognitive control.

Introduction

Adaptive control refers to the ability to dynamically adjust control settings based on previous experience (e.g. the previous trial), thereby optimizing performance in the current trial (Banich, 2009; Botvinick et al., 2001; Saunders et al., 2017). This process has been extensively investigated in conflict tasks on a trial-by-trial basis, most notably through conflict adaptation (Bugg, 2012; Gratton et al., 1992). According to the dominant conflict-monitoring theory, conflict detected in trial N-1 by the dorsal Anterior Cingulate Cortex (dACC) engages the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) to improve cognitive control in trial N, thereby facilitating a more efficient conflict resolution (Botvinick, 2007; Botvinick et al., 2001 Botvinick et al., , 2004)). Traditionally, conflict adaptation has been conceptualized as a cognitive phenomenon, which is probably reflecting a reactive control component (Braver, 2012).

However, growing evidence indicates that conflict is also inherently aversive (Dignath et al., 2020; Ivanchei et al., 2024; Dreisbach & Fischer, 2015), automatically eliciting negative affect, sometimes referred to as integral negative affect (see Inzlicht et al., 2015). In agreement with this framework, empirical evidence showed for example that conflict increased electromyographic (EMG) activity of the corrugator supercilii, which is involved in defensive motivation (Berger et al., 2020). This and other results have been summarized and integrated into the intriguing Affective-Signaling Hypothesis according to which integral negative affect associated with conflict processing does not only result from it but it also triggers the recruitment of additional cognitive control to improve subsequent performance (see Dignath et al., 2020). In this framework, negative affect fulfills a dual role, both as the output of conflict processing and as the input for control adjustments, thus contributing directly to conflict adaptation. Empirical support for this affective account stems from two main lines of research. The first one has shown that externally induced negative affect, via stimuli such as affective music, film clips, or negative feedback, can increase conflict adaptation whereas positive affect decreases it (Schuch & Koch, 2015; Schuch & Pütz, 2018; Schuch et al., 2017; van Steenbergen et al., 2010 van Steenbergen et al., , 2009;; Yang & Pourtois, 2018; Yang et al., 2019 Yang et al., , 2023)). However, because these affective manipulations are mostly extraneous to conflict, it remains unclear whether negative affect bound to conflict is sufficient to modulate conflict adaptation. A second line of research has directly examined negative affect automatically generated by conflict processing. More specifically, subjective ratings of negative affect have been collected on a trial-by-trial basis in conflict tasks such as the vertical Simon task (Fröber et al. , 2017). The results showed that conflict adaptation was observed selectively in trials where participants reported stronger negative affect, yet regardless of whether the trial actually involved conflict or not.

Accordingly, it has been suggested that subjective negative affect, rather than conflict detection per se, could serves as the primary driving force behind control adjustment effects captured by the conflict adaptation effect (CAE; Dreisbach & Fischer, 2015).

In line with these findings and the Affective-Signaling Hypothesis (Dignath et al., 2020), several studies have found that conflict adaptation is not simply a direct consequence of detecting conflict; rather, it originates from a negative affective evaluation, or subjective appraisal even, of conflict. In this framework, the affective experience of conflict is assumed to heighten the need for enhanced cognitive control in subsequent trials, aimed at reducing the subjective discomfort evoked by conflict (Dignath et al., 2020; Dreisbach & Fischer, 2012 , 2015; Desender et al., 2014; Fritz & Dreisbach, 2013). In agreement with it, it has been proposed that distressing experiences, whether arising from conflict or other challenging circumstances, can motivate the implementation of cognitive control as a means of alleviating discomfort and restoring a sense of "cognitive comfort" (Pruessner et al., 2020; Saunders et al., 2015a Saunders et al., , 2015b;; Yang, 2022).

Moreover, in a recent electroencephalogram (EEG) study, the authors investigated how the affective experience of conflict could influence subsequent conflict processing and adjustments (Si et al., 2025). Interestingly, the results showed that focusing on the affective aspect of conflict, without explicitly responding to it, enhanced subsequent performance not only in incongruent (conflict) trials but also in congruent (non-conflict) ones, thereby indicating a general performance improvement (i.e, general adaptation effect, GAE) that could extend beyond the CAE, which is typically bound to the former but not latter trial type. In other words, attending to the affective experience of conflict appears to broaden the performance benefit typically observed with the CAE, facilitating the subsequent processing of both conflict and non-conflict trials. Notably, because the GAE occurs in the absence of an overt response to (and resolution of) conflict but results from its subjective experience at the affective level, this latter emotional component appears critical to drive it.

Presumably, during mere conflict observation, as opposed to the affective experience of conflict, negative affect is also automatically elicited (for a review, see Dignath et al., 2020) and hence it might also yield a GAE, although its magnitude is likely lower than during the explicit affective experience of conflict. A main goal of this study was to test this prediction. More specifically, we sought to assess whether in the absence of a behavioral response (and hence covert conflict resolution), the affective experience of conflict would produce a larger GAE compared to merely observing it or not. A second goal of our study was to examine the boundary conditions of the GAE and confirm that it is related to conflict and not simply attention and/or a (non-affective) monitoring of the stimulus.

To address these goals, we conducted three separate behavioral experiments, all using the same confound-minimized Stroop task (Braem et al., 2019), which we have already used extensively in the past. In Experiment 1, we compared a classical "Do-do" condition with a novel "Experience-do" condition. In Experiment 2, we compared the same "Do-do" with a "Look-do" condition. In the "Dodo" condition, participants performed the Stroop stimulus in both the previous and current 'Do' phases. In the "Experience-do" and "Look-do" conditions, participants either experienced the Stroop stimulus at the affective level or merely observed it without explicit affective engagement in the first phase, before they completed the Stroop task during a second one. In each of them, we assessed if the GAE was elicited and directly compared its size between them. Moreover, as conflict experience (Experiment 1) and conflict observation (Experiment 2) both involved conflict processing without the need to respond to the stimulus, we assessed in Experiment 3 if conflict processing during the first phase was actually required to unlock the GAE or not. To this end, we compared the "Do-do" condition with a "Judgement-do" one. In this latter condition, the participants first attended to the font size of the Stroop stimulus (hence to an orthogonal, non-conflict dimension), yet without giving any response, before they performed the Stroop task in the subsequent phase.

We hypothesized that conflict adaptation should be observed in the "Do-do" condition of all three experiments, mostly expressed by faster (and better) conflict processing on trial X if conflict processing was successful on trial X-1. Moreover, we reckoned that both "Experience-do" and "Look-do" conditions should yield a GAE, and moreover, it should be larger in the "Experience-do" than the "Look-do" condition due to the affective appraisal of the stimulus in the former condition. Last but not least, we also expected that this GAE would not be expressed in the "Judgement-do" condition of Experiment 3 because it does not involve conflict processing (but font size processing instead).

Method Transparency and Openness

We report how we determined our sample size, all data exclusions (if any), all manipulations, and all measures in the study, and we follow the Journal Article Reporting Standards. All data, analysis code, and research materials are publicly available on the Open Science Framework at https://osf.io/zkctw/. Participants

The sample size was determined based on previous studies utilizing the "Look-do" Stroop task to explore the CAE (Chen, Li, et al., 2024; Tang et al., 2013). These studies employed a 2 × 2 × 2 factorial design, manipulating Instructions (Look-do, Do-do), Previous congruency ("Look": congruent, incongruent) and Current congruency ("Do": Congruent, Incongruent). Given our primary focus on how prior emotional experience influences current conflict processing, we calculated the required sample size using G*power based on the reported interaction effect between Previous congruency and Current congruency in the "Look-do" condition (ŋ𝑝2 = 0.231). To achieve a statistical power (1 -β) of at least 0.8, a minimum of 38 participants would be required. Forty-eight participants (5 men, 20.50±2.08) from the Sichuan Normal University participated in Experiment 1. Nine participants were excluded: eight due to a low accuracy (< 60%) on the "Do" phase or excessive undesirable responses (> 100 times) during the "Experience" phase in the "Experience-do" blocks (see below), and one for failing to complete the experiment. The final analysis included 39 participants (4 men; age: 20.44±1.78).

All participants had normal or corrected-to-normal vision, no colorblindness. They were provided informed consent and were compensated 18 yuan for their 1-hour participation in the end. The experiment was approved by the Behavioral Ethics Committee at the Sichuan Normal University (No. 2024LS029).

Stimuli

The Stroop stimuli comprised four Chinese color words ("红"/red, RGB: 255, 0, 0; "蓝"/blue, RGB: 0, 0, 255; "绿"/green, RGB: 0, 255, 0; or "黄"/yellow, RGB: 255, 255, 0) presented in Song typeface (Size 28). The word meaning and font color were either congruent or incongruent. To eliminate contingency learning effects (Schmidt & Weissman, 2014; Weissman et al., 2014), each word appeared in only two colors, resulting in two stimulus-response pairs. Incongruent trials were formed by word-color mismatches within each pair rather than across pairs, yielding 8 stimuli types (instead of 16 types) with an equal ratio of congruent and incongruent trials (Mordkoff, 2012). To prevent feature repetitions across successive trials, we ensured that neither the goal-relevant (color) nor goal-irrelevant (meaning) dimensions were repeated across successive trials.

Each block consisted of 80 Stroop stimuli organized into 40 trials (pairs), representing the previous and current conflict-processing phases, respectively. The stimuli order was pseudo-randomized based on congruency (congruent vs. incongruent stimulus) of the previous and current phase within each trial, yielding equal proportion of congruent-Congruent (cC) trials, congruent-Incongruent (cI) trials, incongruent-Congruent (iC) trials, and incongruent-Incongruent (iI) trials. Stimuli were presented and data were recorded using E-Prime 3.0 (Psychology Software Tools Inc., Sharpsburg, PA).

Procedure and task

The Instructions was manipulated at the block level ("Experience-do" vs. "Do-do"). In the "Experience-do" condition (see Figure 1A), participants were instructed to experience the (Stroop) stimulus as either pleasant or unpleasant (hence with a focus on valence) during the previous "Experience" phase (in analogy with Fröber et al., 2017), followed by a standard color-naming task in the "Do" phase. In comparison, in the "Do-do" condition (see Figure 1B), participants performed the color-naming task in both phases. Each trial began with a fixation cue serving as the inter-trial interval (ITI) (an asterisk '*' for the previous "Experience"; a cross '+' for the previous "Do" phase) presented for 400-600 ms (M = 500 ms). The first Stroop word then appeared at the center of the screen for a maximum of 1,000 ms or until a response was made 1 . Next, a fixation cross (+) was presented for 400-600 ms (M = 500 ms), followed by a second Stroop word presented for a maximum of 1,000 ms or until a response was made. Finally, a blank screen was shown for 500-800 ms (randomly jittered). 1 In the "Experience-do" conditions, if a response was recorded during the "Experience" phase, then it was categorized as undesirable as it indicated that participants did not follow the instructions to experience only the stimulus (in the absence of overt response to it). Before the main session, all participants signed the informed consent form and completed a practice session comprising one "Experience-do" block and one "Do-do" block, each containing 10 trials. At the end of each block, participants received feedback on their mean accuracy; only those achieving at least 60% correct proceeded to the main experiment. The main session consisted of eight blocks: four "Experience-do" and four "Do-do" conditions, each with 40 pairs (320 trials in total).

Two block sequences (i.e., ABBAABBA and BAABBAAB) were created and alternated across participants. At the beginning of each block, participants received task-specific instructions indicating whether they would perform the "Experience-do" or "Do-do" task. After the cross (+), participants responded to the font color as quickly and accurately as possible while ignoring the word meaning.

Responses were made with the left hand: "A" (little finger) for red, "S" (ring finger) for blue, "D" (middle finger) for green, and "F" (index finger) for yellow. Self-paced breaks were allowed between conditions.

Subjective ratings

After every two blocks, participants were asked to rate four times their overall dislike feelings toward the 8 different Stroop stimuli used (i.e., "How much did you dislike the stimulus presented in the block?") by means of a visual analog scale (VAS). The VAS ranged from 0 ("not at all") to 100 ("a lot"), representing a putative continuum of dislike intensity on each assessment.

Accordingly, the VAS was administered four times in total-twice in the "Experience-do" conditions and twice in the "Do-do" condition-to assess whether incongruent (vs. congruent) trials elicited stronger dislike and whether this congruency effect was larger in the "Experience-do" than in the "Do-do" condition.

Data analyses

Negative affect

Mean dislike ratings for the stimuli were computed first. The resulting VAS scores were then analyzed using repeated-measures ANOVAs with Instruction (Experience-do vs. Do-do) and Current congruency (Congruent vs. Incongruent) as within-subject factors.

Behavioral data analysis

For each participant, data were trimmed separately for each condition. In the "Experience-do" condition, trials were excluded from accuracy analyses if they contained "undesirable responses" (see above) during the "Experience" phase or if reaction times (RTs) in the "Do" phase exceeded outliers (±3 SD) from the participant's mean. For RT analyses, error trials in the "Do" phase were further excluded. In the "Do-do" condition, trials were excluded from accuracy analyses if RTs in either the previous or current "Do" phase were outliers (±3 SD); RT analyses excluded trials with errors in either phases. Consequently, 12404 trials remained for accuracy analysis and 10278 trials for RTs analysis.

To account for the categorical nature of accuracy data and the typically right-skewed distribution of RTs (Straub et al., 2022; Whelan, 2008), we employed a Generalized Linear Mixed Model (GLMM) with a binomial distribution and logit link function for accuracy analysis (see results in the Supplementary Materials 2 ), and a Linear Mixed Model (LMM) with log-transformed RTs for the RTs analysis.

Random-effects structures were determined by starting with the maximal structure justified by the design, including by-subject random intercepts and slopes for Instructions, and by-color random intercepts and slopes for Previous congruency and Current congruency. We optimized these structures using likelihood ratio tests (LRTs) to achieve the best fit. For accuracy analysis (GLMM), the optimal random effects structure included by-subjects random intercept and random slope for Instructions, bycolor random intercept in previous condition, and by-color random intercept and random slope for Current congruency (i.e., (1+Instructions|Subject) + (1|color pre ) + (1+Current Congruency|color cur )).

For the RTs analysis (LMM), the optimal random effects structure included by-subjects random intercept and random slope for Instructions, along with by-color random intercept and random slope for Current congruency (i.e., (1+Instructions|Subject) + (1+Current congruency|color cur )).

For fixed effects, we fitted (G)LMMs with a 2 × 2 × 2 structure, including Previous congruency, Current congruency, Instructions, and all possible interactions. The three main factors were meancentered using sum-to-zero deviation coding. The significance of fixed effects was tested via LRTs, comparing the full model against a reduced model omitting the effect of interest, while maintaining the random-effects structure. A standard alpha level of 0.05 was used for all statistical tests.

Discussion

The subjective ratings revealed that participants reported higher dislike feelings toward incongruent than congruent stimuli across both conditions (i.e., "Experience-do" and "Do-do"). This finding supports the idea that conflict processing generates negative affect, as proposed by Dignath et al. (2020). Although conflict was associated with negative affect in both conditions, we observed distinct adaptation effects for them. In the "Do-do" condition, where participants responded to the stimulus in both phases, a typical CAE was observed. In contrast, in the "Experience-do" condition, where participants affectively experienced the Stroop stimulus at the subjective level in the preceding phase without responding, if conflict was present during it, then it enhanced performance on both incongruent (conflict) and congruent trials during the subsequent phase, unambiguously revealing a GAE. This result therefore suggests that affectively experiencing conflict (without responding to it) has a broad influence on subsequent cognitive performance and likely triggers it, which is distinct from the response-based CAE. Although they differ, they are however related to one another, as shown by their significant association. However, an open question is whether experiencing conflict at the affective level or merely observing it could be sufficient to yield this GAE as in both cases negative affect is probably elicited. In Experiment 2, we addressed this question by modifying the experimental design and replacing the "Experience-do" condition by a "Look-do" one.

Discussion

The subjective ratings and behavioral results of Experiment 2 are very similar to those previously observed in Experiment 1. More specifically, conflict adaptation was clearly expressed in the "Do-do" condition, in which participants responded to the Stroop stimuli in both phases. In contrast, the results for the "Look-do" condition showed that merely observing conflict in the preceding phase enhanced subsequent performance across both congruent and incongruent trials during the subsequent one, in agreement with the expression of a GAE.

Combined with the results of Experiment 1, this finding for the "Look-do" condition therefore suggests that conflict observation is actually sufficient to yield it.

This finding also suggests that in Experiment 1, participants did not necessarily experience conflict at the affective level, but they might have simply observed it and this was sufficient to unlock this beneficial effect on subsequent stimulus processing. However, because this GAE was significantly larger in Experiment 1 than 2, we have good reasons to believe that it was not the case and affectively experiencing conflict was different (and more potent to unlock it) than merely observing it. Yet, a lingering question is whether conflict processing (in the absence of response) is needed or not to trigger this GAE because simply observing it appears to be sufficient to yield it. To address this question, we ran Experiment 3 where in one condition ("Judgement-do"), the participants were asked to process a stimulus dimension (i.e., font size) that was orthogonal to conflict. We reasoned that if conflict processing (without responding) is required to trigger this GAE, then it should disappear in this condition because it is not required.

Discussion

Unlike Experiments 1 and 2 where either affectively experiencing or merely observing conflict led to a GAE each time (albeit stronger for Experiment 2 than 1), the results of Experiment 3 showed that if the participants did not process conflict (but attended to a non-conflict stimulus feature instead, here with a focus on the font size), then this gain completely vanished. This null result was corroborated by a Bayesian analysis, which provided decisive evidence against the presence of a GAE when attention was directed away from conflict. In comparison, a clear-cut CAE was observed in the "Do-do" condition, as was observed in Experiments 1&2. Hence, cognitive control turned out to be reduced in the "Judgement-do" condition while it was still elicited, probably by means of a reactive component, in the "Do-do" one. This finding provides direct evidence that the GAE found in Experiments 1&2 actually depended on conflict processing, even though no overt response was required for it. Hence, the results of Experiment 3 reveal some important boundary conditions for this GAE resulting from (covert) conflict processing at the affective level and as documented in Experiments 1&2.

General Discussion

The present study investigated how conflict processing and its affective appraisal could shape dynamic behavioral adjustments in non-response contexts across three experiments. Consistently across all three experiments, participants reported stronger negative affect toward incongruent than congruent stimuli, confirming that cognitive conflict robustly elicits negative affect at the subjective level (Inzlicht et al., 2015; Yang, 2022; Dignath et al., 2020), even in the absence of an overt response (Damen et al., 2018). At the performance level, conflict adaptation was clearly expressed in all "Dodo" conditions, where responding to conflict facilitated the resolution of subsequent conflict via enhanced cognitive control (Botvinick et al., 2001). Interestingly, when participants either affectively experienced conflict (Experiment 1) or merely observed it (Experiment 2), without responding in this first phase, conflict adaptation substantially changed and generalized to non-conflict, congruent trials during the subsequent phase, thus generating a GAE. However and crucially, this general gain in performance disappeared in Experiment 3 where participants' attention was directed to a conflictunrelated feature of the stimulus (i.e., the font size), a finding that was robustly supported by Bayesian evidence favoring the null hypothesis. Accordingly, combined together, these results suggest that this GAE is not restricted to conflict processing (during the current trial phase), but importantly, it is tied to conflict-related processing rather than to the mere absence of a behavioral response (during the previous trial phase).

This GAE observed in the "Experience-do" and "Look-do" conditions differ from previous studies employing similar "Look-do" paradigms (see Chen, Li, et al., 2024; Chen, Gao, et al., 2024; Tang et al., 2013), where it was typically bond to conflict-specific processes (i.e., conflict adaptation) rather than a general performance improvement. To account for this discrepancy, a few methodological elements are worth considering. In these previous studies, the observation phase was pseudo-randomized with the regular Stroop task ("Do-do" condition), thereby maintaining participants' overall response readiness throughout the experiment and yielding conflict-specific adjustments similar to this "Do-do" condition. In contrast, the fixed sequential design used in the present study likely enabled participants to clearly segment at the trial level the initial non-response phase from the subsequent response one, as each non-response phase was invariably followed by the need to respond shortly afterwards (either "Experience-do" or "Look-do"). This predictability may have prompted the use of proactive control by the participants (Chia & Sharon, 2013), resulting in a general performance benefit encompassing both congruent and incongruent trials. Moreover, because in Experiment 1 we found a positive correlation between the CAE ("Do-do") and this GAE ("Experience-do"), we could even surmise that in the former condition, the overt response to conflict could actually mask or block this (proactive) cognitive control changes, thereby leading to a specific CAE (being mostly reactive in essence). Yet, these two adaptation effects (i.e., CAE and GAE) would share some common ground to some extent, although their expressions clearly differ at the behavioral level. For instance, we could reckon that in both cases, the aversive and challenging nature of conflict signals the need for increased control investment (Dignath et al., 2020; Inzlicht et al., 2015; Shenhav et al., 2013).

However, we could argue that these two adaptation effects, although both seemingly related to cognitive control adjustments upon the encounter of conflict, actually differ regarding the locus of control implied as well as its temporal scale, and hence they are probably dissociable. In agreement with this conjecture, in Experiment 2, we failed to evidence a significant correlation between the CAE ("Do-do" condition) and the GAE ("Look-do" condition), while these two effects were clearly visible and significant. Moreover, at the theoretical level, conflict adaptation has been conceptualized as a trial-specific, reactive adjustment effect, likely corresponding to an immediate fine tuning or sharpening of cognitive control in response to conflict (Botvinick et al., 2001; Egner, 2007). In contrast, the GAE observed here appears to reflect a broader, state-level enhancement of cognitive (or perhaps motor) readiness (O'Neil et al., 2013), or alternatively motivation (Inzlicht et al., 2015), that extends beyond conflict resolution per se. This broader and proactive upregulation of cognitive control may depend less on stimulus stimulus-response binding mechanisms, as is the case for conflict adaptation presumably (Verguts & Notebaert, 2009), but more on either arousal (Mather et al. 2016) or endogenous attention (Petersen & Posner, 2012). Moreover, their temporal profiles also likely differ. In the "Do-do" condition, the putative affective signal bound to conflict processing could be translated into immediate, reactive, conflict-specific adjustments, whereas in the "Experience-do" and "Look-do" conditions, where no overt response was required, this signal was perhaps not elicited as such, and hence it could not be used readily by the participants to guide cognitive control, yet another, slower and more anticipatory related endogenous effect could be harnessed to this end (Braver, 2012).

Our new findings are only partly compatible with the Affective-Signaling Hypothesis (Dignath et al., 2020). Although across all three experiments, the participants reported consistently more negative affect for incongruent than congruent stimuli at the subjective level, we failed to observe a direct relationship between negative affect and either of these two adaptation effects (i.e., CAE vs. GAE).

Moreover, even though the GAE substantially changed as a function of the specific manipulation performed during the initial phase of the trial (i.e., it was larger for affectively experiencing conflict than merely observing it, and it was absent when the focus was on the font size), levels of negative affect were comparable across conditions and experiments. Accordingly, we could assume that the emotional appraisal of the conflict stimulus, rather than negative affect bound to it, might be responsible for adjustments of cognitive control across successive trials, including for the GAE.

As suggested here above, the GAE found in Experiments 1&2 could be undergirded by arousal.

Conflict is not only aversive but also inherently challenging (Vermeylen et al., 2023), because signaling the need to mobilize attention and cognitive resources to meet the task demands (Dreisbach & Fischer, 2015; Inzlicht et al., 2015; Dignath et al., 2020). To achieve this, arousal could fuel cognitive control and hence be an important factor of it. From an attentional-arousal perspective, exposure to conflict elicits a transient increase in physiological and neural arousal that functions as a control-alerting signal, prompting adaptive engagement and general performance improvements (van der Wel & van Steenbergen, 2018; Saunders et al., 2017). Empirical evidence indicates that conflict evokes phasic arousal responses, as reflected in increased pupil dilation (van Steenbergen & Band, 2013) and elevated frontal midline theta (FMT) power recorded from the EEG signal (Cavanagh & Frank, 2014; Cooper et al., 2019); the former one which is closely linked to activation of the locus coeruleus-norepinephrine (LC-NE) system. This arousal-related modulation enhances cortical excitability and attentional gain (Aston-Jones & Cohen, 2005; Nieuwenhuis et al., 2011), thereby sharpening attentional vigilance and biasing processing toward goal-relevant information. In line with this account, several studies have demonstrated that brief exposure to conflict or other aversive stimuli can facilitate performance in subsequent unrelated tasks, reflecting a carryover of arousal-induced attentional readiness (Fröber et al., 2017; Bouzidi & Gendolla, 2024; Mather et al., 2016). Within this framework, the GAE observed in the present study therefore does not simply represent the residual aftereffect of conflict monitoring, but might rather reflect an arousal-induced preparatory enhancement of attentional control. Experiencing or observing conflict during the preceding non-response phase likely triggered a transient vigilance state that led to a general gain of stimulus processing during the current response phase, thereby improving not only conflict but also non-conflict trials.

Somewhat related to the previous argument but drawing from effort and motivation science, conflict and the experience of negative affect going along with it, may act as a salient difficulty cue or prime for the participants, signaling in turn the need of greater effort investment (Desender et al., 2021; David et al., 2024 for review; Kool et al., 2010). When individuals experience negative affect during task performance, they perceive this as a challenge and seek to respond to it by mobilizing additional cognitive resources (David et al., 2024). In agreement with this view, in conflict tasks, conflict enhances perceived task difficulty and amplifies effort-related physiological (cardiac) responses (e.g., reduced pre-ejection period; Bouzidi & Gendolla, 2023a , 2023b , 2024). Within the current sequential design, both experiencing and observing conflict likely activated or primed difficulty, thereby fostering a greater engagement during the upcoming response phase. Because of the frequent association encountered between conflict and difficulty, and hence effort as well, this recruitment of additional cognitive resources is primed despite the absence of an overt response to conflict. However and importantly, if conflict is not a stake during the non-response phase, as in the "Judgement-do" condition (Experiment 3), then this priming does not occur and as a result, no GAE is observed. Hence, these additional cognitive resources were not mobilized in this condition because the association between conflict and difficulty was not created (and/or it was made transiently inactive or extinguished). In sum, the mere exposure to conflict stimuli is not sufficient to yield the GAE, but an active processing of them is required for it instead, even if no response is expected.

Alternatively, we could assume that reward-based processing actually played a role in the GAE and might account for the dissociation found between this adaptation effect and the CAE in our study (Botvinick & Braver, 2015). Previous studies have found that successful conflict resolution can be rewarding (when considered using a reinforcement framework for instance) and hence account for the CAE (Schouppe et al., 2015; Braem et al., 2012). In our study, this component was likely present for the "Do-do" condition, but absent or reduced for the "Experience-do" and "Look-do" conditions.

Hence, it remains to be shown in future empirical studies whether attentional, arousal, effort or perhaps (the lack of) reward eventually accounts for the GAE reported in our study.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, negative affect was assessed at a few isolated points only during these experiments, rather than continuously, which inevitably limited the possibility to capture subtle variations along this affective dimension as a function of cognitive control. Hence, the precision of negative affect was limited. Moreover, we only measured and used negative affect at the subjective level. To overcome this limitation, future studies employing trial by trial psychophysiological measures (e.g., EMG, pupil dilation, or cardiac reactivity; Berger et al., 2020; Bouzidi & Gendolla, 2024) as well as more specific subjective ratings could be used, thereby increasing the likelihood to more precisely capture the dynamic interplay between negative affect and cognitive control. In a similar vein, since we can only speculate about the likely dissociable cognitive control mechanisms reflected by these two adaptation effects (i.e., CAE and GAE) based on our behavioral results, future studies using EEG or fMRI could probably shed light on their neurobiological underpinnings and eventually determine whether they are distinct or common instead. Despite these caveats, it is noteworthy that our study also features several methodological strengths, including the use of a within-subject design in each experiment that minimizes interindividual variability, an a priori power analysis ensuring adequate statistical sensitivity, the use of a confound-minimized Stroop task throughout controlling for contingency learning and priming, and robust data modeling through (G)LMMs.

In conclusion, the present findings show that both affectively experiencing and merely observing conflict, in the absence of overt behavioral response to it, can however enhance cognitive control, as revealed by a GAE that encompasses not only conflict trials, but also non-conflict ones. Moreover, a control experiment showed that active or explicit conflict handling, but not simply response-related processes unrelated to conflict, are instrumental in giving rise to it. This GAE is different compared to the classic CAE (although they might be related to each other, as found in Experiment 1); the latter one which is mostly evidenced in our study when an active response is required and which is linked specifically to conflict processing. We propose that when either affectively experiencing or merely observing conflict, this stimulus "automatically" influences attention, arousal, effort-based decision making or perhaps reward-based processing, thereby facilitating the upcoming stimulus processing irrespective of congruency. Future research is needed to pinpoint which of these candidate processes, or perhaps another one even, likely contributes to elicit this GAE. More generally, our findings expand the existing cognitive control literature by revealing the existence of another and more global adaptation effect than the CAE, which has not been explored systematically yet, but open new research perspectives in this area.
