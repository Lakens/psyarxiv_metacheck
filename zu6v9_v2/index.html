Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. Some differences come from the ground truth: it lacks p-values with a correction name (“p Bonf = .061”), and counts partial eta squared written with its subscript after it (“η 2 p = .05”), “p = .05”, and some table notes and figure legends (“† p < .10, *p < .05”), which this module leaves out, as non-significant p-values. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Abstract

Personality traits are linked to important life outcomes, and many people are motivated to change aspects of their personality. This suggests a high potential for volitional personality change interventions, which are on the rise. However, their effect sizes tend to be small and heterogeneous. A key reason may be that intervention designs remain largely disconnected from current work on dynamics relevant to personality change. We argue that personality interventions should be enriched by work on personality dynamics, which focuses on momentary states and their patterns (mean-level, variability, state-state associations, situational contingencies, temporal dynamics). We outline three opportunities for more effective personality interventions incorporating personality dynamics. First, dynamics can serve as novel intervention targets (e.g., reducing stress reactivity in neuroticism interventions), addressing overlooked aspects of personality traits beyond the trait selfconcept. Second, dynamics can be used to personalize interventions at a between-person level (e.g., promoting person-specific activities associated with desired states). Third, dynamics can be used as momentary within-person triggers of adaptive interventions (e.g., encouraging the planning of social activities following a day with little social contact). Key advantages of the proposed approach include potentially more effective and inclusive interventions with stronger downstream effects on life outcomes. We provide guidance on the methodological implementation and discuss limitations and future directions. Ultimately, leveraging personality dynamics could enable a novel generation of personality interventions.

Leveraging Personality Dynamics for Volitional Personality Change Interventions

Personality traits, defined as people's relatively stable tendencies in experience and behavior, are associated with life outcomes in various domains (e.g., health, well-being, occupational success, social relationships; Soto, 2019). In addition, many individuals are motivated to change parts of their personality (Baranski et al., 2021). Recent research supports the effectiveness of these interventions (Haehner et al., 2024).

However, effect sizes tend to be small and variable across studies and individuals, highlighting the need for theoretical and empirical advances.

Based on theoretical arguments concerning the relevance of states (i.e., momentary experience and behavior, on the timescale of seconds to hours) for personality change (on the timescale of months to years), we propose bridging personality interventions and personality dynamics. The present article makes three contributions. Importance of Personality Dynamics for Personality Interventions

Theoretical work on naturally occurring and volitional personality trait change yields insights into how the effectiveness of personality interventions may be improved. A common theme across various approaches is an emphasis on the importance of state-level processes for personality change (Allemand & Flückiger, 2017; Geukes et al., 2018; Hudson, 2021; Jackson & Wright, 2024; Roberts et al., 2017; Wrzus & Roberts, 2017). For instance, Hudson (2021) posits that the effects of volitional personality interventions depend on whether (recurrent) changes in momentary personality states (i.e., states with the same content as corresponding traits such as the Big Five) are elicited, with repeated state expression coalescing into lasting trait change. Similarly, Wrzus and Roberts (2017) suggest that repeated TESSERA sequences (Triggering situations, Expectancy, States/State Expressions, ReAction) result in personality change. Especially when states deviate from a person's typical state level (or pattern) frequently and for long enough, one would expect trait change to occur (although we acknowledge that aspects of traits could also change without altered state expression; e.g., the trait self-concept shifting due to a changing reference group; see below). Importantly, individual differences in state dynamics can be considered relevant aspects of personality traits (see below), highlighting the relevance of enduring change in these dynamics for personality change interventions. Regarding intervention strategies, Allemand and Flückiger (2017) and Wright et al. (2026) differentiate strategies working at the state-level, habit-level, or trait (self-concept)-level. Relatively few intervention strategies aim to change the enduring trait self-concept directly (e.g., psychoeducation). In contrast, many interventions target momentary states (e.g., behavioral activation) or habitual states (e.g., goal setting) (Wright et al., 2026), highlighting the importance of bottom-up strategies for trait change (Jackson & Wright, 2024). Overall, state-level processes thus play a central role in natural and intervention-induced personality change.

Thus, the field of personality dynamics (Kuper et al., 2021; Rauthmann, 2021), which focuses on such state-level processes, is highly relevant for personality interventions. Work in this field provides more detail on the specific relevant types of dynamics (see Table 1). People not only differ in the mean-levels of their personality states (e.g., how often they act extraverted in daily life) but also in how much these states fluctuate across time (e.g., variability in extraverted behavior; Fleeson & Gallagher, 2009). Further, individuals vary in the extent to which different states co-occur (e.g., links between extraverted behavior and positive affect), as emphasized in network approaches to personality (Beck & Jackson, 2020; Cramer et al., 2012). People also differ in how strongly aspects of the situation are linked to certain states (situational contingencies; e.g., the extent to which a person reacts with extraverted behavior to social situations), which has been highlighted as central to personality (Denissen & Penke, 2008; Mischel & Shoda, 1995). 1 Lastly, individuals differ in temporal dynamics that describe the time course of their states (e.g., latency: how quickly a state is triggered, persistence: how long it lasts; Wilt & Revelle, 2023). This also includes so-called attractors (set points) towards which momentary states of a person are drawn over time, with individuals potentially differing in the position and strength of these attractors, as emphasized in dynamic systems approaches (Sosnowska et al., 2019).

Various positions on the link between personality dynamics and personality traits exist in the literature (e.g., Baumert et al., 2017; Fleeson & Jayawickreme, 2025; Kuper et al., 2021; Lee et al., 2026). Based on a broad conceptualization of personality traits as stable tendencies in experience and behavior, the present article uses the working model that individual dynamics (e.g., mean-levels, situational contingencies) are constituents of personality traits. From this perspective, changes in personality dynamics correspond to changes in aspects of personality traits. The trait self-concept (i.e., people's perception of their own trait; captured using classical self-report questionnaires) is conceptualized as another aspect of the personality trait. Importantly, the trait self-concept should not be equated with the trait itself, and it could change without changes to people's actual everyday states (e.g., due to changes in the reference group with which individuals compare themselves). A central limitation of work on personality interventions is that it is largely focused on examining changes in the trait self-concept and gives less attention to other aspects of the trait (e.g., dynamics), which we seek to address here (see Opportunity 1). Notably, we assume causal relations among aspects of the trait system -for instance, multiple situational contingencies could causally contribute to the emergent state mean-level, which in turn could have causal effects on the trait self-concept (for related arguments, see, e.g., Quintus et al., 2021; Wrzus et al., 2021). From this perspective, changing personality dynamics could have both direct downstream effects on a given relevant outcome (e.g., well-being) and indirect effects via influences on the broader trait system (see Advantages below).

Importantly, individual differences in dynamics should be conceptually distinguished from long-term changes therein, which constitute changes in aspects of personality traits (see above). Empirical investigations of such changes in dynamics (naturally occurring or intervention-induced) are only beginning (Allemand et al., 2024; Küchler et al., 2025; Olaru et al., 2025; Quintus et al., 2021; Wrzus et al., 2021). Notably, while theoretical accounts of personality interventions often emphasize the state-level, an explicit integration of dynamics into intervention design is lacking. We outline three opportunities for novel personality interventions which arise from leveraging personality dynamics: (1) dynamics as targets of interventions, (2) dynamics for personalization (between persons), and (3) dynamics as triggers of adaptive interventions (within persons) (see Figure 1). Three Opportunities for Personality Interventions

Opportunity 1: Dynamics as Targets of Interventions

Building on our working model conceptualizing personality dynamics as constituents of personality traits, dynamics may serve as critical targets of personality interventions. The vast majority of work on personality interventions evaluates intervention effectiveness by examining changes in the trait self-concept as captured by global self-report questionnaires (see Haehner et al., 2024). This reliance on global questionnaires reflects both historical tradition and convenience but leads to the underrepresentation of other aspects of personality traits. Individual dynamics could fruitfully serve as alternative targets of intervention and changes in dynamics could be examined to evaluate intervention effectiveness.

First, personality interventions can target mean-levels of personality states. For example, to increase state extraversion, behavioral activation regarding social activities or behavioral challenges focused on sociable behavior (e.g., calling a friend, talking to a stranger) can be used. This should increase the mean-level of state extraversion over time.

Initial evidence supports that personality interventions trigger mean-level changes in personality states (Allemand et al., 2024; Küchler et al., 2025; Stieger et al., 2022). Further, some work suggests that average state changes are related to changes in the trait self-concept (Küchler et al., 2025; Olaru et al., 2025; Wright, Haehner, Hopwood, & Bleidorn, 2025).

Second, personality interventions can target the variability of states. Prior work highlights the relevance of variability as an individual difference variable (Beckmann et al., 2021) and variability shows some associations with classical trait self-concept measures (Fleeson & Gallagher, 2009; Wendt et al., 2020). Notably, links of state variability with outcomes can be complex (Ong et al., 2025) and conceptually, both low variability (e.g., a lack of flexibility) and high variability (e.g., strong volatility) could be maladaptive (Geukes et al., 2017). For instance, extraversion interventions could aim to increase participants' behavioral repertoire in social interactions (e.g., learning to show sociable or assertive behavior) and their ability to adapt this behavior to the context, increasing variability. In turn, neuroticism interventions could target excessive fluctuations in negative affect through mindfulness (Keng et al., 2021), reducing variability. Thus, depending on the specific case, different ways of targeting variability may be beneficial.

Third, patterns of associations among different states and state modalities (e.g., affect, behavior, cognition, motivation) could be targeted. Network approaches to personality highlight that different states can affect each other, and individual differences in these links are central to personality (Beck & Jackson, 2020; Cramer et al., 2012), yielding a key target for interventions. For instance, in participants aiming to become more extraverted, social skills training could increase confidence in one's own behavior, potentially strengthening links between sociable behavior and positive affect (Wright et al., 2026). Similarly, in participants with a socially anxious personality profile (e.g., high neuroticism and low extraversion), certain social behaviors may be followed by post-event rumination, which could be disrupted through cognitive strategies such as thought challenging (McEvoy et al., 2009). Beyond bivariate state-state associations, networks of larger numbers of states belonging to a given trait domain can be considered (Beck & Jackson, 2020). Personality interventions could promote stronger links among these different states (e.g., motivation to interact socially, optimistic cognitions, outgoing behavior, activated positive affect), such that they reinforce each other in daily life, yielding downstream effects on the broader trait system (e.g., higher mean-levels). This may require combining multiple intervention strategies (e.g., cognitive restructuring, implementation intentions, positive reinforcement).

Fourth, personality interventions could target contingencies between aspects of the situation and states (Kuper et al., 2022). Given the theoretical relevance of such contingencies for personality (Denissen & Penke, 2008; Mischel & Shoda, 1995), changing how people react to situations offers a promising avenue for personality interventions. Many psychological interventions implicitly or explicitly target how people react to situations. For instance, the SORKC model (Stimulus, Organism, Response, Contingency, Consequence; Kanfer & Saslow, 1969) is used in clinical practice to identify and modify maladaptive responses. Similarly, some intervention strategies for personality interventions are expected to alter links between certain situations and states (e.g., exposure training or implementation intentions; Wright et al., 2026). For instance, implementation intentions require participants to generate desired behaviors for specific situations, thereby establishing a new contingency (Hudson et al., 2019). In extraversion interventions, participants could generate implementation intentions related to social situations (e.g., "I will initiate small talk with the barista the next time I go to a coffee shop"), linking these situations to extraverted behavior.

In turn, for conscientiousness, implementation intentions linking certain work demands and concentration could be used. However, such implementation intentions typically yield rather specific contingencies, and more work is needed to achieve generalization to broader contingencies linking many situations with desired states. Overall, personality interventions can benefit from identifying key situational contingencies involved in the expression of a certain desired state and then implementing strategies that directly target these contingencies.

Successfully achieving contingency change would ensure that the desired state continues to be expressed when relevant situations are encountered, also after the intervention.

Finally, temporal dynamics could be targeted, including the latency of psychological reactions, the persistence/inertia of states, as well as the position and strength of attractors (i.e., set points towards which states are drawn over time after deviation, with attractor strength reflecting the speed of this return) (Ong et al., 2025; Sosnowska et al., 2019; Wilt & Revelle, 2023). Such parameters are beginning to inform clinical and well-being interventions (Heshmati et al., 2024; Klocek et al., 2024). For example, Heshmati et al. (2025) the attractor landscape itself. For the former, an extraversion intervention could use behavioral activation to help participants move from recurrently showing withdrawn behavior towards a more sociable attractor state they were in before. The latter requires more intensive interventions (e.g., learning new social skills, setting goals to implement them, repeatedly enacting these behaviors across multiple weeks -such that they eventually coalesce into a novel attractor). Generally, if individuals have multiple attractors for a given state, considering the individual attractor landscape during intervention design should provide a more accurate and nuanced picture than focusing on the overall state mean.

In sum, multiple types of personality dynamics could fruitfully serve as targets of personality interventions. This does justice to the central role state-level processes play in theoretical models of personality change. It moves the focus of personality interventions beyond the trait self-concept towards currently neglected aspects of personality traits. While the primary focus here was on the direct value of changing a given dynamic as an aspect of a personality trait, specific dynamics could also be targeted to achieve potential downstream effects on the broader trait system, including other dynamics and the trait self-concept, in line with the working model outlined above. Thus, for instance, rather than attempting to change the trait self-concept directly, targeting relevant dynamics offers an alternative, complementary route. Overall, the inclusion of multiple dynamics as (additional) intervention targets has the potential to increase intervention effectiveness. To fully capitalize on this potential, this needs to be explicitly considered during intervention design. Specifically, intervention strategies should be chosen (or developed) based on which dynamics they are expected to alter, which will initially rely on conceptual reasoning (e.g., implementation intentions directly reflect situational contingencies) but should increasingly be informed by empirical evidence on changes in dynamics following specific strategies. Importantly, considering personality dynamics not only allows designing interventions that are more effective on average, but it also enables the personalization of interventions.

Opportunity 2: Dynamics for Personalization (Between Persons)

Various trends in psychology highlight the importance of considering personspecificity and heterogeneity (Beck & Jackson, 2020; Bryan et al., 2021; van Zyl & Dik, 2025; Wright & Woods, 2020). Accordingly, the personalization of interventions (i.e., tailoring them to their recipients) has been called for across fields, and this has become a central topic in personality psychology as well (Matz et al., 2024; Modersitzki et al., 2026).

Evidence from other fields is emerging that intervention effects vary across individuals (Kaiser et al., 2022) and that personalized interventions may outperform one-sizefits-all approaches (Joyal-Desmarais et al., 2022; Li et al., 2024; Nye et al., 2023; cf. Harnas et al., 2025). For personality interventions, work on the heterogeneity of responses is only beginning, and effects of personalization are largely unexplored. Initial evidence suggests that effects of personality change interventions differ across individuals (Hudson et al., 2019; Kroencke et al., 2026; Olaru et al., 2025). However, relatively little is known about the moderators of intervention effectiveness. This would be necessary for personalization: Only when we know which moderators predict the effectiveness of an intervention component can we, for instance, selectively include this component for individuals who are likely to benefit.

A review of interventions targeting neuroticism and related constructs found that commonly examined moderators, including demographics, psychological variables, and interventionspecific factors, often showed no significant effects (Wright et al., 2026). Intervention engagement seems to have the most robust positive effects (Hudson et al., 2019; Olaru et al., 2025; Wright et al., 2026), with some studies also finding effects of initial motivation to change and enjoyment of the intervention activities (Hudson et al., 2019; Olaru et al., 2025).

Given the relative scarcity of robust moderation effects, it would be fruitful to go beyond commonly examined moderators by also considering personality dynamics as potential moderators that could facilitate personalization (Matz et al., 2024; Ong et al., 2025).

The relevance of dynamics for personalization is particularly clear when the intervention also targets dynamics (see Opportunity 1). For instance, the effectiveness of neuroticism interventions targeting stress reactivity might be moderated by the baseline level of stress reactivity, such that individuals with higher initial reactivities benefit more from interventions targeting their reduction. Baseline dynamics could be used both to decide who receives a certain intervention (i.e., intervention dissemination) and to personalize intervention strategies (i.e., tailoring of intervention content) (Zettler & Strandsbjerg, 2025). Who receives a given personality intervention is currently largely based on participants' motivation (e.g., trait change goals; Haehner, Wright, et al., 2025; Stieger et al., 2021). Eligibility could complementarily be determined using a baseline assessment of relevant dynamics (e.g., a potentially maladaptive level of stress reactivity might be required for an intervention targeting stress reactivity). For the tailoring of intervention content, the baseline assessment of dynamics could be used to decide which of multiple intervention strategies are included for whom. For instance, in a sample with trait change goals for conscientiousness, it could be assessed which dynamics are most relevant for an individual (e.g., high mean-levels of impulsivity, short persistence of conscientious behavior, strong reactivity to distractors), which could then be targeted in personalized interventions.

In the following, we elaborate on the potential of dynamics for personalization, with prior work primarily coming from adjacent fields. Individual mean-levels of symptoms have been used as part of an algorithm that determines which modules to deliver in which order in personalized psychotherapy (Fisher & Boswell, 2016). Similarly, mean-levels of personality states could be used for personalization as an alternative to classical trait self-concept measures (Matz et al., 2024). For instance, mean-levels in different extraverted states (e.g., sociability, assertiveness) could inform what states an extraversion intervention should focus on for whom. In clinical settings, some metrics of affective variability and temporal dynamics may moderate intervention effects (Bosley et al., 2019; Husen et al., 2016), and personalization using such dynamics has been proposed (Ong et al., 2025). Future work should examine how state variability or temporal dynamics moderate personality intervention effects. For instance, a person consistently showing introverted behavior (low variability) and a person fluctuating between reclusive and more rare outgoing behavior (high variability) might benefit from different interventions (e.g., skill building to extend the behavioral repertoire vs. goal setting to express desired behavior more consistently).

Two dynamics are particularly relevant for personalization: state-state associations and situational contingencies. Regarding the former, parameters of network models have been examined as predictors of clinical treatment outcomes (Lutz et al., 2018). Further, initial work has proposed using state-state associations to identify the most relevant symptoms to intervene on (e.g., those with strong links to other symptoms), providing proof-of-concept for personalized therapy (Fisher & Boswell, 2016; Harnas et al., 2021). However, future research on using state-state associations to personalize interventions is needed due to the high data requirements (especially for complex network models; Mansueto et al., 2023) and the difficulty in identifying the most central states (Siepe et al., 2025). Nevertheless, given the heterogeneity in personality state-state associations (Beck & Jackson, 2020), they offer a high future potential to identify which states to intervene on for whom in personality interventions.

For instance, for extraversion, it could be assessed at baseline which specific aspects of state extraversion are most strongly linked to other extraverted states. For one person, being assertive in social interactions might predict subsequent energetic, talkative, and sociable states. In turn, for another person, being humorous might particularly predict multiple subsequent extraverted states. Thus, interventions could benefit from targeting assertiveness and humor for the first and second individual, respectively. Similarly, it could be assessed to what extent certain social behaviors are linked to momentary well-being. For instance, if an individual profits particularly from enacting sociable behavior, they should be encouraged to be sociable more frequently (goal setting), with resulting well-being benefits reinforcing state extraversion (positive reinforcement). In contrast, if an individual experiences low well-being when acting assertive, recommending more assertiveness could have negative side effects and it may be more appropriate to first try to disrupt this link as an intervention target.

Relatedly, situational contingencies (Kuper et al., 2022) offer a high potential for personalization since they represent the extent to which a certain situation can facilitate or hinder the expression of desired states. This has been applied in some clinical interventions.

For instance, to guide treatment of alcohol use disorder, individual high-risk situations triggering alcohol consumption have been assessed using the experience sampling method (ESM) (Litt et al., 2024). Moreover, personalized feedback on contingencies between contexts or activities and affect has been used in interventions for depression (e.g., showing participants during which types of everyday activities they reported the best mood in ESM; Kramer et al., 2014). Personality interventions often target situations, for instance, by requiring participants to create or select into situations in which desired states can be expressed (e.g., social situations for interpersonal behavioral activation; Hudson et al., 2019). Similar to clinical interventions, situational contingencies could be used to tailor such personality interventions (i.e., encouraging participants to enter situations that are particularly beneficial for them given their contingencies). Importantly, individual differences in effects of interventions targeting situations are conceptually closely connected to individual differences in contingencies. For instance, if an intervention fosters sociable behavior by increasing social interactions with peers, this intervention should be more effective in individuals who react with more sociable behavior to situations including peers (i.e., have a strong contingency at baseline). Thus, contingencies should be closer to the relevant processes underlying heterogeneity in treatment responses than classical trait self-concept measures. While more work examining moderation of personality interventions by closely aligned situational contingencies is deeply needed, contingencies may offer a versatile tool for personalization.

In sum, effects of personality interventions are likely heterogeneous across individuals. The resulting potential of personalized personality interventions is hindered by the fact that moderators remain poorly understood. We propose that it is beneficial to go beyond commonly considered moderators by also considering individual personality dynamics. This provides novel tailoring opportunities that are arguably closer to the relevant psychological processes, which should yield more effective personalized interventions.

Having described dynamics for personalization (between persons), we next discuss how personality interventions could additionally be tailored within persons by leveraging dynamics as momentary triggers of interventions.

Opportunity 3: Dynamics as Momentary Triggers of Interventions (Within Persons)

Dynamics can serve as triggers for adaptive interventions tailored to an individual's momentary state and context. This is commonly referred to as a just-in-time adaptive intervention (JITAI), which aims to deliver intervention components precisely when they are most needed and effective (Nahum-Shani et al., 2018; Spruijt-Metz & Nilsen, 2014). For example, relaxation reminders might be presented in an app when a smartwatch detects a heightened heart rate that does not result from physical activity. Work on JITAIs is surging, especially in health and clinical contexts, showing effectiveness for various outcomes. JITAIs can improve mental health (Versluis et al., 2016) or support behavior change (Hsu et al., 2025) and tend to outperform non-adaptive interventions (Wang & Miller, 2020).

Using adaptive interventions is similarly promising for personality interventions. For example, behavioral activation for extraversion may be most effective when an individual's state extraversion has been low for a certain period of time and when the opportunity to initiate social interactions is present. However, JITAIs to support volitional personality change have not yet been applied. Building on work from other fields, we outline in the following how dynamics can be used as momentary triggers in personality interventions.

The following six design elements of JITAIs have been distinguished: distal outcome, proximal outcome, intervention options, decision points, decision rules, and tailoring variables (Liu et al., 2023; Nahum-Shani et al., 2018), and personality dynamics could productively be incorporated into multiple of these elements. JITAIs aim to change a distal outcome by targeting proximal outcomes. In this context, relevant personality dynamics, being an aspect of the trait, could serve as the focal distal outcome (see Opportunity 1).

Alternatively, individual dynamics (e.g., a contingency between certain social situations and outgoing behavior) could serve as the proximal outcome (Haehner et al., 2026) that mediates changes in distal outcomes such as other personality dynamics (e.g., state extraversion meanlevel) or the trait self-concept. Intervention options are strategies used to elicit personality change (Wright et al., 2026), with a larger repertoire increasing the likelihood that strategies can be matched to an individual's state and context. At each decision point, the JITAI evaluates whether to deliver an intervention component, and decision rules specify which component to deliver based on assessed tailoring variables. Such tailoring variables can pertain to momentary states and contexts as well as temporal trends therein (e.g., seconds to days), ensuring that suitable intervention components are delivered at the optimal time point.

For instance, in the context of neuroticism interventions, low state well-being could trigger a reminder of previously learned emotion regulation strategies. Similarly, after low levels of state extraversion for an entire day, the intervention could trigger the suggestion to plan a new social event. Additionally, early warning signs might be detected, such as a continuous decrease in social interactions over a certain time period, which may indicate a tipping point towards social withdrawal. This could signal heightened susceptibility to change, yielding a critical window for intervention (van de Leemput et al., 2014). Social behavioral activation or emotional support could be particularly helpful at this time point to counteract the shift towards withdrawal. Since repeated surveys can be burdensome, researchers are increasingly using mobile sensing to trigger decisions (e.g., based on geolocation data; Zhu et al., 2024).

Decision rules could in principle be identical for everyone. However, they can also be based on individual tailoring variables, with personalized decision rules being a key advantage of JITAIs (Zhu et al., 2024). This can be based on different types of information, including demographic information, classical trait questionnaires, and, importantly, individual personality dynamics (see Opportunity 2). Micro-randomized trials (Liu et al., 2023) can help determine which intervention components are most effective for whom under which conditions. To give an example of decision rules tailored to an individual's personality dynamics, intervention strategies triggered by negative affect could be applied with a higher threshold for an individual with a high mean-level and strong variability to ensure that intervention delivery is not too frequent. Moreover, a strong situational contingency between work contexts and negative affect implies that preventive relaxation interventions delivered at work could be particularly helpful. For an individual with a strong inertia of negative affect after stressful events, interventions could be delivered repeatedly at multiple timepoints following the event. In turn, an individual with lower inertia but stronger initial reactions might benefit more from an intensive, directly delivered intervention.

In sum, dynamics could fruitfully be used as momentary triggers in JITAIs for volitional personality change, yielding interventions tailored within persons. Importantly, personality dynamics can further be beneficially incorporated into multiple additional elements of these JITAIs (e.g., proximal outcome, distal outcome, personalization of decision rules). Personality JITAIs have not yet been applied in practice and thus represent a promising opportunity for more effective personality interventions that remains to be leveraged.

Methodological Implementation

Drawing on personality dynamics in interventions yields certain methodological requirements on which we provide guidance in the following. While some of these requirements may appear demanding, the proposed approach can be adopted in a gradual manner depending on the focal opportunity, research question, and available resources.

Specifically, intensive longitudinal data collection such as ESM or passive mobile sensing needs to be incorporated into intervention studies. This has begun to be implemented in recent work (e.g., Haehner, Wright, et al., 2025; Stieger et al., 2021).

Personality interventions typically assess classical trait self-concept measures before the intervention, afterwards, and potentially at follow-up, sometimes complemented by other methods to assess traits (e.g., informant-report; Stieger et al., 2021). Different design aspects need to be added at a minimum to realize the proposed opportunities. To examine effects of randomized intervention groups on specific dynamics (Opportunity 1), intensive longitudinal assessments of relevant states (and potentially situational information) need to be implemented at least after the intervention. More fine-grained information could be obtained by also assessing dynamics before and during the intervention. For personalization based on dynamics (Opportunity 2), intensive longitudinal assessments are required at baseline. Finally, using dynamics as momentary triggers of interventions (Opportunity 3) requires assessments during the intervention period.

Different software solutions for such assessments exist. For example, the open-source software formR (Arslan et al., 2020) allows researchers to create individualized invitation schedules for ESM and to calculate indices of individual personality dynamics just-in-time using R code. This could be used to automatically assign participants to different interventions based on their dynamics (Opportunity 2) or to implement adaptive interventions (Opportunity 3). Similarly, the software m-Path (Mestdagh et al., 2023) facilitates customized app-based intervention studies which can be combined with both mobile sensing and ESM.

A critical question concerns the required number of assessments per participant. For studies examining intervention effects on targeted personality dynamics (Opportunity 1), the number of assessments is primarily an issue of statistical power to detect changes in dynamics, and fewer assessments per participant could often be counteracted by more participants. In turn, studies personalizing (adaptive) interventions based on individual dynamics (Opportunities 2 and 3) require reliable estimates of person-specific dynamics. For instance, if participants receive a different intervention depending on their baseline meanlevel in state extraversion, this mean-level needs to be estimated precisely. Depending on the type of dynamic and the magnitude of individual differences, large numbers of assessments are required. Wright, Scharf, and Zimmermann (2025) recommend at least 10, 30, and 100 assessments to reliably estimate individual mean-levels, variabilities, and within-person associations, respectively. Requirements for more complex models (e.g., person-specific networks; Mansueto et al., 2023) can be even higher. Thus, assessment numbers at the higher end for ESM studies (Wrzus & Neubauer, 2023) may be necessary. Complementarily, the continuous and passive nature of mobile sensing allows a much larger number of data points, yielding various opportunities for personality dynamics research (Kuper et al., 2026).

Statistical Analyses

Intensive longitudinal data collected alongside personality interventions can be analyzed using various statistical methods. For statistical approaches used in the personality dynamics literature, see Beck and Jackson (2021), Hamaker et al. (2023), Kuper et al. (2021), Kuper, Andresen, et al. (2025), and Rauthmann (2021). Commonly used analysis frameworks include, for example, multilevel models, (dynamic) structural equation models (SEM/DSEM), network models, dynamic systems approaches, and machine learning. To model effects of interventions on mean-levels, within-person variability, associations between personality states (contemporaneously or lagged), or situational contingencies, DSEM offers a high degree of flexibility (Hamaker et al., 2023). DSEM can estimate individual differences in various dynamics, as well as intervention-related changes therein (Opportunity 1). Generally, relevant models can be estimated, for instance, using various R packages (e.g., brms, lme4, mlVAR) or Mplus. For tutorials and accessible introductions, see, for instance, Brose et al. (2022 ), Hamaker et al. (2023), and Revelle and Wilt (2019). Important challenges remain, including (1) high model complexity, which may require simplification when analyzing several dynamics simultaneously; (2) high sample size requirements for complex models and sufficient power, which become increasingly feasible through scalable digital interventions; (3) limited within-person reliability of some state measures, which highlights the need for the (ongoing) psychometric refinement of state scales; and (4) assumptions that do not always hold (e.g., linearity, stationarity), which can be relaxed if supported by sufficient data.

For personalization based on dynamics (Opportunity 2), person-specific dynamics first need to be estimated. This can be done using idiographic models such as N = 1 time series analysis or by combining person-specific and population-level information such as extracted random effects from multilevel models (Kuper, Andresen, et al., 2025). Depending on the type of dynamic, a key challenge is obtaining enough assessments to allow precise parameter estimation and hence sufficient reliability (see Research Methods). To derive a suitable personalized treatment, algorithms combining information about multiple variables can be developed (Fisher & Boswell, 2016). For momentary triggers (Opportunity 3), real-time, automated data processing is required (Nahum-Shani et al., 2018). For instance, intervention components can be delivered when certain thresholds are surpassed (e.g., state anxiety > 7), potentially in conjunction with contextual factors (e.g., state anxiety > 7 and situation = social). Decision rules can vary in complexity and in the degree of tailoring to individual participants. To permit personalization, decision rules can be modified over time (e.g., using reinforcement learning; Liao et al., 2020; Liu et al., 2023; van Genugten et al., 2025).

Advantages, Limitations, and Future Directions Advantages

Integrating personality dynamics into personality interventions offers several important advantages. As outlined above, the three opportunities have the potential to increase treatment effectiveness. Notably, they are not mutually exclusive but can be productively combined (see examples above). Three further advantages should be noted. First, considering personality dynamics allows work on personality interventions to be more directly connected to theoretical approaches to personality traits, increasing theoretical coherence. In addition to our broad working model on the conceptual link between personality dynamics and personality traits (see above), specific dynamic theoretical approaches outline relevant psychological variables that could be considered during intervention design (e.g., socialcognitive variables such as goals, beliefs, and expectancies; Fleeson & Jayawickreme, 2025; individual differences during different behavior-/self-regulation phases; Geukes et al., 2018; Quirin et al., 2020). This includes important internal psychological states or processes central to a given trait that can be precursors of behavior and potential targets of intervention.

Second, the proposed approach can potentially increase positive downstream effects on relevant outcomes. It has been argued that personality interventions should affect various life outcomes (e.g., health, well-being, occupational success, social relationships; Bleidorn et al., 2019). In line with this, personality interventions have been found to increase satisfaction in some life domains related to the targeted trait (e.g., conscientiousness and work; Olaru et al., 2023). However, more systematic work on consequences of personality interventions for different life outcomes is needed (for a discussion, see Haehner, Asselmann, et al., 2025).

Such positive consequences should be enhanced when increasing the effectiveness of personality interventions through the integration of personality dynamics. When targeting dynamics (Opportunity 1), downstream effects on outcomes can occur directly or through changes in the broader trait system. If participants have relatively narrow change goals, our approach can help identify a specific relevant dynamic (e.g., a weak contingency between networking situations and sociable behavior) with direct downstream effects on a specific outcome (e.g., professional network size). In turn, to help participants achieve broader change goals (e.g., increasing extraversion), interventions can simultaneously target multiple dynamics. This can facilitate changes in the broader trait system that should generalize more across contexts, should be potentially self-reinforcing and hence more enduring, and should have downstream effects on a larger set of broader outcomes simultaneously (e.g., well-being, samples such that effective tailoring might not generalize to other populations (Matz et al., 2024). Only when combined with active efforts from researchers (e.g., explicit development of personalized interventions for underrepresented populations) does our approach promise to yield more inclusive and equitable personality interventions.

Limitations and Future Directions

Recently, some challenges and concerns regarding personality interventions have been noted (e.g., uncertain benefits, possible risks, societal implications; Haehner, Asselmann, et al., 2025). While the opportunities outlined above may address some issues (e.g., increasing effectiveness), more fundamental concerns remain (e.g., societal pressure to change, excessive self-optimization). This should be kept in mind by critically evaluating whether in a given population, for a given change goal, personality interventions or alternative intervention approaches are more appropriate (e.g., self-acceptance; Krämer et al., 2025).

A further limitation concerns the assessment of individual dynamics. Beyond the need for high numbers of assessments (see above), systematic work on the stability, structure, convergent and criterion validity, as well as the broad nomological network of different dynamics is only beginning. Especially for more complex dynamics going beyond the meanlevel and variability, links with classical trait or well-being measures are often small or absent (Kuper et al., 2022; Wendt et al., 2020). This could be interpreted such that individual dynamics capture aspects of personality traits that may not be well reflected in trait selfconcepts (Kuper et al., 2022). However, measurement limitations may contribute to this crosssectional finding, and it remains to be examined to what extent changes in which dynamics translate to trait self-concept change. A key future direction is the optimization of the assessment of personality dynamics, including methods other than momentary self-report (e.g., other-reported states: Kuper, Breil, et al., 2025; mobile sensing: Beierle et al., 2024).

The proposed approach is intentionally at a broad level of abstraction and should be combined with domain-specific theoretical work when developing new interventions. Such work can delineate which dynamics are relevant to a given trait (e.g., different narcissism contingencies; Kroencke et al., 2023). Existing trait taxonomies (e.g., Big Five, interpersonal circumplex), including their facet level (e.g., sociability, assertiveness), provide a potentially useful structure regarding which traits require separate dynamic theoretical accounts.

The integration of dynamics into interventions targets only some processes emphasized by theoretical approaches to personality change. Additional relevant processes, for instance, to change the trait self-concept, have been noted (e.g., reflective processes; Jackson & Wright, 2024; Wrzus & Roberts, 2017) and targeted by intervention strategies (e.g., psychoeducation, motivated reflection; Wright et al., 2026). The most effective interventions targeting the broad trait system should incorporate both dynamics and intervention components focusing on such additional factors (Allemand & Flückiger, 2017).

Lastly, research integrating personality interventions and dynamics is only beginning.

First studies have investigated changes in certain dynamics, especially mean-levels of states, following personality interventions (Allemand et al., 2024; Küchler et al., 2025; Olaru et al., 2025). However, it remains to be systematically examined to what extent certain intervention strategies lead to changes in other types of personality dynamics, how specific such effects are, and whether beneficial spill-over or unwanted side effects occur. Similarly, most prior work investigating personalization based on dynamics and dynamics as momentary triggers of interventions stems from other fields. Our approach thus builds on a solid theoretical foundation, initial work in personality psychology, and a larger literature from other fields. Now is the time to put it to the test and investigate its effectiveness.
