This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. Full text

Abstract

Phonological theories provide macroscopic descriptions of order in speech, but have not given much attention to how those descriptions might be derived from the relatively microscopic dynamics of the nervous system. This paper proposes a microscopically inspired theory of phonological organization in which the systems that govern articulatory actions (gesturals) are composed of internal subsystems (gestural excitations). In the preparation of speech, gestural excitations can transiently couple (phase-and frequency-lock) to a generic set of oscillatory coordination modes. In the execution of speech, a feedback-regulated competitive selection process gives coordination modes heightened activation, causing coupled gestural excitations to exert strong influences on control of the vocal tract. Phonological organization in speech preparation is conceptualized as a stochastic process in which gestural excitations couple to coordination modes, governed by probabilities derived from hypothesized constraints. The framework is used to interpret segmental, moraic, syllabic, metrical, and prosodic word-related patterns.

1 Introduction

The acoustic pressure field of human speech is highly ordered. For example, when an acoustic state associated with a consonantal sound occurs, our uncertainty about the acoustic state a tenth of a second later is reduced: the later state becomes more likely to be associated with a vocalic sound. In general, the speech production system produces far less information in its surroundings than the amount of information that it logically could produce. This can be readily observed in distributions of short-time spectral representations of the acoustic waveform, and in distributions of vocal tract states quantified through articulatory imaging. Limitations on information produced in speech are a consequence of not only mechanical constraints but also-and more interesting to phonologists-constraints on the cognitive system that interacts with the acoustic and articulatory environment. From this perspective, one aim of theoretical phonology is to understand how constraints on cognitive systems result in acoustic/articulatory order across a range of spatial and temporal scales.

This manuscript presents a novel model of a particular part of this system and its constraints, as an instantiation of mode-coupling theory. Mode-coupling theory proposes (i) that the systems responsible for controlling vocal tract states (gesturals) are composed internally of subsystems (gestural excitation modes), and (ii) that these internal subsystems can transiently couple to highly generic systems (coordination modes) via a frequency-adaptation mechanism. The theory makes use of several previously developed mechanisms to account for how the coupled states of these two types of systems drive movement.

In the remainder of this section, we elaborate on the motivation and aims of modecoupling theory. Section 2 presents the model in detail. Sections 3 and 4 provide interpretations of various phonological patterns and a general discussion. All code for simulations is documented in https://github.com/tilsen/mode-coupling, including detailed descriptions of the previously developed mechanisms (competitive selection and vocal tract field control), which are too lengthy to include in this manuscript.

1.1 Phonological theory as macroscopic description of pattern

From an information-theoretic perspective, phonological patterns are always associated with joint distributions of acoustic and/or articulatory environmental states that are more ordered (lower in entropy) than they logically need to be. Phonological theories construct vocabularies (systems of conceptual metaphors and image schemas) for understanding the causes of that orderedness, particularly those causes that are associated with processes in the human central nervous system, i.e. cognition. In order to usefully attribute causes, we need clarity on how to define the cognitive systems that give rise to environmental states, and we need to be clever about how we define the environmental states themselves. For current purposes, we refer to the collection of systems outside of the central nervous system (i.e. the acoustic pressure field; vocal organs; and the sensorimotor periphery) as the environment (or surroundings) of the cognitive system.

Imagine a very powerful observer, who continually and simultaneously records a detailed, high-dimensional record of the contribution of every human to their acoustic environment, along with a high-dimensional record of the articulatory/somatic environment, i.e., the mechanical state of the vocal system. The observer may observe a system state x t and at a later time x t+1 , or they may observe a state x associated with some particular sub-space and at the same time another state x associated with some other sub-space. For generality, we refer to states x and y to cover both the spatial and temporal cases. If we think of states x and y as random variables, then the information produced by observing x and y is equivalent to the reduction of uncertainty associated with the observation. We quantify this information as a reduction of entropy (Shannon, 1948): prior to the observation, uncertainty was H(x, y) =ij p(x i , y j ) log p(x i , y j ), i.e., the average value of the surprisal, -log p(x i , y j ). Subsequent to the observation, uncertainty is 0. Hence the information produced is the reduction in uncertainty, equivalent to the entropy before observation.

In the case where the observer has already observed x but not yet y, the information produced by observing y is the conditional entropy H(y|x), which is the expected value of the conditional surprisal, -log p(y|x) = log p(x) -log p(x, y). When x and y are statistically independent, the information produced by observing y (i.e. the reduction of conditional entropy) is maximized, because H(y|x) = H(y). In the dependent case, H(y|x) < H(y) and H(x, y) < H(x) + H(y), so less information is produced by observing y than in the independent case. The dependent circumstance is a form of pattern, and we can say there is order in the joint distribution p(x, y) in the sense that its entropy is lower than the highest entropy possible given its marginal distributions, H(x) + H(y). Put differently, patterns are always associated with a spatial and/or temporal statistical dependence of state variables. Many patterns are not under the purview of phonological theory. Let us imagine that the observer makes observations of x and y that are very proximal in space and time (e.g. the intralabial acoustic pressure field of a particular speaker, observed several milliseconds apart). There is a very large amount of order in the joint distribution of these states. But this orderedness is a consequence of mechanical constraints on changes in vocal tract state (conservation of energy and momentum, i.e., microscopic time-and space-translation symmetry applied to microscopic degrees of freedom). Such order is generally not the concern of phonology.

Instead let us imagine that the observer makes acoustic field observations 100 ms apart. Once again there is substantial order in the joint distribution of the state variables, but this order can no longer be attributed primarily to mechanical conservation laws. Given (i) that the acoustic field is determined by the geometry of the vocal tract and its aerodynamic pressure gradients, (ii) that vocal tract geometry is determined by movements of articulators resulting from muscle fiber contraction, (iii) that said contraction is governed by neurons in the brainstem motor nuclei, and (iv) these brainstem motorneurons interact with neurons in a variety of cortical and subcortical structures, it can plausibly be inferred that the central nervous system is responsible for the observed order.

Unfortunately, not only the environmental state but also the nervous system state is very high dimensional. We are limited in our ability to reason about patterns in such high-dimensional spaces, and particularly for the central nervous system, we are limited in our technological capacity to empirically observe and analyze the highdimensional state. Consequently, for the sake of reasoning about order, it is a practical necessity to construct macroscopic descriptions of the relevant states. The observer must strategically define the macroscopic state as a function of the microscopic one (or at least imagine that such a function exists).

In mainstream phonological theories, the environment is the acoustic field (Fig. 1 (a)), and the macroscopic environmental states are entities called segments (Fig. 1 (b)). The premise of segmental reasoning is that non-overlapping periods of time in the acoustic signal are instances of segment types, which have identifying properties. Most phonology students are at some point taught to temporally partition records of the acoustic field in this way through visual inspection of waveforms and spectrograms. It is not hard to imagine that there is a function from the microscopic acoustic field states to these types, but attempts to elaborate this function not have been very successful, and there are a number of reasons to suspect that the basic premise is misguided.

Figure 1: Phonological theories aim to account for how states of the central nervous system (c) give rise to order in the acoustic and articulatory surroundings (a, a ), defined macroscopically (b, b ). Object-based theories (d) and gestural theories (d ) are alternative macroscopic descriptions of central nervous system states.

Nonetheless, phonological theories have transferred segmental reasoning about the acoustic surroundings to a conceptual model of the cognitive system, where segments (or phones or phonemes) are object-like entities that contain features (which account for their identifying properties), and where segmental entities are related through a hierarchical structure of additional hypothetical entities (syllables, moras, feet, etc.), as shown in (Fig. 1 (d)). The transfer of segmental reasoning from analysis of the acoustic surroundings to a conceptual model of cognition is plausibly a consequence of the fact that most early linguistic theory was done by lexicographers using phonographic writing systems, along with the circumstance that scientific knowledge is predominantly communicated via the technology of writing itself. See Faber, 1992; Linell, 1988 Linell, , 2005;; Ong, 2013; Port and Leary, 2005 for similar critiques.

In an alternative form of phonological reasoning, gestural phonology, the environmental focus is the time-varying state of the vocal system (Fig. 1 (a )), analyzed macroscopically (Fig. 1 (b )) as states (often apertures) associated with vocal organs (lips, tongue, larynx, lungs, etc). In the gestural framework of Articulatory Phonology / Task Dynamics (Browman & Goldstein, 1986; Saltzman & Munhall, 1989), the hypothesized macroscopic cognitive systems are entities called gestures. When gestural systems transition to an active state, they change the equilibria of systems that control the vocal tract more directly, referred to here as vocal tract control systems. Macroscopic analyses in this framework often employ gestural activation intervals and coupling graphs (Fig. 1 (d )). Coupling graphs represent phase coupling between oscillatory systems that have been hypothesized to govern when gestures become ac-tive. The activation intervals indicate periods of time when gestural systems influence vocal tract control systems.

Hence in the status quo of phonological theory, there are primarily two approaches, with fundamentally different vocabularies for macroscopic description of the environment and the nervous system. Segmental/symbolic theories focus primarily on the acoustic environment and construct object-like entities to describe cognitive states; gestural theories focus primarily on the articulatory environment and describe cognitive states in terms of systems that drive movement. Critically, in neither approach has much attention been given to the function that derives the hypothesized cognitive states from the microscopic, high-dimensional state of the nervous system (Fig. 1 (c)). This lack of attention is quite understandable, because our current technological capacity to observe the nervous system state is limited. Nonetheless, it may be of value to speculate upon such derivation in the construction of the macroscopic description.

Any macroscopic description, however useful, can only withstand scrutiny as long as it is not shown to be microscopically incompatible: the extent to which the emergent macroscopic dynamics can be understood as the consequence of microscopic dynamics is an important criterion for the validity of the description. The swarming behavior of a flock of birds and the magnetization of bar of iron are not magic; these phenomena arise from the interactions of the individual sub-systems that comprise the whole, regardless of whether we understand the microscopic details of these subsystems and their interactions. In Marrian terms, computational and implementational descriptions are not independent, and our ultimate goal should be to relate them (Marr, 1982; Marr & Poggio, 1977).

1.2 Theoretical desiderata

From a biophysical perspective, a macroscopic theory of phonological organization should be consistent with, or at least should not be inconsistent with, our understanding of both the microscopic dynamics of the nervous system and the microscopic dynamics of acoustic/articulatory environment. Below we discuss some ways in which inconsistency can be avoided: Empiricality. First, the theory must aim for empirical adequacy, coverage, and unification. Its goal should be a macroscopic description of phonological cognition that provides mutually coherent mechanisms for understanding a wide range of empirical patterns in the environment. These include within-language regularities in the patterns of sound/action that occur contemporaneously (phonotactics), the range of possibilities for such patterns across languages or speech communities (typology), temporal variation in actions themselves, especially variation that is systematically predictable (articulatory timing), developmental changes in the organization of action (acquisition), and possibilities for atypical patterns arising from less probable state space trajectories (phonological errors).

Non-spatialization of time. Second, the theory should avoid metaphors for cognition in which (i) actions are objects and (ii) temporal order is a spatial arrangement of objects. Standard object-based representations such as those in Fig. 1 (d) entail that spatial arrangement (by convention, left-to-right order) maps to temporal order. These representations conceptualize variation in temporal patterning with operations on schemas of hierarchically arranged object-like phonological units (e.g. linking and de-linking, copying or deletion, spatial relocation, etc.). Schemas of this sort treat what should be viewed as state space trajectories as if they were states per se. Hence the schemas are somewhat incoherent as representations of state, despite being pervasively conceptualized as such.

Finiteness. The number of state space dimensions should be fixed, for a given speaker, on the typical timescales of utterances. The description cannot arbitrarily proliferate subsystems and corresponding state space dimensions, just when needed. In object-based structural representation of an action sequence in which some action is repeated, such as bilabial closure [p] in Fig. 1 (d), the representation effectively treats the second instance of [p] as if it is a separate entity from the first, implying that there must be an additional subset of state space dimensions for that instance. This begs the question of how the duplication of state space dimensions translates to a state of the nervous system. No one would claim that the neural populations that drive motor actions are "copied" whenever a sequence of actions contains identical elements, and yet, this is effectively what the schematic/symbolic representation implies, because it allows the same entity to occur in multiple places in the schema. The ad-hoc replication of entities has been called the problem of multiplicity in previous work (Tilsen, 2019c) and has been discussed as a matter of type vs. token representation (Jordan, 1997). We should require that cognitive states be states of systems (types) not trajectories of system states, i.e. the temporal outputs of systems (tokens).

Memorylessness (first-order Markovian dynamics).

In a Markovian model the probability of the system being in a particular state at a particular time depends on prior states over a finite period of time, i.e. P (X t+1 | X t , . . . , X t-k ) does not depend on states before X t-k . In a first-order Markovian model, the next state depends only on the current state, i.e. P (X t+1 | X t , X t-1 , . . .) = P (X t+1 | X t ); for higher order Markovian models, the system can be said to have a "memory" of past states. All fundamental physical dynamical laws are first-order Markovian with appropriate definition of state, but macroscopic/coarse-grained laws need not be. Whenever a model of a physical system possesses memory, it is because the macroscopic description has been constructed so as to relocate some microscopic degrees of freedom to an ad hoc subspace-memory.

A more physically appropriate coarse-graining strategy in such circumstances is to model memory as a slower timescale macroscopic dynamic of the system. To give an example, the compression that occurs when one lays their head on a memory foam pillow might appear to depend on its history, but not because the state of the pillow includes a record of past states. Several additional state variables, such as the configuration of the polymer network, temperature, and air density, evolve more slowly than the load applied by a head. The "memory" that we refer to when describing cognitive systems is not fundamentally different. What appears to be dependence on past states (i.e. memory) is more coherently understood as a dependence of particular focused sub-system states on ignored sub-systems with relatively slow dynamics. Specifically, much of the "memory" associated with lexical knowledge can be understood as coarse-grained description of synaptic efficacy and neuronal connectivity, which are convenient to model on slower timescales.

Non-autonomous dynamics. The nervous system, a far-from-equilibrium open system, interacts strongly with its surroundings. First and foremost of these is the metabolism that provides energy (in the form of electrochemical gradients and molecular configurations) for cycles of neuronal polarization and depolarization. On larger spatial and temporal scales, order in environmental acoustic fields-speech produced by other speakers-is another hefty influence. Whenever we choose to model a particular subsystem of the universe (our focused system), we make an analytical choice to simplify our model of the complement of that subsystem, i.e. the remainder of the universe (the surroundings).

Short of ignoring the surroundings entirely, we may attempt to incorporate some of its influences on the focused system as time-dependent terms in the dynamical law for the focused system. Abstractly, we might start with ẋ = f (x, t), as a practical necessity. Later, we might expand the scope of the model, i.e. ẋ = f (x, y), ẏ = g(y, t), relocating time-dependence (i.e. non-autonomy) to a different, typically smaller portion of the universe. All physical systems, except perhaps the universe as a whole, are inevitably non-autonomous in this sense. For an ideal gas in perfectly rigid, perfectly insulated box, no matter or energy crosses the boundary between the box and its surroundings; in real boxes, however, walls are neither perfectly rigid nor perfectly insulating: some energy and matter will eventually transfer between the system and its surroundings. The "box" of the cognitive system has little insulation and is hardly rigid: metabolic and other surroundings influences are fast and strong. Macroscopic descriptions should therefore embrace non-autonomy by allowing themselves to incorporate influences from exogenous systems.

Microscopic compatibility.

Theoretical descriptions of speech production should not be incompatible with our current understanding of the nervous system. They should not preclude the construction of a bridge between the fine-grained spatial and temporal scales of analysis that are relevant to describing the nervous system and the coarse-grained scales that are more useful for describing phonological patterns. This desideratum is often disregarded in contemporary theories by appealing to the primacy of a "computational" level of description, as discussed by Marr (1982) and Marr and Poggio (1977). Such appeals misinterpret Marr, who, despite emphasizing the importance of the computational level, also repeatedly advocated for integrating the computational (relatively macroscopic) level with lower-level (relatively microscopic) algorithmic and implementational ones (see Tilsen, 2019c, pp. 264-271 for extended discussion and examples).

Compatibility relates to the philosophical issue of strong vs. weak emergence. Relatively macroscopic patterns can be viewed to emerge from microscopic ones, where emerge connotes that the macroscopic dynamics are readily deducible/derivable from the microscopic dynamics. In strong emergence, the disconnect between scales is viewed as an ontological necessity: macroscopic behavior is a priori not reducible to microscopic behavior. Alternatively, weak emergence recognizes that our inability to derive the macro from the micro is due to our ignorance of the latter, and ultimately the complexity thereof. Strong emergence is philosophically untenable in my opinion: it creates a dual space for analysis of causes, one that will never be accountable to what can be physically observed. Weak emergence, in contrast, allows for the possibility that, with diminished ignorance in the future, we could ultimately derive phonological patterns from neural ones.

What is not possible at the current time is to relate neural and cognitive scales of analysis in an empirically testable way-in many cases we can provide only speculative ideas regarding how macroscopic systems may be microscopically derived. Because these are often phenomenologically motivated, there is inherent subjectivity in whether they are plausible. Nonetheless, it is preferable to have some idea in mind (however vague that may be) of how the the derivation could be accomplished. The desideratum of compatibility is in effect a meta-rationale for physically motivated desiderata: non-spatialization of time, finiteness, memorylessness, and nonautonomy. These are corollaries of the insistence that a macroscopic theory should not be aphysical, because the nervous system is unarguably a physical system.

1.3 Heuristics of mode-coupling theory

Mode-coupling theory is an attempt to lay the groundwork for a bridge between microscopic and macroscopic descriptions, to demystify emergence. The first premise of the theory is that some of the order associated with speech is a consequence of constraints on system states/state trajectories, rather than operations on structures of objects. Spatial relations play a very different role, compared to the segmental/symbolic vocabulary: they never encode temporal patterns by using the metaphor that a temporal order of events is a spatial arrangement of objects.

The second premise is that, microscopically, the relevant systems are populations of neurons (most likely in posterior premotor cortex, but also interacting with populations in other cortical areas and with various subcortical systems). Moreover, these populations exhibit collective oscillations that motivate a lower-dimensional, macroscopic description of interactions based on phase-/frequency-locking (coupling of oscillatory systems). Here these systems are called gesturals. The term is intended to evoke the concept of a gesture, first developed in the theoretical frameworks of Task dynamics and Articulatory phonology (Browman & Goldstein, 1986; Kelso et al., 1986).

The third premise is that gesturals, through their interactions with neural populations in other areas, govern the vocal tract system state. Gesturals are differentiated on the basis of cortical topographies-the tonotopy of auditory cortex and the somatotopy of primary motor and somatosensory cortices, both of which are consequences of phylogenetic and ontogenetic characteristics of the human species. The premises regarding collective oscillation and a topographic basis for differentiation are somewhat loosely empirically supported in specific application to articulatory control, but in general the premises are well-established and it is not hard to envision how future advances in neural imaging will make disconfirmation possible.

The last premise, perhaps the most radical one, is that gesturals can be analyzed as comprised of internal subsystems-gestural excitation modes-whose frequencies can adapt to the frequencies of host systems called coordination modes or µ-systems. Mode-coupling theory holds that some of the environmental information produced by motor action and its concomitant acoustic order can be usefully understood as an indirect consequence of constraints on how gestural excitation modes couple to coordination modes. In effect, coordination modes make available a finite set of state space attractors for gestural excitations, and hence are a large source of phonological order.

Before presenting a specific macroscopic model, let us motivate the phenomenology of mode-coupling theory by considering how its macroscopic concepts might be grounded microscopically-in other words, we address how the heuristic abstractions of the theory conform to the desideratum of microscopic compatibility.

Somatotopic organization.

Assume that primary motor cortex is somatotopically organized such that there are subpopulations of neurons associated with geometrically/functionally defined vocal tract state dimensions (Fig. 2 (b)), such as lip aperture, tongue tip constriction degree/location, tongue body constriction degree/location, velum aperture, glottal aperture, etc. Similarly, assume that there is a spatial organization of subpopulations in primary somatosensory cortex that maps in a highly systematic way to the vocal tract state-related organization in primary motor cortex. There is plenty of evidence that gross somatotopies exist in these areas, along with some evidence that more specific speech articulation-related somatotopies exist (Bouchard et al., 2013; Chartier et al., 2018; Conant et al., 2014; Conant et al., 2018). Exactly how these somatotopies are instantiated and what macroscopic description is most appropriate for them have yet to be determined. Relatedly, various studies of saccadic and manual motor control have suggested that targets in the visual field and peripersonal space are reflected in topographically organized neural populations (Graziano et al., 2002; Robinson, 1972).

Somatotopy in primary motor and somatosensory cortices motivates a macroscopic scalar field model (Fig. 2 (b )). The activation at each field coordinate is microscopically a short-term integration of layer V pyramidal depolarization-rate in a local region of the population. The activation field determines the current equilibrium of some dimension of the vocal tract state; microscopically this is due to synaptic projections from primary motor cortex to brainstem motorneuron nuclei that control muscle tension. The centroid of activation (green square) in the field serves as the control target for the corresponding vocal tract state dimension.

Gestural influences on tract fields. Gesturals are macroscopic descriptions of the states of premotor cortical neural populations, which are differentiated on the basis of their patterns of synaptic projection to neurons in primary motor and somatosensory cortices. Macroscopically, this entails that each gestural is associated with some particular vocal tract state control field(s), and that gesturals can excite and inhibit activation in different regions of those fields. For example, in Fig. 2 (a ) the gesturals [LA clo] and [LA op] provide different spatial distributions of excitatory and inhibitory input to the LA (lip aperture) control field: [LA clo] excites the region of the field that corresponds to bilabial closure and inhibits the region that corresponds to an open posture; vice versa for [LA op].

Collective oscillation. Gesturals are assumed to have the ability to transition to and from an active regime in which they exhibit relatively high-amplitude oscillations and an inactive regime in which their oscillation amplitudes are negligible. Microscopically these oscillations are collective oscillations of neuronal populations. There is a large body of empirical and theoretical work that supports this assumption (Buzsáki & Draguhn, 2004; Buzsáki & Wang, 2012; Singer, 2018; Wang, 2010; Wilson & Cowan, 1972) and that relates transient oscillatory activity to working memory and/or motor preparation/execution (Churchland et al., 2012; Feingold et al., 2015; Lundqvist et al., 2016). The transition to the collective oscillation regime is driven by external input and is possible because the individual neurons and/or cortical microcircuits that comprise the population exhibit a bifurcation in their dynamics between a fixed point (polarization) and a limit-cycle attractor (cycles of polarization and depolarization; Brunel, 2000; Izhikevich, 2007; Stiefel and Ermentrout, 2016).

Internal excitation modes of gesturals. One novel premise of mode-coupling theory is that gestural states can be viewed as a superposition of oscillations of a discrete set of relatively independent subsystems, referred to as internal excitation modes, or gestural excitations.

A loose physical analogy for gestural excitations is the vibration of a circular membrane, such as a drumhead. Fig. 3 (i) schematically illustrates various oscillatory modes of membrane vibration, which are characterized by the number of nodal diameters (m) and number of nodal circles (n). Nodes, where membrane displacement is zero, are shown as radial and circular lines. The presence/absence of shading indicates regions of the membrane that are above/below zero displacement in a snapshot of the vibration, and the numeric values show the relative frequency of the vibrational mode. In an idealized membrane the modes are orthogonal and evolve independently, but in real drums there are factors that couple them weakly. The distribution of energy across modes depends on the external excitation of the membrane. A skilled drummer can control the tone of a strike by varying the strike location, area, and force, and can damp modes by selectively touching the membrane. In real drums, the set of possible modes is effectively finite, because the continuum description of the membrane breaks down as wavelengths approach the molecular/microstructural scale, and more practically, because higher-frequency modes are more strongly damped and require more energy to excite to a given amplitude.

The key mappings of the analogy between membranes and gesturals are: (i) the availability of relatively independent (weakly coupled) modes that correspond to periodic components of a state space trajectory; (ii) the allowance for an external system to distribute energy in different ways to different modes; and (iii) effective finiteness of modes due to physical constraints. The macroscopic state of a gestural is likewise characterized as a finite set of excitable modes, each of which has a time-varying amplitude, frequency, and phase. The drummers who excite the internal modes are external systems associated with concepts/meaning. This hypothesis is phenomenologically motivated by the problem of multiplicity: relatively independent sub-systems of gesturals are necessary to account for how it is possible that the same action may occur multiple times in the production of a form.

Regarding a microscopic basis for the existence of gestural excitations, one possibility relates to the notion that multiple cyclic polychronous groups may co-exist within a population of neurons. A polychronous group (Izhikevich, 2006; Vertes & Duke, 2010) is a set of neurons that exhibit a time-locked pattern of spikes, with the specific pattern being a consequence of (1) the topology of synaptic interaction between neurons, (2) their status as excitatory or inhibitory, and (3) axonal conduction delays. A cyclic polychronous group is one that repeats its spike pattern periodically.

A simplified illustration of two polychronous groups is shown in Fig. 3 (ii), where excitatory synapses (directed graph edges) are labeled with their weight and conduction delay. In this toy example, external input to neuron e or f results in periodic spike patterns with different frequencies, as shown in Fig. 3 (iii,iii ). Notice that the sets of neurons involved in each group overlap: neurons a and b are involved in both groups, and spikes of both a and b are required to depolarize c and d, but due to differences in conduction delays, the specific temporal pattern of a and b spikes results in phase and/or frequency differences in the cyclic pattern. Simulations in Izhikevich (2006) showed that many polychronous groups can emerge from a population of excitatory and inhibitory neurons whose synaptic weights evolve according to a spike-timing dependent plasticity learning rule (i.e., Hebbian learning), starting from an initial condition with random weights, connectivity, and delays.

Polychronous groups are a viable neural mechanism for gestural excitation for several reasons. First, the neurons that comprise a particular polychronous group are very often members of multiple such groups, so there is no need to view distinct groups as spatially distinct. Second, different groups within a population can be associated with different periods/frequencies of pattern repetition. Consequently, we can speculate that excitable modes of gesturals are associated with different but overlapping subsets of polychronous groups within the population-not unlike a bank of oscillators. As for why the neurons of a gestural population would exhibit this sort of internal organization, a plausible hypothesis is that longer-timescale dynamics of learning adapt gesturals to interact with another type of system: coordination modes.

Coordination modes.

Another novel premise of mode-coupling theory is that there is a domain-general but nonetheless highly constrained system for the coordination and sequencing of actions. This system is distinct from gesturals, and it is instantiated in cortical-subcortical circuits-most likely cortico-basal ganglia-thalamic loops and the (pre-)supplementary motor area (Guenther & Hickok, 2016; Liu et al., 2025). This system is hypothesized to consist of pairs of sub-systems (coordination modes, or µ-systems) that are distinguished on the basis of their sign and level. The level distinction of µ-systems is presumed to map to a discrete set of frequencies in the beta-band range of oscillation (approximately 13-30 Hz), which has been implicated in the preparation of motor control (Engel & Fries, 2010; Kilavik et al., 2013). By convention the "highest" level is the one with the highest frequency and is depicted in the highest vertical position in schemas, but this level is given the lowest index. The sign distinction indicates whether a µ-system is coupled in-phase (+) or out of phase (-) to the oscillation associated with its level. {µ + , µ -} pairs are viewed as an emergent behavior of cortical-subcortical circuits, rather than being neural populations localized to a particular area of the central nervous system. The phenomenological motivation for coordination modes is that they provide a mechanism for actions to be flexibly coordinated and/or sequenced without requiring direct interactions between the systems that govern action.

Fig. 4 shows how coupling of gestural excitations to coordination modes allows for flexibility in coordination and sequencing. Think of a word form (e.g. poppy) as a sequence of three groups of articulatory actions, {Pa}{P}{i}, where "P" refers to distinct excitations of a bilabial closure gestural ([LA clo]) and [a] and [i] refer to excitations of vocalic gesturals. Note that bilabial closure is associated with two separate groups. The word meaning itself is associated with an external system (a conceptal, see below) that activates the internal excitations of gesturals to varying degrees, as in Fig. 4 (a). The mode-coupling model described below couples gestural excitations to coordination modes (µ m ), the result of which is termed a configuration, shown in Fig. 4 (b). Typically more highly active gestural excitations will couple to higher-level coordination modes, but there are a variety of additional constraints the formation of such configurations.

Competitive selection and feedback. Sequencing is accomplished via a mechanism of competitive selection (Tilsen, 2013), which is a modification of competitive queuing (Bohland et al., 2010; Grossberg, 1978 Grossberg, , 1987;; Houghton & Hartley, 1995). There is a variety of behavioral evidence for this mechanism (Dell, 1986; Rhodes et al., 2004; Sternberg et al., 1988; Sternberg et al., 1978) and some neural evidence (Averbeck et al., 2002; Kornysheva et al., 2019; Silver et al., 2012). Competitive selection dynamics are shown in Fig. 4 (c). In competitive selection, feedback and gating systems result in a series of partially overlapping temporal epochs in which heightened activation is given to a particular coordination mode, and thereby to the gestural excitations that are coupled to it. Starting from an initial pattern of activation that corresponds to µ-system levels and signs (i), the most highly active µ-system exceeds the selection threshold first (ii) and its corresponding gestural excitation modes are given heightened activation with phase-dependent constraints. Feedback systems induce degating of the next mode (iii) and suppression of the initially selected µ-system (iv). Selection and suppression cycles recur until all such systems have been selected and suppressed. The timeseries of vocal tract control fields in this example is shown in Fig. 4 (d). Importantly, in competitive selection, action sequencing derives entirely from the application of change rules to system states. The conceptual advantage of this is that regularity in the temporal order of action arises indirectly from the evolution of activation states, rather than being information that is conceptualized as temporal order per se.

There are important consequences to whether the feedback systems involved in suppression and gating are external (involving peripheral sensorimotor systems and basal ganglia-cortico-thalamic circuits) or internal (involving predictive forward models most likely instantiated with cortico-cerebellar circuits; Tanaka et al., 2020; Wolpert et al., 1998). The crucial difference is that internal feedback does not interact with the surroundings (on utterance timescales) and thus is well-suited for generating predictive feedback states. This in turn allows suppression and degating to be partly dissociated. Looking at a variety of empirical patterns, Tilsen (2016) argued that in early speech development, children control action sequencing primarily with external feedback, and as they acquire more reliable predictive feedback models, competitive selection comes to rely more on internal feedback systems. Theoretical and experimental work supports the hypothesis that speakers can intentionally control speech rate by adjusting their reliance on internal vs. external feedback (Tilsen, 2022 (Tilsen, , 2024)), with internal feedback reliance resulting in faster speech and greater overlap of articulatory actions.

Conceptal activation.

The concept-related systems referenced above are typically associated with meanings or ad-hoc action intentions-and hence referred to as conceptals. Conceptals are presumed to be distinguishable on the basis of their interactions with sensory systems, motor systems, and other conceptals. This paper will not discuss the dynamics of conceptals in any detail, but it is supposed that, like gesturals, they are neural populations predisposed to collective oscillation, likely distributed in prefrontal and multimodal association areas, and that what we experience as meaning corresponds to transiently stationary periodic state trajectories of conceptals (see Tilsen, 2019c). Here we will assume that conceptals, like coordination modes, are selected in the course of generating speech, and so doing, they activate gestural excitation modes. The patterns of gestural activation associated with conceptals are encoded in long-term memories of word forms/concepts; microscopically these are factors contributing to synaptic efficacy of projections from neurons in conceptal populations to neurons in gestural populations.

2 Model implementation

The model discussed here is comprised of three primary mechanisms: (i) mode coupling, (ii) mode selection, and (iii) vocal tract state control via field activation. Vocal tract state control has been described elsewhere (Kim & Tilsen, 2025; Tilsen, 2018 Tilsen, , 2019a) ) and is an extension of the standard Task Dynamics model (Saltzman & Munhall, 1989). Mode selection is a revision of competitive queuing (see Section 1.3; Bullock, 2004; Grossberg, 1987; Tilsen, 2013 Tilsen, , 2022) ) that incorporates internal and external feedback to govern the gating and suppression of coordination modes. Because vocal tract state control and mode selection are not the primary focus of this paper, detailed descriptions of these are provided in supplementary material. The mode-coupling mechanism presented below is a novel contribution, refining and elaborating on ideas described in Tilsen (2019c).

2.1 Mode coupling

Assume a set C of conceptals. When a speaker produces or subvocally simulates speech, a subset of these become simultaneously active above a threshold (i.e., selected). A conceptal selection epoch is a period of time in which some conceptal(s) are above a selection threshold; during such periods the influences of the selected conceptals on gesturals are assumed to remain constant.

Assume a set G of gesturals g i with i ∈ 1, . . . , n g . Each gestural is comprised of distinct subsystems-internal excitation modes. We index all gestural excitations (gsystems) with a single index, writing e j for excitation j, and define g(j) ∈ G as its parent gestural system. Each gestural excitation is associated with an [r, θ, f ] (amplitude, phase, frequency) tuplet.

Assume a set M of coordination modes µ m (or, µ-systems). A coordination mode can also be identified by its level and sign as µ s ℓ , with ℓ ∈ 1, . . . , n ℓ and s ∈ {+, -}; thus m identifies an individual mode, whereas ℓ identifies a mode pair. s m ∈ {+1, -1} is the sign of mode m.

In a given conceptal selection epoch, selected conceptals increase the amplitudes of gestural excitations they are associated with. We refer to this as conceptalgestural activation and assume that activation values are determined by long-term memory/learning mechanisms. In other words, the component of lexical memory that corresponds to phonological form is a vector of scalar values in the space of gestural excitation amplitudes.

Gestural excitation states subsequently evolve to minimize a free energy, subject to constraints on their interactions with µ-systems and other g-systems. The coupling of a gestural excitation to a coordination mode is assumed to involve a rapid phase-and frequency-locking of the g-system to a µ-system. Rather than modeling the fast temporal dynamics of coupling, we adopt a discrete, coarse-grained description in which the system state-a mode-coupling configuration (or, g-µ configuration)-encodes (i) which g-systems are coupled to which µ-systems, (ii) the phases of g-systems (relative to the µ-system to which they are coupled), and (iii) the amplitudes of g-and µ-systems. Free energy is defined as a function of this state. Section 4 elaborates whether this energy can be interpreted as physical.

In order for the evolution of the system to result in a configuration that is phenomenologically compatible with empirical observations of articulatory patterns, conceptal-gestural activation must be located in a subregion of the gestural amplitude subspace in which relative activations of gestural excitations predispose them to interact with µ-systems in an appropriate way. In effect, conceptal-gestural activation imposes additional constraints on g-µ state trajectories, which ultimately constrains how actions are temporalized (via mode selection and vocal tract control). Crucially, conceptal-gestural activation is not per se a specification of temporal order; instead, it represents the amplitudes of internal excitation modes of gesturals when a conceptal is selected.

Here a fairly simple algorithm was used to determine conceptal-gestural activation, based on the gestural composition and timing patterns expected from a conventional linear phone/segmental analysis of the form associated with a conceptal. A phone-gesture map was constructed for this purpose. Specifically, the algorithm works as follows: (i) set the phone sequence index p = 1, and initialize an n g × n e-modes array E of zeros with rows corresponding to gesturals and columns to gestural excitation modes, where n e-modes is the number of internal excitation modes available for gesturals. (ii) For each gestural associated with phone p, assign the value 1 -p-1 np to the first zero column of the array in the row corresponding to that gestural. (iii) Increment p. Repeat steps (ii) and (iii) until p > n p , where n p is the number of phones in the linear segmental analysis. Note that for complex segments (e.g. affricates), the narrowing gestural is assigned a value halfway between that of phone p and phone p + 1, so that it is less active than the closure gestural. Some examples of conceptalgestural activation patterns generated by this algorithm are shown in Fig. 5.

It is critical to emphasize that mode-coupling theory does not presuppose that the nervous system implements the above algorithm; the algorithm is merely a heuristic. Conceptal-gestural activation must be learned and refined over the course of development, via microscale synaptic modulation/growth processes. Thus even though the above algorithm makes use of a linear ordering of segments for convenience, the theory makes no presupposition that a linear order of segments be encoded in memory.

Rather, what conceptal memories encode is merely a pattern of activation of gestural excitations. It seems likely that these patterns could be learned and encoded in the weights of a multi-layer neural network, but this has yet to be verified.

Given conceptal-gestural activation values, mode-coupling couples gestural excitations to coordination modes. To show how this works, we first consider a quasideterministic dynamics for the coupling process, in which the next state is always the state that represents the largest reduction in energy relative to the current state.

In the deterministic model of a system state trajectory, there is one configurational change in each iteration, and that change is always the one associated with the largest reduction in energy. The allowable changes are one of two kinds: (i) coupling, in which a previously uncoupled gestural excitation couples to any one of the coordination modes; or (ii) demotion, in which all of the excitations that occupy one orbit of a mode (i.e. that share its µ-system and relative phase) move together to the next mode in the sequence µ + 1 , µ - 1 , µ + 2 , µ - 2 , . . ., so that an orbit of µ + ℓ moves to µ - ℓ and an orbit of µ - ℓ moves to µ + ℓ+1 . Gestural properties. Fig. 8 shows the classification of gesturals adopted in the model. All gesturals (and their internal excitations) are hypothesized to belong to one of two types, independent (i-type) or dependent (d-type). Within i-type gesturals, there are two classes: V-class (vocalic; involving lingual articulatory gestures associated with vowel postures), and C-class (consonantal; involving lingual, labial, or glottal consonantal constriction gestures). Within d-type gesturals, there are four classes: N-class (nasal; associated with opening/closing the velum), G-class (glottal voicing; associated with abducting/adducting the vocal folds), L-class (laryngeal elevation; associated with raising/lowering the larynx, which occurs in ejective/implosive sounds), and R-class (labial rounding/retraction). Note that [GLO nar] and [GLO clo] are Cclass, being associated with [h] and [ʔ], respectively, while [GLO ab] and [GLO ad] are G-class.

Types and classes play a large role in the calculation of configuration energy. Type/class hypotheses are ultimately phenomenologically motivated: we adopt them because doing so facilitates an empirically adequate characterization of phonological patterns. Nonetheless, there are independent rationales for the classification. Regarding gestural types, the distinction between i-type (independent) and d-type (dependent) gesturals is motivated by the observation that the articulatory actions associated with d-type gesturals never (or perhaps, rarely) occur in the absence of actions associated with i-type gesturals. In energy calculations (detailed below), this distinction is manifested as a cost for coupling d-type gestural excitations to µ-system orbits without a corresponding i-type gestural excitation. All V-and C-class gesturals are i-type, whereas all N/G/L/R-class gesturals are d-type.

Regarding gestural classes, vowel sounds (products of V-class gesturals) always involve a relatively unconstricted lingual posture. This is not the case for consonants. Hence there are somatotopic/functional differences between V-class and C-class gesturals, manifested in their influences on motoric and somatosensory fields. Within d-type gesturals, sub-classes of N (nasal), G (glottal voicing), L (laryngeal elevation), and R (lip protrusion/retraction) are also somatotopically motivated: the sensorimo-tor neural fields that these classes of gesturals project to differ.

In general, V-class gesturals are more constrained in their abilities to couple to µ-systems than C-class gesturals: each {+} mode should contain exactly one nucleic V-class excitation (nucleus occupation), V-class activations set the activation floor of their mode (frequency adaptation), and their preferences for coupling to {+} or {-} modes are encoded in long term memory. This latter property is implemented as sign property of V-class gesturals, and in effect it holds that each vocalic gesture may be associated with two gesturals, one that is nucleic (preferring to couple to {+} modes), and one that is non-nucleic (preferring to couple to {-} modes). The differences between V-and C-class gesturals can be interpreted as a difference in the asymmetry of interactions between these gesturals and coordination modes: vocalic gesturals are likely to have stronger influences on µ-systems than consonantal ones do. In other words, interactions with µ-systems are less asymmetric for V-class than C-class gesturals.

Coordination modes and gestural phasing. Coordination modes (µ-systems) are distinguished according to their sign (i.e. µ + ℓ vs. µ - ℓ ) and their level (ℓ = 1, . . . , n ℓ ). For convenience we refer to {+} and {-} modes when level is not relevant. The sign distinction has a number of consequences for configuration energy. In general, exclusion effects obtain only between gestural excitations that occupy the same orbit (i.e., ones that are coupled to the same µ-system with the same relative phase). For all coupled g-systems, relative phase is defined as ϕ j = θ j -θ µ m(j) , where θ j is the absolute phase of excitation e j , θ µ m is the absolute phase of coordination mode µ m , and m(j) indexes the mode to which excitation e j is coupled. The phase difference between excitations is ∆θ jk = θ j -θ k . The ϕ values of all the gestural excitations coupled to a particular coordination mode are derived from solutions to a system of phase-coupled oscillators, and these values are updated at each iteration of the mode-coupling process. Hence not all atypical configurations are necessarily expected to be observed in the form of speech errors.

Regarding circumstances where the stochastic dynamics allow for multiple configurations that are viewed as standard (and hence different vocal tract state trajectories that are not interpreted as "errors"), the model can predict differences in the relative frequencies of these variants. It should be possible to test model predictions regarding these relative frequencies of configuration variants, but this is complicated by the fact that those frequencies are determined by relative weights of terms in the configuration energy, along with temperature. Additional challenges to this endeavor involve the reliability of empirical data and the fact that relative likelihoods of variants are likely vary as a function of speech rate and other exogenous factors.

3 Interpretation of phonological patterns

There are multiple scales of cognitive and environmental system dynamics responsible for patterns, and accordingly, multiple scales on which patterns can be identified. The order-creation directly associated with mode coupling occurs on a relatively fast timescale within the cognitive system. Based on studies of prepared speech initiation latency (Sternberg et al., 1988; Sternberg et al., 1978), where we can assume that lexical access/retrieval processes are relatively uninvolved, the minimal time for a configuration to stabilize is plausibly around 50-100 ms, with about 20 ms per additional syllable (i.e. mode pair). Hence mode-coupling itself is a fairly rapid and localized process.

Many phonological patterns may be only indirectly associated with mode-coupling because they depend on relatively larger-scale mechanisms: learning, social interaction, and language experience. In a broader analysis of phonological patterns (Fig. 12), each cognitive system has an acoustic, somatic, and visual surroundings. Information regarding the somatic surroundings of any given cognitive system is not directly available to the other cognitive systems. Acoustic environmental information, on the other hand, is prototypically accessible both to the speaker who produced it, and to other speakers nearby. And yet, gestural systems influence their somatic surroundings more directly than the acoustic surroundings. This leads to the following challenge for language learners: to communicate through speech, they must learn conceptal-gestural activation (a CG in Fig. 12) that indirectly results in self-generated acoustic environmental patterns that are similar to patterns created by other speakers.

Microscopically this learning is plausibly a matter of tuning synaptic efficacy of projections from neural populations associated with sensorimotor experiences (conceptals) to premotor populations associated with articulatory actions (gesturals). The acoustic environmental order that cognitive systems encounter is compared against the order they create, and differences between the two drive changes in a CG . This is effectively a longer timescale feedback loop that constrains the system. Crucially, the process depends on there being sufficient regularity in the mapping between gestural organization and acoustic patterns, so that the comparison of self-generated acoustic order and other-generated order can be used to tune a CG.

From this perspective, mode-coupling can be viewed as regularizing mechanism, functioning to oppose perturbative mechanisms that can hinder the acquisition of normative conceptal-gestural activation. One of these is the increase in articulatory overlap that results from reliance on internal feedback for mode selection. When mode selection operates with internal feedback, degating and suppression processes are potentially dissociated. This allows for greater overlap of articulatory movements, increasing the rate of information transmission. But the downside of overlap is it makes acoustic order less readily associated with articulatory actions themselves. In other words, with too much overlap, the actions of a speaker may not be perceptually recoverable by listeners (Chitoran et al., 2002). Other perturbative mechanisms include aerodynamic effects, biomechanical interactions between articulators, and nonlinearity in auditory transduction of acoustic information-all of which can obscure the mapping from acoustic order to articulatory action.

In the absence of the constraints that mode-coupling imposes on speech production, there would be much less order in the articulatory and acoustic environment, and this presumably would make the task of acquiring normative speech patterns more difficult. Many diachronic sound changes have been analyzed as a form of hypocorrection, where learners reinterpret perturbative effects as intentional (Ohala, 1993). Crucial to this theory of sound change is a mechanism that prevents all perturbative effects from being reinterpreted in this way. Mode-coupling is a viable candidate for this mechanism because it constrains the conceptal-gestural activation patterns that can result in stable configurations, thereby serving as regularizing force.

Below we briefly discuss various forms of organization that have played important roles in conventional, segmental/symbolic phonological theories. In each case we consider how mode-coupling theory can inform our understanding of the relevant organization.

3.1 Segmental organization

In symbolic phonological theories, speech is conceptualized as a temporal sequence of segments (or phones, or phonemes; generically, units). These are metaphorically object-like entities arranged in a linear space that maps to a temporal order. Segmental reasoning thereby applies a logic of spatial/temporal exclusion: two distinct units on the same horizontal plane cannot occupy the same space, and vertical connections between units conform to transitivity of ordering (hence association lines cannot cross). In some theories, segmental units are also understood as containers of features, atomic elements that specify properties of sounds (Chomsky & Halle, 1968; Jakobson et al., 1951). Most features refer to characteristics of articulatory actions associated with sounds, although some refer to acoustic characteristics or abstract properties. Alternatively, segments have been conceptualized as nodes of a hierarchical structure that dominate a graph structure of features (i.e., a connected objects structure; Clements, 1985).

The empirical problems with these theoretical constructs are far too numerous to discuss in detail here (see Tilsen, 2016). Many phoneticians (who tend to be more skeptical of phonological abstractions) have questioned the reality of segments (Browman & Goldstein, 1990; Ladefoged, 1984 Ladefoged, , 2001;; Ohala, 1992; Port & Leary, 2005). Non-literate speakers and speakers in non-literature cultures perform worse than literate speakers on various phonemic awareness tasks (Morais, 2021; Morais & Kolinsky, 1994), suggesting that the hypothetical construct of speech segments (and phones, and phonemes) are artifacts of the relatively recent technological innovation of writing, rather than entities that are fundamental to phonological cognition. Likewise, language games in which segmental substitutions or transpositions occur are only found in literature cultures, suggesting that such patterns are mediated by orthophonographic mappings. The subset of speech errors that are commonly interpreted as substitutions or transpositions of segments can be alternatively interpreted as substitutions/transpositions of multiple features, and thus do not constitute evidence of segmental units. On top of this, it is suspicious that no phonological research has uncovered evidence for phonological patterns in which a count of phonemes or segments is necessary to describe the pattern-if segments were cognitively real units of organization, the most basic patterns one might expect to observe might involve counting.

The (somewhat questionable) utility of the hypothetical construct of a segment derives from the observation that somatotopically dissociated articulatory actions tend to co-occur in speech. For consonants, this co-occurrence involves (i) an action (or actions) that form a posture in the oral cavity (V-and C-class) and (ii) actions that govern the state of the velo-pharyngeal valve (N-class), the vibratory state of the vocal folds (G-class), protrusion/retraction of the lips (R-class), and somewhat less commonly, the elevation of the larynx (L-class). Mode-coupling theory accounts for these co-occurrences by positing that the former are i-type gesturals and the latter are d-type gesturals, subject to a dependency constraint. The dependency constraint plausibly derives from the fact that d-type gesturals are either unable to produce their prototypical acoustic effects in the absence of such co-occurrence, or that their prototypical environmental effects are transparently present or absent. The somatotopic systematicity of action co-occurrence that has been pervasively associated with "segments" or "phones" follows from this constraint, rather than the existence of segmental entities.

Furthermore, segmental theories have needed to distinguish between segments that are simplex and those that are complex/contour segments, because the latterwhich typically involve multiple oral constrictions with asynchronous movement initiation (e.g. affricates [t͡ ʃ], liquids ([l] and [ɹ]), diphthongs [ai], vowels with offglides [ei], etc.)-are not amenable to the common object-schematic metaphor that phonemes are containers of uniquely-specified identifying properties (i.e. features). In mode-coupling theory, the distinction is unnecessary because there is no presupposition that i-type gesturals coupled to the same mode should form a coherent entity in the first place. Indeed, the theory readily accounts for the empirically observed asynchrony of movement initiation: exclusion disfavors multiple oral constriction or vocalic gestural excitations from occupying the same orbit. As shown in Fig. 13, the [TT clo] and [Tb nar] gesturals of the affricate (a) and the [TD clo] and [TB j] gesturals of the palatal on-glide (b) differ in phase; in contrast, the d-type labial protrusion [LP round] of a labialized stop can occupy that same orbit as a C-class gestural. Diphthongs (d), long vowels (e), and vowels with off-glides (f) all involve i-type gesturals coupled to different modes, and hence exhibit initiation asynchronies due to they competitive dynamics of mode selection. Furthermore, some classes of complex segments are markedly different depending on how they are organized within the syllable. For example, the English lateral /l/ in coda position is typically produced with asynchronous tongue dorsum and lateral tongue tip gestures (in the model, a (-)-sign [ʌ] and [TT l]), but pre-vocalically only the lateral gesture occurs. The asynchrony is ultimately a consequence of exclusion constraints. From the perspective of mode-coupling theory, the absence of compelling evidence for segmental organization is expected. The prevalence of segments in traditional theories is a consequence of the fact that alphabetic writing systems are used for scientific communication, and this has led mainstream theories to reify graphemes as cognitive entities. Segments may be useful to a limited extent, but only because the constraints that govern mode-coupling apply in somatotopically motivated ways.

3.2 Moraic and syllabic organization

Object-based phonological theories have employed a variety of models of the internal structure of syllables, examples of which are shown in Fig. 14 (a-d). One of the most popular is (a) the onset-rime model (Selkirk, 1982), in which syllable nodes branch to onset and rime subconstituents, and the rime branches to a nucleus and coda. Some theories adopt the body-coda model (b) instead (Chen, 2011; Vennemann, 2011). Moraic models (c, d) are a common alternative, with variants in which onset nodes are strictly layered (Hyman, 1985) or may link to syllable nodes (Hayes, 1989). The notion that there is internal structure to syllables has also been disputed (Pierrehumbert & Nair, 1995). For the sake of parsimony, what is needed is a theory that unifies our understanding of the phenomena that these various models have been designed to capture. The questions to ask are thus (i) why is it the case that syllables as hypothetical constructs are useful for describing phonological patterns, and (ii) how does mode-coupling theory conceptualize various phenomena that are described with subconstituent and moraic models? In mainstream phonological reasoning, syllables are defined by a sonority peakthere is a particular segment in the syllable, often but not necessarily a vowel, that is the most "sonorous" one (roughly the loudest), and this most sonorous segment is often preceded and/or followed by less sonorous segments. Consequently, (C -3 (C -2 (C -1 )) V(C 1 (C 2 (C 3 ))) are possible syllables, but VCV is not. In general the sonority of C |n+1| is greater than C |n| : sonority tends to rise across prevocalic consonants and to fall across postvocalic consonants.

In mode-coupling theory sonority profile tendencies are epiphenomenal: the presence of a sonority peak associated with a vowel is a simple consequence of nucleus occupation: a {+/-} mode pair without a V-class gestural coupled to a {+} mode incurs a high energy cost, and coupling more than one V-class gestural to {+} is also costly. For diphthongs with a V-class gestural excitations coupled to both {+} and {-} modes, either V-class gestural may be the most sonorous, or they may be of equal sonority for long vowels. With mode coupling, there is no need to explain away apparent deviations, such as prevocalic sonority falls (e.g. fricative-stop sequences in spy, stew, etc.) or postvocalic sonority rises (e.g. stop-fricative sequences in cats, naps, etc.).

Phonological patterns that are traditionally analyzed in terms of syllabic organization arise from the circumstance that {+} and {-} coordination modes are grouped in pairs. Intuitions that speakers have regarding the number of syllables in a word form thereby correspond to the number of {+/-} pairs in its configuration. Syllabic organization of segments is generally held not to be encoded in long term memory, based on the observation that no languages appear to contrast words based on syllabification alone (Blevins, 1995). This suggests that syllable count intuitions require at least a motor simulation in which a gestural configuration is generated. For most word forms, speakers have the same count intuitions, but notably in English (and most likely in other languages) there are some forms for which intuitions vary between speakers or are inconsistent. Specifically, for word forms with a tense vowel or diphthong and liquid (r, l) coda such as peel, hire, fool, file, etc. some people have the intuition that these words are a single syllable, others that they are two syllables, and still others express uncertainty (Lavoie & Cohn, 1999; Tilsen & Cohn, 2016). In a standard interpretation, the variation can be viewed as in Fig. 15: the liquid segment may be syllabified (a) as a coda, or (b) as the nucleus of a syllable. In mode-coupling, the distinction amounts to whether the [ʌ] gestural is associated with {-} or {+} sign: in the latter case, coupling to a second {+} mode is energetically favorable. The onset-rime model (Fig. 14 (a)) has been motivated by a number of empirical observations. First, the rime corresponds to a subset of intuitions regarding rhyming, i.e. so-called perfect rhymes (i.e. cat rhymes with hat but not cot or cad). This has been taken as evidence that the nucleus and coda form a unit (i.e. the rime) that speakers can assess for identity, to the exclusion of the onset. However, this particular intuition regarding rhyming is not cross-linguistically universal, and for multisyllabic forms with non-final stress, English rhyming intuitions incorporate the rime of a stressed syllable and all following segments. Thus table rhymes with fable but not staple. The language-specificity of rhyming and its extended scope in multisyllabic forms suggest that rhyming intuitions are not derived from an organizational pattern per se but rather are learned through experience of acoustic similarity in rhyming verse.

Second, there are a number of phonotactic asymmetries that correspond to onsetrime and nucleus-coda structural relations. For one, phonotactic prohibitions are more common between nuclei and coda (i.e. within the rime) than between onsets and nuclei. In English for instance there are many phonotactic constraints that apply to rimes, such as prohibitions against syllables with complex nuclei and non-coronal complex codas (*/aimp/, */aiŋk/ vs. /ɪmp/), or prohibitions against monosyllabic words without a branching rime or nucleus (*/bɪ/ vs. /bɪt, bai/). In contrast, onset-nucleus phonotactic restrictions (the body unit of Fig. 14 (b)) are much less common across languages. These asymmetries in nucleus-coda vs. onset-nucleus combinatorics derive from a fundamental difference in how the gesturals associated with these units are organized. The gesturals of a body (onset and nucleus) are typically coupled to the same {+} mode, where exclusion is less restrictive: C-class gesturals repulsively phase-couple to one another but not with a V-class gestural. In contrast, some of the gesturals of a rime (nucleus and coda) are coupled to {-}, where all i-type gesturals, C-and V-class alike, are repulsively phase-coupled. All things being equal, this results in fewer available configurations for {-} modes.

Third, across languages it is observed that a single prevocalic segment (i.e. onset) is never prohibited but in some cases post-vocalic segments (codas) are restricted (e.g. Hawaiian has no codas and Japanese allows only nasal codas). As shown in Fig. 16, implicational hierarchies of syllable shapes inferred from studies of crosslinguistic typology (Zec, 2007) can be related to the energies of their configurations. All languages allow V and CV shapes, which are the two lowest-energy configurations. In contrast, some languages disallow VX (i.e. VV or VC), while others require VX (see discussion of minimal word requirements below). Implicational relations (arrows in Fig. 16 follow from the hypothesis that whenever a given orbit is allowed, lowerenergy configurations that lack the orbit are also allowed. Other sorts of phonological patterns are more readily described by moraic models (Fig. 14 (c, d)), which provide a notion of syllable "weight" by distinguishing between monomoraic ("light") syllables and bimoraic ("heavy") syllables. Patterns that have been analyzed with moraic structure include minimal word requirements: content words must be comprised of at least two moras, either a single heavy syllable or two light syllables; quantity sensitive stress: heavy syllables must have stress; and contour tone restrictions: contour tones (i.e. lexical tones that involve two or more F0 targets) can only occur on heavy syllables.

Not all languages exhibit weight-related patterns, but in those that do, moraic structural analyses posit that a long vowel or diphthong is linked to two moras within the syllable, as in Fig. 17 (a), rendering it heavy. In some languages, coda consonants are appear to contribute to weight (b), in others, they do not (b ). This variation in coda-weight can also be consonant-specific and can depend on position in word, with word-final syllables often differing from non-final ones. Minimal word requirements are satisfied by heavy monosyllables such as (a) or (b), or by disyllables (c), but are violated by light monosyllabic words (e). For example, in English heavy syllable content words like pie /pai/ or pea /pi:/ (CVV) and pit /pɪt/ (CVC) are common, as are disyllabic words like pity (CVCV), but monosyllabic words with a lax vowel like */pɪ/ or */pε/ (CV) are rare and mostly restricted to truncations or function words/discourse markers.

In mode-coupling theory syllable weight is reinterpreted in terms of gestural coupling to a {-}, but whether this manifests as weight can depend on the classes of those gesturals. In cases where codas are moraic, both V-and C-class gesturals coupled to {-} count for weight (as in Fig. 17 (b)). In languages where codas are nonmoraic, only V-class gesturals count (Fig. 17 (b )). Minimal word requirements can in both cases be interpreted as requirements that words must involve the selection of at least two modes: these are either the {+} and {-} modes of a heavy syllable, or two {+} modes, as in Fig. 17 (c). Similarly, contour tone restrictions are patterns in which a sequence of tone gestures must be associated with a {+/-} mode pair in which the {-} mode is occupied by an appropriate gestural. Such patterns can be viewed as the consequence of an exclusion principle between tone gestures along with language-specific variation in the dependency of tone gestures: in languages where tones are d-type gesturals, it follows that {-} must be occupied by some other gestural, hence the restriction.

Some moraic analyses also posit trimoraic ("superheavy") syllables, as in Fig. 17 (d). However, such analyses are often motivated only by the assumption that some particular consonants are necessarily moraic when post-vocalic, and it is not clear that there are phonological patterns that motivate a distinction between trimoraic and bimoraic syllables. Indeed, purportedly trimoraic syllables are often restricted to be word-final (Hall, 2000). This pattern follows from the relatively high energetic cost of an unbound gestural excitation in combination with an absence of a lowerlevel {+} mode: with no such mode present, word-final gesturals must couple to the lowest {-}, even if the configuration is energetically disfavored.

A fundamental concern in object-based theories is how segments associate with syllables. An often-invoked general explanation is the maximal onset principle, which holds that there is a preference for associating intervocalic consonants as onsets rather than codas. In the absence of other factors, this results in the structure of Fig. 18 (a), i.e. (C)V.CV rather than (C)VC.V. However, it has been argued in some languages, in order to satisfy a constraint that stressed syllables be heavy, the in- tervocalic consonant is ambisyllabic-associated both with the coda of the preceding syllable and the onset of the following one, as in Fig. 18 (b). Mode-coupling theory interprets this as the consequence of coupling the intervocalic consonantal gestural to {-} ℓ rather than {+} ℓ + 1 ; articulatory studies generally support this interpretation. Relatedly, intervocalic geminate consonants are hypothesized to involve coupling of distinct excitations of a consonantal gestural to both {-} ℓ and {+} ℓ + 1 (Fig. 18 (c)). Thus mode-coupling offers an alternative analysis of syllabification patterns, without employing object connection metaphors. From a mode-coupling perspective, syllables are useful for describing phonological patterns because they correspond to {+/-} mode pairs, and moras are useful because they correspond to individual {+} and {-} modes. Cross-linguistic variation in the extent to which syllables or moras are appropriate for describing phonological patterns, rather than being a consequence of a configurational differences, may derive from differences in mode selection. Specifically, recall that internal feedback control allows for degating of µ m+1 before suppression of µ m . Moraic vs. syllabic bias amounts to whether this asynchrony differs between modes within a level {+} ℓ {-} ℓ and between levels {-} ℓ {+} ℓ + 1 . In the current model implementation this asynchrony is set by degating thresholds, the feedback level of the selected mode at which the next mode is released: λ -for transitions within a level and λ + for transitions between levels, with a lower threshold giving greater asynchrony (the defaults, λ -= 0.42 and λ + = 0.20, make transitions between levels the more asynchronous). For moraic bias, degating and suppression are similarly asynchronous. For syllabic bias, degating and suppression are relatively more asynchronous within levels than between levels; consequently syllabic-bias languages exhibit greater overlap within syllables than between them.

3.3 Metrical organization

In many languages, syllables in multisyllabic word forms are analyzed with a structural property called stress, and stress patterns appear to reflect an abstract grouping of syllables in units called feet, a so-called metrical organization in which stressed and unstressed syllables tend to alternate. Syllable stress is often asserted to be associated with acoustic properties of greater acoustic intensity, longer duration, and more extreme pitch movement, along with vowel reduction. However, the only crosslinguistically reliable diagnostic for stress is the ability for a syllable to bear pitch accent (see Tilsen, 2019b for discussion of stress diagnostics in Hayes, 1995). Theories of metrical organization are complicated by the fact that in addition to having a default pattern of stress that is systematically predictable from the number of syllables in a word form (sometimes called fixed stress), some languages also allow for stress to be lexically specified (called free stress) and/or conditioned on the moraic composition of syllables (quantity sensitivity, or "weight-based stress").

Recall from the description of metrical activation dynamics that when only an endinput is present, the default stress pattern is anchored to the lowest-level mode pair, and when only a begin-input is present, the default pattern is anchored to the highestlevel pair. When the end-input is positive/negative, the pattern is iambic/trochaic; the reverse holds for begin-input-these patterns are a consequence of the inhibitory interactions between syllables on adjacent levels. The end-/begin-input parameters thereby allow for the system to capture a range of patterns observed in metrical typology (see Hayes, 1995). For English, which has a default metrical pattern of trochees from word end, I Quantity sensitivity arises from internal input (I µ -). This input increases the activation of a σ-system when its associated {-} mode is occupied. Hence in Fig. 19 (c), the occupation of µ - 1 results in internal input to σ 1 , leading to positive activation of σ 1 . This variety of secondary stress notably arises in the course of the mode-coupling process, rather than being pre-determined.

The interplay between metrical activation and mode coupling can also work in the other direction, where metrical activation creates biases on configurations. For instance, intervocalic consonantal gestures are favored to couple to µ - ℓ rather than µ + ℓ+1 when σ ℓ has sufficient metrical activation. This is a consequence of binding energy being a function of both mode level and metrical activation. Thus intervocalic consonantal gestural excitations couple to the stressed syllables of banana and bananarama, exhibiting an ambisyllabic pattern. Although occupied lowest-level {-} modes contribute internal input like all occupied {-} modes, in languages like English a negative end-input (I e = -1) outweighs it (2/3). This relates to a pattern called extrametricality/extraprosodicity, in which the final syllables of words do not induce stress, regardless of whether they are heavy (e.g. donor, tonal). Another influence on stress patterns is lexical input, as with banal in Fig. 19 (d): here the iambic stress pattern arises due to additional information in the conceptal-gestural activation. In the current model this effect is implemented by associating a particular V-class gestural excitation with an abstract "accentual gesture". As shown for banal, presence of this accentual gesture in an orbit of a µ + 2 substantially augments the activation of σ 2 and thereby suppresses activation of σ 1.

3.4 Prosodic word organization

Beyond metrical organization, various theories have argued for a higher-level unit of phonological organization, called a prosodic word (or phonological word, often symbolized with ω). However, unlike the patterns of organization discussed above (segmental, moraic, syllabic, and metrical), prosodic word organization is generally acknowledged to be quite flexible. For this reason, the prosodic word has been argued by some to be an emergent (as opposed to universal) form of organization (Schiering et al., 2010). In mode-coupling theory, prosodic word organization arises from constraints on conceptal configurations. These constraints limit the number of conceptals that can be simultaneously selected, and this in turn indirectly limits the number of gestural excitations that can occur in a configuration.

Indeed, many of the same principles that govern gestural configurations likely apply to conceptual-syntactic organization. This idea was discussed extensively in Tilsen (2019c). The basic premise is conceptal systems are activated by influences from their surroundings and from other conceptals. In preparing to speak, conceptal systems couple to syntactic coordination modes. Once the syntactic-conceptal configuration has stabilized, competitive selection of syntactic coordination modes results in a series of gestural configurations (prosodic words). One important consequence of this is that morphophonological patterns must occur within a prosodic word (i.e. within a gestural configuration).

For example, consider an utterance such as the taxi is here. The conceptals associated with this utterance can be organized for selection in a variety of ways, shown in Fig. 20. In (a), the definite determiner [def] and [taxi] are selected together, while the verbal conceptal [be] is selected with the predicate adjective [here]. In (b), [be] is selected with [def] and [taxi]. Note that in these two cases, the gestural excitations of [be] differ: in the latter, it is a contraction. Morphophonological patterns of this sort result from modulation of conceptal-gestural activation conditioned on conceptualsyntactic configurations: the activation associated with [be] differs depending on the conceptals it is co-selected with. Conceptal-gestural activation commonly varies in this way for grammatical conceptals such as those involving number (singular vs. plural) and person (1 st , 2 nd , or 3 rd ; e.g. I am, we/you/they are). When a set of conceptal systems is selected, they induce the formation of gestural configurations through mode coupling. Gestural excitations associated with conceptals that are not in the currently selected set generally do not couple to coordination modes. Once the gestural configuration stabilizes, mode selection sequentially heightens gestural influences on vocal tract control fields. In the next conceptualsyntactic epoch, a subsequent set of conceptals is selected, the activation vectors associated with those conceptals give rise to a new gestural configuration and a new vocal tract state trajectory, and so on. Speakers can communicate pragmatic information, such as emphatic or contrastive focus, by organizing conceptals in different ways. For example, the utterance in (c) THE taxi is here, associates focus with the determiner by organizing it into a distinct set; likewise in (d) the taxi IS here, focus is on the verb. It is beyond the scope of this paper to examine principles of conceptualsyntactic organization in detail, but an important hypothesis is that many of the same factors involved in gestural organization via mode-coupling operate in conceptal organization (the parallel domains hypothesis; Tilsen, 2019c). The key point is that prosodic word organization is flexible because it reflects how conceptals are organized for selection.

3.5 Temporal locality

In standard phonological reasoning, a distinction is often drawn between local vs. non-local phonological patterns. This distinction is fundamentally a metaphoric one, based on the image-schematic presupposition that phonological organization is a spa-tial arrangement of objects, where linear space corresponds to time. Patterns involving objects (often segmental units) that are not spatially adjacent are described as non-local. In order to impose locality where segmental schemas do not naturally allow for it, a number of approaches have proposed separate tiers for separate types of segments or for individual phonological features (Goldsmith, 1976; Heinz et al., 2011). Tiers are metaphorically parallel spatial coordinates. The motivation for this is patently the experiential intuition that only when entities are proximal in space can they interact.

It is generally held that there are two types of patterns that are apparently non-local: spreading harmonies and agreement harmonies (Hansson, 2001; Rose & Walker, 2011; Tilsen, 2019a). In spreading harmonies, some articulatory gesture appears to become highly active substantially earlier than otherwise expected (anticipatory or right-to-left R→L spreading), or alternatively remains highly active substantially later than otherwise expected (referred to as perseveratory or L→R spreading; see Gafos, 1999). For example, in some languages, nasality will spread in an anticipatory direction, so that the velum is lowered during sounds that precede a nasal consonant. This pattern always occurs within the domain of a prosodic word or is morpho-phonologically restricted, and it may be either a lexicalized characteristic of word forms, or arise when morphosyntactic combinations provide a suitable context.

Note that these intragestural interactions have not yet been incorporated into the computational model, but they are nonetheless a reasonable consequence of the polychronous groups heuristic, whereby modes with distinct frequency and/or phase are microscopically associated with overlapping subpopulations of neurons. As shown in Fig. 21 (d), when a given conceptal (here the suffix associated with the form /na/) activates an internal excitation of a [VEL op] gestural, the active excitation could induce additional internal excitations of [VEL op] to become active and couple to other modes. Hence the [VEL op] excitations associated with spreading arise not directly from conceptal activation but from within-gestural interactions between modes. For empirical adequacy, it seems sufficient to constrain these intragestural inductions so that induced excitations are on the same level as the source excitation or a level adjacent to the source excitation. Induction to higher/lower levels leads to anticipatory/perseveratory spreading, respectively.

Spreading harmonies can also be "blocked", such that the presence of a segment appears to halt the spreading. For example, blocking occurs in /basa+na/ → [basãnã] due to the presence of the alveolar fricative [s]. The blocking pattern is likely a consequence of a phonotactic constraint that prohibits fricative gesturals from being nasalized. The phonotactic constraint itself derives from perceptual recoverability considerations: the oral pressure gradient required for generating perceptually salient turbulent airflow (i.e. a fricative sound) is compromised by venting of oral pressure through an open velo-pharyngeal valve ([VEL op]).

In segmental/featural terms, agreement harmonies differ from spreading in that segments intervening between the source and the harmonizing segments do not exhibit the articulatory correlates of the relevant feature. Furthermore, there are often constraints on featural properties of the harmonizing segment itself (e.g., stop consonants but not fricative consonants will harmonize). In the nasal consonant agreement harmony of Fig. 21 (c), only the consonantal gestural of the higher-level mode pair is nasalized. The same intragestural excitation induction mechanism that operates in spreading harmonies can account for this, with the additional stipulation that most of the induced excitations fail to couple to orbits between the source and target of the harmony-induced excitations will only couple to orbits occupied by some member of a particular subset of i-type gesturals. Thus in agreement patterns, excitations are induced with the same mechanism as in spreading, but only some of those excitations couple to µ-systems.

4 Discussion and conclusion

Mode coupling theory aims to provide a vocabulary for macroscopic description of phonological pattern and analysis of order. It adopts a biophysical approach, meaning that its vocabulary coheres with vocabularies used in physical and biological reasoning. To accomplish this, it employs concepts from statistical physics to conceptualize both (i) how macroscopic states may be derived from microscopic ones, and (ii) how those states evolve in time. This is manifested in the act of partitioning the universe into cognitive systems and their surroundings; in further partitioning these into interacting subsystems; in construction of system state spaces; in specification of change rules that govern systems (as reduction of free energy); and in interactions that comprise those change rules. These constructions are motivated to some extent from knowledge of the biological organism, particularly knowledge of (or speculation about) the organization and dynamics of its nervous system.

At the same time, we have used information theory to frame the empirical focus of mode-coupling theory as pattern, i.e., statistical dependence associated with reduced entropy. A natural question to ask is whether the physical and information vocabularies are compatible: is information equivalent to some coarse-grained form of negative entropy, or in other words, is information physical? A variety of perspectives exist on this general question (Jaynes, 1957; Landauer, 1991; Parrondo et al., 2015; Sethna, 2021). In mode-coupling theory specifically it is relevant to question whether the free energy of the model is merely metaphorically "energy" or whether it should be interpreted physically; and likewise, to ask whether the order of interest is associated with a physically defined entropy. The physical connections are tenuous but not unimaginable, by reasoning as follows.

Suppose that the second law applies on the scale of an individual neuron. Any entropy reduction we can locally define on this scale will be accompanied by a greater increase of entropy in its surroundings (the complement of the local system). The "state" we define for a neuron (the local system) is necessarily a strategically coarse-grained macroscopic one. The second law extrapolation is possible because the coarse-graining by design ensures that directions of entropy change are preserved across scales. The next most reasonable extrapolation of the second law is to a population of neurons, where interactions between neurons must be accounted for. Suppose again that any local order creation that we can define on this scale is accompanied by a greater destruction of order. We again increase our scale of analysis and renormalize so that the system description preserves entropic principles. At this point it would be strange to decide that no further scale transformation is possible. Hence we can entertain the notion that there exists a macroscopic description of the cognitive state that conforms to the second law, because that description has been derived through recursive application of scale transformation with normalization. The same logic can be applied to obtain a free energy whose changes are consistent with those of our macroscopic entropy (in other words, ∆F = ∆E -T ∆S changes in the same direction across scales). Thus whether the "free energy" of the theory is physical depends on whether recursively applied transformations of this sort exist. If so, then it seems reasonable to draw parallels between how organisms make use of energy flows to create and maintain order within themselves and how conceptals/gesturals make use of metabolic energy flows to transition to highly ordered oscillatory states.

To assess mode-coupling theory, let us consider the biophysical desiderata of Section 1.2. Of these, neural compatibility is the most challenging to achieve, mostly because the nervous system is a high-dimensional complex system and because our capacities for observing and analyzing it are so limited. The motivations for the construction of interacting oscillatory systems-gesturals, their internal excitations, and coordination modes-are speculative but nonetheless motivated by the prevalence of oscillatory dynamics in the brain. Furthermore, the terms of configuration energy are motivated with varying degrees of speculation by reasoning about how those systems interact.

A notable shortcoming of the model presented here is that weights of configuration energy terms were optimized against a relatively moderate set of configurations. This can plausibly be addressed by fitting the weights of configuration energy terms to a wider range of word forms. A more empirically oriented approach is ultimately desirable, in which weights are optimized by evaluating the vocal tract states they generate against empirical data. This endeavor is complicated by the fact that additional parameters associated with mode selection and vocal tract control would need to be jointly optimized with the weights of configuration energy terms. Moreover, the specific formulation of energy terms can be interrogated in revised. The current model adopts a set of terms which are sufficient to generate an empirically consistent range of patterns, but it is likely that the terms themselves can be simplified or formulated differently.

Another implementational shortcoming is the discretization of time in the generation of configurations. A more powerful and microscopically compatible approach would be to simulate the process with Langevin dynamics for continuous time, which would allow for more specific temporal predictions about the mode-coupling process to be made; this would also facilitate modeling phase and metrical activation dynamics in parallel with mode-coupling.

Because the nervous system is a physical system, some of the desiderata of Section 1.2 are effectively physical desiderata entailed by neural compatibility. Modecoupling conforms well to these by design. For example, finiteness is manifested as the postulate that there is a limitation on the number of distinct gestural excitations that can be stable, which follows from the assumption that gestural excitation modes differ in frequency and/or phase; microscopically, the circuits responsible for the population oscillations have constrained ranges of oscillation frequency. Nonspatialization of time is accomplished by conceptualizing temporal sequencing as the consequence of a mode selection mechanism, which incorporates feedback systems into change rules for activation states. Never does this system directly encode time metaphorically as a spatial relation. Non-autonomy is embraced by the model vis-avis the role that conceptals have in activating gestural excitations. Memorylessness, on the other hand, has not yet been modeled in a satisfying way. Specifically, the heuristic rule used to determine conceptal-gestural activation (long-term memories of articulatory actions associated with word forms) is clearly ad hoc. A more desirable approach would be to model the relatively slower timescale learning dynamics that govern conceptal-gestural activation, in the context of multiple cognitive systems interacting over time. Nonetheless, in its current form the model is not inconsistent with memorylessness.

Regarding the desideratum of empiricality, Section 3 showed how a variety of forms of organization can be interpreted in the theory, addressing patterns that are conventionally associated with segmental, moraic/syllabic, metrical, and prosodic word units. Of course, there are far more phonological patterns that must be described by the theory in order to achieve empirical coverage; a comprehensive analysis is necessarily deferred for future work. It is nonetheless evident that modecoupling theory has the analytical power to describe many patterns, and so it is plausible that the theory can provide a fully unified description.

Formal phonologists may see parallels between the use of a Boltzmann distribution for generating gestural configurations and various constraint-based models that either select a surface form via optimization, i.e. optimality theory (Prince & Smolensky, 2004) or assign probabilities to surface variants, i.e. harmony theory (Smolensky, 1986). Indeed, there is a non-trivial similarity in that mode-coupling, like constraint-based models, is based on minimization of an energy-like quantity.

However, there are some consequential differences between these approaches. First, the energy calculations of mode-coupling are derived from speculative hypotheses/heuristic assumptions regarding the microscale dynamics of neural population interactions. Thus the model aims for compatibility between the microscale and macroscale. This contrasts with standard optimization-based frameworks, which define energies in terms of symbolic, object-schematic patterns, without much effort to motivate those energies microscopically. Second, the mode-coupling process is inherently dynamic (albeit discretized); in contrast, most optimization-based models do not generate trajectories of configurations, i.e. sequences of macroscopic states. The importance of temporal dynamicity is apparent when the configuration process is analyzed sufficiently far from the zero temperature limit. In this regime, stochastic influences on configuration state trajectories predict the possibility of alternative stable configurations and degenerate configurations. This feature allows the model to make more specific predictions regarding empirical patterns.

Perhaps the closest existing model to the one proposed here is Gradient Symbolic Computation (GSC) (Smolensky et al., 2014). The main similarity is that both models adopt a relaxation mechanism, in which an energy function defined over macrostates guides the evolution of the macrostate. A key difference is that the macrostates of mode-coupling are not viewed as symbols, but rather, as states of oscillatory systems. One might argue that the simplifications adopted in the implementation of the model effectively make it symbolic, since the macroscopic state variables of gestural modes [r, θ, f ] are not modeled with a continuous dynamics. Under this interpretation, the "symbols" of the current model are markedly different from those of GSC, in regards to the logic of operations that governs their behavior. In GSC, the symbols are related "via the fundamental combinatory operation of symbolic computation, concatenation" (Smolensky et al., 2014). In contrast, the macroscale operations of modecoupling theory are couplings/decouplings between intrinsically oscillatory systems. Moreover, mode-coupling theory adopts a simpler hypothesis regarding the types of symbolic entities on the macroscale: in GSC, symbols are members of a "multi-set of phonological segments related in a particular order and grouped into constituents such as syllables [σ], stress feet (Ft), and prosodic words [PrWd]" (Smolensky et al., 2014); in mode-coupling the macroscale entities ("symbols") are more simply coordination modes and gestural excitations; patterns conventionally associated with a hierarchy of constituent units are epiphenomena of constraints on how the macroscale entities interact, rather than being dimensions of the state space.

Instead of conceptualizing gestural excitations as symbols, I prefer to think of symbols and the constructs of mode-coupling theory as aspects of fundamentally different vocabularies for description of macrostates, requiring entirely different logics of change. Symbols are object-like and their spatial relations encode temporal ones; hence the logic of change is one of object manipulation and containment and/or attachment. Modes are states of oscillatory systems and their relations are couplings; hence the logic of change is a logic of synchronization. The configurations of modecoupling theory are transiently stationary states, forms of local order. Symbolic vocabularies may grossly approximate these transiently stationary states, but the vocabulary of mode-coupling aims to describe them in a way that can be put to test with future advances in neuroimaging.
