Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. Abstract

Music is diverse across human cultures, and animal vocalisations vary across non-human species. Nonetheless, basic principles of interaction and formal structure recur across both. Music, poetry, and animal "song" exhibit structures characterised by repetition, variation, and hierarchical chunking. The degree of variation tolerated depends on style or species, creating a rich space for communicative interaction. We organise formal constraints along two continua: repetition-variation (what is repeated and varied) and choricdialogic (whether vocalisers sing together or take turns). We argue that predictive coding explains how repetition and variation balance predictability and surprise across vocal communication. Regularities established through repetition support individual prediction, coordination, and social bonding. Drawing on the Cantometric database of vocal music across hundreds of human cultures, we find that collective singing tends to involve shorter, more repetitive phrases, whereas solo singing tends to involve longer, less repetitive phrases. We consider these findings alongside poetry and animal vocalisations to explore recurring patterns across species and cultures. We conclude that applying principles of predictive coding to repetition in formal structures across a broad comparative database yields explicit testable hypotheses and exciting insights into why both humans and animals repeat themselves, and often find repetition attractive.

1. Introduction

Repetition is a pervasive feature of human music across the world's cultures (1), including both repetition of a piece of music (replaying) and repetition within a given piece. Crucially, this proclivity for repetition is not limited to professionally recorded or written music: folk tunes, nursery rhymes, children's play songs or religious chants and prayers all tolerate or invite a huge amount of repetition (2).

Our proclivity for repetition in music contrasts sharply with conversational speech, where repetition is often considered boring or even dysfunctional. Yet in songs, exact repetitions of the same chorus 3 or more times are totally standard, and many more repetitions will be happily tolerated (the "nanana" out-chorus of "Hey Jude" by the Beatles repeats 18 times). Furthermore, intriguingly, when spoken words or phrases are repeated exactly, the repetition has a profound effect on our perception of the stimulus, transforming meaningful speech to apparent nonsense ("semantic satiation"), or converting a spoken phrase to sound like a sung melody (the "speech-to-song illusion" (3), Box 1). Repetition has thus been proposed as a "design feature" of human music that sharply distinguishes it from language (2, 4, 5).

A proclivity for formal repetition is not unique to human music: structured repetition is also characteristic of many animal communication systems, particularly those that are often seen as most closely paralleling human music, such as birdsong or whale song (6). Repetition in birdsong in particular is extremely widespread across species. This is often seen as puzzling from an evolutionary viewpoint (7, 8): If the goal of song is to advertise one's vocal versatility and learning ability to a competitor or potential mate, it seems that repetition should be avoided because it will rapidly lead to habituation and a cessation of response (8) (9) (10). So why do so many bird species repeat the same song precisely, often many times?

A puzzling tendency for repetition is thus characteristic of both human music (along with poetry and chant) and birdsong, and sets these domains off sharply from normal human language. The music/birdsong parallel, although rarely mentioned, seems striking enough to warrant deeper exploration, because scholarship in music cognition and birdsong research have generally had rather different foci. The human repetition literature has focused mainly on psychological mechanisms and aesthetic effects, while the animal communication literature often focuses on adaptive functions and phylogenetic comparisons.

The goal of the current review is to synthesise research on the role of repetition at supra-phrasal levels of formal organization in music and poetry, complementing the companion paper by Fitch (11) on birdsong. We introduce a unified terminology and notation for discussing repetition and form in music, poetry, and animal song, using the term "strophic organization" for this higher level of form. We focus on song rather than instrumental music because singing and speech more closely parallel animal song in their production and motor control mechanisms.

We then build a theoretical framework for repetition in form-building across these domains, starting by a twodimensional space. One axis regards the form of social interaction: simultaneous production or turn-taking, termed the "choric/dialogic" continuum. This nicely characterizes some key distinctions between music and language, while leaving room between them for phenomena like poetry and chant. The second axis concerns the degree of repetition. We adopt the theoretical framework of predictive coding, widely accepted in cognitive neuroscience (12) (13) (14) and increasingly popular in music cognition (15) (16) (17) (18). We argue that repetition plays a central role in auditory predictive coding because auditory stimuli are transient, allowing cognitive representations to be established and refined with each re-hearing. Together with stimulus-specific habituation, this account explains the combination of repetition and variation characteristic of music-like stimuli across species.

Why study supra-phrasal repetition across different domains?

Repetition is pervasive and puzzling, yet surprisingly neglected (2). Although higher-level formal structure has been studied in music, poetry, and birdsong, each field has developed its own domain-specific terminology, making cross-domain comparisons difficult. Specific phenomena like iambic pentameter or sonata-allegro form have no straightforward analogues in animal communication, so more abstract notions are needed. Similar limitations apply to broader models of repetition and hierarchical structure that are still designed primarily for Western tonal music (19, 20). At present, however, there is no abstract theoretical and terminological consensus to help build bridges across these domains and allow precise comparisons across cultures and species. Such comparisons are also relevant to understanding how formal structure shapes aesthetic experience: in music, for example, repetition and form provide a context within which moments of high surprisal and aesthetic pleasure unfold, including the "chills" many listeners experience at specific points in a piece, even after hearing the same piece hundreds of times.

Box 1: Speech-to-song illusion

Repetition is central to musical organization (2, 4, 5). The speech-to-song (STS) illusion illustrates this: when a short spoken phrase is repeated without alteration, listeners begin to perceive musical qualities, as though the phrase were a melody (3). The acoustic signal remains unchanged; what changes is how the listener interprets it. Repetition alone can be sufficient to produce this transformation.

Phrases with musical-like acoustic properties, such as stable pitch and regular voicing, tend to produce stronger transformations (21) (22) (23). Linguistic experience also matters: understanding the language generally reduces the effect, particularly for speakers of tone languages (24) (25) (26) (27). Musical aptitude, working memory and long-term memory also modulate the effect (28) (29) (30). Notably, STS can occur even in individuals with reduced sensitivity to musical reward (31), suggesting that the transformation is at least partly independent of the rewarding experience of music.

Several theoretical accounts have been proposed to explain STS (3, 23, 32). Most suggest that listeners initially process speech primarily to extract meaning, but how repetition changes this process remains insufficiently formalized. We propose that predictive coding provides a possible explanation. With each repetition, predictions about the phrase's meaning become more precise, reducing prediction error at the semantic level and allowing lower-level acoustic features to exert greater influence on perception. When these features sufficiently resemble those of music, the listener may recruit musical categories, causing speech to be experienced as song.

Such opportunistic recruitment may be a general feature of predictive perception. Rather than constructing new representations, the system borrows an available framework to interpret incoming signals. For example, Dutch listeners may hear the nonsense phrase "Mama-se, mama-sa, ma-ma-coo-sa" in Michael Jackson's Wanna Be Startin' Somethin' as "mama-se, mama-sa, mama appelsap" (mama apple juice) (33). The interpretation is incorrect, but the borrowed framework is readily available. The STS illusion is particularly striking because it crosses not only a linguistic boundary but also the boundary between speech and music.

Unifying terminology and notation: Parts, Songs and Strophic organization

Here, we use the term strophic organization (or strophic form) to denote a global level of formal organization above the phrasal level: above the measure or musical phrase in music, the syllable or song in birdsong, and above the line or stanza in poetry. We chose this term because strophe is already familiar in musicology, poetics and birdsong, although the composite term has rarely been used or defined. "Strophic form" has been used similarly in ethnomusicology. In Western music theory, "strophic" traditionally denotes a specific song form, where the music repeats precisely but the accompanying text may vary each time (e.g. in successive verses of a song) or also repeat (e.g. in the chorus). In birdsong research, "strophe" is another term for "song type" stemming from the distinction in German between "Strophe" (the component of the song repertoire that repeats) and "Gesang" (the bird's complete song or song repertoire). Our term strophic organization may seem least clear in poetics, where "strophe" has had many distinct meanings throughout poetic history (it originates in classical Greek theatre, where it denoted the first component of a chanted ode by the chorus). Today, "strophe" is often seen as synonymous with "stanza" (a repeated structured arrangement of two or more lines), but calls attention to repetition and musical aspects of the lines -a usage appropriate to our context. Conveniently, across musicology, poetics and birdsong research, individual letters are typically used to represent the units involved in formal patterns of recurrence. Musicians often denote formal organisation via the A and B parts of a song, and birdsong researchers similarly describe song patterning with letters. A bird such as a chaffinch might repeat each song in a sequence like "... AAABBCCC...", termed "eventual variety", while a bird exhibiting "immediate variety" like a nightingale will sing "... ABCDBDCA...." This convention also applies in poetry. For example, an English limerick must follow the simple rhyme "AA BB A", while sonnets or villanelles follow more complex rhyme schemes describable using the same notational system (34, 35). Usefully, poetics traditionally uses an X to represent a "free" position where no formal constraints apply. The rhyme scheme "AXAX" means that the first and third lines must rhyme, but the second and fourth are free to vary. Thus, a simple quasi-algebraic notation already exists and is used similarly across disciplines, providing a ready apparatus to explicitly describe strophic organization across domains. Human vocalisations were recorded by the authors, with additional assistance from Yogyakarta to record the female and male Javanese vocal parts.

A few other terms will be useful moving forward. The term "song" itself is ambiguous across domains. In human music, a "song" typically consists of a piece of music containing one or more "parts" that repeat, as in the ABCCA form of the nursery rhyme Baa, Baa, Black Sheep (Figure 1), or the repeated A part (AAA...) with different lyrics each time, as in many folk songs and ballads. In contrast, in birdsong research, a "song" is typically much shorter: a 1-4-second sequence of 5-20 notes, often repeated within the song and usually followed by a pause. Crucially, however, most bird species sing multiple short songs, which together comprise the bird's "repertoire".

The term "syllable" is used by birdsong researchers to denote a short, nearly continuous sound (visible as a connected line on a spectrogram). Thus, birdsong syllables are quite distinct from speech syllables, which are normally continuous and lack silent pauses between syllables, and closer to "notes" in music, with distinct onsets between notes.

With these terms in hand, we can define strophic organization across domains. Fully aleatoric music (or notes from a random number generator) would be said to lack strophic organization. In music, strophic organization refers to the organization of one or more parts, typically involving repetition either exactly (as in the chorus of a song) or only partially (e.g. the repetition of harmony and melody, but not lyrics, in a verse). A single repeated part or song (AAAA…) would be the simplest form, while the ABCCA form of the nursery rhyme "Baa, Baa, Black Sheep" combines recurring and varying parts. A Shakespearean sonnet follows the rhyme scheme "ABAB CDCD EFEF GG", and a chaffinch's songs repeat in the pattern "AAABBCCC…".

Box 2 Cross-Cultural Example: Form in Indonesian Music

Gamelan refers to an ensemble of musical instruments, primarily bronze idiophones, in Indonesian musical traditions (36). Karawitan denotes the broader musical genre involving Gamelan instruments, including instrumental and vocal music, often accompanying dance and wayang shadow-puppet performances (37) (38) (39). Here, we use Karawitan to focus on vocal performance. Karawitan has distinct Javanese and Balinese styles. Javanese Karawitan is generally slower, softer and more meditative, whereas Balinese Karawitan is more dynamic and virtuosic (36). Both use a cyclical, or colotomic, structure marked by periodic gong strokes. In Javanese style, melodies typically repeat in an AA-BB-AA-BB form (Figure 1).

Vocal performance is integral to Karawitan and is primarily led by a female soloist, known as a pesindhen (40). When several pesindhen are present, they usually sing one at a time or together in unison. In Central Java, a male vocalist called a bawa opens the performance with a long, unaccompanied, virtuosic introduction, followed by gerong, a male chorus singing in unison with the ensemble. In Western Java, male wiraswara typically respond to the pesindhen's vocal lines, creating a musical dialogue (Figure 1).

Viewed through the choric-dialogic framework, these practices illustrate how a shared musical framework accommodates different modes of coordination. Pesindhen use embellished melodic structures that allow variation in shared patterns, sometimes with textual variation (41). Simultaneous female singing exemplifies choric coordination, while the exchange between wiraswara and pesindhen in Western Java exemplifies dialogic coordination. In Central Java, gerong sing alongside the ensemble while the pesindhen performs the melody. Differentiated vocal roles and elaborate vocal ornamentation are also noted in comparative research on Southeast Asian music (42).

Karawitan also illustrates how cyclical repetition can coexist with improvisation. Its colotomic structure allows music to continue indefinitely, punctuated by gong strokes (36, 39). Musicians must therefore exercise creativity to prevent repeated cycles from becoming static. This combination of cyclical repetition and improvisational flexibility creates a highly predictable structural framework without requiring static performance.

Singing Context and Mode of Interaction

Both human music and animal song are produced in many distinct contexts, and can serve many functions, including courtship, defence, and group cohesion. A tempting initial categorization of context is into solo versus group song. Solo songs include the prolonged ballad or epic sung by a bard or griot, or a male bird singing alone from the top of a tree. However, even in such cases there is a strong social element: listeners may respond with applause or movements, or a rival may respond with counter-singing after the song is complete (43). In many birds, counter-singing plays an important role in the territorial functions of song. For example, "song matching" occurs when a rival responds with the same song type as its competitor, often signalling intensified aggression (44). Thus, even "solo" song does not occur in a social vacuum, and often involves turntaking and song exchanges. Turn-taking, with little overlap, is also common in conversational speech and dialogue. This mode of interaction is termed "dialogic" (5).

At the other extreme of social interaction are tightly coordinated group performances: chorusing. In humans, this is a widespread mode of music making, ranging from children's play songs and group singing at celebrations to the sophisticated simultaneity of choirs and ensembles. Such performances may involve an entire group singing the same melody simultaneously or different parts of four-part choir harmony. In either case, group coordination is key and is rarely perfect without extensive practice. Such performances occupy the "choric" end of the spectrum, common in music but rare in speech.

Crucially, this "choric/dialogic" distinction is not a dichotomy: it defines a continuum that neatly accommodates many intermediate forms. For example, chanting is typically a synchronized group speech activity, towards the choric end of the spectrum, and guitarists "trading licks" or jazz musicians taking turns soloing would exemplify music in the dialogic mode. The degree of coordination may also vary considerably, with wolves howling representing a choric display that is not tightly synchronized or melodically coordinated (45), and birds during the "dawn chorus" often overlapping each other (46) and sometimes performing song matching. Even in human conversation, the prototypical "dialogic" context, excited or rude interlocutors may exhibit considerable overlap towards the end of sentences (47).

Bird duetting is another intermediate example, where a duetting pair will typically utter a tightly synchronized but non-overlapping series of syllables or short songs -a phenomenon known as "hocketing" in human music. For example, in Australian magpie-larks, male syllables (A) and female syllables (B) may form a duet pattern such as AABAABAAB... with no overlap. But a newly-formed pair, still practicing, might exhibit considerable "accidental" overlap (48). In any case, even for bird duets, such rapid-fire hocketting seems to "count" as a unified performance to listeners, and in "deceptive duets," a solo bird may perform both parts of the "duet." These examples show the importance of recognising the choric/dialogic continuum as defining a dimension along which song performances can vary, even for the same song type in a single species.

In what follows, taking this choric/dialogic distinction as given, our focus will be on the question of what roles repetition versus variation serves in these different interactive contexts. For a group attempting a tightly synchronized performance, "practice makes perfect" and repetition is an important component of training. Even for less practiced music, the refrain or chorus of a song in a social context, such as sea shanties, drinking songs, or the standard verse/chorus pop song, invites the group to join in singing. However, the role of repetition at the dialogic end of the continuum is less clear, particularly for solo song. The roe of repetition may therefore vary across the choric/dialogic continuum.

2. Western Musical Form

We will begin with definitions and examples from Western traditional and popular music with which English readers are likely to be familiar, before expanding the concepts across cultures and species.

The majority of human music, including most English songs, involves repetition and variation at multiple hierarchical levels (42). Key units include notes, which are combined into phrases, which are in turn combined into strophes (aka stanzas or sections). Other units (e.g., metric "bars" intermediate between notes and phrases, or large-scale sections such as Exposition, Development, and Recapitulation in Western classical sonata form) can also be important but are arguably less useful for comparing across cultures and species. To facilitate comparison of structural elements across cultures and species, we can represent phrases using letters.

We can represent "Baa Baa Black Sheep" as a six-phrase repeating strophe made up of three basic melodic phrases in the form of ABCCAB (Figure 1). In this case, all three phrases are based on the same basic rhythmic pattern (short-short-short-short-short-short-LONG), with minor rhythmic variations to accommodate lyrics with different numbers of syllables.

In vocal songs with words (the majority of human music) (42), notes often correspond to linguistic syllables, but a single syllable can be extended across multiple notes ("melisma"), as when The Beatles extends the "bee" syllable across multiple notes in "Let It Be." The reverse (a single note containing multiple syllables) is rare, and usually only occurs when multiple syllables are compressed into one to fit a rhythmic scheme. Musical phrases often correspond to places where a singer takes a breath between vocalisations. Breath timing is not fixed, however, and does not always coincide with phrase boundaries.

Links between musical repetition, form, and rhythm across cultures

A striking aspect of most music (Western and non-Western) distinguishing it from spoken language is the repetition of a small number of basic melodic phrases -often only 2-4, and rarely more than 8. When Alan Lomax and Victor Grauer created their "Cantometric" cross-cultural song classification scheme, one of their 37 variables was "Melodic Form" (Line 16) (49) (50) (51). Songs could be classified into 13 options, 12 of which represented different types of strophic repetition, such as "simple litany [e.g., AAAA or ABABAB] with little or no variation" or "complex strophe [e.g., ABA ABA CDE] with moderate to great variation". Only one Cantometric option denoted music without repeating phrases: "through-composed" (defined as "A song that has no recognizable litany or strophic pattern, but instead is made up of a series of phrase groups in which consistently repeated sections seldom if ever occur."). Examination of the full Cantometric global database (51) shows that this non-repeating category accounts for only 7% (425/5,776) of the world's songs.

Interestingly, however, this distribution is not random but regionally clustered, with a particularly high frequency in East Asia. Indeed, the frequency of non-repeating forms in East Asia was high enough that "Phrase Repetition" did not qualify as one of the 18 "statistical universal" identified by Savage et al. (42), whose criteria required a feature to predominate in nine global regions represented in the Garland Encyclopedia of World Music. Phrase repetition predominated in the other eight regions but not in East Asia (Figure S1). Importantly, melodic form is associated with other musical variables in ways that suggest functional relationships. Most music tends to be performed in groups, rather than solo (69% vs. 31% of Cantometric codings) and to use equal-timed "isochronous" beats rather than free rhythms (89% vs. 19%) (42, 52). Crucially, these distributions were strongly correlated with one another: across all nine regions, songs that were through-composed were more likely to be part of a network of features that included solo performance and free (non-isochronous) rhythms, as well as being less likely to include other features such as accompanying dance (Figure S1). Thus, strophic organization is a typical feature of group music, but less so with solo styles.

The cross-cultural association between phrase repetition and synchronised group performance in human music, combined with their relative absence in spoken language, is consistent with the proposal that musicality could have evolved to help bond us together (53). This is further supported by evidence that synchronised group singing enhances social bonding more than alternating conversation or synchronised lyric recitation (54). These findings also lead to testable predictions for comparisons across species, such as whether individuals or species whose singing is more integrated display greater social bonding. The effects of coordinated but non-overlapping styles, such as hocketing and call-and-response, may also be worth exploring, particularly in work or marching songs where a solo voice is followed by a synchronised group response.

3. Repetition and Form in Poetry

If repetition is a design feature of music that distinguishes it from language, poetry provides an interesting inbetween case. The history of the English word song illustrates how fluid the boundary between these domains has been: at various times in history, song could refer not only to a "musico-poetic entity" but also to poetry or a literary text alone (55). Similar overlap appears across traditions. In ancient Greece, much of what is now classified as lyric poetry was composed for musical performance (56). In early Japan, waka, a classical poetic form, was referred to as uta, literally "song" (57, 58).

The Unit of Repetition in Poetry

In the villanelle, the repeated unit is the whole line, which recurs verbatim at fixed points across the poem. In contrast, rhyme involves the recurrence of only part of the phonological material, typically the sounds at the end of a line. Because they operate at different levels, both can occur in the same poem, as in "The House Other poetic forms place the repeating unit at different levels. For example, in a sestina, six line-final words recur in a fixed permutation across stanzas, illustrating how poetic repetition can operate at the level of words rather than whole lines or phonological material.

Repetition can also operate through meter, where recurring patterns of stresses, syllables, or other rhythmic units organise the poetic line. Hollander's classification of English verse distinguishes five systems according to the principles used to organise the line: stress alone, a combination of stress and syllable count, syllable count alone, long and short syllables, and no fixed metrical principle (34). Four of these systems organise the line by counting or measuring units: stresses, syllables, or both. In Japanese fixed-form verse, this counting is explicit.

Japanese haiku provides a clear example. The form is conventionally described as three lines of 5, 7, and 5 morae. A mora is a basic unit of phonological timing in Japanese, roughly corresponding to a consonantvowel combination or a single vowel, with some exceptions. The form can be represented in the notation used here as ABA, where the letters correspond to the mora-count pattern 5-7-5.

The Choric-Dialogic continuum

Poetic practices vary in how participants coordinate their contributions, from sequential exchange to simultaneous joint production. These modes of production may constrain how much content can be varied or repeated. One possible explanation is that sequential exchange and simultaneous production place different demands on prediction and coordination (5). In sequential exchange, participants anticipate another person's contribution while preparing their own response (62). In simultaneous production, they must coordinate both the timing and content of their output in real time. Misaligned voices can obscure one another, favouring shared and predictable material, regular pulses, and predetermined durations (63) (64) (65).

Japanese waka exchange (zōtōka, Heian period, 794-1185) lies near the dialogic end, where content can vary most freely. One poet sent a verse with 5-7-5-7-7 mora form, and the recipient responded with another. Although the exchange was sequential, well-formed responses often incorporated and transformed words or images from the preceding poem, maintaining continuity with a new interpretation (58, 66).

An intermediate case is call-and-response, in which a leader and a group alternate sequentially, but the group produces the response jointly. Variation can occur across successive calls, whereas the collective response favours repetition and alignment. Responses are also often compact and rhythmically regular (63, 65).

Near the choric end, synchronous chanting involves multiple participants producing the same words together. For example, Indonesian protests recite a poem by Wiji Thukul, converging on a shared final line in unison (65). A similar contrast between collective synchrony and individual freedom occurs in South Korean Buddhist temple chanting, where group passages follow fixed syllable durations while solo passages allow greater freedom (64).

These examples are illustrative rather than systematic, unlike the music section, which rests on actual musical performance sample. Acoustic work on poetic recitation is also unevenly distributed across traditions. The patterns described here are presented as hypotheses for further investigation.

4. Repetition and Form in Animal Vocalization

There is a considerable bioacoustics literature on various forms of repetition in nonhuman animals ("animals" hereafter), with three main forms standing out. In the first "solo" form, a single vocalizer repeats itself, either immediately (AAA BBB) or after intervening material (ABC ACB). Repetition in solo singing is highly typical of birdsong, and its adaptive roles have been well-studied in several songbird species (cf. 11). Two further forms occur in social calling: "dialogic" repetition involves sequential vocal matching (67), while "choric" repetition occurs in overlapping and/or synchronized vocalizations of social groups, where call convergence is often observed (68, 69). These forms provide an evolutionary perspective on the role of repetition in human speech and music. We consider each in turn.

Repetition by solo singers who can produce multiple song types is particularly relevant to human music. It is common in birdsong (70) and also observed in humpback whale song (71). In these cases, the repetition can occur immediately ("immediate variety") or after other song types have been interspersed ("eventual variety"). Many adaptive hypotheses have been proposed for the evolution of large and varied song repertoires, with territorial defense and courtship among the leading explanations. Fewer studies have addressed why repetition itself is so common in birdsong. Proposed explanations include the "consistent performance" hypothesis, whereby repetition allows singers to demonstrate well-developed motor and learning skills; the "mayday" hypothesis, whereby repetition helps signals overcome poor transmission in noisy environments; and the "mere exposure effect," whereby repetition promotes familiarity in listeners. Evidence from several species suggests that females prefer songs familiar from early development, particularly their fathers' songs. Although many hypotheses have been proposed, experimental work supports only a subset across species (11).

Most animal communication occurs in social networks, where specific listening conspecifics may be targeted and interactions may also be overheard by others (72, 73). Even apparently "solo" interactions occur in a social context: these are "social solos," where it is crucial to consider the audience, intended or unintended. A common dialogic form is "vocal matching," seen in counter-singing (44), where one individual repeats the song or call of another. It is well documented in songbirds, dolphins, and some parrots (74). Three main hypotheses have been advanced for such matching. First, matching may allow a signaller to "address" or designate a specific conspecific (75). Playback experiments document this function in songbirds, parrots, and dolphins (76) (77) (78), and in songbirds it often seems to signal aggressive intent (79). In dolphins and orangefronted conures, however, vocal matching appears to signal positive intent and promote social bonding (69, 74). Finally, matched songs may provide a known signal that allows both singers to estimate their distance, the so-called "vocal ranging" hypothesis. Vocal matching can thus serve multiple purposes, both aggressive and socio-positive. All known examples of this phenomenon occur in vocal learners, species capable of complex vocal production learning.

5. Discussion

>Discussion

Repetition, Variation and Prediction

It has been said that "familiarity breeds contempt," and the very ubiquity of repetition in music may hide its true importance. We suggest that repetition is fundamental to auditory cognition in general and music perception in particular, because it provides the foundation upon which recognition, memory, and statistical learning are based.

Auditory signals often vary considerably over time, even when produced by the same vocalizer, and biological vocalizations and musical performances rarely reproduce identical waveforms (e.g. 80). Against this variability, recurring patterns stand out. But importantly, identifying recurrence does not require acoustic identity: patterns can be recognized at different levels of representation, from phonemes and rhyme in language and poetry to melodic contours, phrases, and song types in music. Across music, poetry, and birdsong, repetition establishes recognizable patterns that persist across variations in their realization. In this sense, repetition and variation are closely related: some variation is compatible with recognizing a pattern as the same.

We hypothesize that the auditory system hungers for such recurrent patterns to interpret the organization of an ongoing sound stream: repetition creates "islands of stability" in an "ocean of otherwise-tedious variety" (11). Listeners are highly sensitive to recurrent structure, even when it is embedded in varying acoustic contexts (2, 81), as shown by pre-attentive neural responses (82) and implicit sequence learning (83). As recurrent patterns accumulate, statistical learning may allow listeners to discover how these patterns relate to one another and to build increasingly rich expectations about the organisation of the signal (84, 85).

Repetition may support the formation of hierarchical structure, a function explored across several lines of research. Repeated exposure can shift attention from more local to more global levels of organisation (86, 87).

Statistical regularities, such as high bigram transition probabilities between elements, may help determine which elements are grouped into larger perceptual units or chunks (84, 85). Hebb repetition learning research similarly suggests that repeated sequences become integrated into unified chunks, with perceptual organization playing a crucial role in learning (83, 88). Repetition may thus support the formation of largerscale structures through successive levels of chunking, helping to overcome the stringent limitations of working memory (89).

Computational models such as IDyOM ( 15) formalize how such statistical relations constrain predictions over time. In IDyOM, predictions are based on contexts of different lengths. As context accumulates, the range of possible continuations can become increasingly constrained, allowing increasingly specific patterns within a sequence to be predicted. Although statistical models such as IDyOM have been extensively validated against listeners' experiences across a range of tasks, how well such model-derived structures correspond to the perceptual units of actual listeners' experience remains an open question.

Repetition may also make deviations from an established pattern more salient (2, 9, 11). Within a predictive coding framework (16, 17), repeated patterns become increasingly predictable, and processing is weighted less towards repeated features, consistent with habituation. Deviations can then generate increasingly specific prediction errors, calling attention to those features that vary. Attention may therefore shift between newlyestablished regularities, and deviations from them, as a sequence unfolds, or as statistical learning occurs over a longer timescales.

Choric-Dialogic, coordination

We now turn to the implications of repetition and form for social bonding and other proposed functions of music. While considerable research has focused on the role of synchronous song, drumming or dancing in social bonding (at the choric end of the continuum), the role of repetition across successive utterances -in dialogic mode -has received less attention. We suggest that such repetition can also play a central social role and may help explain the aesthetic effects of formal structures in music or poetry, even when produced solo.

Forms established through repetition may come to be shared among individuals, providing a common framework for producing and interpreting signals. Shared regularities provide common ground for coordination (90). Within such a framework, participants can converge, diverge and re-merge.

Choric and dialogic interaction are defined as points along a continuum of coordination (5). At the choric end, participants converge by synchronizing in time, whereas toward the dialogic end, they can clearly differentiate their contributions through turn-taking. Repetition helps establish the shared regularities that support both modes of coordination. Indonesian Karawitan provides one such clear example (Figure 1, Box 2): within the same musical tradition, a shared melodic and rhythmic framework accommodates different modes of coordination, such as collective synchrony, dialogic exchange, and individual variation. The Cantometric analysis illustrates this pattern more globally, showing how different levels of phrase structure combine with different modes of performance organisation (solo, unison, group and overlapping performance) across musical traditions. More collectively coordinated forms also tended to use shorter, more repetitive musical fragments, whereas solo performances tended to involve longer and more varied phrases. This may reflect an interaction between the nature of the repeating unit and the demands of coordination.

Choric coordination and dialogic exchanges are both found in animal communication, with dialogic vocal exchanges appearing to be more common than choric coordination in social calling. These exchanges can serve important social functions, including territorial defence and mate attraction. This raises the broader question of how different modes of coordination and exchange contribute to the social functions in human communication.

Coordinated singing and other forms of joint performance require participants to maintain shared temporal and structural frameworks, potentially creating experiences of togetherness and affiliation (53). Joint musicmaking and synchronized movement have also been shown to promote subsequent prosocial feelings and behaviours, including helping behaviour toward others (53, 54, 91), with such tendencies emerging as early as 14 months of age (92). Savage et al. (53) argue that predictable musical structures facilitate synchronisation and coordination, which can contribute to social bonding.

Across music, poetry and animal vocalisations, repetition establishes recognizable patterns, while variation allows those patterns to be adapted and transformed. Viewed through the lens of predictive coding, repetition reveals a richer, multidimensional set of functions: it provides a flexible principle through which recognizable structures emerge, predictions are formed, and coordination becomes possible across different scales, styles and species. The key, then, is not simply the prevalence of repetition and variation in vocal communication, but the capacity to recognize and maintain sameness across variation, and the possibilities this creates for communication, coordination and musical expression.
