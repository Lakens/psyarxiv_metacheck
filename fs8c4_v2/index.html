Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. Abstract

Reward learning in complex, multidimensional environments requires balancing abstract generalisation with stimulus-specific precision. Previous work suggests that humans initially rely on feature-based abstraction to support fast generalisation, before shifting toward more precise, object-based learning. We asked whether humans also generalise these attentional strategies to novel contexts. In a large-scale study (N = 350), participants learned reward contingencies in an environment where one feature dimension (colour or pattern) was informative at a group level but imperfectly predictive at the stimulus level. In a subsequent phase with novel stimuli, the informative dimension either remained stable (Repeat) or changed (Switch). Average accuracy performance was similar between conditions, yet computational modelling revealed a divergence in strategy: when the relevant dimension remained stable, participants maintained a hybrid approach; when it switched, participants showed a tendency toward objectbased learning, reducing reliance on feature-based abstraction. Time-resolved analyses showed that this reconfiguration began almost immediately upon entering the new context. Humans therefore tune the level of abstraction they use based on inferred environmental reliability, striking a balance between rapid generalisation and stimulus-specific precision.

1 Introduction

Adaptive learning in complex environments requires not only learning what to do, but also learning how to learn -acquiring abstract strategies that can be efficiently deployed or reconfigured when contexts change (Binz et al., 2024; Botvinick et al., 2019; Wang et al., 2018; Wang, 2021).

Imagine moving to a new city and deciding which bakery you like best. At first, you rely on abstract cues-expecting bakeries with many locals or a certain aesthetic to be superior-before gradually learning the idiosyncratic quality of each establishment. This initial strategy reflects general features learned from past experiences; only later, you refine your choices with specific knowledge about unique products, such as realizing that a particular sourdough is superior despite a bakery's modest storefront. This tension between rapid generalization based on abstract structure and slower, more precise learning about individual options represents a fundamental challenge in navigating high-dimensional worlds (Farashahi et al., 2017 (Farashahi et al., , 2020;; Radulescu et al., 2019; Wu et al., 2025).

In reinforcement learning (RL) terms, this trade-off is captured by the distinction between feature-based learning and object-based learning. Feature-based learning supports fast generalization by leveraging shared features across options (e.g., assuming that "brown bread" is usually good). In contrast, object-based learning yields higher asymptotic accuracy once sufficient experience with individual items has accumulated (e.g., discovering that brown bread with sesame is excellent at one bakery but not another; Farashahi et al., 2017 Farashahi et al., , 2020;; Niv et al., 2015; Schaaf et al., 2022).

Theoretical frameworks such as "Holistic Reinforcement Learning" propose that humans solve this representational challenge through a bidirectional interaction: structured knowledge, learned via approximate Bayesian inference, acts as a source of top-down selective attention that "sculpts" the state representations used for value learning (Radulescu et al., 2019). While this framework explains how learners trade off efficiency for precision within a fixed environment, existing behavioral, neural, and computational work has shown that humans transfer specific decision policies across contexts, as well as beliefs about environmental volatility, controllability, or task structure, shaping subsequent exploration, learning rates, and strategy selection (Collins & Frank, 2013; Hall-McMaster et al., 2025; Schaaf et al., 2022; Simoens et al., 2025; Tomov et al., 2021 Tomov et al., , 2023)).

Yet real-world expertise often depends on a more fundamental prior: it is unknown whether these sculpted representational policies-the attentional templates that govern the level of abstraction-are themselves treated as transferable meta-knowledge. Do humans carry forward higher-order attentional priors that guide how they balance abstraction and precision when encountering entirely novel environments and stimuli, specifically the level of abstraction (feature-based vs object-based ) at which they represent the task? This is a qualitatively different form of transfer: rather than asking which stored solution to apply, it asks how to represent the task in the first place.

This question aligns with broader theories of learning and, in particular, reinforcement metalearning, which proposes that adaptive decision-making requires generalizing from experience even when no two environments are identical (Gershman & Niv, 2010; Niv et al., 2015; Tomov et al., 2021). Recent work on meta-generalization suggests that humans do not merely transfer specific rules, but also learn higher-order priors about which types of strategies are likely to be effective, flexibly switching between reusing entire structures and recombining task components based on environmental statistics (Franklin & Frank, 2020; Wang et al., 2018). More broadly, this line of research supports the idea that humans "learn how to learn," acquiring meta-knowledge about effective strategies and reapplying it in new contexts.

2 Results

2.1 Behavioral performance

Groups showed comparable overall accuracy and reaction times, with a modest difference in the speed of performance stabilisation during the test phase.

To confirm task engagement in both conditions (Repeat vs. Switch) and phases (Learning vs. Test), we first verified that behavioural performance exceeded the task's chance level (61%) (all ps < .001). As illustrated in Fig. 2, accuracy increased and reaction times decreased across blocks in both phases, reflecting learning. For accuracy, we found a main effect of block (F (3.66, 1251.03) = 295.63, p < .001) and phase (F (1, 342) = 11.41, p < .001), and a block × phase interaction (F (4.27, 1460.22) = 5.57, p < .001). Crucially, the informative dimension stability × phase interaction was not significant (F (1, 342) = 0.12, p = .735), and no higher-order interactions involving informative dimension stability survived correction, indicating that repeating the informative dimension did not boost overall accuracy relative to switching it. RT analyses showed the same pattern, with main effects of block (F (3.52, 1190.07) = 54.07, p < .001) and phase (F (1, 338) = 38.85, p < .001).

Although overall accuracy did not distinguish the groups, the conditions could still differ in how performance improved-in particular, in the trial-by-trial dynamics by which accuracy approached its asymptote within each phase. Such dynamics are invisible to block-wise averages, yet prior work shows that learning can proceed via different strategies with distinct temporal profiles (Farashahi et al., 2020).

We therefore fitted an exponential time constant τ to trial-wise accuracy (Methods), where smaller τ indicates faster approach to asymptote. Because single-trial binary accuracy is too noisy for stable individual curve fits, we first estimated τ at the participant level after smoothing each participant's accuracy time series with a sliding (centred moving-average) window. Independent of using windows of 20, 36, and/or 72 trials, τ did not differ between groups during learning (all ps > .34). However, during the test phase, the Switch group showed a smaller median τ than the Repeat group at every window (20 trials: 28.7 vs. 42.5, p = .014; 36 trials: 38.3 vs. 47.7, p = .027; 72 trials: 56.0 vs. 61.1, p = .036). Exclusion rates (non-convergent or boundary fits) did not differ between groups (all χ 2 ps > .26; Supplementary Table S2).

Group-average fits yielded the same qualitative pattern: comparable initial and asymptotic accuracy; no evidence of a group difference during learning (difference = 13.2 trials, 95% CI [-31.7, 66.9]; permutation p = .551); and a numerically smaller time constant during test in the Switch group (τ = 38.1, 95% CI [28.1, 51.7]) than in the Repeat group (τ = 60.2, 95% CI [43.3, 92.1]). The bootstrap interval for the difference excluded zero (difference = 22.1 trials, 95% CI [0.5, 56.1]), and the permutation test was marginally significant (p = .054). The Group × Phase interaction was not significant at either level of analysis: at the group level (difference-in-differences = 10.2 trials, 95% CI [-48.5, 67.3], permutation p = .696) or at the participant level (ps > .05). Because the main manipulation has occurred in the test phase, we focus on the test-phase comparison, with the learning phase providing a within-study baseline.

Together, these results indicate that overall accuracy and reaction times were comparable regardless of whether the informative dimension was repeated or switched; however, the speed of stabilisation was more affected than asymptotic accuracy. Faster plateauing in accuracy of the Switch group in the test phase is consistent with an earlier shift toward the object-based RL strategy, a possibility we test next with computational modeling.

2.2 Computational modelling results

Behavioural performance alone cannot reveal whether similar outcomes arise from shared or distinct learning strategies. To disambiguate the latent mechanisms underlying performance,we characterized the underlying strategies by fitting three families of reinforcement learning modelsfeature-based, object-based, and hybrid models that integrate both representations -to participants' choices and examining how model preference changed across phases (Farashahi & Soltani, 2021; Habibi et al., 2025). We complemented these global fits with time-resolved analyses of model fit (trial-wise BIC) and of attention weights derived from the models, enabling us to track how reliance on feature-based versus object-based information evolved over time and how this evolution differed depending on whether the informative dimension remained stable or changed across phases.

2.2.1 Global BIC comparison

Feature stability modulates the transition in model preference. We compared the relative fit of 12 reinforcement learning models to participants' choices using the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC). Models differed in representational family (feature-based, hybrid, or object-based) and in how values were updated (standard, counterfactual, or decay; see Methods). Across the full set, models with counterfactual updating-which updates values for both chosen and unchosen options-provided the best account of behaviour (Supplementary Tables S3 and S4); we therefore report the counterfactual-update family in the main text (Table 1). Within this family, the hybrid model, which combines feature-based and object-based representations with two learning rates, achieved the best overall fit during the learning phase in both the Repeat (BIC = 243.106) and Switch (BIC = 239.549) groups. This indicates that initial learning was comparable across groups and best captured by a strategy integrating abstract feature-level information with stimulus-specific values, irrespective of later informative dimension stability. During the test phase, however, model preference diverged between groups. In the Repeat group, the hybrid model remained the best-fitting (BIC = 237.662). In contrast, in the Switch group, the pure object-based model provided a better fit (BIC = 240.723) than the hybrid model (BIC = 242.582). This shift indicates a relative reduction in the contribution of feature-based representations when the previously informative dimension became unreliable.

2.2.2 Time-resolved BIC comparison

Transfer of higher-level strategies is reflected in the accelerated transition from feature-based to object-based learning during the test phase. Global BIC comparisons often obscure the temporal evolution of learning strategies. To quantify the dynamics of strategy reconfiguration, we calculated trial-wise Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC p ) scores for the featurebased and object-based model families using counterfactual updating (see Methods). Although the hybrid model provided the best overall phase-level fit, we restricted this time-resolved contrast to the two pure families so that ∆BIC p (the BIC difference between object-based and feature-based models, ∆BIC p = BIC object -BIC feature ) would index a shift along a single representational axis (feature-based vs. object-based); the hybrid model mixes both components and therefore cannot isolate that transition. Following previous research using the same analysis (Farashahi et al., 2017 (Farashahi et al., , 2020;; Habibi et al., 2025), we expected participants to initially rely on feature-based abstraction before transitioning toward more precise object-based representation. We hypothesised that metaknowledge acquired during the learning phase would transfer to the test phase, manifesting as an accelerated transition toward the optimal strategy (object-based here). Furthermore, we predicted that the speed and nature of this transition would be modulated by informative dimension feature stability (Repeat vs Switch).

As shown in the lower panels of Figure 3, which display the BIC difference between object-based and feature-based models (∆BIC p ), participants in both conditions began each phase with a relative preference for feature-based strategies (positive ∆BIC p ), followed by a gradual shift toward objectbased learning (∆BIC p becoming negative). To formalise this shift, we compared mean ∆BIC p between an early stage (first two blocks; 72 trials) and a late stage (remaining trials; Blocks 3-6) of each phase. This boundary was anchored to the behavioural plateau analysis (see Behavioural performance): learning-phase plateaus fell later (τ = 83.2 and 96.4 trials for Switch and Repeat), whereas in the test phase accuracy stabilised within roughly the first one to two blocks (τ = 38.1 and 60.2 trials). A two-block early window therefore spans the pre-plateau portion of the test phase and the rising limb of learning.

Wilcoxon signed-rank tests confirmed that ∆BIC p decreased significantly from the early to the late stage in both groups and both phases (all ps < .001; Supplementary Table S5), with the late stage more object-leaning in every case (learning-phase change: Switch +0.021 to -0.110, Repeat +0.003 to -0.098; test-phase change: Switch -0.044 to -0.171, Repeat -0.028 to -0.157). Notably, the early stage already differed between phases: averaged over the first two blocks, ∆BIC p was feature-leaning during learning (Switch +0.021; Repeat +0.003) but object-leaning at test (Switch -0.044; Repeat -0.028). Comparing early stages across phases confirmed that both groups entered the test phase more object-leaning than they had entered learning (Switch: W = 3931.0, p < .001; Repeat: W = 6362.0, p = .035).

We next tested whether the trajectory of this shift within the earlier stage (first two blocks; 72 trials) differed between groups and phases using a linear mixed-effects model predicting trialwise ∆BIC p from Phase (learning vs. test), Group (Repeat vs. Switch), Trial (z-scored), and their interactions, with random intercepts and slopes for Phase and Trial by subject. A maximal randomeffects structure was singular; we therefore report a model with by-subject random intercepts and Trial slopes, and confirmed the three-way interaction held in a random-intercept-only model (p = .014). The Group × Phase × Trial interaction was significant (β = 0.0266, t = 2.46, p = .014): ∆BIC p declined more steeply for Switch than Repeat during early test (estimated slopes: -0.053 vs. -0.041), relative to the opposite numerical ordering during early learning (-0.018 vs. -0.032).

Informative-dimension stability modulates the coupling between object-based model support and accuracy during test. The preceding analyses concern when each group shifted toward object-based control. We also asked whether that shift was behaviourally consequential by testing whether trial-wise ∆BIC p predicted concurrent accuracy. For each phase we fitted a logistic mixed-effects model predicting trial accuracy from z-scored ∆BIC p , Group, centred trial number, and their interactions, with random intercepts and slopes for trial and ∆BIC p by participant. In both phases, more negative ∆BIC p (stronger relative support for the object-based model) predicted higher accuracy (learning: β = -1.64, z = -32.59; test: β = -1.68, z = -32.93; both p < .001), and this coupling strengthened over trials (learning: β = -0.340, z = -24.04; test: β = -0.323, z = -22.24; both p < .001), confirming that increasing reliance on object-based learning carried a growing behavioural benefit. Group did not modulate these effects during learning (all Group interactions p > .62). During test, however, a Group × Trial × ∆BIC p interaction emerged (β = 0.084, z = 2.92, p = .0035): the object-accuracy coupling steepened more across the test phase in the Repeat than the Switch group (∆BIC p × Trial slope -0.365 vs. -0.281).

Taken together, these analyses reveal several dissociable effects of prior experience. First, both groups showed reliable within-phase transitions from feature-based to object-based strategies, replicating the canonical pattern (Farashahi et al., 2017 (Farashahi et al., , 2020)). Second, the significant Phase effect demonstrates that participants entered the test phase already shifted toward object-based dominance relative to learning onset, consistent with transfer of higher-order strategic attentional knowledge across disjoint stimulus sets. Third, focusing on the early window (the first two blocks, 72 trials) showed that a group difference in the rate of the object-ward shift differed between phases, with the Switch group tending to transfer toward the object-based strategy more rapidly at test, when the previously informative dimension was no longer reliable. This suggests that the instability of informative feature nudged the Switch group toward earlier strategic reconfiguration, without altering the overall extent of the shift. Finally, greater relative support for the object-based model (lower ∆BIC p ) predicted higher trial-wise accuracy in both phases (p < .001), confirming that the shift toward object-based control tracked genuine gains in performance.

2.2.3 Computational mechanisms of strategic reconfiguration

While our model comparisons identified which representational architectures (feature-based, objectbased, or hybrid) best captured behavior at the phase level, and the trial-wise BIC analyses quantified how behavior shifted between feature-based and object-based control over time, they do not explain the latent computational mechanisms driving these changes. We therefore asked whether the observed shifts were better accounted for by changes in attentional prioritization, learning dynamics, or decision-level biases.

For all subsequent parameter analyses, we conducted repeated-measures ANOVAs including Animal Order (Cat→Dog vs. Dog→Cat) and Informative Dimension (Colour vs. Pattern) as control factors to ensure that representational shifts were not driven by specific stimulus sets or feature identities. We first analysed the attention weights (w) to evaluate how attentional priors were reallocated when participants could generalise versus when they were forced to switch settings.

By evaluating the parameters of the feature-based model, we could examine the weight assigned to the informative versus non-informative dimensions to track the focus of abstraction. In the hybrid model, we assessed the relative weighting of informative feature-level versus object-level representations (the hybrid model did not include the non-informative feature).

Feature-based model: Feature selectivity emerges under stability but is suppressed under discontinuity. We investigated the development of feature-based attention, specifically whether participants learned to selectively tune their attention to the informative dimension across contexts. A repeated-measures ANOVA on feature-based weights (Phase × InfoType × Group (Repeat vs. Switch)) revealed a significant main effect of InfoType (Informative vs. Non-Informative, F (1, 342) = 6.46, p = .011), the Group × InfoType interaction (F (1, 342) = 7.34, p = .007), and, critically, a three-way interaction of Group × Phase × InfoType (F (1, 342) = 3.94, p = .048).

To decompose this interaction, we examined each phase independently. During the initial learning phase, informative feature weights were modestly higher than non-informative feature weights (InfoType: F (1, 342) = 5.49, p = .020), but this difference did not differ between conditions (Group × InfoType: F (1, 342) = 0.20, p = .654), suggesting that feature-level selectivity had not yet fully crystallised. However, during the test phase, a significant Group (Informative Dimension Stability) × InfoType interaction emerged (F (1, 342) = 11.27, p < .001).

Paired post-hoc comparisons (Bonferroni-corrected) revealed that in the Repeat condition, weights for the informative feature were significantly higher than those for the non-informative feature at test (t(175) = 3.26, p = .003, d = 0.25). In contrast, this selectivity was absent in the Switch condition (t(173) = -1.68, p = .189, d = -0.13; see Fig. 4A). Wilcoxon signed-rank tests yielded the same pattern (Repeat: p < .001; Switch: p = .281). Figure . 4A).

Hybrid model: Object-based attention increases during generalisation.

Next, we compared the object (w Object ) and informative feature (w Feature ) weights within the hybrid model to determine how representational priority shifted across phases. A repeated-measures ANOVA (Phase × Weight-Type × Informative Dimension Stability) revealed a dominant role for object-based rep- resentations, evidenced by a robust main effect of Weight-Type (F (1, 342) = 64.08, p < .001). Crucially, a significant Phase × Weight-Type interaction (F (1, 342) = 9.78, p = .002) indicated that the allocation of attention was dynamically reconfigured between the learning and test phases.

Post-hoc comparisons confirmed that object-based weights increased significantly from the learning phase (M = 5.34, SD = 5.24) to the test phase (M = 6.39, SD = 6.05; t(349) = 2.90, p = .004), while feature-based weights remained stable (p = .092; Fig. 4B). This pattern held across both the Switch and Repeat conditions, as the three-way interaction with Feature Stability was not significant (F (1, 342) = 0.32, p = .573).

Learning rates and response bias remain stable. We also examined whether strategic reconfiguration was accompanied by changes in the speed of value updating (learning rates) or baseline choice preferences (response bias). In the hybrid model, learning rates showed a consistent asymmetry, with higher learning rates following unrewarded trials (M = 0.157, SD = 0.217) than rewarded trials (M = 0.133, SD = 0.222; F (1, 342) = 4.82, p = .029). However, there was no main effect of Phase (p = .498) nor any consistent interaction involving Informative Dimension Stability (Group) and Phase that indicated a change in learning speed due to Informative Dimension stability. Similarly, response bias parameters (β) showed no significant effects (all p > .05). Parallel analyses of the feature-based and object-based models yielded no significant effects of theoretical interest. Taken together, we found no evidence that the strategic shift toward object-based attention was accompanied by systematic changes in the rate of value updating or baseline choice tendencies.

Two concurrent mechanisms of strategy generalization. The convergence of these parameter-level analyses reveals two concurrent mechanisms guiding strategy generalization. First, a universal refinement toward stimulus-specific precision: when object and feature representations competed directly in the hybrid model, object-based attention increased significantly from learning to test in both groups, while object weights remained stable when estimated in isolation. Second, a condition-specific calibration of abstract priors: in the Repeat group, selectivity for the informative dimension emerged at test where none existed during learning, indicating that a validated prior acts as an attentional anchor. Conversely, in the Switch group, feature selectivity was absent at test, reflecting adaptive disengagement from an unreliable abstraction. These divergent weighting patterns were mirrored in the model architectures that best captured each group's behavior at test-the hybrid model for the Repeat group, and the pure object-based model for the Switch group. Thus, humans do not reset cognitive policies in new contexts but carry forward attentional policies that are dynamically updated based on environmental reliability.

2.2.4 Trial-wise evolution of attention weights

Trial-wise trajectories reveal distinct adaptation dynamics between groups. Our parameter analyses in the preceding section revealed that strategic reconfiguration was primarily implemented through changes in attentional weights. In particular, the feature-based model uncovered a critical divergence: while both groups showed similar feature-level representations during the initial learning phase, during the test phase the Repeat group showed robust selectivity for the informative dimension, whereas the Switch group did not. Therefore, we further investigated the dynamics of attentional reallocation: Did the Repeat group's selectivity emerge through active enhancement of the informative feature, suppression of the non-informative feature, or a combination of both? And in the Switch group, did the loss of feature reliability lead to uniform disengagement from both dimensions or to selective changes in one dimension over the other?

To characterise these dynamics, we estimated trial-wise weight trajectories using a slidingwindow refit procedure (Methods) and analysed them with linear mixed-effects models. Examining these trajectories revealed how feature selectivity emerged over the course of the test phase (Fig. 5). There was a significant main effect of Feature (β = -0.921, SE = 0.042, t(349) = -21.89, p < .001), indicating that informative features maintained substantially higher attention weights than non-informative features throughout the test phase. Significant Feature × Trial interactions for both linear (p < .001) and quadratic (p = .008) terms indicated that infor- mative and non-informative weights followed distinct trajectories. Informative weights exhibited a concave-down pattern-increasing early then stabilising-while non-informative weights showed the opposite concave-up pattern (Fig. 5). This opposing curvature is consistent with selectivity in the Repeat group arising from concurrent enhancement of the informative dimension and suppression of the non-informative dimension. The main effect of Group was not significant (p = .052). However, the two groups differed in their overall trajectory shapes. A significant Group × Trial quadratic interaction (β = -0.057, SE = 0.025, t(348) = -2.23, p = .026) indicated that the Switch group exhibited more pronounced concave-down curvature compared to the relatively flat trajectory of the Repeat group, while the linear interaction remained non-significant (p = .967). This pattern is consistent with an attentional re-engagement process following environmental discontinuity: participants in the Switch condition began the test phase with lower attention weights-consistent with disruption of their previously learned prior-before showing a pronounced upward curve as they re-engaged with the new task structure. By the middle of the test phase, their attentional weights reached levels comparable to the stable baseline maintained by the Repeat group (Fig. 5).

Together, these trial-wise dynamics clarify how strategy transfer interacts with environmental feedback. Both groups entered the test phase with similar attentional settings. However, their trajectories diverged as the test phase unfolded. In the Repeat group, where the prior remained valid, selectivity was strengthened through opposing trajectories of enhancement and suppression. In the Switch group, where the prior was violated, participants showed transient disengagement from feature-based attention overall-rather than selective decay of one dimension alone-before reengaging as they adapted to the new structure. This pattern suggests that transferred attentional priors are not rigidly maintained but are dynamically revised based on ongoing environmental feedback.

3 Discussion

In the current study, we showed that humans do not relearn how to navigate the world from scratch every time they enter a new environment. Instead, they carry forward abstract attentional priors that govern the level of representational abstraction applied to novel tasks, a meta-learned repertoire of representational templates that guide how they attend to and represent a task. Here, we show that these expectations are not limited to which features to track, but also concern the level of abstraction at which the task is encoded. Participants first learned reward contingencies in an environment where one feature dimension was partially informative at the group level but imperfectly predictive at the stimulus level, then were tested in a new environment where that dimension either remained informative (Repeat) or became misleading (Switch). Crucially, computational modelling revealed that similar behavioural profiles masked a clear strategic divergence. In the Repeat condition, participants efficiently redeployed a hybrid strategy, whereas in the Switch condition, they shifted toward object-based learning.

Our findings integrate naturally with established work on feature-based and object-based learning in multidimensional environments. Consistent with earlier studies, participants initially relied on feature-based abstraction to support efficient generalisation before progressively prioritising object-level precision (Farashahi et al., 2017; Farashahi & Soltani, 2021). However, whereas this literature has primarily focused on how the balance between abstraction and precision is dynamically adjusted within a single task, our results reveal that learners carry forward expectations about the usefulness of abstraction itself across different contexts. In our paradigm, participants used their prior experience from the learning phase as a strategic bias in the test phase, facilitating a faster transition toward a more object-compatible strategy. This suggests that what is meta-learned is not merely a reward map, but a prior over the environmental statistics that determine whether feature-based generalisation constitutes an optimal inference strategy or a misleading heuristic, and that this prior is flexibly updated when the reliability of feature structure changes.

Our findings also extend recent work on multi-task reinforcement learning in humans by extending the focus beyond transfer of action policies to include transfer of representational policies. Previous studies have shown that humans can generalise across tasks with different reward functions by reusing learned policies, formalised in terms of successor features and generalised policy improvement (Hall-McMaster et al., 2025; Tomov et al., 2021). In those studies, however, the state space was held fixed and shared across tasks, so transfer primarily concerned reward functions and action selection. Here, by contrast, we show that humans also transfer the representational policies that govern the level of abstraction applied during learning. By introducing entirely novel stimuli in the test phase, we demonstrate that participants generalise abstract attentional settings across contexts that share no overlapping physical features.

The tendency to favour lower-dimensional, feature-based representations at the onset of learning is often treated as a fixed cognitive constraint for overcoming the curse of dimensionality. Our results offer a complementary perspective: this bias may itself be a meta-learned prior, gradually shaped by accumulated experience with the statistical structure of natural environments. In support of this, recent evidence shows that semantic structure, such as encountering stimuli with meaningful anchors versus arbitrary features, significantly influences how learners weight feature dimensions in multidimensional reinforcement learning tasks (Habibi et al., 2025). Converging evidence from development further supports this view: children rely on feature-based learning longer than adults and show less consistent strategy shifts (Decorte et al., 2026), consistent with the idea that attentional abstraction biases are gradually calibrated through experience rather than representing a fixed architectural constraint.

Together, our findings suggest that human attentional biases are not static but function as dynamic control parameters shaped by prior experience, aligning with theoretical accounts of metalearning and structure learning, which propose that humans infer abstract properties of their environment, such as feature relevance or reliability, and use these inferences to guide learning and attention (Botvinick et al., 2019; Collins & Frank, 2013; Gershman & Niv, 2010; Hall-McMaster et al., 2025; Held et al., 2025; Schaaf et al., 2022; Simoens et al., 2024 Simoens et al., , 2025;; Tomov et al., 2021 Tomov et al., , 2023;; Wang et al., 2018; Wang, 2021). In this sense, attentional strategies operate as meta-learned control parameters that can be retrieved and adjusted when encountering a new environment (Abrahamse et al., 2016; Braem et al., 2024; Xu et al., 2024).

The time-resolved analyses on attention weights offered further insight into how these strategies are reconfigured and reveal that these strategic differences reflect the interaction of processes operating on distinct timescales: slower meta-learned expectations about the appropriate level of abstraction, and rapid within-task reconfiguration when those expectations are violated. In the Repeat condition, this prior remains valid and the hybrid strategy is maintained; in the Switch condition, however, early violations of this prior drive a rapid shift away from feature-based abstraction toward a more stimulus specific but unbiased object-based strategy (see also Binz et al., 2024; Simoens et al., 2024; Xu et al., 2026). This suggests that transferred attentional policies are not rigidly cached but remain open to error-based revision -a distinction between the initial act of transfer and the subsequent recalibration of transferred priors.

This rapid reconfiguration is consistent with Bayesian non-parametric accounts of latent cause inference and holistic reinforcement learning, which propose that learners maintain hypotheses about task structure and selectively assign observations to latent causes (Gershman & Niv, 2010; Gershman et al., 2015; Radulescu et al., 2019). In these frameworks, abrupt changes in contingency are interpreted as switches between latent causes, allowing agents to gate in alternative representations rather than incrementally adjusting a single mapping (Courville et al., 2006; Gershman & Niv, 2010). Reward prediction errors signal that the current structural hypothesis is unreliable, prompting inference of a new latent cause and retrieval of an alternative representation (Radulescu et al., 2019). Analogously, when the previously informative feature dimension became misleading in the Switch condition, participants appear to have inferred a new structural context and gated in an object-based representation that bypassed the now-unreliable feature abstraction-rather than incrementally adjusting a hybrid strategy.

This interpretation is directly supported by our attention-weight analyses (Fig. 4A): during the test phase, Switch participants showed no reliable difference between informative and noninformative feature weights, indicating disengagement from feature-based abstraction. The trialwise evolution of attention weights (Fig. 5) further revealed that both feature weights converged to equivalent values at the onset of the test phase in the Switch group, indicating rapid abandonment of the previously learned attentional prior. In contrast, Repeat participants maintained a clear asymmetry from the outset of test, assigning higher weight to the informative dimension and lower weight to the non-informative dimension consistent with a consistent redeployment of their acquired attentional template.

From a cost-benefit perspective (Kool et al., 2016 (Kool et al., , 2017)), such early disengagement may be adaptive when initial evidence suggests that abstraction will systematically bias value estimates, favoring a rapid shift toward more precise object-based learning. Importantly, this reconfiguration was implemented primarily through changes in attentional weighting rather than changes in learning rates or response biases, suggesting that abstract attentional templates act as key control variables for meta-learning (Leong et al., 2017; Niv et al., 2015; Radulescu et al., 2019; Tomov et al., 2021 Tomov et al., , 2023)). Beyond improving theories of meta-learning and generalization, our findings create new potential avenues for computational psychiatry. For instance, individuals with autism (traits) have been proposed to rely more heavily on initially learned stimulus values and show reduced exploration (Goetmaeckers et al., 2025; Goris et al., 2021; Sapey-Triomphe et al., 2022; van de Cruys et al., 2014; Van Overwalle et al., 2025). An open question is whether these individuals would show a sustained reliance on these abstract attentional templates despite evidence of unreliability, resulting in maladaptive persistence of broader attentional strategies (Geurts et al., 2009; Lage et al., 2024). A complementary perspective comes from recent developmental work emphasizing that learning strategies themselves are shaped by long-term environmental statistics across development (e.g., Kidd et al., 2013; Gopnik, 2020). Nussenbaum & Hartley (2024) argue that early-life environments influence reward learning through meta-learning mechanisms that operate across multiple nested timescales, extending beyond the short horizons typically studied in laboratory tasks (see also Sheridan & McLaughlin, 2014; Humphreys & Zeanah, 2015). Applied to our task, this framework may predict that individuals with different developmental historiessuch as prolonged exposure to volatile or unstable environments -may differ in how readily they maintain or abandon abstraction when entering new contexts, making our task a tractable way to link short-term strategy reconfiguration to long-term developmental influences on meta-learning.

The present study is not without limitations. A first limitation concerns the scope of our computational modelling. We fitted separate models to each phase and inferred attentional transfer indirectly by comparing parameter estimates between groups, rather than modelling the transfer process itself. Additionally, our approach does not formally capture the timescale at which attentional priors are revised within the test phase. Future work could address both limitations by developing a unified model spanning both phases, integrating approximate Bayesian inference for structure learning with incremental reinforcement learning for value updating, to directly quantify knowledge transfer and characterise how attentional priors are recalibrated over time. A further limitation is that attentional states were inferred exclusively through computational modelling. Although this approach revealed strategic differences less visible at the behavioural level, it remains indirect. Future studies could combine our paradigm with eye-tracking measures to obtain trialby-trial indices of overt attentional allocation (Krajbich et al., 2010; Leong et al., 2017), providing converging evidence for the representational shifts identified here.

In conclusion, our results show that humans flexibly generalize not only what they learn, but also how they learn. By dynamically calibrating the level of abstraction based on inferred environmental reliability, learners strike a balance between efficient generalization and stimulus-specific precision. This ability to reuse -or rapidly reconfigure -attentional strategies, while continuously weighing them against the costs and benefits of object-specific learning, may be a central component of adaptive behavior in complex and changing environments.

4 Method

4.1 Participants

We recruited 450 undergraduate students from Eötvös Loránd University, who participated for course credit. The ethics committees of Ghent University and Eötvös Loránd University approved all procedures, and all participants provided informed consent.

Participants were randomly assigned to one of eight conditions in a 2 (informative dimension in the learning phase: colour vs. pattern) × 2 (informative dimension stability across phases: repeat vs. switch) × 2 (animal order across phases: dog→cat vs. cat→dog) between-subjects design. The critical manipulation was informative dimension stability: in the Repeat group, the informative dimension remained the same between the learning and test phases; in the Switch group, it changed. Animal categories (dogs vs. cats) always changed between phases to prevent stimulus-specific carryover.

We excluded 70 participants for non-compliance (single-key responses on > 90% of trials or mean reaction time < 300 ms per block) and an additional 30 participants who showed no evidence of learning by the end of the learning phase. Evidence of learning was defined as performance significantly above 50% accuracy in a one-sided binomial test on the final three blocks (Blocks 4-6, 108 trials; minimum accuracy for p <.05 = 57.4% correct). The true chance level in our task was 61% because of trials with equally rewarded options (8 of 36 trials per block); we adopted the more conservative 50% baseline to ensure that only participants performing at near-minimal chance levels were excluded. The resulting 4% discrepancy between this threshold (i.e., 57.4%) and the true chance level meant that very few, if any, participants were affected by this choice.

The final sample comprised 350 participants (mean age = 23.08 years, s.d. = 5.76; 273 female, 74 male, 3 other). Our primary comparison involved the Repeat and Switch groups (n = 176 and n = 174, respectively). To control for potential confounding by low-level stimulus properties, participants were further divided into eight subgroups defined by the combination of animal order (dog→cat vs. cat→dog) and informative dimension in each phase (colour vs. pattern). Subgroup assignments and sample sizes are provided in Supplementary Table S1; subgroup sizes were broadly comparable and fully counterbalanced across these factors. Although starting animal type and informative dimension were orthogonal to our hypotheses, we included these factors in our analyses to rule out any confounding effects of specific stimulus assignments.

4.2 Stimuli

Stimuli consisted of two animal categories: cats and dogs. For each category, nine unique images were generated from a 3 × 3 factorial design crossing three levels of colour with three levels of pattern (Decorte et al., 2026), yielding nine unique exemplars per category. Colour levels differed by category (cats: grey, white, orange; dogs: black, beige, brown). Pattern was operationalised using distinct accessories or body features (for example, ear shape, spots, or head accessories).

4.3 Task and Experimental Design

The experiment consisted of two phases: learning and test (Figure 1A). In each phase, participants performed a value-based decision-making task adapted from Farashahi & Soltani (2021). They completed six blocks of 36 trials, with self-paced breaks (up to 2 min) between blocks. Participants were told that they were visiting an animal park where they would encounter pairs of animals (cats or dogs) and would need to choose which animal would collect more balls.

Each trial began with a fixation display, followed by two simultaneously presented animals. Participants had up to 10 s to select one, using the F key for the left and the J key for the right animal. Visual feedback indicated whether the choice was correct (green outline) or incorrect (orange outline), and showed the exact number of balls each animal collected (Figure 1C). Cumulative performance was displayed during inter-block breaks.

Each of the nine stimuli within an animal set appeared four times per block (twice on the left, twice on the right). All possible pairwise combinations were presented, with the constraint that animals sharing the same level of either feature (colour or pattern) were never paired on the same trial.

During a given phase, one feature dimension was informative: its three levels were associated with different average reward values, whereas the other feature dimension was non-informative (Figure 1B). The informative dimension was only partially predictive of reward: each level yielded a different average reward, but this relationship did not hold for every individual stimulus. Thus, relying on feature-level information enabled generalisation but was not always optimal; perfect accuracy required learning the value of each unique stimulus. For example, in Figure 1B, pattern 3 has the highest average value, yet the specific stimulus "cat 9" yields less reward than "cat 7". Participants were not told which feature dimension was informative and learned reward contingencies exclusively through trial-by-trial feedback.

The assignment of animal category (cats/dogs) to phase (learning/test), the identity of the informative dimension (colour/pattern), and the mapping of feature levels to reward magnitudes were counterbalanced across participants. Critically, although the animal set always changed between phases, the informative dimension stability was manipulated. For participants in the Repeat group, the same dimension (e.g., colour) remained informative in both phases. For those in the Switch group, the informative dimension changed (i.e., from colour to pattern or from pattern to colour). This manipulation allowed us to test whether participants would generalise a learned attentional strategy to novel stimuli when the relevant feature dimension was stable.

We employed a hierarchical model comparison strategy, first optimising the random effects structure, then evaluating additional fixed effects.

Step 1: Random effects structure (Models 1-3): Models 1, 2, and 3 share identical fixed effects but differ in their random effects structure. Likelihood ratio tests revealed that adding random slopes for Trial (Model 2) significantly improved fit compared to the random-intercept-only model (Model 1; χ 2 (5) = 5,521.2, p < 0.001). Adding Feature-specific random slopes (Model 3) further improved fit compared to Model 2 (χ 2 (15) = 21,802, p < 0.001). Model 3 was therefore retained as the baseline for subsequent comparisons.

Step 2: Group × Feature interaction (Model 3 vs. 3.1): We next tested whether adding a Group × Feature interaction to the fixed effects (Model 3.1) would improve model fit. The likelihood ratio test was significant (χ 2 (1) = 5.93, p = 0.015), indicating that Model 3.1 provided a better fit in terms of raw likelihood. However, when penalising for model complexity, Model 3 was preferred: BIC favoured Model 3 (378,044) over Model 3.1 (378,050). Although AIC slightly favoured Model 3.1 (∆AIC = 3.9), BIC imposes a stronger penalty for additional parameters and is more appropriate given our large sample size. Model 3 was therefore retained.

Step 3: Three-way interactions (Model 3 vs. 4): Finally, we tested whether adding three-way interactions (Group × Feature × Trial) would improve fit. The likelihood ratio test comparing Model 3 to Model 4 was not significant (χ 2 (3) = 6.35, p = 0.096), indicating that the three-way interactions did not reliably improve model fit.

Therefore, Model 3 was selected as the final model, as it provided the best balance between fit and parsimony.
