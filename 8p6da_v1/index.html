Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Marginal Significance works

The marginal module searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The list of terms is a subset of those listed in a blog post by Matthew Hankins. The module returns all sentences that match terms describing ‘marginally significant’ results.

Some of the terms identified might not be problematic in some contexts, and there are ways to describe ‘marginal significance’ that are not detected by the module.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Abstract

Background: Attending school can be emotionally distressing for some children and adolescents. When school attendance becomes difficult for a child and is accompanied by emotional distress, the condition is called school refusal. Contextual factors at the individual, parental, and school levels that interfere with school attendance may complicate school refusal. A 2015 systematic review of psychosocial interventions, including both randomized and non-randomized studies, reported mixed results for treating anxiety in school refusal, while showing promising results for school attendance.

No systematic review or meta-analysis restricted to randomized controlled trials, however, has been conducted. We will therefore conduct a systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials to determine whether psychosocial interventions reduce anxiety and improve school attendance among school-refusing children and adolescents. Methods: Our protocol followed the PRISMA-P guideline. We will include individuallyand cluster-randomized trials in which most children and adolescents are aged 18 years or younger, are enrolled in primary or secondary education, have exhibited school absence, and received psychosocial interventions for school refusal. Our primary aim is to reduce anxiety and increase school attendance. We will search CENTRAL, MEDLINE, PsycINFO, CINAHL, ERIC, ClinicalTrials.gov, and the ICTRP. Two reviewers will independently assess the study selection, data extraction, risk of bias in included studies, and reporting bias. We will also investigate heterogeneity and conduct sensitivity analyses.

We will present 'summary of findings' tables to show the estimated effects and the certainty of the evidence.

Discussion: This will be the first systematic review to include only randomized trials and to broaden the concept of school attendance to capture the child's engagement with learning in education.

INTRODUCTION

Attending school can be emotionally distressing for some children and adolescents (1).

When school attendance becomes difficult and is accompanied by emotional distress, the term school refusal describes the condition. School refusal has traditionally been referred to as school phobia, school anxiety, emotional absenteeism, and, more recently, emotionally based school avoidance/non-attendance (EBSA or EBSNA). These terms share four criteria recently reformulated by Heyne et al. (2) from those originally proposed by Berg in 1969 (3). First, children and adolescents who are reluctant or refuse to attend school experience temporal or chronic emotional distress. This may manifest as excessive fearfulness of attendance, temper tantrums and late arrivals to school, or as school absence ranging from missing a few sessions to missing consecutive weeks, months, or years. Second, children and adolescents do not intend to hide their absence from their caregivers, either at the outset of school refusal or once caregivers discover it.

Third, children and adolescents do not demonstrate severe antisocial behavior. Fourth, caregivers have repeatedly attempted to intervene to secure attendance at some point during the absence period, usually at the beginning, and/or they express their intention for the child to attend school. Children and adolescents who meet the four criteria are said to have the school refusal condition when they 1) are absent for at least 25 percent of school time for two weeks, 2) experience difficulty attending school for two weeks, or 3) are absent for at least 15 percent of school days for a 15-week period (4).

Epidemiological studies in high-income countries indicate that the prevalence of school refusal is approximately 2-7% among primary and secondary school students (5). In the US, the 3-month prevalence of school refusal, defined as school absence due to anxiety and at least a half-day absence reported by both the parent and the child, was 2.0% among a representative sample of 4,500 children aged 9, 11, and 13 years (6). In Switzerland, when school refusal was defined as answering "very true or often true" to the item "I'm afraid of going to school" in a self-report questionnaire, measured at two time points, 57 (6.9%) and 30 (3.6%) of 832 children with mean ages of 13.6 (SD 1.6) and 16.6 (SD 1.6) were identified as having school refusal, respectively (7). In a study using a similar criterion to Heyne et al. above, where self-report of more than 10 days' absence in the last three months defined school refusal alongside students reporting "quite often" to schoolrefusal-related reasons for the absence, 3.6% of students met the condition, totaling 5,465 students aged 11 to 15 years in Norway (8). In Japan, Fukuya et al. followed a cohort of 4,141 children from first grade (6 to 7 years old) into second grade (7 to 8 years old) and fourth grade (9 to 10 years old) (9). The prevalence of school refusal was 1.8% and 2.0%, respectively, based on caregiver report. The authors defined school refusal as having more than one day's absence during the past six months, with the reason being that "the child did not want to go to school".

These prevalences appear to have risen during and after the COVID-19 pandemic.

Although not exclusively about school refusal, the Department for Education in England reported that the proportion of severe absentees, defined as missing 50% or more sessions, increased from 0.81% to 2.26% between 2018/19 and 2024/25 (10). The US Department of Education noted that the proportion of students absent for more than 10% of school days doubled after the pandemic and remained high afterward (11).

Child and adolescent mental health (CAMH) is concerned with school refusal because it is associated with significant emotional distress, most commonly anxiety (5). Given the high prevalence of school refusal after the COVID-19 pandemic, Ratnamohan et al.

emphasized that CAMH services need to address severe school refusal and suggested that school refusal is classified as severe when characterized by complete or near-complete absence lasting one school term or longer (12). Moreover, school refusal, alongside other forms of absence except school dropout or exclusion, is associated with self-harm and suicidal ideation (13) -another area CAMH services should address. Additionally, school refusal is associated with deteriorating mental well-being, which can limit formal education and preparation for adulthood (14, 15). However, treating school refusal as a single disorder, such as a subtype of anxiety disorders, may be unsuccessful. Contextual factors at the individual, parental, and school levels that interfere with school attendance complicate the condition as described below.

At the individual level, mental health professionals should be aware that about half of all anxiety disorders in the general population emerge before age 18, with peak onset at 5.5 and 15.5 years (16). Diverse learning needs such as verbal comprehension difficulties, poor academic achievement, anxiety symptoms related to others' perceptions, and the child's somatic condition are risk factors for school refusal (17). Therapeutic approaches that treat anxiety in children and adolescents may miss the best opportunity to intervene if they do not acknowledge the remarkably early onset of anxiety disorders. Also, focusing only on anxiety may overlook the unmet needs of those with diverse learning needs closely connected to anxiety.

At the family level, school refusal may burden caregivers of school-refusing children and adolescents. When a group of 738 parents of children with school refusal were asked to select the 10 most stressful life events from 18 categories of the modified List of Threatening Life Experiences and rank them, school refusal was the second most stressful life event, after the death of a first-degree relative (18). Parents of children with autism spectrum who refuse to attend school reported that they need to arrive late to work or forgo paid work entirely to care for their child at home (19). A preliminary survey found that 1 in 4 parents of school-refusing children resigned from their employment (20).

Mental health professional and school personnel attitudes toward parents with schoolrefusing children negatively influenced caregiver burden, leaving them feeling blamed (18, 19). On the other hand, family dysfunction (21, 22), parental psychopathology, and maternal overprotection may be other contextual factors at this level (21). Interventions focused solely on the child may overlook the needs of their parents, potentially complicating the child's therapy.

At the school level, contextual factors include bullying (17, 22), perceptions of poor classroom management, and social isolation (17). Environmental sensitivity, defined as heightened responsiveness to environmental stimuli (23), may also play a role in the school environment (24). Without addressing school-based stimuli, returning to school may trigger another episode of school refusal. These contextual factors underlying school refusal may prevent affected children and adolescents from accessing and benefiting from standard anxiety treatments. Therefore, rather than treating anxiety as a single, isolated symptom, it is more useful to conceptualize it as being shaped by contextual factors at the individual, family, and school levels. Accordingly, psychosocial interventions for school refusal should address these contextual factors alongside the child's own anxiety treatment.

Among psychosocial interventions, tailored cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) has become a popular approach for treating emotional distress and improving school attendance (5, 25). School-refusal-focused CBT typically involves families and school personnel, through parent and/or teacher sessions alongside the child's sessions, conjoint sessions including all participating members (26, 27), or by selecting modules that suit the child's psychiatric condition (e.g., anxiety or depressive disorders) and/or the needs of parents and school staff (27). Session content may include psychoeducation, gradual exposure, behavioral therapy and/or cognitive restructuring. In a socially oriented intervention, Brouwer-Borghuis et al. reported a CBT-based alternative educational program (28) in which adolescents learned CBT skills within the program's favorable school climate, defined across five domains: order, safety, and discipline; academic outcomes; social relationships; school facilities; and school connectedness (29).

Adolescents participated in a collaborative approach involving parents, program staff, staff from the original school, the therapist, and the regional student attendance officer.

In a systematic review of psychosocial interventions, Maynard et al. included 8 studies involving 435 school-refusing students, 5 of which incorporated CBT (25). Most interventions were specifically tailored to school refusal. The review reported mixed results for treating anxiety in school refusal, while showing promising results for school attendance. However, the review included non-randomized studies, in which unmeasured confounding could not be excluded. Such confounding could have influenced the estimates and potentially biased the estimated treatment effect in either direction. To our knowledge, no systematic review or meta-analysis restricted to randomized controlled trials has been conducted since the previous review.

Our primary aim is therefore to conduct a systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials to determine whether psychosocial interventions that specifically target school refusal through psychological and social approaches reduce anxiety and improve school attendance among school-refusing children and adolescents.

Our secondary aim is to examine the effects of these psychosocial interventions on depression, self-efficacy, health-related quality of life, family functioning, behavioral problems, functioning and impairment, and any adverse effects.

METHODS

We used the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-analysis Protocols (PRISMA-P) to guide the development of this protocol and presented its checklist in Appendix 1 (30, 31). This systematic review protocol will be registered with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) on 13 October 2026 (registration number: CRD420261528371). If we need to amend this protocol, we will document the date of each amendment, describe the change, and provide the rationale.

Eligibility criteria

Participants

We will include studies whose participants meet the following five criteria: 1) Children and adolescents enrolled in primary or secondary education in any country or region; 2)

The majority of study participants are under 18; 3) School absence is present, regardless of frequency or duration. We will measure school absence using either a self-or caregiverreported questionnaire or a school official record (see the primary outcomes section below); 4) Children and adolescents do not demonstrate antisocial behavior; and 5) Children and adolescents experience emotional distress, which may be clearly documented in the study's inclusion criteria or indirectly described elsewhere in the study's manuscript-for instance, in the aim as a target for reduction or in the methods as an outcome measure-indicating that included participants experience emotional distress.

Our review may include studies of children and adolescents with school absence not explicitly described as school refusal. For example, a study may describe those as truant under the broader definition of truancy: "unwarranted absence from school more generally (2, 32)". Studies with such children and adolescents may be eligible for inclusion when emotional distress is documented under our criteria.

We assume that studies that meet our eligibility criteria satisfy the second and fourth criteria of Heyne et al.'s criteria for school refusal in the introduction section (2). As for the second criterion (i.e., no concealment from the caregivers), the caregivers are likely to know the child's whereabouts at the time of enrollment in the study. As for the fourth criterion (i.e., the caregivers' attempt to intervene), the child's enrollment in the study itself is the caregivers' attempt to support the child's return to education.

Interventions

We will include studies in which the intervention contains psychosocial components specifically targeting school refusal, as claimed by the study's authors. We decided to include psychosocial interventions across modalities because our aim is to determine overall effects and because we expect the number of participants will be too small to allow separate analyses of intervention effects within each modality, given the limited number of studies in the previous review (25). Accordingly, our review will include psychological therapies such as cognitive behavioral, behavioral, family-based, play-based, personcentered, and psychodynamic therapies. Social components may include, but are not limited to, peer support groups, adjustments to the school environment, and communitybased approaches.

Interventions may be provided in outpatient or inpatient settings, at home, at school, in other settings, or in combinations of these. Interventions may be provided individually or in groups, with or without caregiver or school staff involvement. We will include interventions of any session duration and varying frequencies. We may include studies in which the intervention aimed at parents/caregivers or school staff only if the studies assess outcomes in their children who meet our inclusion criteria.

If a study has more than two intervention arms, we may combine all arms that meet our inclusion criteria and compare the combined intervention with a control arm. Otherwise, we discard intervention arms that do not meet our inclusion criteria and use a single intervention arm and a control arm.

We will combine all eligible studies with a variety of intervention components and participant types in a meta-analysis for each outcome. Heterogeneity may arise in such an analysis; we will conduct subgroup analyses by modality (i.e., CBT versus other modalities) and by participants (i.e., with or without school staff involvement). When studies use pharmacotherapy as a co-intervention, we will include them and conduct a subgroup analysis examining heterogeneity between studies with and without cointervention.

Comparators

Comparators will include no intervention, wait-list control, usual care, and active control (e.g., pharmacotherapy, psychotherapy not specifically tailored for school refusal). We will define usual care as care provided by local healthcare or educational services. Caregivers, their children, and/or adolescents seek this care, or local authorities order it, to re-engage school-refusing children and adolescents in formal education.

We will include studies with an active control in our systematic review but exclude them from meta-analyses, as we intend to examine how the condition of school-refusing children and adolescents improves when novel interventions are implemented, with only non-established, conventional care provided in the control arm.

Studies with more than two comparators will also be included. Regarding cointerventions, such as usual care provided to both intervention and control arms in a study, we plan to include such studies.

Study design

We will include only randomized controlled trials. That is, only studies that explicitly use a randomization procedure to assign interventions are eligible. We will include individually randomized trials and cluster-randomized trials.

Primary outcomes

Our primary outcomes will be school attendance and anxiety. We decided to include anxiety as a primary outcome among the forms of emotional distress, examples of which were provided by Heyne et al. (2), because it is a main point of contrast between children and adolescents with and without school refusal (17). Anxiety also appears more specifically associated with school refusal than depression-another form of emotional distress-which is linked to both school refusal and truancy, another form of absence (6, 33).

We will define school attendance as the proportion of attendance relative to the total attendance required during the specified period. For example, attendance can be measured as hours spent in the classroom or the number of days/half days present at school. The total attendance and the assessment period can also be the number of hours school was in session and five days/two weeks preceding the time points, respectively. We will focus on the mean difference (MD) in proportions between the intervention and comparison groups. When a study reports several proportions of school attendance for a group, we will select the outcome to extract in the following order: 1.

School attendance is prospectively and officially recorded in the school's or the local authority's registry 2. School attendance is prospectively recorded as reported by the child or caregiver.

3. School attendance is retrospectively recorded in any way.

We will include school attendance as both returning to conventional schools and attending alternative education settings. Wherever possible, we will also include "school attendance" for self-directed, home-based education (e.g., homeschooling) (34), as long as the attendance is recorded as described previously above. We broadened the concept of school attendance, ensuring that our review captures the participant's engagement with learning in education (35).

We will define anxiety as a patient-reported outcome measured using standardized, validated self-report questionnaires, such as the Spence Children's Anxiety Scale (Spence, 1998), the Screen for Child Anxiety Related Disorders (36), or the Revised Children's Manifest Anxiety Scale (37). In studies that do not use validated scales, we will include data on author-defined patient-reported anxiety symptoms and conduct a sensitivity analysis to assess the impact of including unvalidated questionnaires. For studies that report more than one scale for an anxiety outcome, we will prioritize scales for inclusion in the following order: 1.

A scale that is a primary outcome of the study 2. A standardized scale in children and adolescents 3. A scale that is not validated in children and adolescents.

Secondary outcomes

1. Depression. We will define depression as a patient-reported outcome measured using standardized, validated self-report questionnaires, such as the Mood and Feelings Questionnaire (38) or the Children's Depression Inventory (39). For studies that do not use validated scales or that report more than one scale for a depression outcome, we will prioritize these scales for inclusion, as described for an anxiety outcome.

2. Self-efficacy. We will include self-efficacy, measured using standardized scales such as the Self-Efficacy Questionnaire for School Situations (40).

3. Health-related quality of life. We will include standardized scales such as KIDSCREEN 10 (41). 4. Functioning and impairment. We will include standardized scales, such as the Strengths and Difficulties Questionnaire (42).

5. Behavioral problems. We will include standardized scales such as the Child Behavior Checklist (43). 6. Family functioning. We will include standardized scales such as the Family Assessment Device (44).

7. Any adverse effects. We will include any adverse events (e.g., dropout, conflict with caregivers or school staff) during treatment and follow-up.

Timing of outcome measurement

We will define three time points for all primary and secondary outcomes: immediately after treatment, short-term (less than 12 months), and long-term (12 months or more). For adverse events, we will seek data during treatment and at follow-up.

Search methods for the identification of studies

We will not impose any restrictions, such as language or publication status, on the searches outlined below.

Electronic searches

We will identify relevant trials from the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL), MEDLINE (Ovid), PsycInfo, CINAHL, ERIC (eric.ed.gov), ClinicalTrials.gov (clinicaltrials.gov), and the World Health Organization International Clinical Trials Registry Platform (ICTRP; trialsearch.who.int). We will report the date of the most recent search and any updates we will make to the search.

Searching other sources

We will search the reference lists of relevant articles to identify potentially eligible studies.

Search strategy

The search terms for the databases listed above are provided in Appendix 2. To search articles on children and adolescents, we modified a Special Ovid Filter for MEDLINE (https://ospguides.ovid.com/OSPguides/medline.htm) by omitting terms related to infants.

For synonyms of school refusal, we consulted Heyne et al. (2).

Data collection and analysis

Data management

We will upload the results of the literature search to Rayyan, a web-based collaborative screening tool (45). We will also upload citation abstracts and full-text articles for data extraction to Covidence, another web-based collaboration software platform (46).

Selection of studies

Several teams of two review authors (AS will complete all; SF, YL, MS, and MK will complete a quarter each) will independently screen the titles and abstracts for all citations identified by our literature search and assess them against the eligibility criteria. We will obtain full-text articles for all titles and abstracts that appear to meet the eligibility criteria.

Several teams of two review authors (AS will complete all; SF, YL, MS, and MK will complete a quarter each) will then screen the full-text articles against the eligibility criteria and decide whether to include them in our review. Before the formal screening process begins, we will conduct a calibration exercise to ensure consistency in screening among reviewers. Where necessary to resolve questions about eligibility, we will contact study authors for further information. We will resolve discrepancies between the two review authors through discussion. When they do not reach consensus, a third review author (SF or YL) will resolve the differences. We will also record the reasons for excluding studies.

Data extraction

Two review authors (AS will complete all; SF and YL will complete half each) will independently extract data using a pre-piloted form, and a third review author (MS) will cross-check the extracted data. We will ensure consistency among reviewers by conducting calibration exercises before the review begins. For trials with multiple publications, we will use only the most up-to-date data or the complete data for each outcome. If the necessary data are not reported, or are not reported in a form suitable for direct meta-analysis, we will contact the study authors for further information.

Data items to be extracted

We will extract participant characteristics: mean age with a measure of variability (e.g., SD), gender, ethnicity, level of education, academic performance, level of verbal comprehension, and environmental sensitivity. We will extract school refusal-related characteristics: the pre-specified period the study uses to assess the severity of school absence (e.g., past two weeks, past three months), the amount of current absence (e.g., hours or days of school absence), the proportion of current absence or its qualitative description (e.g., intermittent or partial) during the pre-specified period, the definition of school refusal in the study (e.g., Berg's criteria or its modification), number of prior episodes of school refusal, age and duration of the first episode of school refusal. We will extract symptom-related characteristics: developmental and/or psychiatric disorder(s), somatic conditions, prior psychiatric hospitalisations, adverse childhood experiences relating to the child, including physical, emotional, and sexual abuse, and physical and emotional neglect.

We will extract characteristics relating to family: number and age of caregivers in the household, number of siblings in the household, current or past history of school refusal among siblings or caregivers, change in employment after the child's school refusal, type of parenting style, perceived stress from other adults after the child's school refusal, the socioeconomic status of parent(s), and adverse childhood experiences relating to family (i.e., family dysfunction), including parental psychopathology.

For school-related characteristics, we will extract bullying and/or cyberbullying, corporal punishment by school staff, perceptions of poor classroom management, social isolation.

For psychosocial interventions, we will extract theoretical orientations (e.g., cognitive therapy, psychoanalysis) and format of delivery (e.g., individual/family/group; inperson/online) of the intervention, the total number of sessions, session duration, session content (e.g., counselling vs. school meetings vs. parent training), the frequency of sessions, involvement of adult participants (e.g., parents, school personnel), a brief description of the intervention, the extent to which the intervention is tailored to an individual, and the type of comparator. For comparators, we will extract the same details of usual care and active comparators as specified for the intervention arm above.

We will also extract the trial design (e.g., individually-or cluster-randomised), the year and country in which the trial took place, the sample size, the follow-up duration, and the source of financial support.

Assessment of risk of bias in included studies

Two review authors (AS will complete all, SF and YL will complete half each) will independently assess the risk of bias in included studies for all outcomes using the Cochrane's revised Risk of Bias in Randomized Trials (RoB 2) tool (47). The types of bias include the following domains: bias arising from the randomization process; bias due to deviations from the intended intervention; bias due to outcome data; bias in the measurement of the outcome; bias in the selection of the reported results; and overall bias.

We will assess the primary and secondary outcomes included in the 'Summary of findings' tables and focus on the assessment of the effect of assignment to the interventions at baseline (i.e., 'intention-to-treat effect'). For the first five domains, we will use the signaling questions in the RoB 2 tool to assess the risk of bias as low risk, some concerns, or high risk. For the last domain, we will categorize the overall risk-of-bias judgment as follows: l Low risk of bias: the study is considered to show a low risk of bias.

l Some concerns: a few concerns are expected to be associated with the study in at least one domain, but they do not warrant categorization as a study with a high risk of bias in any domain.

l High risk of bias: the study is considered at high risk of bias in at least one domain or exhibits a few concerns across multiple domains that significantly lower confidence in the study results.

We will use the RoB 2 Excel tool to implement RoB 2 (available from riskofbias.info) and summarize our findings in the 'risk of bias' tables. We will resolve any disagreements first by discussion between the two review authors and then by consulting a third review author (SF or YL).

Measures of treatment effect

For categorical data, we will use the ratio of the number of people who present with the outcome to the number of people at risk in each group to calculate a risk ratio (RR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI).

When a study uses a dichotomous outcome as an effect measure for school attendance, we will try to convert it to an MD wherever possible. For example, when participants who returned to a school are compared with those who did not, and the comparison is expressed as a dichotomous outcome, such as an odds ratio, we will obtain an MD from a standardized mean difference (SMD) and a standard deviation (SD); the former is directly calculated from the odds ratio and the latter from a standard error (SE) derived from the SEs for the odds ratio, as specified in Chapters 6.5.2.3 and 10.6 of the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions (48, 49). Otherwise, we will use the measure as is.

For continuous data, when studies use the same scale, we will use means to calculate an MD and the number of participants and SDs to calculate a 95% CI. When studies use different scales, we will use an MD and SDs to calculate an SMD with a 95% CI, except for the school attendance outcome, for which we aim to use MDs as described above.

When an outcome is reported either as post-intervention or change-from-baseline values across studies, we will use post-intervention SDs to calculate an SMD as described in Chapter 10.5.2 of the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions (48).

If data to calculate RRs or MDs are not reported, we will use the most appropriate numerical data available to calculate the actual numbers or means and SDs (e.g., SEs, confidence intervals, test statistics, or P values).

Unit of analysis issues

For studies with multiple groups, we will combine participants across interventions or controls into a single intervention or control group, respectively, to avoid double-counting, loss of information from omitting some arms, or unnecessary correlation from splitting a single arm into two groups. Our review focuses on the overall effect of psychosocial interventions on school refusal. Combining treatment groups should therefore be appropriate for this review.

For cluster-randomized trials that estimate the effect measure using an appropriate analysis accounting for the cluster design (such as a multilevel model or generalized estimating equations), we will extract an effect estimate directly. For cluster-randomized trials that did not report appropriate analyses, we will include them as long as the following data are available: the number of clusters randomized to each intervention group; the total number of participants in the study or the average size of each cluster; the summary data; and the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC). We will attempt to correct the analysis, using the methods described in Chapter 23.1 of the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions (50).

Dealing with missing data

We will contact the authors to request missing outcome data (e.g., effect estimates in the study report) and/or summary data (e.g., means and SDs for each intervention group).

Where this is not possible, we will proceed as follows. We will use effect estimates directly when summary data for each group in a study are unavailable or when the study used sophisticated analyses such as mixed-effects models for repeated measurements or multiple imputation methods. In these cases, we will use confidence intervals or P-values to calculate SEs. For studies with missing SDs, we will calculate them using standard errors, CIs, t-statistics, or P-values wherever possible. If we cannot obtain these values for such calculation, we will impute SDs from either other studies in the same analysis or from studies in another published meta-analysis, as per the guidance in Chapter 6.5.2 of the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions (49), and conduct a sensitivity analysis to assess the impact of imputed SDs.

Data synthesis

We will include all studies that provided data regardless of the overall risk of bias as assessed by the RoB 2 tool. We will use Review Manager (RevMan) for data synthesis.

We will perform a meta-analysis if participants, interventions, comparisons, and outcomes are judged to be sufficiently similar against our eligibility criteria. Otherwise, we will report them narratively. We will pool RRs for dichotomous outcomes and present MDs or SMDs for continuous outcomes. We will use a random-effects model with the generic inverse-variance method to perform a meta-analysis, as we anticipate considerable heterogeneity across studies.

Assessment of heterogeneity

We will assess the heterogeneity of each outcome across studies using visual inspection of forest plots, the Chi² test for heterogeneity, the I² statistic, and the estimate of betweenstudy variance (Tau 2 ). We will calculate the 95% prediction intervals using Tau 2 when the number of studies is five or more, and there is no clear funnel plot asymmetry. We will interpret the I² statistic according to Chapter 10.10.2 of the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions (48), which suggests that: values of 0% to 40% might not be important; 30% to 60% may represent moderate heterogeneity; 50% to 90% may represent substantial heterogeneity; 75% to 100% considerable heterogeneity (48).

We will aim to determine and discuss possible explanations for heterogeneity.

Subgroup analysis and investigation of heterogeneity

If the data allow, we will perform the following subgroup analyses to explore potential explanations for any heterogeneity we detect.

l Severity of school refusal: We will compare studies with high versus low proportions of 'severe or extreme' school refusal (12). That is, studies in which more than half of the participants had at least 50% school absence versus those in which fewer than half did. l Level of education: We will compare studies in which the majority of participants are enrolled in primary education with those in which the majority are not.

l Group of participants involved: We will compare studies in which the intervention targets children and/or adolescents and/or their caregivers alone with those in which it also targets school staff.

l Type of intervention modality: We will compare studies in which cognitive behavior techniques (e.g., cognitive restructuring, graded exposure) are key components with studies that employ other modalities (e.g., play therapy, family therapy, psychodynamic psychotherapy).

l Use of pharmacotherapy as co-intervention: We will compare studies without pharmacotherapy with those with pharmacotherapy.

Assessment of reporting biases

We will assess the risk of bias arising from non-reporting and selective reporting using ROB-ME, a tool for assessing Risk Of Bias due to Missing Evidence in a meta-analysis available at www.riskofbias.info/welcome/rob-me-tool (51), which framework is described in Chapter 13 of the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions (52). We will assess each meta-analysis of our primary and secondary outcomes that are included in our 'summary of findings' tables. Implementation of ROB-ME will be similar to that of the RoB 2 tool, with a series of signaling questions that lead to a judgment about the risk of bias due to missing evidence in meta-analyses included in the review. Two review authors (AS will complete all; SF and YL will complete half each) will independently assess the risk of bias due to missing evidence for each meta-analysis.

We will resolve discrepancies between the two review authors through discussion. We will generate and visually inspect funnel plots and perform an Egger's test when the metaanalysis includes 10 studies or more (53).

Sensitivity analysis

We will perform sensitivity analyses to examine the robustness of our findings as follows: l Excluding studies with non-validated scales.

l Excluding studies with wait-list comparisons.

l Excluding studies with missing SDs for each outcome.

l Excluding studies at high risk of bias for each outcome included in the 'Summary of findings' tables.

Summary of findings and assessment of the certainty of the evidence

We will produce 'Summary of findings' tables to present the estimated effects for all primary and secondary outcomes and the amount of pooled data on which they are based.

We will use the time point immediately after treatment, except for adverse events, which we will assess during treatment and follow-up. We will also assess the certainty of the evidence for each outcome using the GRADE approach (54), which considers five domains: risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias in the studies pooled for each outcome of interest. Two review authors (AS will complete all; SF and YL will complete half each) will independently decide the extent to which the certainty of the evidence should be downgraded, resolving any disagreements first through discussion between the two review authors and then by consulting a third review author (SF or YL). We will use GRADEpro GDT software to produce the 'Summary of findings' tables (55).

DISCUSSION

To our knowledge, this will be the first systematic review to rigorously estimate the effects of psychosocial interventions for school refusal by including only randomized trials and to broaden the concept of attendance to capture the participant's engagement with learning in education. The strengths of our review include: 1) we will extend the school attendance outcome to include attendance at alternative education settings as well as at previously attended conventional schools; 2) we will set anxiety as another primary outcome because how anxiously children and adolescents experience school is important.

A limitation is that not all forms of alternative education may be included in our review.

For example, homeschooling may not be included as a school attendance outcome because studies do not report attendance in a way that allows inclusion in our review, given the nature of schooling at home.
