Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

What we found

We found 6 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

OBJECTIVE To examine the association between social media addiction and mental health among members of Generation Z in Greece. Moreover, we examined the association between social media addiction and social media-related behaviours.

We conducted a web-based cross-sectional study with a convenience sample of 599 individuals aged 18 to 29 years. We measured social media addiction with the Bergen Social Media Addiction Scale. Outcomes were anxiety, depression, loneliness, social media fatigue, sleep quality, life satisfaction, physician visits for mental health issues, artificial intelligence (AI) chatbot use for mental health issues, second-screen use, social media distraction, fear of missing out, and no-social-media phobia. We performed multivariable logistic regression analysis adjusting for sex, age, and financial status, and we examined interactions between social media addiction and these variables. RESULTS Overall, 56.6% of participants were addicted to social media. The prevalence of anxiety was 57.5% among addicted participants and 38.1% among non-addicted participants, while the prevalence of depression was 39.2% and 25.8%, respectively. After adjustment for confounders, social media addiction was associated with higher odds of anxiety, depression, loneliness, social media fatigue, poor sleep quality, life dissatisfaction, and AI chatbot use for mental health issues. Also, social media addiction was associated with higher odds of second-screen use, social media distraction, fear of missing out, and nosocial-media phobia. Sex, age, and financial status did not substantially modify these associations. CONCLUSIONS Social media addiction is highly prevalent among Generation Z and is associated with several mental health problems and problematic digital behaviours. Interventions to prevent social media addiction and to facilitate access to professional mental health care among young people are needed.

Introduction

Social media have become an integral part of everyday life, with more than five billion people using social media platforms regularly worldwide. The average time spent on social media has increased steadily, from 90 min per day in 2012 to 143 min per day in 2024. 1 Young people are the most intensive users of these platforms.

Generation Z, i.e., individuals born between 1997 and 2012, 2 is the first generation to have grown up entirely within a digital environment, and a substantial proportion of its members created social media accounts in early adolescence. 3 In Greece, a recent study showed that members of Generation Z spent on average 4.0 h per day on social media, compared with 2.9 h among Millennials and 2.1 h among Generation X, and also had the highest levels of problematic social media use. 4 Although social media offer opportunities for communication, information, and selfexpression, a growing body of evidence suggests that a subgroup of users develops a maladaptive pattern of use. Social media addiction, also referred to as problematic social media use, is characterised by symptoms similar to those of substance use disorders and other behavioural addictions, namely salience, mood modification, tolerance, withdrawal, conflict, and relapse. 5, 6 Several features of social media platforms, such as infinite scrolling, algorithmically personalised content, and variable reward schedules, may reinforce compulsive use, since each new piece of content functions as a potential reward. 7 Although social media addiction is not formally recognised as a mental disorder, scholars increasingly conceptualise it within a behavioural addiction framework and consider it a public health concern. 1, 8 A metaanalysis including 51 studies estimated the global prevalence of social media addiction among university students at 18.4%, 9 while another meta-analysis of 139 studies from 32 countries using the Bergen Social Media Addiction Scale found that prevalence was higher in low-income countries. 10 The association between social media use and mental health appears to depend on how social media use is measured. Studies measuring time spent on social media have found small and often inconsistent associations with depression and anxiety, whereas studies measuring problematic or addictive use have found more robust associations. 4, 11 In particular, a meta-analysis of 18 studies among adolescents and young adults showed moderate positive correlations between problematic social media use and depression, anxiety, and stress. 12 Similarly, problematic Facebook use was positively correlated with psychological distress and negatively correlated with well-being, 13 and problematic social media use was strongly associated with social anxiety. 14 Moreover, excessive and problematic social media use has been associated with poor sleep quality, which may in turn mediate the association between social media use and poor mental health. 11, 15 Regarding platform-specific addiction, a metaanalysis found that problematic TikTok use was positively associated with depression and anxiety, 16 while in Greece problematic TikTok use has been associated with anxiety, depression, poor sleep quality, loneliness, and procrastination. 17, 18 Nevertheless, the consequences of social media addiction may extend beyond psychological symptoms. Individuals with social media addiction may differ in the way they seek help for mental health issues, and the rapid diffusion of artificial intelligence (AI) chatbots offers young people a new, easily accessible source of mental health advice. Moreover, social media addiction may be accompanied by other problematic patterns of digital behaviour, such as fear of missing out, 19 distraction from daily activities, 3 use of social media as a second screen while watching television or streaming content, and fear of being unable to access social media.

However, these outcomes have rarely been examined together with mental health outcomes in the same population.

The growing concerns about the impact of social media on young people have also triggered policy responses. For example, Australia introduced legislation establishing a minimum age of 16 years for social media accounts, 20 while in Greece most adults agreed with the implementation of a social media ban for children. 21 Moreover, a recent randomised clinical trial showed that health warnings on social media discouraged teenagers and young adults from wanting to use social media. 22 Such policies require robust evidence on the populations at highest risk. However, most studies in Greece have included participants from different generations and have focused on a limited number of mental health outcomes. 4, 18 To the best of our knowledge, no study has examined the association between social media addiction and a broad range of mental health outcomes, help-seeking behaviours, and social media-related behaviours specifically among Generation Z in Greece.

Therefore, the aim of our study was to examine the association between social media addiction and mental health among members of Generation Z in Greece. In particular, we examined the association between social media addiction and anxiety, depression, loneliness, social media fatigue, sleep quality, life satisfaction, doctor visits for mental health issues, and AI chatbot use for mental health issues. Moreover, we examined the association between social media addiction and second-screen use, social media distraction, fear of missing out related to social media, and no-social-media phobia.

Finally, we examined whether sex, age, and financial status modified these associations.

Materials and Methods

Study design

We conducted a web-based cross-sectional study in Greece. We developed an online questionnaire in Google Forms and disseminated it through Facebook, Instagram and TikTok. Eligible participants were adults aged 18 years or older belonging to Generation Z, i.e., born between 1997 and 2012; 2 thus, our sample included individuals aged 18 to 29 years. We did not collect any information that could identify participants, such as names, email addresses, phone numbers, or social media usernames. Google Forms was configured not to record IP addresses or email addresses, and participants were not required to sign in with a Google account. Before starting the questionnaire, participants were informed about the aim and design of the study, the content of the questions, the estimated completion time, the voluntary nature of participation, and their right to withdraw at any time by closing their browser; our contact details were also provided. To minimise duplicate responses, participants were instructed to complete the questionnaire only once, and those who reported that they had already completed it were excluded. Thus, we obtained a convenience sample. Data collection took place between June and November 2026.

After completing the questionnaire, participants were informed that free psychological support was available 24 h a day via a telephone helpline, and the helpline number was provided. Participants with high anxiety or depression scores were also advised to consult a mental health professional where feasible. We applied the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines 23 in our study.

We calculated the sample size with G*Power version 3.1.9.2. We considered a logistic regression model with social media addiction (addicted vs. non-addicted) as the independent variable and assumed that half of participants would be addicted to social media. Assuming a small effect size (odds ratio=1.68), 24 a probability of the outcome of 0.30 among non-addicted participants, a squared multiple correlation of 0.10 between social media addiction and the confounders (sex, age, and financial status), an alpha level of 5%, and a statistical power of 80%, the minimum required sample size was 569 participants. Our final sample of 599 participants exceeded this requirement.

Measurements

We measured the following demographic variables: (a) sex (males or females), (b) age (continuous variable), (c) and financial status. We measured financial status as a selfperceived variable on a scale from 0 (very low) to 10 (very high). Moreover, we asked participants how many hours per day they spent on social media.

We used the Bergen Social Media Addiction Scale (BSMAS) 25 to measure social media addiction. The BSMAS comprises six items reflecting the core components of behavioural addiction, namely salience, mood modification, tolerance, withdrawal, conflict, and relapse, with reference to the preceding 12 months. Responses are given on a five-point Likert scale from 1 (very rarely) to 5 (very often), yielding a total score from 6 to 30, with higher scores indicating higher levels of social media addiction. Participants with a score ≥13 were classified as addicted to social media. 26 We used the valid Greek version of the BSMAS. 26 In our study, Cronbach's alpha for the BSMAS was 0.838.

We used the Patient Health Questionnaire-4 (PHQ-4) 27 to measure anxiety and depressive symptoms over the preceding two weeks. The PHQ-4 includes two items for anxiety and two items for depression, each rated on a four-point Likert scale from 0 (not at all) to 3 (nearly every day). Subscale scores range from 0 to 6, and a score ≥3 on each subscale indicates clinically relevant anxiety or depressive symptoms, respectively. We used the valid Greek version of the PHQ-4. 28 In our study, Cronbach's alpha was 0.848 for the total PHQ-4, 0.814 for the anxiety subscale, and 0.749 for the depression subscale.

We used the three-item UCLA Loneliness Scale (UCLA-3) 29 to measure loneliness.

Each item is rated on a three-point scale from 1 (hardly ever) to 3 (often), yielding a total score from 3 to 9, with higher scores indicating greater loneliness. Participants with a score ≥5 were classified as experiencing high levels of loneliness. We used the valid Greek version of the UCLA-3. 30 In our study, Cronbach's alpha was 0.797.

We used the Social Media Fatigue Scale-3 items (SMFS-3) 31 to measure fatigue from social media use. Items are rated on a five-point Likert scale from 1 (very rarely) to 5 (very often), yielding a total score from 3 to 15, with higher scores indicating greater fatigue. Participants with a score ≥8 were classified as experiencing social media fatigue. In our study, Cronbach's alpha was 0.759. We used the Sleep Quality Scale (SQS) 32 to measure sleep quality. Participants rated their overall sleep quality during the preceding seven days on a single item from 0 (terrible) to 10 (excellent), considering total sleep duration, ease of falling asleep, night-time awakenings, early awakening, and feeling refreshed upon awakening.

Participants with a score ≤6 were classified as having poor sleep quality, whereas those with a score of 7 to 10 were classified as having good sleep quality. We used the valid Greek version of the SQS. 33 We used the Satisfaction With Life Scale (SWLS) 34 to measure life satisfaction. The SWLS comprises five items rated on a seven-point Likert scale from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree), yielding a total score from 5 to 35, with higher scores indicating greater life satisfaction. Participants with a score ≤24 were classified as not satisfied with their life. In our study, Cronbach's alpha was 0.828.

Participants were asked how often they had visited a doctor for mental health issues, on a scale from 0 (not at all) to 10 (very much). Similarly, the frequency of use of AI chatbots (e.g., ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude, Copilot) for mental health issues was measured with the single-item AI Chatbot Use for Mental Health scale (AICU-MH), on a scale from 0 (not at all) to 10 (very much). For both variables, participants with a score of 0 were classified as not having visited a doctor or not using chatbots, respectively, whereas those with a score ≥1 were classified as having visited a doctor or using chatbots for mental health issues.

We measured five social media-related behaviours with single-item scales developed by our research team, each rated from 0 to 10, with higher scores indicating higher levels of the respective behaviour. In particular, we used the Social Media Second-Screen Use scale to measure second-screen use while watching television or streaming content alone (solo viewing) and with others (co-viewing); participants who reported any second-screen use (score ≥1) were classified as second-screen users. We used the Social Media Distraction Scale (SMDS) to measure distraction caused by social media; participants with a score ≥3 were classified as distracted individuals. We used the Single-Item Social Media Fear of Missing Out Scale (SM-FoMOS) to measure fear of missing out related to social media; participants with a score ≥2 were classified as experiencing fear of missing out. Finally, we used the No-Social-Media Phobia Scale (NoSMPS) to measure the fear of being unable to access social media; participants with a score ≥2 were classified as experiencing no-social-media phobia.

Ethical issues

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. 35 The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Nursing, National and Kapodistrian University of Athens (Protocol Approval #01, September 14, 2025).

Before accessing the online questionnaire, participants were presented with an informed consent statement, and only those who provided their consent could proceed. We did not collect any data that could identify participants; thus, participation was anonymous and voluntary.

Statistical analysis

We present categorical variables as numbers and percentages, and continuous variables as means, standard deviations (SD), medians, interquartile ranges, skewness, and kurtosis. We used the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test and Q-Q plots to examine the distribution of continuous variables. Social media addiction (BSMAS score) was the independent variable. Our dependent variables were anxiety, depression, loneliness, social media fatigue, poor sleep quality, life dissatisfaction, doctor visits for mental health issues, AI chatbot use for mental health issues, second-screen use alone, second-screen use with others, social media distraction, fear of missing out related to social media, and no-social-media phobia. All dependent variables were analysed as dichotomous variables according to the cut-offs described above. Sex, age, and financial status were considered potential confounders.

First, we compared the prevalence of each outcome between participants who were addicted and those who were not addicted to social media with the chi-square test.

Then, we performed binary logistic regression analyses for each outcome. We constructed univariate models with the BSMAS score as the independent variable, and multivariable models adjusted for sex, age, and financial status. The BSMAS score was entered as a continuous variable in our main analysis, while in a secondary analysis we entered social media addiction as a dichotomous variable (addicted vs. non-addicted). We present crude and adjusted odds ratios (OR), 95% confidence intervals (CI), and p-values. We assessed the goodness of fit of the multivariable models with the Hosmer-Lemeshow test, and we report Nagelkerke R² values. We assessed multicollinearity between independent variables by calculating variance inflation factors (VIFs), with values greater than 4 indicating multicollinearity. 36 Moreover, we confirmed that the number of events per variable exceeded nine in all multivariable models. We examined whether sex, age, and financial status modified the association between social media addiction and each outcome by adding the corresponding interaction term (BSMAS score × sex, BSMAS score × age, BSMAS score × financial status) to the multivariable models, one at a time. Continuous variables were mean-centred before the calculation of the interaction terms. We assessed the statistical significance of each interaction term with the likelihood ratio test. When an interaction term was statistically significant, we performed simple slopes analysis by estimating the association between the BSMAS score and the outcome in males and females separately, or at the mean and ±1 SD of age and financial status. Since these analyses involved 39 interaction tests, we considered them exploratory, and we also report the Bonferroni-corrected threshold (p<0.0013). P-values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant. We used the IBM SPSS 28.0 (IBM Corp. Released 2021. IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 28.0. Armonk, NY: IBM Corp) for the analysis.

Results

Demographic characteristics

Our sample included 599 participants from Generation Z. Of these, 80.6% (n=483) were females and 19.4% (n=116) were males. The mean age was 21.1 years (SD; 3.0), and the median age was 21.0 years (range; 18-29). The mean self-rated financial status was 5.9 (SD; 1.6) on a 0-10 scale, indicating a moderate level. Participants reported a median daily social media use of 4.0 h (interquartile range; 3.0-5.0). Table 1 presents the demographic characteristics of our sample.

Study scales

The mean BSMAS score was 14.04 (SD; 4.98), and 56.6% of participants (n=339) were classified as addicted to social media. The mean PHQ-4 anxiety score was 2.74 (SD; 1.82) and the mean PHQ-4 depression score was 2.12 (SD; 1.70). Almost half of participants (49.1%, n=294) reported clinically relevant anxiety symptoms, and one in three (33.4%, n=200) reported clinically relevant depressive symptoms. Moreover, 51.4% of participants experienced high levels of loneliness, 72.8% experienced social media fatigue, 57.1% reported poor sleep quality, and 68.4% were not satisfied with their life. Also, 39.7% of participants had visited a doctor for mental health issues, and 74.3% had used AI chatbots for mental health issues. Regarding social mediarelated behaviours, the prevalence was 93.8% for second-screen use alone, 81.0% for second-screen use with others, 79.8% for social media distraction, 78.5% for fear of missing out related to social media, and 84.8% for no-social-media phobia.

Descriptive statistics for the study scales are shown in Table 2. All multi-item scales showed acceptable to good internal consistency, with Cronbach's alpha ranging from 0.749 to 0.848. Prevalence of mental health problems and social media-related behaviours according to social media addiction status

Participants who were addicted to social media reported a higher prevalence of all outcomes except doctor visits for mental health issues (Table 3). In particular, the prevalence of clinically relevant anxiety symptoms was 57.5% among addicted participants, compared with 38.1% among non-addicted participants (χ2 =22.26, p<0.001). Similarly, the prevalence of clinically relevant depressive symptoms was 39.2% among addicted participants and 25.8% among non-addicted participants (χ2 =11.99, p<0.001). Moreover, addicted participants more frequently experienced high levels of loneliness (59.6% vs. 40.8%), poor sleep quality (66.1% vs. 45.4%), and life dissatisfaction (74.0% vs. 61.2%) than non-addicted participants (p<0.001 in all cases). The largest differences were observed for social media fatigue (91.2% vs.

48.8%), social media distraction (92.0% vs. 63.8%), and fear of missing out related to social media (90.0% vs. 63.5%). Additionally, 80.8% of addicted participants used AI chatbots for mental health issues, compared with 65.8% of non-addicted participants (p<0.001). In contrast, the proportion of participants who had visited a doctor for mental health issues did not differ significantly between addicted (41.9%) and nonaddicted (36.9%) participants (χ2 =1.51, p=0.218).

Association between social media addiction and study outcomes

Table 4 presents the results of the logistic regression analyses with the BSMAS score as a continuous independent variable. After adjustment for sex, age, and financial status, higher BSMAS scores were associated with higher odds of anxiety (adjusted OR=1.089, 95% CI=1.051 to 1.128, p<0.001), depression (adjusted OR=1.123, 95% CI=1.082 to 1.166, p<0.001), loneliness (adjusted OR=1.093, 95% CI=1.054 to 1.132, p<0.001), social media fatigue (adjusted OR=1.418, 95% CI=1.328 to 1.515, p<0.001), poor sleep quality (adjusted OR=1.081, 95% CI=1.043 to 1.120, p<0.001), and life dissatisfaction (adjusted OR=1.068, 95% CI=1.028 to 1.109, p<0.001).

Moreover, higher BSMAS scores were associated with higher odds of AI chatbot use for mental health issues (adjusted OR=1.106, 95% CI=1.060 to 1.154, p<0.001). In contrast, we found no statistically significant association between the BSMAS score and doctor visits for mental health issues (adjusted OR=1.027, 95% CI=0.993 to 1.062, p=0.122).

Regarding social media-related behaviours, higher BSMAS scores were associated with higher odds of second-screen use alone (adjusted OR=1.183, 95% CI=1.082 to 1.293, p<0.001), second-screen use with others (adjusted OR=1.189, 95% CI=1.127 to 1.255, p<0.001), social media distraction (adjusted OR=1.313, 95% CI=1.233 to 1.398, p<0.001), fear of missing out related to social media (adjusted OR=1.274, 95% CI=1.203 to 1.351, p<0.001), and no-social-media phobia (adjusted OR=1.129, 95% CI=1.069 to 1.192, p<0.001). The Hosmer-Lemeshow test indicated adequate fit for all multivariable models (p-values from 0.114 to 0.978) except the model for nosocial-media phobia (p=0.005). VIFs ranged from 1.008 to 1.047, indicating no multicollinearity. When social media addiction was entered as a dichotomous variable, the findings were similar (Table 5). After adjustment for confounders, addicted participants had approximately twice the odds of anxiety (adjusted OR=2.05, 95% CI=1.46 to 2.86, p<0.001), depression (adjusted OR=1.82, 95% CI=1.27 to 2.60, p=0.001), loneliness (adjusted OR=2.02, 95% CI=1.44 to 2.83, p<0.001), poor sleep quality (adjusted OR=2.28, 95% CI=1.63 to 3.19, p<0.001), life dissatisfaction (adjusted OR=1.87, 95% CI=1.30 to 2.67, p<0.001), and AI chatbot use for mental health issues (adjusted OR=2.14, 95% CI=1.47 to 3.13, p<0.001) compared with non-addicted participants.

The strongest associations were found for social media fatigue (adjusted OR=10.49, 95% CI=6.68 to 16.47, p<0.001), social media distraction (adjusted OR=6.46, 95% CI=4.03 to 10.36, p<0.001), and fear of missing out related to social media (adjusted OR=5.18, 95% CI=3.34 to 8.05, p<0.001). Social media addiction was not associated with doctor visits for mental health issues (adjusted OR=1.18, 95% CI=0.85 to 1.66, p=0.325).

Interaction analysis

Table 6 presents the interaction terms between the BSMAS score and sex, age, and financial status. Age did not modify the association between social media addiction and any outcome (all p-values ≥0.050). Sex modified only the association between the BSMAS score and no-social-media phobia (p=0.022); simple slopes analysis showed that this association was stronger among males (adjusted OR=1.316, 95% CI=1.135 to 1.527, p<0.001) than among females (adjusted OR=1.095, 95% CI=1.032 to 1.163, p=0.003). Financial status modified the association between the BSMAS score and loneliness (p=0.023), with a stronger association among participants with higher financial status (+1 SD: adjusted OR=1.135, 95% CI=1.079 to 1.193, p<0.001) than among those with mean (adjusted OR=1.090, 95% CI=1.052 to 1.130, p<0.001) or lower financial status (-1 SD: adjusted OR=1.048, 95% CI=0.997 to 1.101, p=0.063).

Also, financial status modified the association between the BSMAS score and secondscreen use alone (p=0.029); this association was stronger among participants with lower financial status (-1 SD: adjusted OR=1.319, 95% CI=1.148 to 1.516, p<0.001) than among those with mean (adjusted OR=1.203, 95% CI=1.097 to 1.319, p<0.001) or higher financial status (+1 SD: adjusted OR=1.097, 95% CI=0.997 to 1.207, p=0.057). However, none of these three interaction terms remained statistically significant after Bonferroni correction (p<0.0013). Therefore, the associations between social media addiction and the study outcomes were largely consistent across sex, age, and financial status.

Discussion

In this study, we examined the association between social media addiction and mental health among members of Generation Z in Greece. After adjustment for sex, age, and financial status, social media addiction was associated with higher odds of anxiety, depression, loneliness, social media fatigue, poor sleep quality, life dissatisfaction, and AI chatbot use for mental health issues. Moreover, social media addiction was associated with higher odds of second-screen use, social media distraction, fear of missing out related to social media, and no-social-media phobia. In contrast, social media addiction was not associated with doctor visits for mental health issues. Finally, sex, age, and financial status did not substantially modify these associations.

More than half of our participants (56.6%) were classified as addicted to social media.

This figure is considerably higher than the pooled prevalence of 18.4% among university students worldwide. 9 However, prevalence estimates depend strongly on the instrument and the cut-off applied. 10 We used the cut-off of 13 that was identified as optimal in the validation study of the Greek version of the BSMAS; 26 this cut-off maximises the balance between sensitivity and specificity, and it is therefore expected to yield higher prevalence than stricter thresholds. Notably, the mean BSMAS score in our sample (14.04) was almost identical to the mean score of Generation Z participants in a previous Greek study (13.93), and considerably higher than the scores of Millennials and Generation X. 4 Similarly, the prevalence of clinically relevant anxiety (49.1%) and depressive symptoms (33.4%) in our sample was higher than the prevalence reported in a Greek sample that included all three generations (35.0% and 24.9%, respectively). 4 Taken together, these findings suggest that Generation Z constitutes a high-risk group for both social media addiction and mental health problems.

We found that participants with social media addiction had approximately twice the odds of clinically relevant anxiety and depressive symptoms. Our findings are in line with meta-analyses showing moderate positive correlations between problematic social media use and depression, anxiety, and stress among adolescents and young adults, 12 as well as positive associations between problematic social media use and depression and anxiety in a large meta-analysis of 98 studies. 11 Similar findings have been reported in the Middle East and North Africa region, 37 in Greece, 4 and for problematic TikTok use. 16, 38 Several mechanisms may explain these associations.

Excessive engagement with social media may expose users to unfavourable social comparisons, may displace face-to-face interactions, sleep, and physical activity, and may lead to emotional dysregulation due to preoccupation with social media. 39, 40 Moreover, the reward-based design of social media platforms may foster compulsive use, which in turn may increase psychological distress. 7 However, the association may also run in the opposite direction, since individuals with anxiety or depressive symptoms may use social media as a coping or escape strategy. 40, 41 Given the crosssectional design of our study, we cannot determine the direction of these associations.

Social media addiction was also associated with loneliness, poor sleep quality, and life dissatisfaction. Our findings regarding loneliness are consistent with Greek studies showing a positive association between problematic social media use or problematic TikTok use and loneliness. 17, 42 In contrast, a study in the United Kingdom found that problematic social media use did not predict loneliness. 43 One possible explanation is that lonely individuals may turn to social media to compensate for unmet social needs, while online interactions may not provide the emotional intimacy of offline relationships. 42 Regarding sleep, our findings agree with systematic reviews that link excessive and problematic social media use with poor sleep quality, 11, 15 and with a previous Greek study. 4 Late-night use, exposure to stimulating content, and difficulty disengaging from infinite feeds may delay sleep onset and reduce sleep duration. 15 Moreover, our findings regarding life satisfaction are in line with a metaanalysis that found a negative correlation between problematic Facebook use and well-being. 13 We should note that the associations with poor sleep quality and life dissatisfaction were weaker than the associations with anxiety and depression.

The strongest association that we found was between social media addiction and social media fatigue. Participants with social media addiction had approximately ten times the odds of social media fatigue compared with non-addicted participants. This finding suggests a paradox in which addicted individuals continue to use social media despite feeling exhausted by it, which is consistent with the compulsive nature of behavioural addictions. 5 Similarly, social media addiction was strongly associated with social media distraction, fear of missing out, second-screen use, and no-socialmedia phobia. These findings are in line with studies showing that TikTok use during daily activities is associated with fear of missing out, 19 and that the pervasiveness of smartphones in key moments of the day mediates the negative effect of early social media use on learning outcomes. 3 However, we should acknowledge a degree of conceptual overlap between these behaviours and the components of social media addiction, such as salience and withdrawal, which may partly explain the large magnitude of these associations.

An interesting finding was that social media addiction was associated with higher odds of AI chatbot use for mental health issues but not with doctor visits for mental health issues. Indeed, almost three out of four participants reported using AI chatbots for mental health issues, whereas approximately four out of ten had visited a doctor for such issues. Given the higher prevalence of anxiety and depressive symptoms among addicted participants, the absence of an association with doctor visits may indicate an unmet need for professional mental health care in this group. Individuals with social media addiction may be more familiar with digital tools and may prefer the immediacy, anonymity, and low cost of AI chatbots to formal health services.

Although AI chatbots may offer an accessible first source of support, the quality and safety of their mental health advice remain uncertain, and they cannot replace professional assessment and treatment. Further research is needed to examine whether AI chatbots complement or substitute for professional help-seeking among young people with social media addiction.

We found that sex, age, and financial status did not substantially modify the associations between social media addiction and the study outcomes. Only three of 39 interaction terms were statistically significant, and none of them remained significant after correction for multiple comparisons. Our findings are consistent with a metaanalysis that found no heterogeneity in the correlations between problematic social media use and depression, anxiety, and stress by age or gender, 12 and with a metaanalysis showing that the prevalence of problematic social media use did not vary by age or gender. 10 In contrast, previous Greek studies including all three generations found that sex and generation moderated some associations between problematic social media use or problematic TikTok use and mental health. 4,17 A possible explanation is that the narrow age range of our sample (18 to 29 years) limited the variability needed to detect moderation by age. Moreover, the small number of males in our sample reduced our statistical power to detect differences by sex. Thus, the exploratory finding of a stronger association between social media addiction and nosocial-media phobia among males should be confirmed by future studies.

Our findings have implications for practice and policy. Healthcare professionals, especially in primary care and university health services, should screen young people for social media addiction and its psychological consequences, since brief and valid tools such as the BSMAS and the PHQ-4 are available. Interventions should aim not only to reduce problematic use but also to facilitate access to professional mental health care, given the lack of association between social media addiction and doctor visits that we found. Universities may integrate digital literacy and digital well-being programmes into their curricula. 38 At the policy level, health warnings on social media platforms 22 and restrictions of addictive platform features 21 may contribute to a healthier digital environment. However, policies should be evidence-based and should balance protection from harm with the benefits that social media offer to young people. 1, 20 Our study had several limitations. First, we conducted a cross-sectional study, and thus we cannot infer causal relationships between social media addiction and the study outcomes. Longitudinal studies are needed to examine the temporal order of these associations. Second, we used a convenience sample, which consisted mainly of females (80.6%) and university students, and therefore our findings cannot be generalised to all members of Generation Z in Greece. Third, we collected data through self-report instruments, which may introduce information bias, such as recall and social desirability bias. Fourth, we measured several outcomes, such as doctor visits, AI chatbot use, and social media-related behaviours, with single-item scales, which cannot capture the multidimensional nature of these constructs. Fifth, we dichotomised our outcomes according to cut-offs, which may lead to loss of information; moreover, the cut-off of the Greek BSMAS has moderate sensitivity and specificity, 26 and thus some misclassification of social media addiction is likely. Sixth, we adjusted our analysis for sex, age, and financial status, but residual confounding by other variables, such as pre-existing mental disorders and personality traits, 44 is possible. Finally, we performed a large number of statistical tests, which increases the probability of type I error, and thus our interaction analyses should be considered exploratory.

In conclusion, our findings suggest that social media addiction is highly prevalent among members of Generation Z in Greece and that it is associated with a wide range of mental health problems, including anxiety, depression, loneliness, social media fatigue, poor sleep quality, and life dissatisfaction. Moreover, social media addiction was associated with problematic digital behaviours, such as distraction, fear of missing out, second-screen use, and no-social-media phobia. Young people with social media addiction were more likely to use AI chatbots for mental health issues, but they were not more likely to visit a doctor. These associations were largely consistent across sex, age, and financial status. Healthcare professionals, educators, and policymakers should develop and implement interventions to prevent social media addiction and to facilitate access to professional mental health care among young people. Further longitudinal studies are needed to clarify the direction of these associations.
