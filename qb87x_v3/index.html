How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Abstract

Economically and socially conservative attitudes are frequently presented as stemming from distinct values about inequality and social control, respectively. However, existing research often relies on psychological scales, like Social Dominance Orientation (SDO) and Right-Wing Authoritarianism (RWA), that were not designed to measure values about inequality and social control directly, and which blur the boundary between values and attitudes. Here, we explore the relationship between political values and attitudes in an online USA sample (N=893), taking advantage of the new Dual Foundations Scale, which was specifically designed to measure inequality and social control values. Using multilevel Bayesian models to examine relationships at the level of individual attitudes, we find that, as predicted, values of inequality were positively associated with economic conservatism, and values about social control with social conservatism. However, associations varied across individual items, and social conservatism was overall less strongly associated with social control values than with inequality values and religious identification. Exploratory follow-up analyses indicate that whereas economically conservative attitudes cluster as expected with inequality values and SDO, socially conservative attitudes are not reliably related to social control values (or RWA's Submission or Aggression subdimensions) and instead cluster with RWA Traditionalism, which is made up of items that some argue simply restate socially conservative attitudes. Our novel approach and findings therefore support some of the main claims of two-dimensional theories of ideology while challenging others, and suggest relationships between social conservatism, social control values, and authoritarianism are more complex than standard two-dimensional approaches to political ideology often assume.

How are people's attitudes towards taxation and crime, healthcare and women's liberation, racial injustice and gay rights organised? And how might the coalescing of attitudes about these seemingly disparate aspects of our social lives be related to people's broader political values?

One of political psychology's clearest findings is that these kinds of attitudes about contemporary political issues and policy options cannot be reduced to just one dimension, such as 'left' versus 'right'. For example, libertarians reject both typically right-wing attitudes towards issues like religious traditionalism and typically left-wing attitudes towards issues like economic redistribution (Iyer et al., 2012). Instead, existing research identifies two particularly distinctive dimensions of variation in political attitudes (Johnston & Ollerenshaw, 2020). One dimension comprises issues including taxation, provision of healthcare, regulation of business, and the benefits of markets and competitive capitalism, and is thus often called 'economic conservatism', while the other comprises issues such as traditional gender and sexuality, crime and punishment, and ethnocentrism, and is thus called 'social ' or 'cultural' conservatism (e.g., Carmines & D'Amico, 2015; Everett, 2013; Johnson & Tamney, 2001).

These two distinct dimensions of economic and social conservatism have been familiar to researchers for a long time (e.g., Kerr, 1952), with recent studies showing that they are relatively independent of each other. While economic and social conservatism do often correlate positively (Azevedo et al., 2019), there are as many contexts where they correlate negatively (Johnston & Ollerenshaw, 2020; Malka et al., 2019). Even in those contexts where economic and social conservatism do correlate positively, two dimensions better reflect the diversity in individuals' attitudes (Feldman & Johnston, 2014), and the positive correlation may be driven by the most politically engaged citizens adopting the 'left versus right' ideological packaging espoused by politicians and the media (Johnston & Ollerenshaw, 2020; though compare Azevedo et al., 2019).

An important hypothesis advanced by many researchers is that the two dimensions of political attitudes are embedded in a wider system of ideology in which they map onto two complementary dimensions of values (Federico, 2022; Johnston & Ollerenshaw, 2020).

Here, we take 'ideology' to mean a system of political beliefs and attitudes that covary with one another under the influence of psychological constraints. This hypothesis, that psychology provides an important, if partial, constraint on the organisation of attitudes, has not always been a popular position, with some researchers arguing that the term 'ideology' should be abandoned and that the structure of political attitudes can be understood through self-interest, demographics, and media messaging (Kalmoe, 2020; Weeden & Kurzban, 2017). However, recent decades have witnessed 'the end of the end of ideology' in the form of a resurgence of research on the role of psychological differences in explaining political attitudes (Jost et al., 2003). In particular, values have emerged as a key means of explaining the structure of political attitudes and individual differences therein (Federico, 2022; Feldman, 2003). Whereas 'attitudes' describe individuals' responses to particular issues and problems, 'values' are broad principles about the way people ought to behave and the way society ought to be (Bergh & Sidanius, 2021; Schwartz et al., 2014), and should thus provide a structure to attitudes towards specific political issues and policy options beyond concerns about self-interest. Under this ideological account, basic differences in psychological processing are, together with environmental factors, thought to generate different political values, which in turn underpin individual differences in attitudes towards specific economic and social issues (Zmigrod et al., 2021).

Recent research indicates that economic and social attitude dimensions do reflect different constellations of psychological traits. Economic conservatism has been linked to competitiveness (which envisions a 'dog-eat-dog' world), selfishness, anger, and disagreeableness (Bakker, 2017; Claessens et al., 2023; Kerschbamer & Müller, 2020; Kettle & Salerno, 2017), whereas social conservatism has been linked to 'dangerous' worldview, needs for security and certainty, cognitive rigidity, and religiosity (Costello et al., 2023; Feldman & Johnston, 2014; Johnston & Ollerenshaw, 2020; Malka et al., 2014). Existing evidence thus supports the view that economic and social conservatism are psychologically distinct.

Furthermore, work on the nature of values has produced a suite of two-dimensional models that bear resemblance to the attitudinal dimensions of economic and social conservatism (Claessens et al., 2020). These include 'Equality' and 'Freedom' (Rokeach, 1973), 'Self-Enhancement versus Self-Transcendence' and 'Conservation versus Openness to Change' (Schwartz et al., 2014), 'Tolerance of Inequality' and 'Opposition to Change' (Jost et al., 2003), 'Nurturant Parent' and 'Strict Father' values (Feinberg et al., 2020), as well as 'Social Dominance Orientation' (SDO) and 'Right-Wing Authoritarianism' (RWA), which researchers treat as expressions of complementary dimensions of political values (Bergh & Sidanius, 2021; Duckitt et al., 2010). Thus, despite their different names, many scales seem to reflect the same two-dimensional core of ideology and values (Duckitt & Sibley, 2016).

Research also provides empirical support for the link between values and attitudes, by showing that SDO and RWA differentially correlate with economic and social conservatism (Johnston & Ollerenshaw, 2020). SDO-the preference for unequal relations between social groups-has been linked to economic conservatism due to their shared concern with social inequality (Duriez & Van Hiel, 2002), while RWA-a preference for submission to group norms and leaders-has been linked to social conservatism through their shared concern with social conformity and control (Van Hiel & Kossowska, 2007).

These proposed links are supported by evidence that SDO is more strongly related to economic attitudes, whereas RWA is more strongly related to social attitudes (Crawford et al., 2013; Duriez et al., 2005; Duriez & Van Hiel, 2002; Perry & Sibley, 2013; Van Hiel & Kossowska, 2007).

A considerable amount of work thus provides convergent evidence consistent with a 'dual-process' account of ideology in which a two-dimensional core of values is hypothesised to account for attitudinal diversity regarding economic and social issues, respectively. But this account has had less to say about why these specific value dimensions exist, what they are ultimately about, and why they are important for political life. These questions have recently been addressed through the evolutionary lens of the dual foundations framework. The dual foundations framework extends the 'dual-process' account by arguing that these value dimensions can be understood as the product of two key trade-offs that had already been independently identified as fundamental to the evolution of human group living (Claessens et al., 2020; Lavender Forsyth et al., 2025). First, as humans became increasingly dependent on each other for survival, individuals encountered trade-offs between cooperation and competition in their social networks.

Second, as group size increased and coordination became both more profitable and more difficult, humans developed conventions, norms and institutions that could promote group viability, generating individual-level trade-offs between autonomy and social control.

The two-dimensional structure of political ideology is thus proposed to ultimately reflect individual-level differences in responses to these two central trade-offs of human group living. The inequality dimension (reflected by SDO, Tolerance of Inequality, Nurturant Parent, etc.) responds to trade-offs over cooperation versus competition in resource allocation and management of public goods. The social control dimension reflects conformity versus autonomy in regards to the following of norms and leaders and the punishment of deviance (reflected by RWA, Opposition to Change, Strict Father, etc.). Individual differences along both dimensions are supported by the existence of mixed benefits and costs accruing to cooperative or competitive and conformist or autonomist strategies, including reputation-building versus freeriding and delayed versus immediate fitness returns (Claessens et al., 2020). Evidence consistent with this hypothesis includes findings that differences in political values, as measured by SDO and RWA, are predicted by behaviour in abstract incentivised tasks that measure responses to trade-offs over inequality and social control, respectively (Claessens et al., 2023; Fischer et al., 2021).

Because all human groups face these same two trade-offs, these two dimensions should be politically important across societies. Consistent with this, the framework draws support from evidence for the political contestation of inequality and social control not just in modern political contexts, but across the ethnographic literature's documentation of political life spanning diverse modes of human group living (Lavender Forsyth et al., 2025).

The dual foundations framework suggests that some refinement may be necessary in how we measure the contributions of values about inequality and social control to political attitudes. Firstly, the SDO and RWA scales that are widely used in two-dimensional approaches to political ideology are less than ideal as measures of each dimension's evolutionary foundation, as conceptualised by the framework. The dual foundations framework points out that economic issues like taxation and provision of public goods and services can be understood as dilemmas of resource distribution, and argues that SDO and economically conservative attitudes are connected through general preferences towards inequality. Yet SDO was devised to measure preferences for dominance relationships between discrete social groups (Ho et al., 2015) and, while it may serve this purpose well, a focus on group-level inequalities will fail to capture support for other kinds of social inequality. Likewise, the dual foundations framework argues that RWA and socially conservative attitudes are connected in part via preferences for social control over autonomy. However, RWA's notably strong relationship with socially conservative attitudes might be explained by content overlap due to the overtly right-wing framing of the RWA scale, which itself incorporates political issues that are socially conservative and religiously traditionalist (the latter is problematic as religion is a significant independent predictor of social conservatism; Feldman & Johnston, 2014; Lee et al., 2018). Some even consider RWA and social conservatism to measure essentially the same thing, which would mean using RWA to draw inferences about the underlying psychological underpinnings of social conservatism risks circularity (Crowson, 2009; Malka et al., 2017; Van Hiel et al., 2007). SDO and RWA may therefore not provide a complete picture or compelling test of the relationship between political values and attitudes.

Another problem that the dual foundations framework highlights is that, because preferences towards inequality and social control arise in response to independent tradeoffs, people can combine inequality values with social control values in various ways: valuing inequality and valuing social control may not consistently co-occur. If the strong positive relationship between RWA and social conservatism can be explained by content overlap, as argued above, how strongly would social control values predict social conservatism without a right-wing framing? Recent work has aimed to show that authoritarianism can be found across other parts of the political spectrum, including by constructing scales of 'left-wing authoritarianism' (Conway et al., 2018). Counter-arguments to this claim rely on measuring 'pre-political' preferences for social control, through 'family values' or Schwartz's 'conservation' values, and showing that these still predict socially conservative attitudes (Azevedo et al., 2019; Feinberg et al., 2020; Schwartz et al., 2014). These alternative measures, however, are not directly equivalent to authoritarianism or social control. Doubts exist over how reliably questions about family values measure authoritarian preferences (Bizumic & Duckitt, 2018; M. J. Hooper, 2020). These family values items may also tap cultural tropes coded as socially conservative, such as "When grownups talk children ought to be quiet" from Feinberg et al. (2020). Moreover, scales that measure personal values, like Schwartz's, do not measure the central component of authoritarianism or social control: that other people should submit to norms and to leaders and that their deviance should be punished. The extent to which social conservatism and authoritarianism coincide due to an inherent link between social conservatism and political values of social control, versus one based merely on right-wing framing, thus remains contested.

Lastly, despite the widespread view that economically and socially conservative attitudes can be understood in terms of two dimensions of values (Duckitt & Sibley, 2016; Johnston & Ollerenshaw, 2020), some empirical findings indicate a more complex relationship between values and economic and social attitudes. Some studies indicate that both SDO and RWA predict economic and/or social conservatism, though these results are generally produced by analyses that do not include both SDO and RWA within the same model (e.g., Azevedo et al., 2019; Beattie et al., 2022; Everett, 2013). As SDO and RWA themselves tend to correlate moderately to strongly, studies that do not control for both likely have difficulty disambiguating the independent effects of each. Nevertheless, another study does find that egalitarianism, a construct closely related with SDO, is associated with both economic and social attitudes (Feldman & Johnston, 2014). Uncertainty therefore does remain in the extent to which a one-to-one relationship exists between values about inequality and social control and attitudes about economic and social political issues.

To explore how values about inequality and social control relate to economic and social conservatism, it is useful to measure values in a way that operationalises preferences towards inequality and social control, rather than relying solely on existing scales that were created for other purposes (i.e., SDO and RWA). To do this, we use the Dual Foundations Scale (DFS), which has been developed specifically to measure preferences towards the twodimensional scales of political values in terms of the basic trade-offs over inequality and social control identified by the dual foundations framework (Lavender Forsyth et al., 2023).

The Dual Foundations Scale comprises two dimensions that measure values of competitiondriven inequality (henceforth DFS Inequality) and conformity-induced social control (henceforth DFS Social Control), striking a balance between measuring political values, rather than a proxy such as child-rearing or personal values, and avoidance of 'dog-whistle' political issues like those included in the RWA scale. Like existing scales, each DFS dimension is measured with items spanning more than one type of content: DFS Inequality comprises two types of items (about whether people who have more resources should share them, and whether people with fewer resources should be helped), and DFS Social Control comprises three types (about following rules, punishing rule-breaking, and following leaders' decisions; see Table 1 below). The DFS thus elicits value judgements about the extent it is 'good' or 'bad' for other people to share (inequality) and for other people to conform to norms and leaders (social control). Existing work provides initial support for the DFS's internal validity, showing that its factor loadings are consistent with the dual foundations framework's proposed two-dimensional structure, and for its external validity, with DFS Inequality measuring a construct convergent with SDO, the Nurturant Parent dimension of Feinberg et al.'s (2020) family values scale, and Schwartz values including Benevolence-Caring, and DFS Social Control measuring a construct convergent with RWA, Strict Father, and Schwartz values including Conformity-Rules. Furthermore, both DFS dimensions independently predict self-identification with a right-wing orientation. Past results thus support the use of the DFS as a valid, theoretically grounded measure of the two value dimensions, both of which correlate with the common left-versus-right distinction in contemporary politics.

Here, we use the newly-developed Dual Foundations Scale (DFS) to test whether attitudes towards various economic and social issues are associated with distinct value preferences for inequality and social control, respectively. We focus on the USA because this is where most of the prior research on the relation of political values and attitudes that we review and critique above has been conducted. We therefore collect a large online sample of American adults' attitudes towards a broad range of economic and social issues. We employ a Bayesian multilevel modelling approach that enables us to test whether DFS Inequality and Social Control are differentially associated with overall scores of economic and social conservatism, while also enabling us to explore variation in the relationships between the two value dimensions and each of the different economic and social items.

Within this modelling strategy, we measure the contributions of values about both inequality and social control simultaneously, allowing us to account for the independent effects of each. We also include a set of covariates that allows us to examine whether variation in key demographic and socioeconomic factors account for the link between values and attitudes. We compare a model containing the DFS against one containing only participants' gender, age, income, and education, as these index variation in basic political interests (as per Weeden & Kurzban, 2017) that we can quantify and control for. We also investigate the effects of identification with a religious group, a well-known predictor of social conservatism (Feldman & Johnston, 2014; Lee et al., 2018), to see whether this might account for some of the relationship between socially conservative attitudes and values of social control. We preregistered our hypotheses, study design, planned sample size, exclusion criteria, and planned primary analyses with the Open Science Framework (OSF) before collecting the data (https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/ED524). Below, we report all preregistered analyses, as well as all manipulations, measures, and exclusions, and clearly identify additional analyses that were not preregistered.

This study represents an initial test of the relationship between the Dual Foundations Scale of political values and the widely studied two-dimensional structure of economic and social political attitudes. Although it is not intended as the final word on the structure of political attitudes and values, we highlight two novel aspects of our approach that deserve attention. First, the DFS offers a view of political values that does not assume the centrality of group-based dominance (like SDO) or a particular set of dog-whistle authoritarian and socially conservative content (like RWA), but rather elicits evaluations of inequality and social control that follow directly from an evolutionary first-principles account of the dual foundations of political ideology. Second, our Bayesian multilevel modelling framework pools across multiple items measuring conservative attitudes while still allowing estimation of relationships between each DFS dimension and each individual attitude item. Due to the novelty of our measures and modelling strategy, and the limitations of our sample as set out in the Discussion, we present our findings as the promising basis for future work exploring a more diverse range of measures and populations.

Methods

We used the online recruitment tool, Prolific, to sample participants from the United States.

To increase sample diversity, sampling was stratified by age, gender, and ethnicity to be representative of US census data. In December 2021, during the COVID-19 pandemic, 918 participants completed our survey hosted by Qualtrics. We chose this sample size because it is in line with our prior work using Bayesian multilevel models to predict political attitudes.

We finished data collection before looking at the data. Participants answered the survey in a mean completion time of 11 minutes (SD = 7). All were compensated US$2.49 for participating in the study. Twenty-four participants failed an attention check and were excluded (see the Supplementary Materials). We also fully deleted one further participant's responses because they gave their age as 14, leaving a final sample of 893.

We collected some sociodemographic data. This includes participant gender, age, education, income, and religion. Men made up 48.8% of the sample (N = 436). Mean selfreported age was 35.4 (SD = 13.6). Most identified as White (70%; N = 627). Religion was measured with a binary variable ("Do you identify with a religion and/or spiritual group?"): 39.8% (N = 355) said they were religious, compared to 60.2% (N = 538) who said they were not. Education was measured on a seven-point ordinal scale with a median of five (corresponding to college with bachelor's degree). Income was a self-reported estimate of yearly household income before tax, measured on a thirteen-point ordinal scale with a median of six (US$50,000-59,000).

We administered the ten-item Dual Foundations Scale to elicit participants' values about inequality and social control. Items were presented in random order. Participants responded to the items with a slider displaying values from 0 to 100. After reverse-coding the appropriate items, a score of 100 indicated a participant thought inequality or social control was 'very good', 50 indicated 'neither good nor bad', and 0 indicated 'very bad'.

Mean responses for the averaged DFS items were: 22.81 (SD = 17.13; range 0-88) for Inequality and 54.55 for Social Control (SD = 14.95; range 5-100). Item wordings and reliability statistics are reported in the results.

To measure attitudes towards economic and social issues we used twenty items from Burton (2016). Within a larger project to map the fissures of American politics, Burton whittles down an initial set of 214 items into 69 that load onto two dimensions without cross-loading. The first dimension contains economic-related issues, whereas the second contains social-related issues. For brevity, we selected ten economic items and ten social items, making sure to choose those that load strongly onto each dimension whilst ensuring some counterbalancing. While representing a small selection of the many plausible indicators of economic and social conservatism, they do cover a range of different issues used by previous work on economic and social conservatism (e.g., Feldman & Johnston, 2014; Malka et al., 2019; Everett, 2013). The items were presented in random order.

Participants responded with a slider displaying values from 0 to 100. Mean responses for the averaged items were: 32.85 (SD = 20.32; range 0-96) for economic conservatism and 30.51 for social conservatism (SD = 20.54; range 0-96). We reverse-coded the appropriate items so that higher scores indicated more conservative attitudes. Item wordings are provided in the results.

In addition, we collected data for Ho et al.'s (2015) 16-item SDO scale and Duckitt et al.'s (2010) 18-item RWA scale. We preregistered no predictions regarding these but do use them in exploratory analyses reported below and in the Supplementary Materials, where the wordings of all items are also presented.

Analysis was conducted using R (R Core Team, 2025). Factor analyses were done using the lavaan package (Rosseel, 2012). The psych package (Revelle, 2019) was used for calculating McDonald's total omega reliability (ω). For Bayesian regressions we used the brms and tidybayes packages (Bürkner, 2017; Kay, 2022). These regression models used the two DFS dimensions (and covariates) to predict participants' economic and social attitudes.

We use 'predict' and 'predictor' in the statistical sense only: all analyses are correlational and cross-sectional, and do not license causal conclusions. With the economic and social attitudes as the outcome variable, we included random effects for participants and fixed effects for DFS Inequality and Social Control, with random slopes to allow the fixed effects to vary across the economic and social dimensions and across individual economic and social items. This approach thus allowed us to model the DFS's relationships not only with overall scores for economic and social conservatism but, simultaneously, with each of the twenty economic and social items. It thereby enables us to sensitively investigate the generalisability of our predictors' effects across the range of outcome items. Since brms does not support latent variable models, we used point estimates for the DFS dimensions derived from confirmatory factor analyses. All non-binary predictors were standardised. All models converged normally after 20,000 iterations (all Rhats = 1). Further details are available in the Supplementary Materials.

Analysis code, de-identified data, a codebook, and the original Qualtrics survey are available on the OSF to ensure reproducibility of all reported figures, tables, and results

Figure 1 shows that, as expected, economic conservatism was reliably predicted by DFS Inequality whereas it was not by Social Control. Also as expected, DFS Social Control predicted social conservatism. In contrast to our predictions, however, DFS Inequality also predicted social conservatism, and with a larger effect size than DFS Social Control. To explore these results further, Figure 2 displays the same Bayesian model's estimated effects of DFS Inequality and Social Control on the twenty individual economic and social items. We here highlight two important results.

First, there was variation in the effects of the two DFS dimensions across the different items. This result supports our approach in analysing the effects of DFS Inequality and Social Control on individual items, and not only on overall economic and social conservatism. For example, whereas Social Control was unrelated to most economic items (as reflected in the overall effect plotted in Figure 1), we see in Figure 2 that the seventh economic item, on the benefits of 'competitive capitalism', was positively predicted by DFS Inequality and Social Control independently. We see variation too amongst the social conservatism items. Of all the socially conservative items, Social Control most strongly predicted attitudes towards cracking down on drugs. It was a reliable if relatively weak predictor of the other socially conservative items, with three exceptions. Social Control did not reliably predict attitudes towards gay adoption, women's place in the home, or trusting the non-religious. These results are consistent with the idea that values about social control alone may not account for all socially conservative attitudes.

Second, Figure 2 highlights the unexpectedly reliable relationship between DFS Inequality and social conservatism. DFS Inequality, independent of Social Control, positively predicted every socially conservative item. Since prior work offers initial support for the DFS as a measure of independent preferences for inequality and social control, these results are unexpected. While economic conservatism thus seems aligned with values regarding inequality, social conservatism, at least as measured by the ten items here, seems related to values regarding both social control and inequality.

Does DFS Social Control only predict social conservatism amongst conservatives or does

Social Control exist at both political extremes?

A potential reason that DFS Social Control did not predict social conservatism as strongly as we had anticipated could be that values of social control only relate to right-wing socially conservative attitudes amongst people who already hold some form of conservative attitudes, such as people who favour inequality. We therefore explored the possibility that DFS Social Control more strongly predicts social conservatism amongst those that have high scores for DFS Inequality (a non-preregistered analysis). A model that interacts DFS Inequality and Social Control does not support this hypothesis, however. The interaction was not reliably positive for overall social conservatism or any of the individual items (Figure S1 in the Supplementary Materials). It therefore does not seem that DFS Social Control predicts social conservatism more strongly amongst those that favour inequality.

Does the DFS interact with religion?

To investigate the possibility of a connection between religious identity and values of social control in predicting social conservatism, we checked for evidence of an interaction between the DFS and being religious (a non-preregistered analysis). Of ten items, only for two, about heterosexual marriage and gay adoption, was there evidence for an interaction whereby DFS Social Control predicts social conservatism more strongly amongst religious people (Figures S2 and S3). Therefore, while religious identity may be important in linking values of social control to these specific social issues, being religious did not in general create a stronger link between values of social control and social conservatism.

What is the relationship between the DFS, SDO and RWA, and economic and social conservatism?

If socially conservative attitudes are not strongly connected to values of social control as previously assumed, what explains prior work demonstrating a close relationship between social conservatism and another ostensible measure of social control values, RWA? To explore this further, we conducted an exploratory factor analysis of the items of the DFS's two dimensions, of SDO and RWA, and of economic and social conservatism. This approach allows us to investigate, at an item-by-item level, the amount of covariance between the DFS dimensions and two alternative measures of values -SDO and RWA -and between each of these measures of political values and economic and social attitudes. This analysis was exploratory: we made no preregistered predictions about the number or composition of factors, and, as detailed below, the fit of the models was modest. We therefore treat the resulting structure as descriptive and hypothesis-generating.

Based on a scree plot of eigenvalues (Figure S5), we estimated factor loadings of all items onto one, two, three, and four dimensions. All items loaded positively onto the onedimensional model (Figure S6), but model fit was poor. The two-dimensional model showed improved fit and distinguished an 'inequality' dimension (DFS Inequality, SDO, and economic conservatism) from a 'social' dimension (DFS Social Control, RWA, and social conservatism), except that most economic conservatism items cross-loaded onto both dimensions in a positive direction (Figure S7). The three-dimension model showed further improved fit relative to the two-dimension model, though overall fit remained mixed, with good RMSEA and SRMR scores but a CFI score below conventional thresholds for good fit (CFI = 0.805; RMSEA = 0.069; SRMR = 0.048). As presented in Figure S8, the three-dimensional model also indicated an 'inequality' dimension defined by DFS Inequality, SDO, and economic conservatism (and which some RWA items cross-load onto). This is consistent with our findings above regarding the DFS and economic conservatism, and in keeping with past work that uses the SDO scale to measure values regarding inequality. The second and third dimensions distinguished between apparently different kinds of social preferences. The second dimension was defined by preferences for social control: it was loaded on by DFS Social Control and by two of RWA's facets, Authoritarian Submission and Authoritarian Aggression. The third dimension was defined by social conservatism: loaded on by the social conservatism items and by RWA's third facet, Traditionalism, which is the facet of RWA that contains the items about religious traditionalism and sexual morality. All three dimensions correlated positively: the first and second at 0.493 (SE = 0.056), first and third at 0.504 (SE = 0.063), and second and third at 0.571 (SE = 0.048). The 'social conservatism' dimension thus correlated almost as strongly with the 'inequality' dimension as it did with the 'social control' dimension. This general pattern was corroborated by the four-dimensional model (Figure S9). These exploratory factor analyses thus suggest one possible reason why DFS Social Control did not predict social conservatism as strongly as might be expected: social control, as measured by the DFS but also by RWA's Submission and Aggression subscales, appears to be only moderately related to social conservatism, whereas RWA's Traditionalism facet's religious and sexual content seems to tie more strongly to social conservatism.

Summary

Overall, our results are consistent with a link between pro-inequality values, as measured by DFS Inequality, and economic conservatism. Differences in economically conservative attitudes were associated with differences in values about competition-driven-inequality and not values about social control. We did not find a similarly clear mapping of DFS Social Control onto social conservatism. Multiple-regression analyses indicate that socially conservative attitudes were independently associated with three variables: social control values, inequality values, and being religious. Our follow-up exploratory factor analyses suggested a plausible reason for this: values of social control, as measured by DFS Social Control and RWA Submission and Aggression, appeared distinguishable from socially conservative attitudes, as measured by the socially conservative items and RWA's Traditionalism subscale.

Discussion

We began by asking how attitudes about particular political issues are organised: why do opinions about seemingly disparate aspects of our social lives coalesce the way they do?

Drawing on the dual foundations framework, which proposes that political values reflect heuristic responses to two fundamental problems of human group living, namely trade-offs over cooperation-versus-competition and over social control-versus-autonomy, we investigated the association of political values about inequality and social control with variation in attitudes towards economic and social issues. Using the newly devised Dual Foundations Scale, we found that political values regarding inequality and social control do predict attitudinal variation, though in ways more complex than we anticipated. These findings: a) highlight some advantages of the first-principles approach of the dual foundations framework and its operationalisation in the Dual Foundations Scale; b) are consistent with elements of the dual foundations framework's predictions about political ideology, especially the close connection between values regarding inequality and economically conservative political attitudes; but c) raise questions about the claimed relationship between socially conservative attitudes and social control or authoritarian values. We stress this is a preliminary study using one large online sample (collected during the COVID-19 pandemic): it is a first step that suggests promising directions for future research rather than an exhaustive test.

Our results are consistent with the DFS being a useful two-dimensional measure of political values that may provide a more nuanced understanding of the relationship between political values and attitudes. Rather than shoehorning existing scales designed for other purposes into a two-dimensional framework, the DFS allows us to elicit preferences for inequality and social control in ways that are consistent with a first-principles account of the evolution of human social living. Our confirmatory factor analyses of its items replicate previous findings and the two-dimensional structure predicted by the dual foundations framework (Lavender Forsyth et al., 2023). We then found that its two dimensions were differentially associated with political attitudes in our US sample, beyond variation accounted for by the sociodemographic variables of age, gender, education, and income. As predicted in our preregistration, values about inequality were reliably associated with economically conservative attitudes, and more strongly than with socially conservative attitudes. Social control values were also associated with social conservatism, and more strongly than with economically conservative attitudes. However, socially conservative attitudes in our sample were also generally more strongly associated with inequality values and religious identity than with preferences for social control. In the remainder of this section, we discuss tentative implications of these results, limitations of the present study, and directions for further research using the DFS.

Our results offer insight into how political values relate to economically conservative attitudes. First, in confirmatory and exploratory factor analyses, we found a cluster of economically conservative attitudes that was distinguishable from socially conservative attitudes. This finding aligns with prior work on the two-dimensional structure of political attitudes in the USA (Carmines & D'Amico, 2015; Everett, 2013; Johnson & Tamney, 2001) and is consistent with the dual foundations' two-dimensional account of political ideology (Claessens et al., 2020; Lavender Forsyth et al., 2025). Second, we found that all economically conservative attitudes in our sample were reliably associated with DFS Inequality. This concurs with past research that uses SDO to predict economic conservatism (Duriez et al., 2005; Duriez & Van Hiel, 2002; Perry & Sibley, 2013; Van Hiel & Kossowska, 2007). This relationship was also reflected in our exploratory factor analyses of the DFS, SDO-RWA, and economic and social attitudes, which suggested a factor defined by DFS Inequality, SDO, and economically conservative attitudes. These results are compatible with the dual foundations framework's contention that values about competition and inequality in general, rather than SDO's specific focus on dominance relationships between discrete social groups, account for the relationship with economic conservatism. The results of our multilevel models also showed that at the level of individual items, economic attitudes can demonstrate more complex relationships to political values. For example, although we found that support for 'competitive capitalism' loaded strongly onto the economic dimension in factor analysis and that economic conservatism was not predicted by social control values overall, attitudes towards 'competitive capitalism' were nevertheless independently predicted by values about social control as well as inequality. Such results illustrate the value of investigating individual items in relation to the two DFS dimensions, rather than simply presenting overall results for economic and social conservatism.

Our results also bear on how political values relate to socially conservative attitudes.

Generally, participants who endorsed values of social control were more socially conservative than others, as predicted by the dual foundations framework. But our results also suggest that the degree of overlap between social control and social conservatism may not be as clear-cut as assumed by the dual foundations account and related theories (e.g., Duckitt & Sibley, 2016; Johnston & Ollerenshaw, 2020). Social conservatism was more reliably associated with inequality values and religious identity than with values of social control. Perhaps it is not so surprising to find a role for values about inequality in the case of some socially conservative attitudes. Our results complement, for example, prior work on the contributions of both dimensions to attitudes towards immigration (Thomsen et al., 2008), with both DFS Social Control and Inequality independently predicting conservative attitudes towards immigrants' assimilation. But the fact that inequality values were more strongly associated with social conservatism than were values of social control contrasts with much contemporary discussion of the independent psychological foundations of economic and social attitudes (e.g., Duckitt & Sibley, 2016; Johnston & Ollerenshaw, 2020).

On the other hand, this result accords with Feldman and Johnston's (2014) finding that egalitarianism can negatively predict social conservatism and with Azevedo et al.'s (2019) argument that many 'social' issues are related to values of inequality. Our finding for the role of religion in predicting social conservatism is also in line with past research (Feldman & Johnston, 2014; Lee et al., 2018), though the strength of the effects of a simple binary measure of being religious or not is perhaps still surprising. Overall, therefore, our results caution against a one-to-one mapping between social conservatism and values of social control.

These results also raise some questions for the dual foundations framework. This framework, like other two-dimensional models of ideology, anticipates that values about inequality map onto economic attitudes and that values about social control map onto social attitudes. Our data are broadly consistent with the first mapping but suggest the second may be less direct than often assumed. If these patterns replicate, they would suggest the standard two-dimensional approach, in which each set of values is mapped to one attitude dimension, may need to be complemented by more complex models that allow overlaps or cross-relationships between the two dimensions of ideology in predicting variation in political attitudes. Nevertheless, as we detail below, the current study has limitations in its sampling that means further work is required to substantiate and replicate our findings.

The comparatively limited role of social control values in predicting social conservatism is noteworthy given prior work (Lavender Forsyth et al., 2023) confirming the internal validity of the DFS in a large sample and the expected relationship between DFS Social Control and existing measures of authoritarianism, further supported by our exploratory factor analysis, which confirmed that social control values reliably clustered with both the Submission and Aggression dimensions of RWA as expected. Despite this support for DFS Social Control as a reliable measure of values of social control, it did not consistently predict attitudes towards all socially conservative items. Instead, social control values seem to contribute to some socially conservative attitudes more than others. Notably the strongest effect of DFS Social Control was for the item about cracking down on drugs - the only item about punishment and the item perhaps most explicitly about following rules.

Our results may thus suggest that values about social control contribute to social conservatism to the extent that social conservatism is explicitly about control versus autonomy, the trade-off identified by the dual foundations framework. Our results also suggest, though, that social conservatism encompasses more than this. Social conservatism in our US sample was reliably associated with pro-inequality values and many socially conservative attitudes were strongly related to religious identity. A potential explanation for the weaker than expected relationship between social conservatism and values of social control may be that these values are not exclusively associated with the political right. If those who are not right-wing also hold social control values, then this could explain why DFS Social Control was only inconsistently associated with social attitudes that are understood to be right-wing. A growing literature indeed seeks to show that authoritarianism is not restricted to the political right. Some research argues that parallel to right-wing authoritarianism is a left-wing authoritarianism characterised by tight adherence to the norms of left-wing movements (Conway et al., 2018). Other research indicates that in some countries, people with left-wing economic attitudes are perhaps more likely to hold the kind of socially conservative attitudes associated elsewhere with the political right (Beattie et al., 2022; Malka et al., 2014 Malka et al., , 2019)). Still other work argues that authoritarian values may be exhibited from any point of the left-centre-right political spectrum or that aspects of authoritarianism may be more pronounced at the political centre than the extremes (Adler, 2018). We must emphasise that our results do show that social control is related to social conservatism, which is understood to be right-wing. We find no evidence that values of social control predict left-wing attitudes. Nevertheless, that DFS Social Control did not strongly predict socially conservative attitudes may indicate that values of social control are not solely restricted to the political right in our sample.

If social conservatism is not uniquely associated with values of social control, what explains prior research's findings linking social conservatism to RWA? To investigate this further, we undertook a holistic analysis of the DFS's items alongside those of SDO and RWA and of economic and social conservatism. Exploratory factor analyses suggested that whereas RWA's Submission and Aggression subscales cluster with DFS Social Control as expected, its Traditionalism subscale clusters together with the social conservatism items. This is understandable given the content of this subscale, with items like "God's laws about abortion, pornography, and marriage must be strictly followed before it is too late;" "This country will flourish if young people stop experimenting with drugs, alcohol, and sex, and pay more attention to family values;" and "There is nothing wrong with premarital sexual intercourse." Therefore, despite being interpreted as a measure of preferences for control and conformity in social groups (Duckitt et al., 2010; Duckitt & Sibley, 2010), our results are consistent with criticisms from past research that a strong relationship between social conservatism and RWA exists due to content overlap, irrespective of whether preferences for social control uniquely underly social conservatism (Crowson, 2009; Malka et al., 2017; Van Hiel et al., 2007). In contrast to RWA, DFS Social Control avoids content that provides right-wing 'dog whistles'. Our results with this new, principled measure suggest that preferences regarding submission to group norms and leaders and punishment of deviantssocial control -correlate with social conservatism, but not as strongly or consistently as suggested by prior work. Our approach therefore suggests that the extent to which political values of social control covary with social conservatism may be overestimated when relying on scales like RWA.

Although our results do not support the idea that holding values of social control defines social conservatism, this does not necessarily undermine previous work on social conservatism's other psychological correlates, such as beliefs in a dangerous world (Duckitt & Sibley, 2016), personality features including risk perception and agreeableness (Zmigrod et al., 2021), personal values of conservation (Schwartz et al., 2014), and cognitive rigidity (Costello et al., 2023). Instead, our results raise the question of whether these variables are necessarily linked to social conservatism by way of explicit values of social control. Further research should therefore aim to extend our understanding of the relationships between political values, attitudes, and pre-political features of psychology including beliefs about the world and features of personality.

By contrast, religious identification emerged in our analyses as a significant predictor of socially conservative attitudes, independent of the two dimensions of political values.

Although incidental, this finding resonates with other work that emphasises the role of religion in social conservatism (e.g., Feldman & Johnston, 2014; Lee et al., 2018). Important questions remain, though, about why religion is associated with social conservatism in this way. For example, recent research indicates it is identification specifically as 'religious', rather than 'spiritual', that predicts conservatism (Lockhart et al., 2020). Future research could explore possible explanations for the relationship between religious identification, social conservatism, and its psychological correlates, such as religion acting as a proxy for cognitive rigidity or dogmatism, reflecting preferences for system justification (Jost et al., 2014), or indexing notions of a sacred community, perhaps intersecting other politically salient identities such as those of ethnicity and nationality (see, e.g., Crowson, 2009; Johnson & Tamney, 2001; Zmigrod et al., 2021). Lastly, America is a country where religion has come to occupy a strong role in party politics, and so future research should investigate the cross-cultural ubiquity of this link by testing whether religious identification demonstrates similar relationships to social conservatism in other countries where the political-religious divide is not so stark and in non-Western countries where Christianity is not the dominant religion and where non-religious identification is rare.

Some limitations are worth mentioning. First, the data are cross-sectional, so we cannot speak to the direction of any causal relationship between political values and attitudes or the existence of a third variable that influences both. This question is beyond the scope of our current analyses, which are focused on measuring associations between two dimensions of political attitudes and two dimensions of values. Our findings indicate that this relationship is more complex than is often acknowledged.

One contingent factor that may have influenced our findings is the context of the COVID-19 pandemic (our data were collected in December 2021), which could have led people on the political right to be less likely to profess explicit values of social control with regards to the national government. In the USA and other places, many right-wing actors adopted the rhetoric of personal autonomy to argue against public health measures in response to COVID-19, for example embracing the slogan 'my body, my choice' to oppose vaccine mandates (Grunawalt, 2021). Research from the UK shows that people who oppose public health restrictions for COVID-19 indeed profess values of personal freedom (Fischer et al., 2023). This may mean that, at the time of data collection, people who held socially conservative attitudes (anti-abortion, anti-gay marriage, etc.) were perhaps less likely to report agreement with social control as an explicit value, thus weakening observed relationships between social control and social conservatism. On the other hand, the fact that many conservatives responded this way is another reason to question a simple association between social conservatism and social control. Further research would therefore be useful to test whether our results can be replicated or whether the relationship between stated values of social control and social conservatism changes over time and depending on context.

To measure political attitudes, we employed an array of items drawn from prior research that cover a range of topics used to study economic and social conservatism.

Results of our factor analyses support the expected two-dimensional structure of these items. However, our selection of items was not exhaustive and our conclusions are limited to this selection. For example, the set of socially conservative items contains several with religious themes. Future work should extend our findings to a broader array of socially conservative issues, such as censorship, restrictions on protest, and support for police, as well as non-conservative-coded issues that have a basis in the need to control others' behaviour, such as vaccine mandates (see Fischer et al., 2023). Whether items that focus more clearly on socially controlling themes would reveal a stronger role for values of social control in political attitudes is an open empirical question. Research on political attitudes can also overlook some topics because they do not neatly fall into the economic-social dichotomy, such as foreign policy (e.g., Feldman & Johnston, 2014), which may nevertheless relate to inequality and social control values. Further work should include more topics such as this that escape the economic-social dichotomy. Further study of the roles that DFS Inequality and Social Control play across a wider diversity of attitudes would clarify how these preferences relate to political attitudes. Further work should also explore the relevance of the Dual Foundations Scale beyond our current sample. Here, we endeavoured to increase sample diversity by collecting a large US sample (N = 893) and by stratifying recruitment by gender (roughly half the sample is female), age (average >35), and ethnicity (30% non-White). Most of our participants had an annual household income less than $60,000. Nevertheless, we were limited by recruiting from Prolific, which is not representative of the US population. This is problematic given the likelihood that more educated people are somewhat overrepresented on platforms like Prolific (Peer et al., 2017) and the established link between greater levels of education and progressive political attitudes (Duriez et al., 2005). We therefore make no final claims about the general structure of political ideology in the US population as a whole. That said, our sample does represent a broad range of political opinion, with responses covering the breadth of the response scales for the DFS and for economic and social conservatism. We also note that the Dual Foundations Scale is short enough to be included in longer surveys and so we recommend its inclusion in established survey projects. This would provide an opportunity to study how the dual foundations of political ideology relate to political attitudes and a host of other useful variables across truly population-benchmarked samples. Although we have focused on testing the relation between political values and political attitudes in the USA, as this is where most of the research that we critique and build upon has been conducted, we also recommend expanding the dual foundations approach beyond the US context. US politics is relatively strange in the dominance of its two-party system, the significance of its religiousnonreligious divide, and its global context as a world superpower. The DFS could be used in other cultural contexts to investigate whether the two value dimensions have similar or different roles in predicting political attitudes as we found here in the USA.

Conclusion

In an online US sample, economic and social conservatism were related to different political values. Economically conservative attitudes were related to general preferences regarding inequality, and socially conservative attitudes were related to a combination of preferences for inequality, social control, and religious identity. Our work with the Dual Foundations Scale is consistent with some key predictions of the dual foundations framework, while raising questions about assumptions that were built on past research conducted with other scales of ideology. In line with the framework's predictions, we found that economic attitudes were associated with values regarding inequality, and values of social control were associated with social conservatism and generally not with economic conservatism. However, social conservatism was independently associated with values about both social control and inequality, values about social control predicted most but not all socially conservative attitudes, and religious identification was a stronger predictor of most socially conservative attitudes than values of social control. While the dual foundations framework, and two-dimensional approaches to ideology more generally, have largely assumed a tight relationship between values of social control and socially conservative attitudes, our results suggest that social conservatism is not uniquely associated with explicit values of social control. Exploratory factor analyses of the DFS, SDO-RWA, and economic and social conservatism suggested an inequality dimension (defined by DFS Inequality, SDO, and economic conservatism) and a distinction between social control (DFS Social Control and RWA Submission and Aggression) and social conservatism (which also includes RWA Traditionalism), although the fit of these models was modest. These results illustrate two novel aspects of our approach: the Dual Foundations Scale offers an alternative view of political values to SDO and RWA, and our Bayesian multilevel framework allows relationships to be examined at the level of both overall conservatism and individual items.

Due to the study limitations we discuss above, our findings should be treated as a promising starting point rather than final answers. Replication in more comprehensively populationbenchmarked samples, with expanded measures of political attitudes, will be useful to test how far the complications we document for the standard two-dimensional model of political ideology hold more generally. We hope this encourages further analyses of this kind and informs further work with the Dual Foundations Scale across new and more diverse samples.
