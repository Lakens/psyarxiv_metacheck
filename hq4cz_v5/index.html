Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. Some differences come from the ground truth: it lacks p-values with a correction name (“p Bonf = .061”), and counts partial eta squared written with its subscript after it (“η 2 p = .05”), “p = .05”, and some table notes and figure legends (“† p < .10, *p < .05”), which this module leaves out, as non-significant p-values. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. Introduction

Young people experience high levels of loneliness and social disconnection, yet they often make limited use of formal mental health services. Social prescribing aims to support wellbeing through connection to community-based activities, including arts and cultural participation. In practice, however, prevailing social prescribing models are commonly organised around practitioner-mediated pathways that remain poorly aligned with how many young people seek and negotiate support. This exploratory co-design study examined the potential role, contextual appropriateness, and legitimacy of a youth-facing digital pathway as a complement to existing social prescribing models. This paper aimed to identify the key domains that emerged from that process and offer recommendations for future research involving digital pathways in youth social prescribing.

Method

An exploratory qualitative study used co-design informed by participatory action research principles.

Three workshops involved 24 young people, six community leaders participated in interviews, and three workshop participants subsequently contributed as paid youth co-researchers. Written activity outputs were summarised descriptively, and qualitative data were analysed to identify themes. An exploratory prototype of an AI-enabled digital connector supported examination of the proposed pathway's purpose, potential value, and boundaries before commitment to a particular design or implementation approach.

Results

The inquiry identified three key domains relating to (1) the use of codesign to test legitimacy and purpose of digital pathways, (2) pre-engagement conditions which shape whether participation in communitybased social opportunities feels possible in practice -including discovery, identity and social fit, feasibility, readiness, and trust, and (3) system-level considerations relevant to youth social prescribing.

Discussion

Eight practice-oriented recommendations are presented that position early-stage co-design as a means to support informed judgement about whether, how, and under what conditions youth-facing digital pathways to offline social connection should proceed within health and community systems, before tools are built.

Introduction Social Prescribing for Young People

Social connection is a central determinant of mental health and wellbeing. Across decades of longitudinal research and large-scale meta-analyses, the quality of close social relationships has been shown to predict life satisfaction, physical health, and longevity more strongly than many traditional clinical risk factors (Holt-Lunstad et al., 2015; Waldinger & Schulz, 2010). Conversely, loneliness, understood as the distress that arises when social relationships fall short of desired connection, is associated with substantial risks to both mental and physical health, elevating social connection from a personal concern to a significant public health issue (Hawkley & Cacioppo, 2010).

Young adults (aged 18 to 29 years) represent a particularly important group within this landscape. Despite being the most digitally engaged and formally educated generation to date, young people report heightened levels of loneliness and social disconnection, alongside persistent reluctance to engage with formal mental health services (Orygen, 2017; Pretorius et al., 2019; Rickwood et al., 2007). This period of life is characterised by significant transitions in identity, relationships, and social roles, alongside shifts from structured, institutionally organised social settings to more self-directed and fragmented forms of interaction. The COVID-19 pandemic further disrupted opportunities for in-person connection during a formative period, with effects that continue to shape young people's social trajectories (Caduff, 2024; Magis-Weinberg et al., 2025). Experiences of marginalisation, shaped by intersecting identity, structural, and geographic conditions, further influence how some young people encounter and navigate formal systems of support.

Social prescribing has gained increasing attention as a response to social disconnection, linking individuals to non-clinical, community-based activities that support wellbeing and participation. Arts and cultural activities, in particular, are consistently identified within social prescribing frameworks as offering opportunities for shared experience, expression, and connection (Jensen et al., 2024). However, despite the relevance of social connection for youth wellbeing, young people remain largely absent from the social prescribing evidence base. A recent synthesis reported mean participant ages between 38 and 82 years (Dubbledeman et al., 2024), with only one of 36 studies focusing exclusively on young people (Cartwright et al., 2022). Recent reviews identified only four youth-focused studies in 2023 (Hayes et al., 2023) and seven by 2025 (Muhl et al., 2025). These patterns suggest not only an evidence gap, but a deeper misalignment between prevailing social prescribing models and the ways young people experience, seek, and negotiate social connection.

Traditional models of social prescribing typically involve referral by a general or other health practitioner through a link worker or other trusted connector, who collaborates with the individual to identify and engage in social participation opportunities. Alternative models of social prescribing are, however, now being used more frequently, with multiple referral and uptake pathways (Husk et al., 2020; Oster et al., 2023). In their recent commentary, Husk and Berry (2025) argued that targeted approaches tailored specifically for young people are required to address the limited uptake of social prescribing within this population, including more varied entry points and stronger integration with community-based activities.

They observed that universal, all-age social prescribing offers are often not taken up by those most in need, highlighting the absence of youth-appropriate pathways within current models.

More broadly, traditional social prescribing pathways that presume young people will voluntarily seek support through general practitioners or other health professionals within clinical or health-service settings are likely to underestimate the psychosocial, structural, and relational factors that shape young people's engagement with formal systems of support (Bertotti et al., 2022; Hayes et al., 2024; Ending Loneliness Together, 2023). As a result, many existing models risk systematically missing young people who are socially disconnected yet disengaged from clinical systems. Husk and Berry (2025) further note that young people are rarely involved in the design or evaluation of social prescribing interventions, identifying Ostojic et al. (2025) as one of the few examples of co-design in this domain, where a social prescribing program was developed with children with cerebral palsy and their caregivers.

Against this backdrop, digital tools may offer a complementary pathway to community-based social activities alongside traditional social prescribing models, particularly for young people who are unlikely or unable to engage with GP-referral-based systems. AI-enabled conversational agents may further support this role by facilitating more personalised discovery of community opportunities, adapting recommendations to young people's interests, preferences, and readiness rather than relying solely on static search processes. Digital pathways might, for example, allow young people to explore opportunities in familiar, low-pressure environments, aligning with preferences for autonomy, self-directed information seeking, and non-stigmatising forms of support. A digital extension to social prescribing may also address some of the structural limitations of traditional models, including geographic remoteness and gaps in linkworker coverage (Bertotti et al., 2022; Muhl et al., 2025; Sharman et al., 2022). In this sense, a digital pathway represents a plausible response to a recognised gap.

However, plausibility alone does not establish legitimacy. In this paper, legitimacy refers to the extent to which a proposed digital pathway can reasonably be regarded as appropriate, acceptable, and warranted within its intended context of use. This understanding is consistent with broader conceptualisations of legitimacy as a socially constructed judgement about whether an intervention is perceived as appropriate within a particular context (Suchman, 1995). We conceptualise legitimacy not as an inherent property of digital technology, but as a question that should be examined with those who may use, deliver, or be affected by it. For AI-enabled pathways, this includes consideration of purpose, trust, ethical justification, transparency, system fit, and the appropriate boundaries of AI, rather than assuming acceptability in advance.

Examining these questions requires attention to young people's lived experiences of social connection and community participation, alongside the perspectives of organisations that provide or support these opportunities.

Co-Design as Early-Stage Inquiry

Co-design provides a collaborative approach to examining complex problems by bringing lived experience into dialogue with professional and research expertise (Sanders & Stappers, 2008; Scholten & Granic, 2019; Trischler et al., 2019). In youth mental health and digital contexts, it can surface expectations, concerns, and conditions of engagement that may be overlooked in expert-led development (Ospina-Pinillos et al., 2025). For proposed digital pathways in youth social prescribing, early-stage codesign offers an opportunity to examine both the problem being addressed and the appropriateness of a technological response. This includes exploring whether a proposed pathway offers value beyond existing approaches, what young people would require to consider using it, and how it might fit within community practices. These questions extend co-design beyond refining features to examining a pathway's purpose, potential role, and boundaries.

Aim

This paper reports findings from an exploratory co-design project examining whether an AI-enabled digital pathway could meaningfully extend social prescribing to young people aged 18-29. Situated within a broader social prescribing landscape in which young people remain largely absent from both the evidence base and the design of existing models, the project was structured around a central question: could a digital pathway play a useful role in connecting young people to community arts-based social opportunities, and if so, under what conditions? Drawing on co-design workshops, community leader interviews, and youth co-researcher input, this paper aims to identify the key domains that emerged from that process and offer recommendations for future research involving digital pathways in youth social prescribing.

Materials and Methods

Project Design and Context

This exploratory qualitative study used co-design informed by participatory action research principles to examine the potential role and legitimacy of an AI-enabled digital pathway connecting young people with community arts-based social opportunities. It formed part of a broader youth social prescribing project, with technical development and associated findings reported separately (Rickard et al., 2026). Co-design was grounded in shared sense-making, active participation, and iterative reflection (Kemmis et al., 2014; Reason & Bradbury, 2008). Young people contributed accounts of existing discovery and participation practices, assessed an exploratory prototype, and identified conditions under which a digital pathway might be useful or acceptable. Community leader interviews contributed perspectives on organisational responsibilities and the pathway's relationship to existing community provision. Subsequent involvement of paid youth co-researchers supported interpretation and refinement, while final decisions remained with the academic research team.

Co-Design Methods

Youth Workshops: Three youth co-design workshops were conducted, each accommodating between six and nine participants and lasting approximately 1.5 hours. Both face-to-face and online workshop options were offered to maximise accessibility for diverse youth. Workshops were facilitated by members of the research team (authors SM and NR). Each session opened with facilitators working to establish a psychologically safe environment, setting clear expectations and using supportive prompts to encourage open and honest contribution, including perspectives that differed from those of other participants. The workshops incorporated three structured participatory activities designed to elicit both written and verbal responses regarding young people's experiences of, and decision-making around, social and arts-based participation, as well as their preferences for platform design. Together, these activities provided contextual information on participation patterns and discovery pathways to inform design parameters. The activities were Engagement and Discovery Mapping, Plan or Pass, and Mock-up Feedback (see Figure 1).

The Engagement and Discovery Mapping activity comprised an Activity Wall and 'Quick 6' activity. The

Activity Wall served as an opening warm-up: participants identified arts-based activities they already participated in and those they were interested in trying. The accompanying discussion explored what distinguished current participation from interest without participation, including reasons participants had not pursued activities that interested them. The Quick 6 activity then prompted participants to consider how they usually discovered activities. Participants identified discovery methods, selected a preferred method, and discussed the advantages and limitations of those methods. Verbal and written responses were recorded, and photographs of the Activity Wall taken.

The Plan or Pass activity explored the factors influencing participants' decisions to attend or not attend social and arts-based activities. Participants identified three barriers and three enablers to participation.

Participants then shared their notes in two smaller groups in the metropolitan workshops and as a whole group in the six-participant Mildura workshop, and discussed what helps them decide whether an activity is for them or not, providing insight into the practical, social and personal considerations involved in deciding whether to take part. As a group, they grouped sticky notes into clusters and labelled each cluster. Final groupings and labels were photographed for analysis.

The Mock-up Feedback activity invited participants to review an exploratory prototype of an AI-enabled conversational platform, Connie, and identify its strengths, limitations and possible improvements. The prototype was used to make the proposed pathway tangible and support discussion of its purpose and potential role; it did not represent a settled intervention design. Prompts included: "Is the voice relatable?

Does it feel like it's trying too hard? Would you trust it with info? Does it give you choice, or feel pushy?" All verbal and written responses were recorded. The activity concluded with a postcard reflection, prompted by "This would work for me if…", inviting participants to articulate conditions under which such a tool would be useful or acceptable to them and share any additional thoughts anonymously. Data analysis

The data from the codesign workshops, community leader interviews and co-researcher input were coded, summarised and collated with the intention of identifying the key domains of importance, and informing recommendations for future research involving digital pathways for social prescribing. Written responses from the three workshop activities, recorded on butcher's paper, sticky notes and worksheets, were compiled and analysed separately by activity, with frequency counts used to summarise repeated or similar responses (see Supplementary Tables 1 2 3). Engagement and Discovery Mapping responses were compiled descriptively to characterise participation patterns and discovery pathways. Activity Wall responses were collated and analysed descriptively to summarise activities participants already undertook and those they were interested in trying. Quick 6 responses were collated to summarise discovery methods mentioned, participants' selected preferred methods, and the perceived advantages and limitations of those methods. For Plan or Pass, barrier and enabler mentions were coded and grouped into higher-level categories (e.g., cost, accessibility, timing), with frequency counts used to identify the most commonly raised decision-making factors. Mock-up Feedback responses were sorted into positive, negative, and suggested improvement categories, with counts used to summarise recurring feedback; a summary of the most cited items was subsequently reviewed with the three youth co-researchers, who validated findings and helped prioritise design considerations. Responses to the closing postcard reflection were compiled and considered alongside prototype feedback.

Audio recordings from the workshops and community leader interviews were transcribed and analysed independently by two members of the research team. Reflexive thematic analysis (Braun & Clarke, 2022) was applied across all qualitative data sources using an inductive approach, with findings iteratively compared and refined to produce higher-order themes. Related themes were then organised into three overarching domains. The analysis considered collated written outputs from all workshop activities alongside participants' explanations in workshop transcripts, online chat contributions, and video reflections. Interpretations were explored and refined through discussion and return to the source data.

Findings across the workshop activities, community leader interviews and youth co-researcher input also informed the recommendations

The large language model, ChatGPT (o4-mini), was accessed via the University's secure internal environment (SparkAI) to support limited secondary analytic tasks -specifically, to critique researcherdeveloped theme labels and propose alternatives based on summarised material. Raw transcripts and datasets were not uploaded. This use was approved by the project ethics committee and complied with University guidance on AI in research. All final analytic decisions remained with the research team.

Given their dual roles as workshop facilitators and qualitative analysts, SM and NR maintained a reflexive stance throughout, acknowledging that their positionality as wellbeing researchers with an interest in digital tools for youth mental health both informed and potentially shaped interpretation.

Consistent with Braun and Clarke (2022), researcher subjectivity was treated as a resource for active theme construction rather than a source of bias to be controlled. Trustworthiness was supported through cross-source comparison, ongoing team discussion, and co-researcher validation of emerging findings.

Results

The key themes derived from the collated and synthesised data (see Supplementary Materials, Tables 1 2 3) are organised below according to the principal domains that emerged from the thematic analysis.

Domain 1: Legitimacy and Purpose of Digital Pathways

The Discovery activity identified what participants already valued in their approaches to finding opportunities for connection, alongside needs those approaches did not consistently meet. Social media and word of mouth through friends, family and personal networks were the most frequently selected discovery methods. Youth participants valued recommendations through personal networks because recommenders knew participants' interests, provided reassurance about the people attending, and could offer someone to attend with. However, these recommendations did not always reflect participants' wider interests or introduce unfamiliar activities, and often required additional searching for information. Social media was valued for quick access to visual information, opportunities aligned with participants' interests, and ease of sharing activities with friends.

Its limitations included incomplete or misleading promotional information and repeated exposure to familiar, popular or sponsored content, which could make unfamiliar opportunities harder to discover. Prototype feedback also raised questions about the proposed pathway's distinctive value and format. Participants questioned how it differed from existing tools such as ChatGPT and whether it offered a compelling reason to use another AI application.

Feedback also expressed reluctance for the platform to rely entirely on a chatbot interface.

From an organisational perspective, a youth music leader described limitations in reaching young people beyond existing networks. Outreach through youth organisations, community centres and other programmes relied on young people already having some connection with those settings: "We know that the challenge for us is reaching the already disengaged."

(Youth Music Leader)

Domain 2: Pre-Engagement Conditions for Participation

Findings concerned conditions shaping young people's participation in both the research process and community activities. Within co-design, these related to accessible workshop formats and opportunities to contribute. For community participation, they encompassed discovery, identity and social fit, trust, practical feasibility, and readiness to engage.

Modes of participation in co-design

The regional Mildura workshop involved adaptations to both session delivery and modes of contribution.

The planned in-person session was conducted online in response to participants' preferences. During the session, participants contributed both verbally and through chat. Facilitators also provided written prompts in the chat following a participant request and incorporated chat responses into the shared activity boards. Online delivery therefore accommodated several ways of contributing within the same session.

Several video reflections also described the co-design process as a safe and inclusive experience:

I also felt very safe and comfortable being a part of it from the start." (Metro FG1 participant, video reflection) "My highlight on this project so far is just being able to bring my lived experience as a regional queer person to the table, which a lot of the time my perspective isn't really considered." (Youth co-researcher) Discovery and visibility of opportunities Participants described difficulties finding opportunities beyond familiar or prominent activities and obtaining sufficient information to evaluate them. In the Discovery activity, written responses indicated that Google/web searches required knowing what to search for and could favour popular or sponsored results.

Participants also described how differences in promotional resources affected which activities they encountered. One participant explained that smaller community activities could be less visible than those with greater advertising budgets: "So you might not be seeing the more like community level, small group activities that don't have the means to promote financially."

(Metro FG1 P1)

Finding an opportunity did not necessarily provide access to all its details. Information could be distributed across several channels, requiring participants to piece it together: "And then half the information is on Instagram, half is on the website, and half is like, we'll send you an e-mail" (Metro FG2 P9)

In the Plan or Pass activity, vague or confusing event descriptions were identified as reasons to pass on an opportunity. A participant in the regional Mildura group also described missing practical information in social media posts: "But the problem I have with it is that most times they do not add the ticket prices to these art shows." (Regional Mildura FG P2) Across these accounts, discovery involved both locating opportunities and assembling enough information to understand what they involved and assess whether to attend.

Identity, belonging and anticipated social fit

When considering an activity, participants took account of who would attend, whether they would feel welcome and how it aligned with their interests and identity. In the Plan or Pass activity, relevance to personal interests, familiar companions, similar-aged attendees and welcoming environments were identified as enablers of participation. Inclusivity statements and an Acknowledgement of Country were also identified as welcoming cues. Barriers included perceived exclusivity, racism, a mismatch with the intended demographic, an unappealing venue, and discomfort associated with the people, place or event.

One participant described difficulty finding a community around activities they were interested in: "Sometimes I find it hard to like find a community in some of these like hobbies as well."

FG1 P6)

Seeing who attended could help participants assess an unfamiliar opportunity. Asked what had helped them try something new, one participant said: "I think seeing videos of the people at the events. And seeing what the demographic's like."

The discussions also included uncertainty about how participants' gender or cultural identity might be received, and whether others would offer support if an uncomfortable interaction occurred. For a participant in the regional Mildura group, finding an activity compatible with their established cultural practice was another consideration. An Indian classical dancer described reluctance to join a school that would require changing the dance style they had learned: "So like I have been learning one style for the past 15 years, then I have to shift from that and then it's all totally a new thing."

(Regional Mildura FG P1)

These accounts linked anticipated social fit to the people and atmosphere participants expected to encounter, signs of welcome and inclusion, and opportunities to participate in ways consistent with their interests and identity.

Trust and transparency

Participants' accounts connected trust with the reliability and provenance of information, the institutional and commercial relationships behind recommendations, and transparency about the system and its handling of personal data. These concerns arose in discussions of existing discovery methods and responses to the Connie prototype.

In the Discovery activity, written responses identified misleading promotion and outdated information as limitations of social media. One participant described a discrepancy between an event's promotional appearance and their experience of attending: "I remember there was a Buddha festival here. It seemed quite big, but when I went there, it was just in a small car park. So it sometimes can be deceiving." (Metro FG1 P8)

When considering Connie, written prototype feedback identified incorrect event details and the burden of fact-checking results as concerns. Participants also wanted to know where information came from. One explained that the source would affect how they evaluated an answer: "Can we ask if we got this like how it came to these conclusions?

Just because like obviously like for me it would be very different if like it says like I went to the Herald Sun [a popular tabloid that leans conservative] and this is what the Herald Sun said." (Metro FG2 P1)

Honesty also involved acknowledging limitations and providing balanced assessments. Written feedback called for the tool to say when it did not know an answer rather than leave a question unanswered, and criticised overly positive responses. One participant explained: "Because that is the thing, if we just ask about everything, if it finds a positive in everything, then it's not very useful."

(Metro FG2 P8)

Participants also questioned commercial influence and the institutional relationships behind the tool.

Written prototype feedback criticised the prioritisation of sponsored content, while a participant sought clarification about funding and future ownership:

Do you know why Google's funding it? Is it going to be a Google product eventually?" (Metro FG2 P1) Transparency extended to the system's identity and use of personal information. Participants questioned attempts to make the chatbot sound human, with one describing this as inauthentic. Written feedback proposed allowing users to choose whether their preferences were stored, while postcard responses included a preference for information not to be retained. A participant asked: "I'm just more worried about like, is there a statement at the beginning that tells you like what it does with your data." (Metro FG1 P4)

Practical feasibility of participation

Participants identified practical conditions that shaped whether an activity was manageable, alongside their interest in it. In the Plan or Pass activity, cost, distance, transport complexity, scheduling conflicts and the time and energy required were recorded as barriers. Enablers included affordable activities, proximity, access by public transport and opportunities that fitted around existing commitments.

The postcard activity connected these considerations directly to the proposed pathway. Responses to "This would work for me if…" included finding activities that were affordable, easy to reach, personally interesting and available at suitable times.

Participants also identified information needed to assess the sensory and physical demands of an activity before attending. One participant explained:

"I think accessibility information, like the sensory aspect, the sound level … any quiet area … sometimes you don't know if you're standing, or if you're sitting down" (Metro FG2 P4)

In an anonymous postcard response, a participant who identified having ASD and PTSD described avoiding events without quiet spaces or the ability to step outside, and needing this information before attending.

Together, these findings identified affordability, travel requirements, timing, energy demands and environmental conditions as relevant to assessing whether an opportunity could realistically be taken up.

Participants described interest in activities alongside uncertainty about their ability or willingness to take part. In the Plan or Pass activity, confidence to try an activity was identified as an enabler, while burnout and feeling that an activity required existing expertise were recorded as barriers. During the Activity Wall discussion, one participant expressed interest in an activity but doubted their ability to take part: "I was looking at the circus aerial and I was like oh that would be fun but I could never do that."

Discussion

This study examined the potential role and boundaries of an AI-enabled digital pathway connecting young people with community arts-based social opportunities. Findings indicated that identifying an activity was only one part of deciding whether participation felt possible. Young people also considered anticipated social fit, trustworthiness of information, practical feasibility, and their confidence and readiness to participate. Prototype feedback questioned the distinctive value of another AI application and the suitability of an entirely chatbot-based interface, while community leaders highlighted responsibilities associated with recommending activities and supporting participation. Together, the three domains provided an empirically informed framework for examining the purpose, participation conditions, and system fit of proposed digital pathways in youth social prescribing. The following eight recommendations draw on this framework and relevant literature to guide future co-design research and development.

Practice-Oriented Guidance for Co-Designing Digital Pathways to Social Connection with Youth

Domain 1: Legitimacy and Purpose of Digital Pathways

The first consideration for early-stage co-design was how legitimacy should be understood across individual and system levels. For youth-facing digital pathways, this may include questions of relevance and trust for young people alongside responsibilities relating to safety, governance, and system fit.

Legitimacy therefore requires consideration not only of whether a pathway may be acceptable to potential users, but also of whether its purpose, role, and boundaries can be justified within the wider systems in which it would operate.

Recommendation 1. Use co-design to test legitimacy, not just refine solutions

Participants identified gaps in existing discovery methods while questioning the proposed prototype's distinctive value and chatbot format. These findings highlight the importance of examining both the need a pathway might address and the form it should take. Early-stage co-design should leave open the possibility that a proposed digital pathway may not be necessary or warranted. Where technology is introduced as a possible response to social disconnection, co-design should create space to interrogate the underlying problem framing, consider whose interests a proposed pathway serves, and explore whether alternative responses might better address the problem. This is particularly important in emerging technology contexts, where technological innovation can otherwise become implicitly equated with progress or benefit (Pronzato, 2025).

We therefore argue that early-stage co-design should remain conceptually open rather than treating digital interventions as valid or necessary by default. Young people should be able to question whether a digital pathway is needed, challenge the assumptions underpinning its development, and contribute alternative priorities that may not involve technological mediation (Figueroa et al., 2025). Used in this way, codesign can support informed judgement about whether a pathway should proceed, what role it might appropriately play, and where its boundaries should sit in relation to young people's lived realities and existing systems of support.

Domain 2: Pre-Engagement Conditions for Participation

Beyond questions of legitimacy, our findings indicated that early-stage co-design should attend to the conditions that may shape whether participation in community-based opportunities feels possible before formal engagement occurs.

Recommendation 2. Include and represent diverse voices in co-design

Inclusive co-design requires attention not only to who is invited to participate, but also to whether the available modes of participation are accessible to them. Conventional in-person, group-based approaches may create barriers for some young people, including those experiencing social anxiety, neurodivergence, geographic isolation, or discomfort with institutional environments. Existing work on youth co-design similarly highlights the importance of considering how participation processes themselves can enable or constrain meaningful involvement (Bevan Jones et al., 2020; Vella-Brodrick et al., 2023). The regional workshop was initially planned as an in-person session but moved online in response to participants' preferences. Participants contributed verbally and through chat, and written prompts were provided following a participant request. These adaptations illustrate how flexibility in both workshop delivery and modes of contribution can support participation in co-design.

Early-stage co-design with marginalised youth should therefore accommodate multiple modes of participation, potentially including asynchronous digital methods, individual conversations, online workshops, and other low-pressure engagement opportunities. At the same time, researchers must make deliberate decisions about whose experiences are foregrounded. Attempting to represent every form of marginalisation within a single exploratory project may produce superficial inclusion rather than depth.

Purposefully engaging particular groups can enable closer examination of relevant experiences while requiring explicit acknowledgement of the limits of representational reach.

Recommendation 3. Treat discovery as a potential barrier to participation

Our co-design findings underscored that access to community-based social opportunities, including arts, music and cultural events and activities, depends not only on their existence, but also on whether they can be found, understood, and evaluated. For young people navigating fragmented online and offline information environments, discovery may therefore represent an important pre-engagement consideration rather than simply a usability issue (Creative Australia, 2025).

Early-stage co-design can be used to examine how existing discovery pathways make some forms of participation more visible than others, how young people search for and interpret information about opportunities, and what information may be needed to judge whether an activity feels relevant or appropriate. Treating discovery as a design consideration shifts attention away from the assumption that making information available is sufficient, and towards the broader conditions required for informed participation.

Recommendation 4. Account for identity, belonging and social fit

Our findings indicated that decisions about community participation involved more than logistical accessibility. Questions of identity, belonging, and anticipated social fit may shape whether participation feels viable, including whether an activity feels welcoming, personally relevant, or "for people like me" (Haslam et al., 2024). These relational considerations warrant explicit attention alongside practical factors such as location, timing, and cost.

Digital pathways should therefore support users to assess opportunities not only in terms of logistical access, but also in relation to social atmosphere, belonging, and perceived fit. Early-stage co-design can help identify the types of information and cues that may be relevant to these anticipatory judgements.

Treating identity, belonging, and social fit as design-relevant considerations may reduce uncertainty surrounding participation while preserving young people's autonomy in deciding which opportunities feel appropriate for them.

Recommendation 5. Co-design trust and transparency

Trust in digital systems should not be assumed to follow from technical functionality alone, particularly design problem rather than as a secondary communication issue (Stade et al., 2025).

Early-stage co-design can therefore be used to explore what transparency and trustworthy operation mean from young people's perspectives. Approaching trust as a condition to be negotiated through design enables legitimate scepticism about digital systems to be considered prospectively, rather than treated simply as resistance to adoption or addressed only after disengagement has occurred.

Recommendation 6. Treat feasibility as essential design knowledge

Practical feasibility is an important consideration in whether community participation can realistically occur. Relevant factors may include sensory demands, social demands, accessibility, energy requirements, transport, cost, timing, and flexibility around arrival, participation, and exit (Commonwealth of Australia, 2018; Willmersdorf Steffen et al., 2025). These constraints may vary across contexts and over time and therefore warrant consideration before a digital pathway is designed around assumptions of availability or motivation.

Early-stage co-design can be used to explore how potential participants assess whether an opportunity is practically manageable and where barriers may arise despite interest in participation. Treating feasibility as design-relevant knowledge rather than as a downstream implementation concern can help reduce the risk that digital pathways inadvertently privilege people for whom participation is comparatively straightforward.

Recommendation 7. Acknowledge readiness as dynamic

Young people may approach community participation from different starting points and with varying levels of confidence, motivation, energy, and willingness to engage. Readiness should therefore not be treated as a fixed trait or simple prerequisite for participation. Instead, it may vary according to context, identity, previous experience, and changing life circumstances (Kwasnicka et al., 2016; Willmersdorf Steffen et al., 2025).

Digital pathways could be designed to accommodate this variability by enabling low-pressure exploration, observation, and phased engagement without imposing urgency or an expectation of immediate attendance. Designing with readiness in mind can support autonomy and allow engagement to unfold at a pace that is appropriate to the individual.

Domain 3: System and Stakeholder Conditions

Finally, our co-design process highlighted that the legitimacy of a youth-facing digital pathway should be considered not only from the perspective of potential users, but also in relation to the community and service systems within which it might operate. Relevant considerations revealed in our sessions included safety, responsibility, governance, feasibility, and how digital pathways might complement existing human and organisational practices. Digital systems intended for young people must manage risk appropriately, but highly restrictive approaches may also have implications for autonomy, transparency, and perceptions of institutional control (Figueroa et al., 2025). Safety should therefore be considered as a design and governance question involving the balance between protection, user autonomy, and the appropriate limits of system curation, rather than as risk minimisation alone (Stade et al., 2025).

Community leaders' concerns about endorsement, safeguards, and local relationships highlighted the importance of examining these questions across stakeholder groups. Multi-stakeholder co-design can create a forum in which potential benefits, ethical responsibilities, implementation realities, and system boundaries are considered together. This can help clarify how a proposed digital pathway might complement existing social prescribing and community practices without positioning technology as a replacement for human or organisational support.

Study Contributions

This study contributes an empirically informed account of the conditions under which a digital pathway might support young people's access to community arts-based social opportunities. Across the three domains, participants' accounts highlighted the importance of the needs a pathway would address, the conditions shaping participation, and its relationship to existing community practices. Implications and Future Directions

For young people poorly served by GP referral and link-worker pathways, digital discovery may offer an additional entry point to community opportunities. Organisations commissioning or developing such pathways should examine their intended role with young people and community stakeholders while key decisions remain open. In this study, the exploratory prototype made the proposed pathway tangible so participants could examine its purpose, potential value, and boundaries before commitment to a particular design or implementation approach. Such inquiry can inform decisions about whether to proceed, how a pathway might complement existing community supports without displacing them, and which aspects require further development or reconsideration.

Future research should build on this inquiry through continued co-design with youth co-researchers to translate the identified pre-engagement conditions into design concepts for further testing before implementation. Development could explore conversational, visual, and combined discovery formats, including integration with existing services or platforms. Subsequent evaluation could examine uptake, readiness to attend, actual participation, and experiences of connection and wellbeing, alongside organisational feasibility and wider system effects.

Further research should examine the framework across different populations and community settings. For AI-enabled pathways, transparency, autonomy, recommendation logic, and role boundaries warrant further ethical and participatory scrutiny (Stade et al., 2025). Young people and community stakeholders should remain involved throughout development and evaluation, with accessible participation methods and clear opportunities to shape decisions. Partnerships with community organisations will be important for assessing the accuracy and coverage of local information and the practical requirements of maintaining a pathway.

Limitations

This exploratory study drew on a purposively selected sample of young people and community leaders in Victoria, Australia. Although recruitment sought diverse perspectives, the sample does not represent the full diversity of marginalised young people or community settings. The framework therefore requires further examination and adaptation across populations and contexts. Findings concern participants' accounts of community participation and responses to an exploratory prototype. They do not establish how young people would use a developed pathway or whether its use would lead to participation. This exploratory study did not evaluate effects on participation, social connection, wellbeing, or service systems.

The academic researchers facilitated the workshops and led the analysis, and their interest in digital approaches to youth wellbeing may have shaped interpretation. Youth co-researchers contributed to interpretation and refinement, while final decisions remained with the academic team. The prototype also provided a particular representation of the proposed pathway, which may have influenced the possibilities and concerns participants identified.

Conclusion

Extending social prescribing through youth-facing digital pathways requires more than technical capability. It requires judgement about whether, where, and for whom a pathway is warranted. Young people and community leaders identified participation conditions and organisational responsibilities that extend beyond finding available activities. The three domains and eight recommendations provide an empirically informed basis for early-stage co-design, grounding decisions in young people's lived experiences and the ethical responsibilities of community participation. Exploratory prototypes can make a proposed pathway tangible while its purpose, role, and boundaries remain open to question. This approach may help identify potential misalignments before design and implementation decisions are finalised. Before there is a tool, there is a question to examine with those it is intended to support: what role, if any, should it play?

Their legitimacy therefore cannot be assessed solely in terms of user acceptability. Early-stage co-design should also consider questions of governance, organisational responsibility, safety, feasibility, and the relationship between digital and human forms of support. Safeguarding requires particular care.
