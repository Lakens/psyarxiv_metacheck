Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

Text Sentence
p = 0.236 Although the prevalence estimate was numerically higher among premenopausal women, the difference between groups was not statistically significant (Q(1) = 1.41, p = 0.236).

For metascientific articles demonstrating the rate of misinterpretations of non-significant results is high, see:

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Migraine can improve after menopause, but prior reviews assessing postmenopausal migraine were limited by the available literature, finding improvement in some populations and worsening in others following menopause. This review and metaanalysis investigated the effect of 1) Menopausal status (premenopausal, postmenopausal) on migraine prevalence 2) Menopause Hormone Therapy (MHT) use in postmenopause on migraine prevalence 3) Menopausal status (premenopausal, postmenopausal) on migraine characteristics (headache frequency, migraine frequency, pain intensity).

Searches were conducted across Medline, Scopus, Web of Science and APA PsychINFO in accordance with PRISMA guidance. Included studies measured the prevalence or characteristics of migraine in postmenopausal and/or premenopausal women. Random effects meta-analysis of proportions was conducted to analyse migraine prevalence data, and Robust Bayesian Meta-analysis for migraine characteristics data. Study quality was assessed by modified version of JBI critical appraisal tool for prevalence studies.

From 1174 records screened, 30 studies were included (16 prevalence meta-analysis, 10 characteristic meta-analysis). The pooled prevalence of migraine was 22% for premenopausal and 16% for postmenopausal women, but the prevalence was not significantly different between the groups. Migraine prevalence was significantly higher Registration in postmenopausal women using MHT compared to those not.

Robust Bayesian metaanalysis showed strong evidence for an effect of menopausal status on migraine characteristics (BF10 = 63.84, g = -0.31), with postmenopausal women exhibiting more severe migraine than premenopausal women, weak evidence in favour of heterogeneity (BFrf= 1.54), and weak evidence against publication bias (BFpb = 0.72). There was weak evidence in favour of a moderation effect of measurement of migraine characteristic used (BF = 1.35) and weak evidence against a moderation effect of study quality (BF = 0.45).

Conclusion

Migraine prevalence did not differ significantly but was consistently reported as more severe in postmenopausal relative to premenopausal women. Whether this results from different (clinical, non-clinical) samples used in studies or reflects an overall increase in severity in migraine populations in postmenopause is unknown and requires longitudinal investigation. Future studies should consider comorbidities, menopausal symptoms, time since menopause, surgical menopause populations as well as MHT type and initiation to understand migraine course across postmenopause.

1. Introduction

Migraine is a highly burdensome [1] headache disorder, the prevalence and burden of which is twice that in women as in men [1, 2]. Often emerging around puberty [3] [4] [5], migraine changes are frequently associated with the menstrual cycle [6], as well as other reproductive timepoints such as pregnancy [7] and the menopause transition.

Around 40% of a women's life is spent in postmenopause, where migraine remains problematic in a relevant population of women [8]. The prevalence of migraine globally is projected to rise in coming decades [9], so effective treatment is of increasing importance. However, little guidance for migraine treatment is available specific to this population [10, 11].

During reproductive years, in premenopause, migraine in many women is highly related to hormone changes during the menstrual cycle [6]. The oestrogen withdrawal hypothesis [12] states that when oestrogen is rapidly declining in the days leading up to menstruation there is an increased chance of migraine attack [12] [13] [14]. Women with migraine additionally exhibit steeper oestrogen decline in this late luteal phase [15]. It is likely that other factors occurring at this premenstrual phase additionally contribute to this vulnerability [16], with exact menstrual migraine mechanisms unclear [13, 16, 17].

The menopause is timestamped by the final menstrual period. Beyond this pointconfirmed after 12 months of amenorrhea-a women is considered to be in postmenopause [18, 19]. Symptoms of the menopause transition may arise years prior, in the perimenopausal period, which spans from the menopause transition start, when menstrual cycles become irregular to the postmenopause diagnosis [18]. During the late menopause transition and early postmenopause (first 2 years), when oestrogen levels decline rapidly, vasomotor symptoms (VMS) most commonly occur [18, 20] and migraine may worsen. VMS, such as hot flushes and night sweats that occur in the peri and postmenopause can be incredibly bothersome, and severe hot flushes are 30% more likely in those with migraine [21, 22]. An increase in comorbidities common in perimenopause too such as depression, anxiety and insomnia, along with VMS can contribute to worsened migraine [10]. Later in postmenopause, from 5-8 years since menopause [18], oestrogen stabilises at low levels, from which migraine is expected to improve.

The classification of reproductive life stages is based off menstrual regularity, as well as longitudinal sampling of hormones across natural menopause [18, 20]. However, early menopause may occur such as in premature ovarian insufficiency (menopause at <40 years), which can occur naturally, or be induced medically such as by surgery. Around 12% of postmenopausal women are surgically postmenopausal, by removal of the ovaries [23]. Hormonal decline is drastic following surgical menopause, resulting in higher prevalence of disruptive symptoms [24]. In surgical menopause populations, migraine prevalence is additionally higher than naturally menopausal women [11, 25, 26]. The surgical menopause population is therefore recommended to be prescribed Menopause Hormone Therapy (MHT) [27]. The first-line treatment of VMS is MHT, previously referred to as Hormone Replacement Therapy, traditionally consisting of an oestrogen alone or in combination with progesterone. The effect of MHT on migraine is previously reported as variable, with use often-causing worsening, but this depends heavily on the type and administration [8, 11, 28].

In 2021 a systematic review was published [8] looking at the patterns of migraine in postmenopausal women, which similarly proceeded one published in 2015 [28].

Neither of these systematic reviews conducted meta-analysis of the included studies.

Most recently, a 2026 narrative review was published looking at migraine across the menopause transition, but focussed more on pathophysiology and clinical management [11]. Although migraine is often seen to improve in postmenopause, particularly in migraine without aura [11], systematic searches yield conflicting results, differing by populations sampled [8, 28]. In some studies sampling from headache and migraine clinics, that may represent a more severe sample, migraine is seen to worsen in the postmenopause [29], whereas other population-based studies observe migraine improvement after menopause [28]. Evidence as gathered in prior reviews overall is mixed and limited [8, 28].

Although postmenopausal migraine has gained attention in reviews in recent years [8, 11, 17], there has not yet been an updated systematic review and meta-analysis incorporating the latest evidence. This is required to objectively quantify the effect of postmenopausal status on migraine, between-study heterogeneity, and evaluate evidence of publication bias. Individual prompts were created for each extraction variable (e.g., study_authors -> Prompt: State the author names from this study in APA format) and are available on the OSF page (https://osf.io/qhw4v/overview). Running through RStudio [33] and using Google Gemini (Gemini 2.5 flash [34]), the tool provided responses and source information from the included articles. Each response given by the AI tool was cross-checked with the cited location within the article and changed/edited as necessary by both reviewers independently. Data extraction outputs for each study variable were checked and consensus reached between the two reviewers (AG, RJ).

Quality assessment items were rated by both reviewers (AG, RJ) using the AIDE tool.

Assessment items were adapted from the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) critical appraisal tool for prevalence studies [35], and are available on the OSF page (https://osf.io/qhw4v/overview). A quality assessment score was created for each included study, calculated as a response of yes=1 or no/unclear=0. A final score was calculated by adding the scores and dividing this by the total items applicable to the study. The final quality score was used as a moderator in the meta-analysis. Quality assessment of all studies in the review was overviewed by calculating the % of studies meeting each of the outlined criteria.

Where data could not be extracted from the included articles, the corresponding study authors were contacted by email to request this information. In instances where values were reported non-parametrically, they were converted to mean and standard deviation using RStudio package "estmeansd" [36] . Additionally, where the data range and sample size were reported, standard deviations could additionally be estimated by the formula proposed by Wan and colleagues in 2014 [37], using this same R package [36].

2.4. Method of synthesis

Meta-analyses were conducted in the statistical software environment R version 4.5.2 [33]. Data and analysis code are available at the OSF page (https://osf.io/qhw4v/overview).

All prevalence studies reporting migraine prevalence in premenopausal and/or postmenopausal populations were eligible for inclusion in the prevalence metaanalysis. Meta-analyses of proportions were run using the R package meta [38]. A random-effects model was used to account for expected between-study heterogeneity.

Prevalence estimates were logit transformed prior to analyses. This approach is employed in probability modelling to remove bounds from values to allow for linear regression [39]. Between-study heterogeneity was assessed by Cochran's Q, I² , τ, τ² , and prediction interval statistics. Menopause stage (premenopausal, postmenopausal) was specified as a subgroup variable, allowing pooled prevalence estimates to be calculated across all included studies and separately within each menopause stage, and test for differences between subgroups.

Studies which additionally reported migraine prevalence in postmenopausal women by MHT use (MHT use, non-MHT use) were included in a separate meta-analysis of proportions. This model similarly used random effects modelling, but with MHT use (MHT users, non-MHT users) as the subgroup. For both meta-analyses of proportions, a p value of < 0.05 was considered significant.

For inclusion in the migraine characteristics meta-analysis, studies must have reported at least one characteristic of migraine in both premenopause and postmenopause populations.

For the meta-analysis on migraine characteristics, effect sizes were calculated as Hedges' g, representing the standardized mean difference between premenopausal and postmenopausal groups for each migraine characteristic within each study. Robust Bayesian Meta-analysis (RoBMA) was employed using package RoBMA (4.0.0) [40].

RoBMA simultaneously estimates 36 competing models that vary in their assumptions regarding the presence of an effect, heterogeneity, and publication bias. Bayesian model averaging is then used to weight each model according to its posterior probability based on how well it predicts the observed data. RoBMA uses Bayes factor to quantify the evidence in favour of the presence or absence of an effect, heterogeneity, and publication bias. This represents a major advantage over the standard frequentist methods which can only reject the null hypothesis [41, 42]. Evidence was interpreted using the classification proposed by Lee and Wagenmakers (2014) [43], whereby Bayes factors (BF₁₀) of 1-3, 3-10, and ≥10 were considered as weak, moderate, and strong evidence, respectively. In other words, the observed data were 1-3 times, 3-10 times, or more than 10 times more likely under the alternative hypothesis than under the null hypothesis.

As a general rule-of-thumb in meta-regression, 1 moderator can be included for every 10 papers in a model [44] . Due to the inclusion of 10 papers in the RoBMA analysis, this was modelled twice, with one moderator in each: migraine characteristics (headache frequency, migraine frequency, pain intensity) and quality assessment scores.

3.4. Narrative Synthesis

>Narrative Synthesis

3.4.1. Changes across menopause

Of the studies assessing migraine prevalence by menopause status, 3 studies within this review reported a lower migraine prevalence in the postmenopausal compared to premenopausal women [45, 47, 74], and an additional study similarly found lower migraine prevalence in natural menopause compared to premenopause, but not for surgical menopause, where migraine risk was higher [51]. One of these comparisons [47] was limited by the premenopausal group containing women of unknown menopausal status, aged between 45-59, so may include perimenopausal women also.

Contrary to this, the largest study in this review, capturing data from over 1.8 million women from the South Korean National Health Insurance Service (NHIS) cohort reports higher postmenopausal migraine prevalence at 21.37%, as compared to premenopausal at 19.39% [54], contradicting the other comparison studies of migraine prevalence.

Characteristic studies comparing menopausal groups most consistently reported more severe migraine outcomes in postmenopausal populations than in premenopausal [55, 61, 63-67, 69, 70]. The exception to this is a study of women with symptoms of androgen deficiency, for which the perceived severity of migraine (0-4 scale) was reported on average to be higher 3.72 ( 0.52) in premenopausal women than in postmenopausal women 3.50 ( 0.59), at the baseline timepoint [73].

Using cross-sectional data of migraineurs who were premenopausal, perimenopausal and postmenopausal, a 2016 study [69] found that the risk of experiencing high frequency headache (>10 days/month) was highest for those in perimenopause.

Despite a similar prevalence of high frequency headache exhibited in the postmenopausal group, in the adjusted model accounting for BMI, medication use/overuse and depression, the increased risk was not significant in postmenopause, implying that migraine worsening with hormonal changes is most significant in perimenopause [69].

In reference to change across menopause, none of the studies report on longitudinal change in migraine across the stages. However, majority of evidence in characteristic studies suggests a worsening of migraine as experienced in postmenopausal women, where many prevalence studies suggest migraine lessened in postmenopausal women, with the exception of the NHIS cohort [54].

3.4.2. Surgical vs natural menopause

In the general postmenopausal population, approximately 10-15% are postmenopausal due to oophorectomy surgery [23] (removal of the ovaries), but this population is not always reflected in the research samples. A majority of studies reported the exclusion of surgical menopause from the postmenopausal population [46, 47, 54, 56, 58, 65, 66, 68, 69, 71, 72]. Of those including surgical menopause, this population made up 10-35% of the postmenopausal cohort [48, 49, 51, 53, 57, 63, 64, 73, 74] (% not obtainable in 3 papers [49, 73, 74]), with only one study of a purely surgical postmenopause population [50].

Compared to natural menopausal populations, surgical postmenopausal women have a higher prevalence of migraine [51, 53, 57], more often experience worsening of migraine following menopause [57] and experience an increased risk of migraine with MHT use [53].

In one study [51], the difference in migraine prevalence between menopause types is amplified in those with a history of premenstrual syndrome (PMS), in which the surgical menopause group is the highest at 44% migraine prevalence, and with PMS history, natural menopause prevalence is lowest, at only 7%.

3.4.3. Migraine with/without aura

Five studies in the review explicitly include migraine with aura and report based on migraine type (with or without aura) [48, 50, 51, 58, 70], with only two studies comparing the risk of each migraine type in postmenopausal women [47, 50]. Both found that migraine without aura only is associated with the menopause in populations of surgical [50] and natural menopause [47]. In natural postmenopausal populations, migraine prevalence is reduced by time since menopause for migraine without aura only, and migraine with aura had no association with menopausal status [47]. In the study of entirely surgically postmenopausal women, the migraine risk in this population was associated with migraine without aura, and not seen to elevated for migraine with aura [50].

In summary, while only a few studies report on the differentiation between migraine types (without/with aura), it is migraine without aura exclusively that is associated with menopause [47, 50]. This is in line with findings in menstrual migraine research, in which migraine without aura is more commonly related to oestrogen withdrawal [6, 76].

3.4.4. MHT types

The administrative route by which MHT is received may affect migraine outcomes. The use of systemic oral MHT, such as in pill form, is associated with increase migraine frequency [71, 72], but this is not the case for transdermal MHT [71]. Similarly, the prevalence of migraine is higher in postmenopausal users of systemic oestrogens (21%) compared to local MHT (14%) [52].

With respect to formulation of the MHT, a randomised control trial found that for migraine without aura, regular low dose MHT had a negative effect on migraine outcomes, increasing the frequency of migraine, but that tibolone, a prohormone alternative to oestrogen and progesterone, did not have this effect [68]. In women presenting with androgen deficiency, migraine severity can improve with testosterone therapy in both pre and postmenopausal women [73]. Users of oestrogen only MHT had higher prevalence of migraine compared to combined MHT or progesterone alone formulations [53]. The associations with menopause type may be driven by the fact that oestrogen and progesterone (combined) containing MHT is less likely to be taken by surgical menopausal populations [53].

Overall, oral MHT formulations seem to pose a risk to increasing migraine, whereas alternatives to systemic oestrogens such as transdermal MHT, tibolone and testosterone may reduce this risk. All of the included studies are either cross-sectional real-world population studies [52, 53] or MHT trials [68, [71] [72] [73], so real world longitudinal studies of different MHT types on migraine are needed. Additionally, given that MHT use is more commonly required in the surgical menopausal population, investigations in this population are required, unfortunately this population is most often excluded from clinical trials [68, 71, 72].

3.4.5. Time since menopause

According to the oestrogen withdrawal hypothesis, and evidence of oestrogen decline in early postmenopause followed by stabilisation, it can be assumed that migraine in postmenopausal women might improve with time since menopause. Despite this assumption, only 20% of the studies reported the time since menopause in their populations [47, 56, 68, 71, 72, 77], and only two report the effect on migraine [47, 77].

Generally clinical trials in this review (measuring migraine characteristics with MT use) represent the early postmenopause population (average time since menopause 10.4 months to 2.4 years [68, 71, 72]), where observational studies have longer and more varied time since menopause within study populations (mean = 7  4.5 years [56]; mean = 8.3 6.5 years [77]; mean = 12.8 7.3 years [47]).

When analysed with reference to migraine prevalence, one observational study found that time since menopause was associated with migraine without aura prevalence, where shorter time since menopause was mostly highly associated (χ2 = 9.0, p =.003) [47]. Contradictory to this, from the largest cohort in this review (National Health Insurance Survey Cohort), authors reported that the incidence of migraine increased with time since menopause (grouped by: <5 years, <10 years, <15 years, 15+ years) [77].

In early postmenopause, within the first 5 years, incidence of migraine was 14.8%, but this increases within each grouping reaching 16.9% in the late postmenopause (15+ years). However, the generalisation of findings from this study may be limited by their exclusion of migraine history. In the studies reporting on this cohort [54, 77], women with a history of migraine in the 4 years prior to the observation period were excluded.

Considering that the majority of other studies in this review do not exclude migraine history -with some studies actually stipulating migraine of premenopausal onset as an inclusion criteria [65, 66, 68, 71] -this cohort may represent a different population.

Overall, few studies report on time since menopause, and even fewer incorporate this into analysis of postmenopausal migraine [47, 77]. The evidence for migraine prevalence lessening with time since menopause is conflicting, with support from some research [47], but not in other populations [77]. This may in part be due to differences in inclusion/exclusion of migraine history between the two studies.

4. Discussion

>Discussion

4.1. Overview of findings

This review is the first of its kind to perform a meta-analysis assessing migraine in postmenopause. The meta-analysis aimed to investigate the effects of: menopausal status (premenopausal, postmenopausal) on migraine prevalence, postmenopausal MHT use on migraine prevalence, and menopausal status on migraine characteristics.

Findings suggest that estimates of migraine prevalence by menopausal status are highly heterogeneous. Although the pooled estimates were in the direction of lower prevalence among postmenopausal than premenopausal populations, the difference was not statistically significant. Our results also showed that migraine prevalence was significantly higher among postmenopausal women using MHT than among those not using MHT. Narratively, it is observed that migraine is consistently more prevalent in surgically postmenopausal populations compared to natural menopause [51, 53, 57].

For studies assessing migraine characteristics, the literature was less heterogeneous, with postmenopausal women consistently exhibiting higher migraine frequency, headache frequency and pain intensity than premenopausal women with migraine.

The results of this review partly support the oestrogen withdrawal hypothesis [12] which stipulates that migraines would improve over time during postmenopause with the stabilisation of female hormones. Only two of the studies reported migraine prevalence by time since menopause [47, 77], with one in support of the hypothesis [47], and one not [77]. The quality of included studies in this review was assessed collectively. Studies with high quality ratings describe procedures, analysis and use validated migraine characteristic assessment. When study quality was included as a moderator in the RoBMA analysis, there was evidence against (that is, weak evidence for no effect of) study quality as a moderator.

4.2. Previous reviews

The 2021 review by Ornello and colleagues aimed to assess patterns of migraine in postmenopausal women, looking also at the role of sex hormones [8]. There are similarities therefore in that both our review and theirs assessed prevalence and characteristics in migraine and examined MHT effects. Our review however differed by search strategy, inclusion criteria and analytic approach. Our searches used broad Boolean operator terms, citation searching and grey literature searching as strategies to capture relevant research. Our inclusion criteria differed also, as our review included papers from premenopausal populations where migraine was assessed. Additionally, our review had more exclusive criteria for study population such as for postmenopause grouping (not by age alone, not including perimenopause). Finally, in both previous reviews [8, 28], meta-analyses were not conducted due to a limited number of relevant articles retrieved, where in the present review, sufficient data was retrieved to conduct 3 meta-analyses addressing the study aims.

Previous systematic reviews reported mixed findings regarding prevalence and characteristics of migraine in postmenopause. This is due to the limited number of studies included but could also be attributed to difference between community and clinic-based populations. Clinical populations may present with more severe migraine and differences between samples may drive differing results across studies [8, 28].

The present meta-analysis reflects mixed findings across prevalence studies. Our review found that studies comparing migraine prevalence by menopausal status recruited from the community/public [45, 47, 54, 74], and majority reported lower prevalence in postmenopausal populations [45, 47, 74]. Clinical populations were sampled exclusively in studies assessing the characteristics of migraine, and reported more severe outcomes in postmenopause [55, 61, [63] [64] [65] [66] [67] [68]. The differences highlighted may indeed be due to differences in the sampling method as stated previously [8, 28].

However, given there are no studies directly comparing results between these groups (comparing clinic vs community on prevalence and characteristics), we cannot assume that sampling differences alone account for these mixed findings.

It is possible that for a proportion of women, more likely those having undergone natural menopause, migraine improves following menopause as reflected in retrospective data [57] and overall lower postmenopausal prevalence. The worsened migraine characteristics found in postmenopausal women therefore could reflect either that migraine worsens for some individuals following menopause, or that those with less severe migraine are more likely to remit, and the remaining population is more severe.

Without longitudinal data, the reason for these findings remains unknown.

Structural heterogeneity is common in prevalence meta-analysis, arising from differing methods of assessing prevalence as well as disease definition and recruitment sampling [78], of which there was diversity in the included studies (see section 3.2.3.).

Variation in study design can be observed for the characteristic studies also, but this is better accounted for by the statical model, as RoBMA is designed to perform under high heterogeneity [41]. In this way, the method of analysis may have contributed to the different overall findings between prevalence and characteristic data.

4.3. Recently published studies

Almost half (14) of the studies included in this review were published after the previous systematic search in the topic was conducted in 2020 [8], highlighting the need for an updated search. With a larger sample of published studies, we were able to conduct meta-analyses on the prevalence and characteristics of migraine in postmenopausal women that could not be done previously due to smaller samples [8]. Notably, the new studies included data published from NHIS [79] with over 1.8 million participants, the largest dataset assessing migraine prevalence by menopausal status published to date.

From this cohort it was reported that in comparison to premenopausal women, migraine was more prevalent in postmenopausal women [54], contrary to the findings of other comparative papers [45, 47, 74]. The exclusion criteria for this study however may have contributed to the opposing finding. Individuals with a history of migraine prior to the study were excluded, which meant that women with preexisting migraine, whether this persisted or improved in postmenopause were not included. Other studies, which reported a lower prevalence in postmenopausal women did include individuals with a history of migraine, for some it was even a required inclusion criterion [65, 66, 68, 71].

4.4. Migraine comorbidities

Migraine is associated with many comorbid conditions, so certain samples will exhibit higher prevalence rates. The highest migraine prevalence values reported in the included studies (41% [58] and 47% [49]) were from populations of women with endometriosis [58] and angina [49] respectively, conditions previously identified as related to migraine [80, 81].

Some comorbidities of migraine are independently exacerbated by the menopause transition and therefore relevant to consider in postmenopausal populations. One study within this review finds that in postmenopausal women, symptoms of anxiety and insomnia are significantly higher in those who experience headache and migraine, compared to those who do not [57]. Insomnia, as well as anxiety and depression exhibit bidirectional relationships with migraine, with depression and anxiety often additionally comorbid with insomnia in the migraine population [82] and are each commonly reported as symptoms of menopause [83].

Insomnia as a comorbidity especially may be of interest as a therapeutic target for postmenopausal migraine, however only two studies in the review measure sleep or insomnia [57, 70]. Sleep disturbances, exacerbated by VMS [84] and declining melatonin [85] at the menopause can trigger the development of insomnia, which perpetuates and is perpetuated by migraine via behavioural consequences [86]. Initial evidence suggests that the first line treatment, cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia, in comorbid insomnia and migraine populations can reduce migraine severity following face-to-face [87, 88] and digital sessions [89]. In perimenopause populations, it is seen that the relationship between poor sleep and migraine is influenced by menopause associated factors such as depression, anxiety and VMS [90]. This relationship in postmenopausal populations and efficacy of insomnia treatment in postmenopausal migraine merits exploration.

Overall, the effect of highly correlating comorbidities on migraine outcomes should be considered in interpreting prevalence rates. Comorbidities relevant to postmenopause should be investigated in future studies, such as insomnia where there is therapeutic potential for postmenopausal migraine.

4.5. MHT findings

The meta-analysis supports and adds further weight to the existing evidence base that migraine risk can increase with MHT use, which has been reported narratively in previous reviews [8, 11, 17, 28]. Additionally, risks relating to oral use MHT, but more safety in newer formulations and with transdermal use has been identified in prior literature [11], and clinically observed for decades [91, 92]. Transdermal administrations of MHT, such as patches or gels, allow the medication to bypass initial metabolism, providing more stable concentrations of oestrogen [93], avoiding fluctuations which may trigger a migraine attack.

There is little available data to date assessing migraine risk with MHT use by migraine type (with/without aura). Given the increased migraine risk with MHT [52, 53], the reduced risk with transdermal formulations [71] and an increased stroke risk that is observed in women taking MHT with migraine with aura, the European Society of Endocrinology recommends that transdermal oestrogen is preferred for those diagnosed migraine with aura [94]. Additionally, case reports show that where migraine with aura is induced by oral MHT, changing to transdermal and lower doses would be beneficial [92].

The timing hypothesis, or critical window often discussed in MHT research hypothesises that MHT should be taken earlier in postmenopause for a favourable risk benefit ratio. This is evidenced by studies where earlier MHT initiation lowers the risk of cardiovascular events [95, 96], but contested by some experts [97]. The timing of initiating MHT in relation to migraine risk may merit exploration in future studies.

Finally, there is a lack of research investigating MHT use in surgical menopause populations with respect to migraine risk. Current guidelines recommend that all women undergoing surgical menopause < 45 years should be offered MHT until at least age 51 [27]. Given that MHT use [53], menopausal symptoms [24] and general migraine risk [51, 53, 57] are higher in this population than in natural menopause, this population should be a priority in research moving forward.

4.6. Recommendations for future studies

Ongoing longitudinal studies such as the Study of Women's Health Across the Nation, of which one study in this review reports baseline data [46], and Australian Longitudinal Study on Women's Health [98] include migraine related questions in their data collection across menopause (e.g., "Since your last study visit, has a doctor, nurse practitioner or other health care provider told you that you had any of the following conditions or treated you for them-Migraines?", "Have you in the last 12 months experienced headaches/migraine?"). However, longitudinal analysis of migraine prevalence across stages is yet to be published, and information on the characteristics of migraine is not captured. Future longitudinal studies measuring both prevalence and migraine characteristics across the postmenopause period would be of benefit to understanding what happens on an individual level during this time.

Comorbidities such as insomnia, depression and anxiety, as well as VMS that may worsen migraine should be considered in future analyses as moderating variables.

4.8. Days/attacks distinction

Another problem which may arise in assessing migraine frequency is the units of measurement (e.g., attacks per month or days per month). The diagnosis of migraine defines the headache phase as lasting 4-72 hours [75], commonly spanning more than a day. Diagnosis for chronic migraine is assessed based on migraine and headache frequency by days per month [75], commonly reflected in literature, but some studies instead use attacks per month [55, 61, 71, 72]. One study in this review measures migraine frequency by attacks per month and headache frequency by days per month [71].

Given the differences in prior literature, future studies should consider assessing both headache and migraine frequency distinctly, recording both the number of attacks and days with each. This data can be captured via headache diaries, often used in clinical care, an increasingly efficient form of data collection as digital diaries rise in popularity [102]. Usually used in clinic to monitor treatment efficacy, diary data allows for rich daily data collection, and is recommended for use in clinical trials for migraine research, also so that chronic diagnosis can be prospectively assessed [103]. Utilised in previous literature [63, 67, 68, 71, 72], migraine diaries should be considered in future research as thorough data collection, which can be minimally inconvenient because many participants are already recording this information to support their clinical care.

4.9. Limitations of the evidence base

In reviewing the quality of studies, some limitations within the current literature were identified. Firstly, 57% of studies in this review did not report response rate, so it could not be assessed if the study samples were representative of their target population.

Similarly, in assessing if sample sizes were adequate or in the identification of menopausal status, around 1/3 of studies were missing information on statistical power of findings, or in how women were categorised by menopausal status. Finally, half of studies did not use valid methods to identify migraine status, however this was due to the use of outdated diagnostic criteria in 9/15 of these studies, that would have been valid at the time of publication (ICHD [47, 51-53, 57, 71], ICHD II [45, 55, 68]). Despite these limitations in the literature, the moderation analysis in our meta-analysis provided weak evidence against a moderating effect of study quality on the association between menopausal status and migraine. In other words, the average effect did not appear to vary systematically according to study quality, suggesting that between-study heterogeneity is more likely attributable to other study characteristics.

As discussed previously, a limitation of the literature is that most studies of migraine prevalence are conducted in the general postmenopausal population, recruited through the community, but most characteristic studies were clinical populations. As migraine is such a common disorder, many women are not referred to specialist care, with the clinical population distinct and potentially representing more severe and less treatable cases of migraine. For this reason, a limitation of the literature, and therefore of this review also, is that the prevalence values and characteristic findings reported may not be generalisable.

4.10. Strengths of the review

The analytical methods of the present meta-analysis are a strength of this review.

Particularly, the use of Robust Bayesian Meta-Analysis (RoBMA) in the assessment of menopausal status on migraine characteristics. RoBMA is a recent and advanced approach to meta-analysis commended for improving accuracy through its multiple model method which adjusts for publication bias, heterogeneity and effect, considering uncertainty [40]. Together, this decreases the likelihood of false positives, which may occur in frequentist or single model Bayesian meta-analysis [104]. The present metaanalysis contributes to the literature strong evidence of migraine being more severe in the postmenopause population than in premenopause populations, as well as pooled migraine prevalence values for postmenopausal women, premenopausal women, as well as MHT users and non-users. This review provides recommendations and foundational evidence for future longitudinal and cross-sectional investigations of migraine across postmenopause. The real-world applications of summarising the migraine trends seen in previous literature is to create evidence-based expectations of migraine changes for women approaching menopause: although some studies see lowered prevalence postmenopause, in the clinical population migraine may persist if not worsen following menopause. Importantly, risk varies by PMS history, type of menopause and use and type of MHT, which can be considered by clinicians in estimating postmenopausal migraine prognosis.

5. Conclusion

This review and meta-analysis is the first of its kind to quantitatively assess migraine prevalence and characteristics in postmenopausal women. The present data suggest that migraine prevalence does not differ significantly but is consistently more severe in postmenopausal women relative to premenopausal women. Whether this is a product of different (clinical, non-clinical) samples used in studies or reflects an overall increase in severity in migraine populations postmenopause is unknown and requires longitudinal investigation. Longitudinal studies should consider comorbidities, menopause associated symptoms and analyse migraine outcomes by time since menopause to investigate the oestrogen withdrawal hypothesis and menopause associated factors in migraine course. Migraine risk is additionally heightened with postmenopausal MHT use, as found in the present meta-analysis. Type and point of initiation of MHT in real-world populations of women, inclusive of those who underwent surgical menopause is relevant to capture. This would allow investigations of; the longitudinal effects of MHT use on migraine, the timing hypothesis of MHT initiation for migraine risk and surgical menopause MHT use.
