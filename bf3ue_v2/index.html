Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Abstract

The way in which induction is applied shapes the field of consciousness science, as scientists draw inferences about experience, generalise neural signatures, and make projections across different contexts. Although deduction establishes ideals of certainty, induction directs experimentation and decision-making. The present article gives a comprehensive examination of the role of induction in consciousness science by looking at its philosophical roots, from Aristotle and Hume through to Popper, Salmon, and Peirce, and by considering its formal expression in Bayesian and predictive-processing frameworks. It then looks at how induction functions in the areas of neuroimaging, comparative cognition, and clinical practice, pointing out the achievements (such as pattern detection, the monitoring of anaesthesia, and the detection of covert awareness) as well as the structural dangers (including the slip between correlation and causation, overgeneralisation, anthropomorphism, and misclassification at high stakes). Recent syntheses that are oriented towards sufficiency (for example, Coppola, 2025;Mashour et al., 2020; Hohwy et al., 2020) show both the strength and the dangers of inductive generalisation by extending correlational results into assertions of ontological sufficiency. Drawing on the latest criticisms and on adversarial comparisons of the major theories, the article advocates for an explicitly deflationary approach which maintains that induction is essential but must be kept within limits. The key tools involved are: (i) operational vocabularies that distinguish between constructs (such as access, arousal, valuation, and metacognition); (ii) clear bridge principles that connect observables to experiences; and (iii) prediction tables that record both positive predictions and potential falsifiers. This practical framework does not resolve Hume's problem; rather, it makes our inductive commitments manageable, testable, and ethically responsible. The study of consciousness should continue to be inductive, but it should not be inflationary. To show the feasibility of this system, the paper includes examples of bridge principles and quantitative criteria for "bounded" inference, such as Bayesian probabilities, model-comparison metrics, and causal diagrams. The ethical and institutional aspects are also broadened to cover AI, animal, and algorithmic induction. These additions connect philosophical modesty with the practical implementation of methodology and with moral accountability.

1. Introduction: Induction as the default logic of practice

Scientific discourse tends to give more weight to deduction, but in reality research is mostly inductive, involving the formulation of generalizations based on empirical cases and the application of observed regularities to new situations (Popper, 2005; Salmon, 2017). In the case of consciousness research, this inductive approach is particularly clear: scientists deduce subjective experience from behavioral and neural data, generalize correlations across different paradigms, and extend their findings from one species to another or between different clinical states (Crick & Koch, 1990; Koch et al., 2016). The present article claims that induction is both essential and epistemically risky in this area: while it allows for empirical generalization it also carries the danger of confusing correlation with necessity (Block, 2019; Seth & Bayne, 2022). Drawing on existing methodological debates concerning inference and the comparison of models (Etz & Vandekerckhove, 2018; Feest, 2022a; 2022b), this article presents a deflationary inductive framework which stresses operational lucidity and sets out explicit limits on generalisation. This system regards tools such as prediction tables and Bayesian comparison metrics (Penny et al., 2010) as heuristics for adjusting the epistemic confidence attached to inductive claims.

2. The philosophical problem of induction

Since Aristotle's time with epagōgē, through Hume's skepticism and up to Popper's falsificationism, philosophers have always regarded induction as both essential and logically weak (Aristotle, 1984; Hume, 2016; Popper, 2005). Yet the history of induction goes further than that of formal logic and includes aspects of probability, methodology, and sociological practice. Early probabilists such as Keynes (2013) and Reichenbach (1938) reinterpreted inductive reasoning within probabilistic terms, whereas Carnap (1950) developed a logic of probability which anticipated Bayesian formulations (see also Etz & Vandekerckhove, 2018). 3. Neuroscience as inductive science 3.1 Imaging and statistical generalization

Studies using neuroimaging and EEG are designed to distinguish between consciousness and unconscious processing by statistically averaging the data across different trials and subjects (Koch et al., 2016; Wu & Morales, 2018). It has been found that the posterior cortical areas, which are referred to as the "hot zone", are consistently linked with conscious contents (Boly et al., 2017). Nevertheless, electrophysiological markers such as the P3b component do exhibit notable exceptions, showing that correlational regularities do not imply necessity (Pitts et al., 2014; Chen et al., 2022). Nowadays, quantitative evaluation makes increasing use of effect-size distributions, meta-analytic reproducibility measures, and Bayesian model comparison (Etz & Vandekerckhove, 2018; Penny et al., 2010). These techniques offer graded metrics of evidence which enable researchers to assess the strength of inductive claims, thus reflecting a more careful and deflationary stance when it comes to inference in the field of consciousness neuroscience (Feest, 2022a; 2022b).

3.2 Comparative inference

Similar to neuroimaging, comparative cognition uses inductive reasoning in different biological settings by extending results obtained from humans and mammals to species that are more distantly related. Scientists conclude that an organism is conscious based on its behavioural flexibility, its ability to solve problems with specific goals, and the presence of similar neural structures (Birch et al., 2020; Feinberg & Mallatt, 2017). Studies of corvids illustrate this method: the problem-solving skills and social cognition displayed by crows and ravens indicate the development of similar kinds of intelligence (Emery & Clayton, 2004). Evolutionary theories suggest that affective consciousness could be present in all vertebrates, because this kind of consciousness is based on homologous subcortical circuits which are responsible for evaluative and affective states (Feinberg & Mallatt, 2017; Godfrey-Smith, 2016). Computational ethology plays a role by providing a formal method for comparing different species, modelling the behavioural hierarchies within Bayesian similarity spaces in such a way as to measure the degree of evidential support without making any assumptions regarding the equivalence of subjective experience (Martínez, 2025; Poth et al., 2025; Etz & Vandekerckhove, 2018). However, these inductive inferences, although plausible, are still open to being refuted: since there are no explicit principles that link neural homology or behavioural analogy to subjective experiences, assertions about animal consciousness have to remain tentative (Feest, 2022a; 2022b; Koch et al., 2016).

3.3 Clinical induction

In cases of disorders of consciousness (DOC), clinicians have in the past used a simple inductive rule stating that if there was no motor report then there must be no awareness (Laureys et al., 2004). This idea was challenged when studies using mental-imagery fMRI showed that patients who showed no behavioural response still exhibited covert command following (Owen et al., 2006). Since then, standardised behavioural scales, for example the Coma Recovery Scale-Revised, have been found to offer greater diagnostic reliability than informal consensus (Schnakers et al., 2009). Inductive EEG markers have also contributed to better monitoring of the depth of anaesthesia and to the prediction of transitions into recovery (Purdon et al., 2013).

Since these inferences are used in clinical classification, their ethical consequences are important: diagnostic errors have both moral and epistemic consequences (Yuste et al., 2017). In this light, there is a clear requirement for explicit bridge principles and for counterfactual modeling systems that test causal hypotheses rather than simply relying on raw correlation (Woodward, 2005; Penny et al., 2010). The use of formal Bayesian model comparison, through Bayes factors or information criteria (Etz & Vandekerckhove, 2018), allows for a systematic ranking of the different neural explanations of consciousness by quantifying the gradients of evidence rather than making categorical claims about sufficiency. Recent integrative analyses have suggested that subcortical sufficiency is responsible for consciousness, on the basis of converging evidence from lesion and stimulation studies (Coppola, 2025). A similar sufficiencyoriented approach is also found in major theoretical models. The Global Neuronal Workspace models (Mashour et al., 2020) and the predictive-processing accounts (Hohwy & Seth, 2020) both describe consciousness in terms of hierarchical integration of evidence, whether this is described as cortical "ignition" or as precision-weighted inference. Each of these frameworks captures important regularities in both clinical and experimental situations, but both are at risk of overreaching when they treat functional or statistical coherence as if it were causal closure. In this context, such models require bounded induction: their explanatory power depends on transparent bridge principles linking brain activity to conscious access and on formally identifying falsifiers through model comparison and counterfactual testing. These suggestions highlight a recurring epistemic risk, namely that when empirical regularities are collected without formal qualification, inductive plausibility can become fixed as ontological necessity (Feest, 2022a; 2022b; Koch et al., 2016). The deflationary inductive framework seeks to prevent this kind of drift by maintaining evidential proportionality and methodological caution.

3.4 Case: cortical necessity vs. subcortical sufficiency

In consciousness research, evidence from lesions, stimulation, and imaging is frequently interpreted inductively in opposite ways: one view stresses the necessity of cortical activity, holding that consciousness depends on cortical activation, while the other suggests subcortical sufficiency on the grounds that purposeful affective behaviours are observed in decorticate states (Merker, 2007; Boly et al., 2017). Unless they clearly state the bridge principles on which they base their interpretations and then subject them to falsification or counterfactual testing, both of these interpretations may go beyond what the evidence actually supports (Feest, 2022a; 2022b; Koch et al., 2016). Modern causal-inference and modelling approaches (Pearl, 2009; Woodward, 2005; Friston, 2010; Penny et al., 2010) provide a means of carrying out such tests by expressing philosophical ideas about necessity and sufficiency in terms of probability and dynamics. With these tools it is possible to carry out simulations and compare quantitative models, turning conceptual differences into analyses that can be tested empirically. The idea of sufficiency also needs to be made clearer: empirical sufficiency means statistical adequacy in terms of prediction, while ontological sufficiency implies a metaphysical adequacy for experience (Achinstein, 2001; Feest, 2022a; 2022b). The first of these supports valid inductive generalisation; the second is a modal overextension. By keeping this distinction, a deflationary inductive programme can be maintained which is empirically rigorous yet philosophically cautious.

4. Induction inside theories of consciousness 4.1 Evolutionary/comparative induction

In theoretical models of consciousness, induction is not limited to empirical data but also involves evolutionary reasoning that connects neural organisation to supposed forms of experience (Birch et al., 2020; Godfrey-Smith, 2016). Approaches that aim to establish affective consciousness in vertebrates generally base their conclusions on the shared subcortical architectures and behavioural repertoires found in different species (Feinberg & Mallatt, 2017). These theories suggest that the conserved neural circuits which play a role in affective regulation might be the basis for similar experiential abilities among various animals, for example pain-like states in fish. Yet the leap from homology to phenomenology is still inductive and not deductive: it assumes that functional or structural continuity means there is also continuity of experience, a assumption which must be carefully examined in order to prevent anthropomorphic projection (Birch et al., 2020; Feest, 2022a; 2022b). Within a deflationary inductive framework, it would be necessary to have explicit bridge principles that specify how neural homology could correspond to phenomenological analogy and also identify possible empirical falsifiers of that correspondence (Koch et al., 2016; Feest, 2022a; 2022b).

4.2 Phenomenology-first programs

In the same way that evolutionary programs arrive at the concept of consciousness by drawing inferences from neural homology, phenomenology-first approaches do so by referencing experiential examples. These programs frequently assert that they are able to deduce universal structures from lived experience. However, in reality they depend on inductively gathering phenomenological cases from pathology, psychiatry, and perception in order to support their claims regarding minimal selfhood and temporal experience (Zahavi, 2025).

In recent years, research in the philosophy of mind and neuroscience has aimed at bringing firstperson phenomenological accounts in line with neural data, with the goal of connecting subjective experience to physiological explanations (Feest, 2022a; 2022b; Martínez, 2025). Although these 'neurophenomenological' approaches strive for integration, they are still based on inductive reasoning: in order to make generalisations from individual cases, it is necessary to have explicit bridge principles that specify how experiential categories such as agency, affect, or the feeling of temporal flow relate to neural measures (Craver, 2007). If those mappings are not established, these programmes could end up replacing metaphysical overreaching with methodological indeterminacy, thus highlighting the importance of adopting a deflationary approach when extending phenomenological inference into empirical settings (Feest, 2022a; 2022b; Koch et al., 2016).

4.3 NCC programs

In the field of theoretical models, the search for the Neural Correlates of Consciousness (NCCs) shows both the strengths and weaknesses of inductive reasoning in the study of consciousness (Koch et al., 2016; Seth & Bayne, 2022). Since Crick and Koch's initial suggestion in 1990, the NCC project has advanced by inferring neural substrates of awareness from observed correlations. Various competing theories highlight different statistical patterns: posterior cortical activity for phenomenal content (Boly et al., 2017), prefrontal activation for global access (Baars, Geld, & Kozma, 2021; Dehaene & Changeux, 2011), recurrent interactions for temporal synthesis, and informational complexity for intrinsic structural unity (Tononi, 2004). As these features often occur together, the same data sets can lead to several incompatible inductive generalisations. None of the current NCC frameworks can assert that a particular feature is necessary or sufficient without having undergone counterfactual validation or having carried out explicit causal tests (Feest, 2022a; 2022b; Koch et al., 2016). In order to reduce inductive inflation, formal prediction tables and falsifiability matrices are needed in order to clearly differentiate between positive predictions and disconfirming results (Michel & Morales, 2020; Seth & Bayne, 2022). These tools transform the NCC endeavour from one of simply accumulating correlations to one based on a deflationary and testable logic of inference.

Although there are legitimate reasons to be cautious about the use of induction, there is no doubt about its necessity for the study of consciousness. Since subjective experience cannot be directly measured, it has to be inferred from behavioural, physiological, and neural regularities (Koch et al., 2016; Seth & Bayne, 2022). Induction therefore provides a link between observable patterns and the unobservable phenomenon of subjective experience, allowing empirical access to subjective phenomena by means of systematic inference (Feest, 2022a; 2022b). The practical advantages of induction are evident in a variety of methodological situations: for example, in detecting patterns in complex or noisy data (Koch et al., 2016), in guiding prediction and control in clinical situations such as those involving EEG trajectories during anaesthesia (Purdon et al., 2013), in making cross-species and developmental inferences wherein verbal reports are not available (Emery & Clayton, 2004; Feinberg & Mallatt, 2017), and in helping to make prognostic and diagnostic judgments in disorders of consciousness (Owen et al., 2006; Schnakers et al., 2009). Induction thus continues to function as a practical tool for the purpose of finding and prediction. Its worth is not in proving metaphysical necessity yet in securing empirical adequacy-this being precisely the methodological humility at the heart of a deflationary inductive approach (Feest, 2022a; 2022b; Koch et al., 2016).

5.1 Operationalizing Inductive Discipline

The deflationary inductive programme can be put into practice using explicit and testable tools such as prediction tables and bridge matrices which link observed neural and behavioural variables to phenomenological constructs while assigning evidential weights (Feest, 2022a; 2022b; Craver, 2007). In a Bayesian framework, each link in a bridge principle can have a prior level of credibility determined by empirical solidity and then be revised in light of likelihood ratios derived from new evidence (Friston, 2010; Penny et al., 2010; Etz & Vandekerckhove, 2018).

In consciousness research, such formalization can take various illustrative forms:  For example, assign Bayesian prior probabilities to the candidate neural markers (such as setting the prior probability that the P3b indicates awareness at 0.7) and then update them using the likelihood ratios derived from the new data-an method which focuses on transparent uncertainty assessment rather than on categorical interpretation (Etz & Vandekerckhove, 2018).

 As an example, ROC analysis should be used to estimate the discriminative accuracy of the proposed NCCs, measuring how reliably neural signatures such as the P3b or the posterior 'hot zone' can predict reportable awareness (Pitts et al., 2014).

 As an example, when assessing competing explanatory hypotheses-such as the posterior cortical and fronto-parietal workspace accounts-model comparison metrics (such as AIC/BIC or Bayes factors) should be used to determine which one has greater evidential support (Block, 2019; Penny et al., 2010).

These quantitative heuristics convert the philosophical principles of inductive discipline into practical empirical applications, making the reasoning process transparent, capable of being falsified, and reproducible. This illustrates a persistent risk in reasoning: when statistically established regularities are taken to be causal or sufficient, empirical inference tends to move towards metaphysical claims (Feest, 2022a; 2022b; Woodward, 2005). Recent syntheses that focus on sufficiency (Coppola, 2025) show how findings that converge across cortical, subcortical, and cross-species studies can seem to imply necessity even in the absence of direct tests of causal relevance. Comparable inferential difficulties also arise in major theoretical approaches.

In the Global Neuronal Workspace theory (Mashour et al., 2020), cortical 'ignition' is often presented as a sign of conscious access, while predictive-processing accounts (Hohwy & Seth, 2020) see hierarchical precision-weighting as a means of carrying out phenomenological updating. Although both models present plausible causal structures, each can shift from being based on evidence to asserting ontological sufficiency when their formal coherence is regarded as proof of necessity. Within a deflationary inductive framework, such theoretical inferences must remain explicitly provisional-limited by bridge principles that specify how neural integration or predictive optimisation translate into attributions of experience, and open to being refuted by adversarial model testing. There is a danger in this line of reasoning in that it could replace explanatory adequacy with predictive accuracy -just as happens in the case of overfitting, where generalization goes beyond what the evidence allows (Etz & Vandekerckhove, 2018; Penny et al., 2010). In a deflationary inductive approach, this kind of risk can be reduced by making explicit the bridge principles that define the intended causal link -whether that is statistical dependence, mechanistic participation, or phenomenological co-variance (Craver, 2007; Woodward, 2005). Moreover, by systematically publishing prediction tables which include counterfactual falsifiers (for example, lesion, stimulation, or pharmacological dissociation studies) the inductive reasoning remains firmly grounded in empirical evidence and is also methodologically capable of self-correction (Koch et al., 2016; Feest, 2022a; 2022b).

6.2 Overgeneralization

In consciousness science, inductive generalisations often go beyond the evidence available (Feest, 2022a; 2022b). On the basis of limited data from lesions and stimulation, researchers tend to conclude that a cortical area is necessary or that the subcortex is sufficient for conscious experience (Merker, 2007; Boly et al., 2017). These types of extrapolations illustrate the traditional issue of overgeneralisation; that is, treating empirical plausibility as if it were an ontological claim (Koch et al., 2016; Feest, 2022a Feest, , 2022b)). What starts as a restricted correlation may be taken for a universal principle, particularly when statistical recurrence is regarded as theoretical closure (Etz & Vandekerckhove, 2018). Although findings from decorticate patients or subcortical activations during anaesthesia do show a correlation and functional continuity, they do not prove metaphysical sufficiency (Laureys et al., 2004; Purdon et al., 2013; Koch et al., 2016). On the other hand, cortical lesion data indicating reduced responsiveness are sufficient to support claims of functional necessity, not of phenomenal exclusivity (Boly et al., 2017; Giacino & Smart, 2007). Both approaches risk overreaching their inductive justification by failing to account for the bridging assumptions that connect brain activity to experiential attribution (Craver, 2007; Woodward, 2005; Feest, 2022a; 2022b). A deflationary inductive framework reduces this risk by asking researchers, in every inference, to specify three parameters of generalization:  What is the scope?

That is, which organisms, states, and modalities are covered?

 What is the causal or functional structure that underlies the generalization?

 What empirical results would lead to its retraction?

By including these parameters in the prediction tables (Penny et al., 2010), inductive reasoning would remain empirically rigorous while preventing it from being inflated into metaphysical necessity.

6.3 Anthropomorphism

In the field of comparative cognition, inductive projection typically takes the form of anthropomorphism, which involves inferring human-like consciousness from the flexible or intelligent behaviour of non-human species (Birch et al., 2020; Godfrey-Smith, 2016). Cases like corvids solving problems, dolphins showing self-recognition, and cephalopods engaging in social planning lead people to draw comparisons with human mental abilities (Emery & Clayton, 2004; Godfrey-Smith, 2016). Although these behaviours indicate a high level of cognition, they do not on their own imply any phenomenological similarity. The leap from functional similarity to experiential resemblance relies on unexamined bridge principles that claim mechanistic homology or behavioural convergence implies experiential homology-a kind of conceptual shift that has not been supported by empirical evidence (Birch et al., 2020; Feest, 2022a; 2022b). The evolutionary model proposed by Feinberg and Mallatt (2017) shows this double-edged reasoning: their careful inference based on conserved subcortical architectures allows for a reasonable generalisation regarding affective consciousness among vertebrates, but when applied to shared phenomenality it may go beyond what the evidence justifies. This excessive reach is similar to the claims about cortical or subcortical sufficiency in human neuroscience (Koch et al., 2016; Feest, 2022a; 2022b), showing a common tendency to move from empirical regularities to ontological conclusions. In order to prevent this kind of drift, a deflationary inductive framework demands that three parameters be explicitly stated when making inferences across species:  What aspect of consciousness is in question here (for example, affect, perception, or metacognition)?

 What empirical relationship justifies mapping functional analogies onto phenomenological claims?

 What counterexamples-for instance, those showing a discrepancy between neural homology and behavioral flexibility-could be used to refute that inference?

By including these criteria in the prediction tables (Penny et al., 2010; Feest, 2022a; 2022b) the heuristic value of the comparative data is maintained while avoiding the situation in which similarity turns into assumed sameness. It is true that consciousness might be multi-realizable, but its recognition has to be carried out with methodological restraint rather than through empathetic projection.

6.4 Clinical misclassification

In the field of clinical neuroscience, the most serious inductive mistakes occur when clinicians incorrectly classify consciousness on the basis of behaviour. For many years, the lack of a motor report was considered to be enough proof of unconsciousness -a practical yet fragile inductive rule (Laureys et al., 2004; Koch et al., 2016). This belief was challenged when studies using neuroimaging and EEG techniques showed that patients who had been diagnosed as being in a vegetative state could in fact carry out covert commands (Owen et al., 2006; Schnakers et al., 2009). What had previously seemed to be a stable empirical generalisation -"no behaviour, no awareness" -turned out to be an overfitted inductive heuristic (Feest, 2022a; 2022b; Etz & Vandekerckhove, 2018). These results show that the inference fails not because it is irrational but because there is an insufficiently specified bridge principle connecting motor output to inner awareness (Woodward, 2005; Craver, 2007). The predictive model had no counterfactual falsifiers, that is, no conditions under which awareness could continue even in the absence of observable behaviour. A deflationary inductive framework would overcome this by requiring explicit scope conditions (i.e. specifying which patients, states, and modalities the inference applies to) and counterfactual disconfirmers (for example, covert imagery tasks or evoked potential tests) for each diagnostic inference (Koch et al., 2016; Penny et al., 2010). Including these in standardised prediction tables would make clinical inference more transparent and replicable while helping to prevent misclassification of patients in a categorical way. The ethical consequences are important: in cases involving disorders of consciousness, inductive errors have both moral and epistemic implications, influencing decisions about treatment, rehabilitation and life support (Yuste et al., 2017). Inductive discipline therefore takes on the role of a form of clinical care -ensuring that epistemic humility informs practice in situations where error is existential (Feest, 2022a; 2022b).

6.5 Epistemic opacity and algorithmic induction

A further structural risk is the increasing use of algorithmic induction in both neuroscience and clinical diagnosis. Nowadays, machine-learning models are able to identify putative "neural signatures of consciousness" and to classify different brain states from high-dimensional EEG or fMRI data-yet frequently lack any clear causal or conceptual basis (Burrell, 2016; Creel, 2020; Samek et al., 2017). Such systems show what Burrell (2016) refers to as epistemic opacity: the pathways by which they make their inferences may be impossible for even the developers to understand, which in turn makes it hard to validate or falsify them. Algorithmic induction therefore inherits and, in many cases, worsens the same weaknesses as human inductive reasoning (Feest, 2022a; 2022b). The correlation-causation slip occurs again when black-box models attain high predictive accuracy while hiding any mechanistic explanation (Samek et al., 2017; Creel, 2020). Overgeneralisation happens when models that have been trained on limited datasets are used across species, different modalities, or various clinical groups without first being revalidated (Topol, 2019). Anthropomorphic projection also appears when people take the representational outputs of machines to be signs of cognitive or phenomenological processes (Metzinger, 2021). These epistemic failures have ethical implications: the opacity of algorithms used in anaesthesia monitoring or in the diagnosis of disorders of consciousness can result in misclassifications with serious moral and clinical consequences (Yuste et al., 2017; Topol, 2019). Therefore, a deflationary inductive framework must also apply to machine inference, requiring interpretable architectures, explicit statements of prior assumptions, and auditable prediction tables that make algorithms' inductive claims empirically testable and falsifiable (Samek et al., 2017; Feest, 2022a; 2022b).

6.6 Ethical dimensions of inductive inference

The moral significance of induction in the study of consciousness goes beyond mere attention to epistemic rigor; for when inductive inferences are not carefully examined, they lead to errors which have effects on humans, animals, and society. It is for this reason that a disciplined approach to induction is both a methodological and a moral requirement-because it determines how recognition, rights, and care are allocated in situations of uncertainty (Feest, 2022a; 2022b; Yuste et al., 2017).

 Clinical ethics: in cases of disorders of consciousness, making an inductive misclassification has a direct impact on questions of life, death, and dignity. The longstanding assumption that the lack of motor response means there is no consciousnessalthough once a helpful rule of thumb-has now been refuted by empirical research (Owen et al., 2006; Schnakers et al., 2009). Nevertheless, the moral consequences of this assumption remain, affecting choices regarding care, rehabilitation, and the status of a person as a person (Laureys et al., 2004; Giacino & Smart, 2007). In this context, inductive humility should be regarded as an ethical principle: doctors must treat any diagnostic inference as temporary, open to being proven wrong, and willing to adjust it in the light of counterevidence (Yuste et al., 2017; Feest, 2022a; 2022b).

 Ethics from a comparative and regulatory point of view: inflationary inductive arguments about animal or subcortical consciousness have important consequences for welfare policy. Examples of programmes which conclude that affective consciousness exists on the basis of neural homology or behavioural flexibility (Feinberg & Mallatt, 2017; Birch et al., 2020; Godfrey-Smith, 2016) show the conflict between moral caution and evidential rigor. It is possible to give ethical protections a prudent justification-on the grounds of uncertainty and the possibility of harm-without having to assume that there is phenomenological sameness. Hence, inductive discipline promotes both compassion and epistemic caution (Feest, 2022a; 2022b).

 The idea of artificial intelligence and neuro-rights is that anthropomorphic projection reappears in new forms in AI and neurotechnology. Adaptive or conversational machinelearning systems are frequently described in mentalistic language-that is, by going from pattern recognition to claiming sentience (Metzinger, 2021; Gabriel, 2020). When it comes to ethical responsibility, there must be care regarding ontology: it is necessary to differentiate between computational inference and subjective self-modeling. A similar caution is needed in the case of brain-computer interfaces, since drawing too hasty a conclusion from neural decoding to personhood could lead to distortion of policy. Frameworks for neuro-rights (Yuste et al., 2017) exemplify bounded induction: they aim to safeguard mental privacy and autonomy without confusing a neural signal with experience itself.

In all clinical, comparative, and artificial situations, a deflationary inductive framework thus serves as a moral and methodological guide. It achieves this by incorporating clear bridge principles, transparent prediction tables, and falsifiable hypotheses into the chains of inference, thereby establishing epistemic humility in which inferential errors have ethical implications (Feest, 2022a; 2022b; Penny et al., 2010). Induction must therefore be both empirically limited and morally reflective: a science of consciousness that bases its integrity on its own recognition of limitations (Feest, 2022a; 2022b; Koch et al., 2016).

6.7 Social and Institutional Amplifiers

Induction in science is never entirely a matter of individuals alone; it is organised in a social way and supported by institutions (Feest, 2022a; 2022b; Chambers, 2019). The reward systems which determine scientific value, the incentives encouraging novelty, the citation hierarchies, and the practice of selective publication all tend to favour bold generalisations over careful inference (Fanelli, 2010). Correlations which might still be regarded as tentative if epistemic humility were taken into account are often inflated into assertions of necessity because of the pressures related to attention and prestige that characterise the publication culture. The kind of inferential overreach that is apparent is therefore usually just an institutional feature (Feest, 2022a; 2022b). The replication problems in psychology and neuroscience (Nosek et al., 2015) show that the weakness of inductive generalization usually stems from social factors rather than merely from methodological defects. If replication, falsification, and transparency are given less importance than innovation and narrative coherence, inductive conclusions tend to become accepted without being examined (Chambers, 2019; Feest, 2022a; 2022b). The issue is not generalization per se but the social imbalance between the incentives for making discoveries and those for correcting them; an imbalance which encourages overreaching while failing to provide sufficient funding for self-criticism (Fanelli, 2010).

A pragmatic inductivism that is to be sustainable therefore has to serve as both an epistemic virtue and an institutional design principle. Procedures such as pre-registration, open data requirements, and adversarial collaboration (Chambers, 2019; Nosek et al., 2015) turn humility into a feature of collective practice. Meta-scientific innovations, registered replication reports and Bayesian meta-analysis (Etz & Vandekerckhove, 2018) extend the deflationary attitude of bounded induction from individual reasoning to the regulation of scientific practice (Feest, 2022a; 2022b). In this respect, bounded induction functions as a social technology; it is a decentralized system of epistemic safeguards which prevents overgeneralization, reduces confirmation cascades, and brings modesty in line with integrity. The logic of induction, after having been incorporated into methodological frameworks, has now to be established within the natural environment of science.

7. Bayesian and Probabilistic Approaches: Induction Formalized

The Bayesian and predictive-processing approaches treat induction as probabilistic updating, a dynamic process in which hypotheses are improved by reducing prediction error (Clark, 2015; Hohwy, 2013). Perception, action, and learning are all brought together within a hierarchical system of belief revision: the brain functions as a kind of prediction machine that works out the causes of its sensory inputs. The Free Energy Principle (Friston, 2010) takes this line of reasoning and turns it into a general theory of adaptive systems, stating that all self-organizing agents act in order to minimize surprise. However, although this broad scope is attractive in its simplicity, it calls for epistemic caution since it may obscure the inductive assumptions that are built into its own formalism (Gershman, 2019; Feest, 2022a; 2022b). Bayesian inference might seem to bring induction under control: priors limit the extent of generalization, posteriors measure belief revision, and precision-weighting adjusts the level of confidence (Etz & Vandekerckhove, 2018). Nevertheless, as Parvizi-Wayne (2025) points out, mathematical tractability does not mean that a theory is explanatorily adequate. In the field of consciousness research, predictive and free-energy frameworks have a tendency to treat inductive assumptions as if they were actual empirical discoveries, confusing a high degree of model fit with real causal or phenomenological insight (Koch et al., 2016; Feest, 2022a Feest, , 2022b)). The recent reinterpretation of predictive processing as a model of adaptive control rather than as a complete explanation (Poth et al., 2025) is an example of a careful and deflationary stance: by focusing on embodiment, ecological constraints, and bounded rationality, it maintains formal induction within the bounds of empirical and conceptual limitations. A similar development occurs in the area of causal discovery. Directed acyclic graphs (DAGs) and causal Bayesian networks (Pearl, 2009) express inductive structure by connecting statistical dependencies to possible interventions. When bridge principles are included as constraints in such graphs, inductive relationships become falsifiable; that is, correlations can be tested against counterfactual simulations or causal experiments (Woodward, 2005; Penny et al., 2010). The combination of probabilistic and causal modelling reflects the practical spirit of bounded induction: it involves explicit priors, clear bridges, and testable falsifiers (Feest, 2022a; 2022b). Therefore, Bayesian frameworks do not resolve Hume's problem of induction-they instead operationalize it. Their philosophical strength does not lie in logical certainty but in computational accountability: they make inductive commitments explicit, quantifiable, and open to correction (Carnap, 1950; Keynes, 2013; Etz & Vandekerckhove, 2018). In consciousness science, where inferences can affect theoretical validity and moral responsibility, this formalization acts as a form of ethical discipline (Feest, 2022a; 2022b; Yuste et al., 2017).

8. Critiques and cross-checks

The most sharp criticisms of contemporary consciousness science all lead to the same conclusion: there is inductive inflation caused by a lack of empirical and conceptual constraints (Block, 2019; Seth & Bayne, 2022; Feest, 2022a; 2022b). Block (2019) cautions that neural markers which are correlational in nature, for example P3b activity or posterior "hot-zone" activations, are too easily treated as necessary or sufficient conditions for consciousness, particularly in "no-report" paradigms in which behavioural verification is separated from subjective inference (Duman et al., 2022). Seth and Bayne (2022) go on to develop this criticism by pointing out that major theories, such as the global workspace, recurrent processing, and integrated information, tend to gather data that is compatible with them without making any predictions that can be used to discriminate between the models or that are capable of being falsified. The resulting diversity of views does not stem from an abundance of evidence but rather from a lack of constraint: different models are able to fit the same data because their inductive commitments are not clearly defined (Michel & Morales, 2020). Philosophers who have considered scientific explanation have for a long time warned against such uncontrolled generalisation. Mechanistic approaches call for the explicit identification of the components and the way in which they are related (Craver, 2007), while interventionist accounts require that causal claims be testable in a counterfactual sense (Woodward, 2005). When these standards are applied to research into consciousness, they demand the introduction of bridge principles that connect neural observables to experiential constructs and prediction tables which list both the results that confirm and those that disconfirm the theories (Feest, 2022a; 2022b; Koch et al., 2016). The latest suggestions for methodological reform show this shift towards disciplined induction. Michel and Morales (2020) propose a criterion of theoretical discriminability, which means that competing theories must produce incompatible and testable predictions, not merely gather empirical evidence. The present framework goes further than these reformist ideas by incorporating three methodological elements: operational vocabularies (in order to make the theoretical constructs such as access, arousal, and valuation clearer), bridge principles (to define the mappings from observables to supposed experiences), and prediction tables (to spot falsifiers and counterfactuals). In this way, criticism is turned into method. In both philosophy and neuroscience, a single methodological requirement arises: induction should be kept as being empirically bounded, conceptually transparent, and causally testable (Feest, 2022a; 2022b; Koch et al., 2016).

9. Conclusion: Induction, Deflated but Indispensable

Syntheses based on empirical evidence that are intended to find the minimal sufficient conditions for consciousness (Coppola, 2025) are useful for integrating different views but show the main epistemic risk that this paper has identified: the gradual shift of inductive plausibility into categorical sufficiency. This epistemic dilemma also applies to the theoretical frameworks that form much of current consciousness research. Global Neuronal Workspace theory (Mashour et al., 2020) and predictive-processing frameworks (Hohwy & Seth, 2020) each convert inductive regularities into formal causal schemes-the first by means of the idea of cortical ignition and the second through hierarchical prediction error minimization. Although both have contributed to empirical unification, they both run the risk of treating inductive coherence as if it were ontological sufficiency. A deflationary inductivism regards these models not as opposing metaphysical programmes but as disciplined tools within an evidential economy: their claims stay local, their bridge principles are made explicit, and their potential falsifiers are listed. Within this limited framework, integration becomes a methodological rather than a metaphysical issue, allowing for pluralism without leading to overreach. Induction's strength is in its scope, but its danger is in taking that scope to mean necessity. Consciousness science, more than any other field, relies on inference while at the same time risking overreach (Seth & Bayne, 2022). A pragmatic philosophy of science must therefore recognize the indispensable role of induction while at the same time restraining its tendency to overextend (Feest, 2022a; 2022b; Brigandt, 2010). As Martínez (2025) points out, cognitive representation itself involves a balance between informational rate, distortion, and complexity; a balance that is also present in inductive reasoning. The empirical richness must be balanced against evidential distortion and conceptual excess: over-compression (drawing universal conclusions from limited data) simplifies at the expense of truth, while over-complexity diminishes explanatory clarity. The task, then, is not to go beyond induction, but to bring discipline to its economy. This deflationary inductivism is in line with pluralist and pragmatic philosophies of science which prefer local, model-relative generalizations to universal unification (Feest, 2022a; 2022b; Brigandt, 2010; Colombo & Wright, 2017). On this view, progress in consciousness science does not come from discovering a final, sufficient substrate, but from constructing bounded bridges between evidence and experience-bridges that remain transparent, open to revision, and ethically reflective (Koch et al., 2016; Feest, 2022a; 2022b). The framework set out here, consisting of operational vocabularies, bridge principles, and prediction tables, offers a method for keeping induction testable, modular, and morally accountable (Feest, 2022a; 2022b; Penny et al., 2010). It turns criticism into practice by making inductive commitments clear, the inferential boundaries obvious, and the predictions falsifiable (Woodward, 2005; Feest, 2022a; 2022b). Consciousness science cannot avoid using induction; it can only decide how consciously it practices it. A modest, transparent, and testable inductivism-one that places local adequacy above metaphysical aspirations-offers the most responsible way forward in a field where epistemic error and ethical consequences are inseparable (Feest, 2022a; 2022b; Yuste et al., 2017).

Limitations and prospects.

Although the framework presented here makes clear the epistemic structure of induction in the study of consciousness, it does not address its formal integration with computational and mechanistic models. Future research should turn the prediction tables into explicit priors in the context of Bayesian model comparison, thus turning conceptual transparency into quantitative accountability (Etz & Vandekerckhove, 2018; Penny et al., 2010). In the case of cognitive architectures and neural simulations, bridge principles could be implemented as representational constraints that serve to guide both the construction and the interpretation of the models (Martínez, 2025; Craver, 2007). Further theoretical work is required in order to clarify how inductive and abductive reasoning interact during the process of theory formation. Even though this paper has stressed the logic of generalization, hypothesis generation (abduction) and deductive testing still remain essential components of the inferential cycle (Peirce, 1992a; 1992b; Woodward, 2005). If we can map out this interaction, it might show new ways of formalizing how theories of consciousness develop from initial exploratory conjectures to empirically limited generalisations (Feest, 2022a; 2022b). Consciousness science cannot escape the need for induction, but it can discipline it. The proposal put forward is practical rather than revolutionary in that it seeks to keep induction modest, open and capable of being tested by:  precisely defining theoretical constructs (e.g., access, arousal, valuation, metacognition, self-model);  making bridge principles explicit, linking observables to experiential claims; and  Producing prediction tables which record both cases that confirm and those that falsify the predictions, in connection with counterfactual interventions such as lesion, stimulation, or pharmacological manipulation (Koch et al., 2016; Penny et al., 2010; Woodward, 2005).

A science that is inductive yet open to falsification, pluralistic yet disciplined, and based on empirical evidence yet carefully attentive to ethical considerations is the most reasonable way to proceed in a field in which errors in inference have both conceptual and moral significance (Feest, 2022a; 2022b; Yuste et al., 2017). When it comes to the study of consciousness, humility does not mean retreating from knowledge but rather consists in methodological precision-that is, the discipline which makes genuine understanding possible.
