Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. Abstract

Political polarization along gender lines is well documented among adults, but it remains unclear how early the gender gap is evident and whether it extends beyond broad ideological identification to specific policy issues. We examine gender differences in policy attitudes among Dutch adolescents aged 12-18 in a sample broadly representative of the population in terms of gender and age (N = 1,697; 52% girls; 42% lower secondary school).

We assess gender differences across 15 policy issues, within-gender polarization, and ideological profiles identified through latent class analysis.

We find a systematic gender gap, with boys consistently expressing more conservative views than girls. Importantly, the magnitude of this gap varies across policy issues and is strongly associated with whether an issue is gender-related (r pb = .66).

Gender differences also extend beyond mean attitudes: boys show greater within-group polarization on gender-related issues than girls. Latent class analyses further identify distinct ideological profiles, with girls more likely to be classified as progressive (54% vs. 16%) and boys as conservative (37% vs. 10%). Together, these findings show that gender differences in policy attitudes are already evident in adolescence, highlighting adolescence as an important stage for research on interventions addressing gender-based political polarization.

Introduction

The political views of young people have increasingly attracted attention. Recent media coverage points to a rapidly growing "global gender divide" among younger generations (Burn-Murdoch, 2024), sparking considerable interest in both public and academic debates. Although a large body of research has documented persistent gender differences in policy attitudes among adults (e.g., Clark, 2017; Gethin et al., 2022), it remains unclear whether such differences are already present in adolescence.

Research on adolescents has primarily examined gender differences in other facets of political life (e.g., Milosav et al., 2026; Rekker, 2025), with limited attention to policy attitudes. This is a notable gap, as policy attitudes -defined as individual evaluations or preferences of specific policies or policy proposals (Staerklé, 2009) -are key predictors of later voting behavior (e.g., Flavin & Law, 2022), and therefore central to understanding the early structuring of political preferences.

Adolescence is a critical period for self-and identity development (Crone & Fuligni, 2020; Sears & Brown, 2023, e.g., ), during which policy attitudes may become increasingly structured and differentiated. Longitudinal research on other domains of political attitudes indicates increasing stability throughout secondary education (e.g., Abdelzadeh & Lundberg, 2017; Van de Werfhorst et al., 2025). Yet evidence on gender differences in adolescents' policy attitudes remains scarce, leaving open whether such differences are already present at this stage of development.

The present study examines the gender gap in policy attitudes among adolescents aged 12-18. We focus on the magnitude and scope of gender differences across policy domains, as well as underlying patterns of ideological organization.

The gender gap

The gender gap has been extensively studied across political and sociocultural domains. Large-scale analyses of electoral behavior in Western democracies between 1948 and 2020 show a marked reversal since the mid-20th century: whereas women were generally more conservative than men up until the 1950s, they have since become increasingly likely to support left-wing parties (Gethin et al., 2022). Evidence from the United States between 1972 and 2012 similarly indicates persistent gender differences in sociocultural attitudes over time, alongside a gradual leftward shift among both men and women (Clark, 2017). Comparable patterns are observed across 14 Western European democracies between 2017 and 2020, where younger cohorts tend to be more progressive than older generations across multiple policy domains (Langsæther & Knutsen, 2025).

Notably, this generational shift is more pronounced among women, resulting in a larger gender gap among younger cohorts. Recent evidence similarly suggests that contemporary gender gaps may be particularly pronounced among younger generations. Using a large-scale age-period-cohort design, Milosav et al. (2026) found that the gender gap in support for the far right was particularly pronounced among Millennials and Generation Z.

Beyond these aggregate trends, research documents consistent gender differences across specific policy domains, including environmental attitudes (e.g., Bush & Clayton, 2023; Gyurián Nagy, 2025), gender egalitarianism (e.g., Off et al., 2022; Qing, 2020), lifestyle practices such as vegan or vegetarian diets (Modlinska et al., 2020), and support for populist radical right parties (e.g., Donovan, 2023; Milosav et al., 2026). Across these domains, men tend to express more conservative positions than women, although the magnitude of these differences varies. Importantly, prior work also highlights systematic such as climate change, conflict, migration, social polarization, and the long-term consequences of the COVID-19 pandemic, which may shape the salience and content of policy attitudes. This developmental stage also increasingly unfolds in digital environments. As parental influence declines and peer networks gain importance, social media platforms have become central arenas for exposure to political and cultural content (Crone & Konijn, 2018). These online environments include politically charged communities that explicitly engage with gender roles and identities, such as the manosphere (e.g., Botto & Gottzén, 2024) and feminist spaces (e.g., Vachhani, 2024).

Together, these developmental and contextual factors make today's adolescents a particularly relevant group for studying gender differences in policy attitudes.

Current study

The present study examines the gender gap in policy attitudes among Dutch secondary school students aged 12 to 18. We focus on the magnitude and scope of gender differences across different issues. To move beyond mean differences, we additionally examine within-gender variation in attitudes and latent class patterns of ideological organization. This allows us to assess whether gender differences reflect isolated issue-specific gaps or more general differences in the structure and clustering of policy attitudes.

Our findings show that the gender gap is pervasive across policy issues, with the largest differences observed on gender-related topics, where boys consistently report more conservative policy attitudes than girls. Beyond mean differences, we also find systematic differences in within-gender heterogeneity, with boys showing greater dispersion in attitudes. Finally, the gender gap is reflected in distinct ideological profiles, with girls more likely to belong to a progressive profile and boys more likely to belong to a conservative profile

Method Dataset and respondents

Respondents were recruited from eight educational institutions across the Netherlands. The dataset comprised 1,928 Dutch adolescents aged approximately 12 to 18 years. Respondents who failed the attention check half way through the questionnaire were excluded from the analyses (n = 114, 5.9%). Furthermore, analyses were restricted to adolescents identifying as either boys or girls, because non-binary (n = 9) and other gender categories (n = 69) were insufficiently represented. Within the latter category, the majority of responses (at least 60) appeared non-substantive, and an additional 39 respondents selected "prefer not to say," resulting in the exclusion of an additional 117 respondents (6.4%). The final analytic sample consisted of 1,697 adolescents, of whom 47.6% (n = 808) identified as boys and 52.4% (n = 889) as girls.

Most respondents in the analyses sample were enrolled in secondary education (n = 1,629, 96.0%), including 711 students (43.7%) in lower secondary education (years 1-3) and 917 students (56.3%) in upper secondary education (years 4-6). A small proportion attended the first two years of vocational education (n = 68, 4.0%). Among students in secondary education, 165 (10.1%) followed the pre-vocational track, 700 (43.0%) the general secondary track, and 763 (46.9%) the pre-university track.

Regarding religion, the majority reported no religious affiliation (n = 1,248, 73.5%).

Among those with a religious affiliation (n = 449, 26.5%), 270 identified as Christian (16.1%), 100 as Muslim (5.9%), and 79 as belonging to another religion (4.7%). Most respondents reported no migration background (n = 1,278, 75.4%), whereas 418 (24.6%) indicated a first-or second-generation migration background. Procedure

Main study

The final questionnaire was developed through a multi-stage process. First, three focus group sessions were conducted in autumn 2023: two with students (n = 28) and one with teachers (n = 5). These qualitative insights informed the initial design of the instrument. Second, the questionnaire was refined using pilot data collected in winter 2023/2024 (n = 402). Data for the main study were collected between September 2024 and January 2025 (n = 1,928).

Prior to data collection, all students and their parents or guardians were informed about the study procedures and participants' rights. The study employed an opt-out design with age-differentiated procedures. Parents or guardians of all students received information about the study. For students aged 12 to 15 years, parents or guardians could opt their child out of participation, whereas students aged 16 years and older could independently opt out.

The questionnaire was administered during regular class time using digital devices, either school-provided or personal depending on school arrangements. Completion took approximately 10-15 minutes. Students who were opted out were assigned an alternative activity on the same device during data collection. Only class mentors were aware of opt-out status; this information was not accessible to teachers or peers.

Before starting the questionnaire, participants were presented with study information and asked to indicate whether they wished to proceed. Students who did not proceed were automatically redirected to the end of the survey.

Measures

Policy attitudes

This study assessed adolescents' policy attitudes across 15 issues, comprising nine general issues and six youth-specific issues. The issues covered a range of sociopolitical domains, including environmental, economic, cultural, and institutional topics. An overview of all items is provided in Table A2 (Appendix A).

Analytic approach

Between-group differences: the gender gap in policy attitudes

Gender differences in adolescents' policy attitudes were examined using Welch's t-tests in R (version 4.3.2). Welch's t-test was chosen because it does not assume equal variances and performs well under unequal group sizes, making it appropriate for the present data structure (Welch, 1947). The sample showed minor imbalances in group sizes, and variance heterogeneity across several items. Although some items exhibited skewed distributions, Welch's t-test is robust to moderate deviations from normality in large samples.

To ensure robustness, we conducted robustness checks including Bayesian independent-samples t-tests (as an alternative inferential framework providing evidence via Bayes factors), Wilcoxon rank-sum tests (to evaluate rank-based differences), and z-tests for differences in proportions (to assess categorical outcomes). Across all approaches, the results were consistent. Results of the robustness checks are presented in Table A3 (Appendix A).

Domain-specific gender differences: the magnitude of the gender gap

To assess whether gender gaps were systematically larger for gender-themed issues, we computed a point-biserial correlation between the magnitude of the gender difference (Cohen's d) for each issue and a binary indicator denoting whether the issue was gender-themed (1) or not (0). Gender-themed issues included gender beliefs, gender roles, gender-neutral toilets, and gender and sexual diversity.

Within-group differences: polarization within gender groups

Within-group variability was examined using the ReMoDe function in R (Hoffstadt et al., 2026), which identifies local maxima (modes) in ordinal distributions. Fisher's exact tests and binomial tests assess statistical significance, while resampling evaluates the stability of modes. This approach allows for the quantification of both the number and location of meaningful peaks, providing a robust measure of within-group polarization.

To statistically complement the ReMoDe classifications, chi-square tests of independence compared response distributions between boys and girls. Cramer's V was reported as a measure of association strength, with > 0.05 considered weak, > 0.10 moderate, > 0.15 strong, and > 0.25 very strong (Akoglu, 2018).

Ideological profiles: latent class analysis (LCA)

Latent class analysis (LCA) was used to identify unobserved subgroups based on the 15 attitudinal indicators, treated as categorical in their recoded form. Models were estimated in R (version 4.3.2) using the poLCA package (Linzer & Lewis, 2011).

Initial models without covariates determined the optimal number of classes. Gender was then included as a covariate using a one-step approach, allowing class membership probabilities to vary by gender. Models with two to five classes were estimated, each with 10 random starting values (nrep = 10) and a maximum of 1,000 iterations to reduce local maxima. Model selection considered AIC, BIC, and log-likelihood values (Burnham & Anderson, 2004), as well as class sizes and substantive interpretability.

Missing data were handled via casewise deletion. Posterior probabilities were examined to evaluate classification certainty. Associations between gender and class membership were tested using chi-square tests of independence, both for the overall table and separately for each class.

Results

A systematic gender gap in policy attitudes

Welch's t-tests revealed statistically significant gender differences in policy attitudes on 12 of 15 issues (Table 1). Figure 1 shows the gender gap in policy attitudes in deviations from the pooled mean for boys and girls. The corresponding average scores on the issue statements are displayed in Figure A2 (Appendix A). Overall, boys reported more conservative policy attitudes than girls, except for smartphone policy, where girls scored slightly more conservative 1 . No statistically significant gender differences were observed for voting age, Israel-Palestine, or online gossip accounts. Effect sizes for significant differences ranged from d = 0.18 to 1.49 ( d = 0.47), indicating medium to very large effects according to Funder and Ozer (2019).

To benchmark these results, we compared our Cohen's d values to a meta-analytic synthesis of gender differences in social attitudes (Zell et al., 2015). This study used a metasynthesis of 106 meta-analyses and 386 individual meta-analytic effects to re-evaluate psychological differences between males and females. In total, 9 of the 12 statistically significant gender gaps in our sample exceeded the average meta-analytic effect size ( d ≈ 0.21), with several differences more than twice as large as this benchmark, suggesting that the gender gaps we observe in these policy attitudes are comparatively large relative to typical gender differences in social attitudes.

Results were robust across alternative analyses, including Bayesian independent-samples t-tests, Wilcoxon rank-sum tests, and z-tests on proportions.

Domain-specific variation in the gender gap

Gender differences in policy attitudes were not uniform across issues, with the largest gaps observed for the gender-themed issues. Effect sizes for these issues were very large ( d = 0.78) (Funder & Ozer, 2019), with boys consistently reporting more conservative policy attitudes than girls (see Figure 1). Boys showed stronger agreement with traditional gender roles, more fixed gender beliefs, and lower support for gender diversity initiatives and gender-neutral toilets compared to girls (for detailed statistics, see Table 1). The magnitude of the gender gap was positively associated with whether an issue was gender-themed, with larger gaps observed for gender-themed issues (r pb = .66, p = .008).

Taken together, these results indicate that gender differences in policy attitudes are pervasive but particularly pronounced for gender-themed issues, with boys consistently reporting more conservative views than girls.

Gendered sorting into ideological profiles

Building on the previously observed gender differences in response distributions and within-group polarization, we next identified latent ideological profiles across all issues.

Latent class models with two to five classes were first estimated without covariates to determine the optimal solution, with model selection guided by BIC, AIC, log-likelihood, class size, and substantive interpretability. A three-class solution provided the best balance between statistical fit and interpretability (BIC = 76,991.95; AIC = 75,552.25).

Gender was then included as a covariate predicting class membership. Fit indices remained comparable (BIC = 76,766.45; AIC = 75,316.16), indicating that the latent structure was stable and that adding gender did not alter class composition, while enabling estimation of gender differences in class probabilities. Discussion

Our study shows a clear and pervasive gender gap in adolescent policy attitudes, with boys consistently expressing more conservative views than girls, particularly on gender-related issues. Beyond mean differences, we also find evidence of structured ideological patterns: girls are more likely to align with a progressive profile, whereas boys cluster more strongly in a conservative profile. This suggests that gender is not merely associated with differences in specific attitudes, but already functions as an organizing dimension of ideological belief systems in adolescence.

Taken together, these findings point to the early emergence of gender as a cognitive and social organizing principle in adolescents' political attitudes. One possible explanation is that gender becomes a salient basis for structuring social perception and ideological beliefs during this developmental period. From a social identity perspective (Tajfel & Turner, 1979), this can be understood as the consequence of increasing gender salience in adolescence. During this period, heightened social comparison (Crone & Fuligni, 2020) and contextual reinforcement (Hill & Lynch, 1983) make gender a more central basis for self-definition and intergroup perception, particularly in domains that are socially or normatively gendered. As a result, policy attitudes may increasingly be filtered through group-based identities and interests.

Complementing this identity-based mechanism, Social Role Theory (Eagly & Wood, 2012) highlights how gendered social structures generate systematically different role expectations for boys and girls. These expectations are learned and reinforced over development and may translate into stable attitudinal differences through exposure to gendered norms and normative feedback from salient reference groups (Crone & Fuligni, 2020). Consistent with research on adult populations (Eagly et al., 2004, e.g., ), such differences reflect the influence of gendered social roles and their associated normative expectations.

Although these mechanisms provide relatively stable developmental explanations, their expression may be amplified by contemporary social and political conditions. In contexts characterized by uncertainty and overlapping crises, individuals may rely more strongly on social identities as organizing frameworks for policy attitudes rather than issue-specific considerations. This tendency may be reinforced by ideologically segmented digital environments and algorithmic curation. In particular, exposure to gender-focused online subcultures, including both masculinist (e.g., the manosphere) and feminist communities, may further increase the salience of gender as a social identity, thereby strengthening gender differentiation in policy attitudes.

Consistent with this integrated perspective, recent cohort-based research shows that gender gaps in ideological self-identification peak in middle adolescence and decline toward late adolescence (Rekker, 2025). The present findings extend this pattern by showing that similar gender differences are also evident in concrete policy attitudes. However, it remains unclear whether such developmental variation generalizes across broader policy domains.

Because the current data do not allow for reliable estimation of age-specific differences, future longitudinal research is needed to examine how these patterns develop over time and whether they reflect a broader "new global gender divide" or are primarily driven by adolescent-specific developmental processes.

Limitations

Our findings reveal a sizable and pervasive gender gap across a range of policy issues, suggesting that adolescents' attitudes are already meaningfully structured. Several limitations should nevertheless be noted. First, although the sample was diverse in educational track and background, it was not nationally representative. However, previous research on citizenship outcomes suggests relatively limited variation across Dutch secondary schools (Dijkstra et al., 2025), making substantial differences in the overall pattern of findings less likely. Second, the cross-sectional design and single-country context limit conclusions about developmental change and generalizability. Longitudinal and cross-national research is needed to examine whether these gender differences persist across developmental stages and institutional contexts; to facilitate such work, the measurement instruments used in this study are made available online. Third, each issue was assessed using a single item designed to maximize response variation, which may have influenced the magnitude of the observed gender differences. Future research should therefore consider more fine-grained measures.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates a clear gender gap in policy attitudes among adolescents, with boys expressing more conservative views than girls. The findings suggest that ideological structuring of policy attitudes is already present during adolescence and systematically differentiated along gender lines. This indicates that gender differences in policy attitudes are not solely the product of later-life socialization, but are also shaped by earlier developmental and socialization processes. Future research should further examine the mechanisms underlying these patterns and their implications for political socialization.
