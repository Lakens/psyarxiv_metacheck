Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. Abstract

Background: The Life in Recovery (LiR) survey has been used internationally to examine addiction recovery, but little is known about how its concepts and content transfer across sociocultural and service contexts. This study culturally adapted the LiR survey for use in Spain and examined evidence based on test content and response processes.

Methods: A sequential mixed-methods process included review of previous LiR versions, translation, contextual adaptation, expert review (N = 13), and cognitive interviews with people in recovery (N = 6). Expert agreement was examined using Item-level Content Validity Indexes (I-CVI), and qualitative data were analysed using framework analysis.

Results: Twenty-five of 37 survey questions met the predefined I-CVI criterion, while most items met criteria for clarity (76/80) and relevance (60/80). Expert review and cognitive interviews led to substantial conceptual, linguistic, and contextual modifications: contextual information was expanded and repositioned; recovery questions were reformulated to better capture recovery onset, relapse, and self-identified recovery stage, incorporating the non-linear nature of recovery; recovery pathways were adapted to the Spanish addiction care system; and the SABRS was reduced from 32 to 24 items while retaining its core domains and facilitating cross-cultural comparability.

Conclusions: Cross-cultural adaptation of the LiR required changes beyond linguistic translation to reflect local recovery meanings, lived experiences, and the Spanish addiction-care system. The findings illustrate the importance of balancing international comparability with cultural and contextual relevance when adapting recovery measures across settings.

Introduction

Over the last decade, recovery has become increasingly prominent as a framework for understanding the needs and change processes of people with substance use problems from a holistic and person-centred perspective (Bellaert et al., 2024). This approach moves beyond symptom reduction and abstinence-based understandings of recovery by framing it as a comprehensive process of change across personal, social and community domains, emphasising self-determination, hope, social connection and a meaningful life (Ashford, et al., 2019; Laudet and Best, 2015).

This broader understanding has important implications for addiction care systems. In Spain, recent strategic plans in mental health and addictions have increasingly promoted person-centred, community-based and recovery-oriented approaches (Ministerio de Sanidad, 2022). However, implementing these approaches requires tools that can capture recovery from the perspective of lived experience while remaining sensitive to the cultural, social and organisational conditions in which recovery takes place. This is especially relevant because much of the recovery literature has been developed in English-speaking contexts, whereas the meanings, resources, barriers and pathways associated with recovery may vary across sociocultural settings (Bird et al., 2014; Slade et al., 2012). Instruments used to study recovery therefore do not simply translate words; they also carry assumptions about what recovery is, how it is experienced and which forms of support are socially and institutionally available.

One of the most influential international instruments for examining recovery journeys is the Life in Recovery (LiR) survey (Laudet, 2013). Originally developed by Faces and Voices of Recovery in the United States, the LiR survey has since been adapted and used in Australia, the United Kingdom, Canada and several European contexts (Best, et al., 2015 (Best, et al., , 2018 (Best, et al., , 2020;; McQuaid, et al., 2017). Across these versions, the survey has progressively incorporated relevant developments, including self-categorisation of recovery status (Best, et al., 2015), barriers and stigma (McQuaid, et al., 2017), standardised recovery pathways (Best, et al., 2018) and the Strengths and Barriers Recovery Scale (SABRS), which quantifies changes in positive and negative recovery capital (Best, et al., 2020). Overall, LiR studies have provided valuable evidence on positive changes across life domains, the diversity of recovery pathways and the importance of long-term recovery-oriented support (Martinelli, et al., 2020). Further adaptations of the LiR survey have therefore been encouraged to better understand recovery, build recovery communities and strengthen recovery-oriented care in different countries (Best et al., 2024).

However, although the value of adapting the LiR survey to other cultures has been recognised (Best, et al., 2018 (Best, et al., , 2024)), previous studies have provided limited systematic documentation of how this adaptation has been conducted. This is a relevant limitation because cross-cultural adaptation requires more than linguistic equivalence. It also requires examining whether the content, wording, concepts, response formats and interpretations of an instrument are appropriate for the target population and context (International Test Commission, 2017; Lira and Caballero, 2020). A transparent adaptation process can strengthen methodological rigour, improve the systematisation of the survey and support validity evidence (Beaton, et al., 2000; Boateng, et al., 2018). Without such a process, an adapted instrument may reproduce categories that are not fully meaningful in the new context, fail to capture locally relevant experiences or introduce interpretation bias.

Methodological guidelines recommend combining translation, expert review and input from the target population to examine different forms of equivalence and validity evidence (Boateng, et al., 2018; Gjersing, et al., 2010; International Test Commission, 2017). Expert judgement is particularly useful for evaluating content relevance, conceptual clarity and contextual appropriateness, while cognitive interviews with people in recovery provide evidence on how participants understand, retrieve information, interpret questions and formulate responses (Lecroy, 2019). Together, these procedures can strengthen validity evidence based on test content and response processes, and help ensure that the adapted survey reflects the assumptions, values and lived realities of the population for whom it is intended.

Accordingly, this study aimed to culturally adapt the Life in Recovery survey for use in Spain and examine validity evidence based on test content and response processes. Specifically, the study aimed to: 1) document the methodological procedures used to adapt the survey; 2) update and refine the instrument by incorporating recommendations from previous LiR versions and adapting key sections to the Spanish social, cultural and service context; and 3) examine the semantic, conceptual and content adequacy of the adapted survey through expert review, together with response-process evidence from cognitive interviews with people in recovery. In doing so, the study addresses limitations identified in earlier adaptation processes and provides a culturally grounded Spanish version of the LiR survey for studying lived recovery experiences and informing recovery-oriented research, practice and policy in Spain.

Methods

Study Design

This cultural adaptation study followed internationally recognised guidelines for crosscultural adaptation, instrument development, and content validation (Boateng et al., 2018; International Test Commission, 2017; Muñiz & Fonseca-Pedrero, 2019). A sequential mixedmethods design was used to examine validity evidence based on test content and response processes. Content validity refers to the extent to which an instrument adequately represents the construct of interest and its content is relevant, comprehensive, and comprehensible for the target population and context (Mokkink et al., 2018). This source of validity evidence is particularly important in cross-cultural adaptation, as inadequate content validity may compromise subsequent evaluation of measurement properties and the interpretation of scores (Terwee et al., 2018).

The study formed the first phase of a broader research project preregistered in December

Stage 1. Review and integration of previous LiR surveys

This stage aimed to identify which elements should be retained to preserve international comparability and which sections required updating or cultural adaptation for the Spanish context. After obtaining permission from the original authors, previous LiR versions were comparatively reviewed, focusing on their common structure, country-specific adaptations, conceptual and methodological developments, and recommendations from previous studies.

Stage 2. Translation

A multi-strategy translation process was conducted. Two professional translators with experience in similar instruments completed forward-and back-translation procedures. The translated materials were then reviewed by a bilingual reviewer with expertise in addiction research and instrument design, focusing on syntactic and typographical errors, wording discrepancies, overly complex terms and potentially problematic recovery-related concepts.

Finally, two researchers with experience in addictions and instrument translation reviewed all discrepancies with the bilingual reviewer until consensus was reached on the most appropriate wording. Although some LiR questions and items had previously been translated into Spanish by Best et al. (2020), a full translation process was conducted to ensure the internal coherence and cultural equivalence of the complete instrument.

Stage 3. Contextual Adaptation

This stage focused on three main areas. First, contextual and social position questions were incorporated to capture the broader ecosystem in which recovery takes place. Second, the Strengths and Barriers Recovery Scale (SABRS) was used as an updated development of the original LiR items assessing life experiences in active addiction and recovery. Best, et al., (2020) reclassified these items to provide a quantifiable measure of positive and negative recovery capital, allowing changes in strengths and barriers to be examined across recovery.

Third, recovery pathways were adapted to the organisation of addiction care in Spain, as previous LiR studies have shown that pathways to recovery are sensitive to local service systems and cultural contexts (McQuaid, et al., 2017).

Stage 4. Expert Review

A structured expert judgement review was conducted in two rounds. In the first round, experts independently reviewed the survey using a fillable PDF and an instruction manual. For open-and closed-ended questions, they indicated whether each question was appropriate and important using a dichotomous response format. For items, they assessed clarity and relevance on a 4-point scale ranging from 1 (not clear/not relevant) to 4 (very clear/very relevant). Openended spaces were included to collect comments and suggestions. In the second round, two online focus groups were conducted to discuss the main concerns identified in the individual review. Each group lasted an average of 90 minutes, was moderated by two researchers via Zoom and was recorded with participants' informed consent. Questions and items with lower agreement or substantial qualitative comments were discussed to clarify conceptual, cultural, practical or wording concerns and to reach a shared decision on whether they should be retained,

Participants

The expert panel comprised 13 addiction professionals selected to ensure experience, diversity of professional profiles, representation from different levels of Spain's addiction care network and gender balance. The panel had a mean clinical and/or research experience of 19.69 years (SD = 6.87; range = 10-30; Mmale = 53.8%), and included psychologists, social educators, social workers and physicians. Experts were recruited through snowball sampling, beginning with key professionals who were asked to recommend other eligible participants. Cognitive interviews were conducted with six people in recovery, a sample size consistent with recommendations for cognitive interviewing studies (Beatty and Willis, 2007). Inclusion criteria aimed to ensure diversity in recovery stage, addiction severity, living conditions, treatment modality, primary substance, age and gender. The sample had a mean time in recovery of 19.67 years (SD = 12.34; range = 5-35; Mage = 52.40; Mmale = 66.7%). Participants varied in primary substance, recovery pathway and social circumstances. Participants were purposively selected with support from key informants and specialised addiction services, and were contacted by email or telephone to explain the study, procedures and interview scheduling (See Table 1)

Life in Recovery Survey

The Life in Recovery (LiR) survey is a self-report instrument designed to examine recovery journeys, recovery pathways and life changes from active addiction to recovery (Laudet, 2013). For the present study, the LiR survey was adapted for use in Spain while retaining the core structure of previous versions. The survey includes open-and closed-ended questions and items assessing sociodemographic information, substance use history, recovery experience, recovery pathways, the SABRS, and health and quality of life. Specifically, it explores substance use trajectories, including onset of addiction-related problems, primary substance, polydrug use and duration of the problem; recovery trajectories, including recovery onset, relapses and relevant motives; formal and non-formal pathways used to support recovery; and lived experiences during active addiction and recovery. The full adapted survey is provided in OSF 'Spanish Life in Recovery Survey'.

Data Analysis

A mixed-methods analytical strategy was used to integrate quantitative expert agreement, qualitative expert feedback, focus group discussions and cognitive interview findings (Ivankova, et al., 2006). First, expert agreement was analysed using Lynn's Item-level Content Validity Index (I-CVI) (Lynn, 1986). For items rated on a 4-point scale, the I-CVI was calculated by dividing the number of experts who rated each item as 3 or 4 by the total number of experts. For open-and closed-ended questions, the same proportion-of-agreement logic was applied to dichotomous judgements of appropriateness and importance. The following criteria guided decisions after the individual expert review: 1) questions or items with I-CVI values above .78 and no substantial concerns were retained; 2) questions or items with I-CVI values above .78 but comments suggesting refinement were reviewed; and 3) questions or items with I-CVI values below .78, or those generating greater conceptual, cultural or practical concerns, were discussed in the focus groups to reach consensus. I-CVI scores, means and percentages were calculated using an ad hoc Excel macro.

Second, qualitative data from expert comments, focus groups and cognitive interviews were analysed using framework analysis (Bryman and Burgess, 1994). Transcripts and written comments were reviewed and coded by question or item to identify comprehension problems, conceptual ambiguities, cultural concerns, response-process issues and suggestions for improvement. Quantitative scores, qualitative comments, focus group conclusions and cognitive interview findings were then integrated into comparative decision tables. These tables documented the issue identified, evidence source, decision taken and rationale for each relevant modification.

Results

Review, Translation, and Contextual Adaptation

Review of Previous LiR Surveys

The comparative review of previous LiR surveys identified a common core structure across international versions, while also highlighting structural, conceptual and methodological changes introduced in each context. This shared structure provided a basis for preserving international comparability, while also identifying sections that required updating or cultural adaptation for use in Spain. The review showed that the LiR survey has progressively evolved through refinements aimed at improving clarity, internal coherence and analytical usefulness, as well as responding to recommendations from previous versions. However, substantial variability was observed in how adaptations were implemented and documented, supporting the need for a more systematic and transparent adaptation process for the Spanish version.

Comparative implementations also suggest the potential of the LiR survey to examine recovery outcomes in relation to differences in public policies, care systems and structural conditions.

Across studies, authors consistently reported limitations related to sample diversity and cultural coverage, including the underrepresentation of minority groups and people who do not explicitly identify with the term "recovery". Accordingly, the review highlighted the relevance of using broader and more inclusive wording, collecting information on cultural diversity and living conditions, and adapting recovery pathways to local service systems and social contexts (Best et al., 2020; McQuaid et al., 2017). The full comparative review is available in the OSF document 'International Evolution of the LiR Survey'.

Translation and Conceptual Equivalence

The translation process identified several terms that required conceptual rather than literal translation, including recovery, became addicted, active addiction, drug of choice, back into active addiction and relapsed. Most difficulties concerned how addiction and recovery were labelled, particularly for people who may experience change, improvement or problem resolution without necessarily identifying with recovery-based terminology. Terms such as became addicted, active addiction or back into active addiction therefore required careful adaptation to avoid stigmatising or identity-based wording. The review committee therefore prioritised simple, accessible and non-stigmatising language. This stage also identified two sections requiring deeper cultural adaptation: recovery pathways and SABRS items. Full translation process is available in the OSF document 'Results of the Translation Process'.

Adaptation to the Spanish Social and Service Context

The contextual adaptation to the Spanish setting focused on two complementary areas.

First, questions on sexual diversity, migration status, ethnic identity, socioeconomic status, housing conditions and cohabitation were incorporated to capture objective and subjective information on social position and living circumstances. These additions were intended to situate recovery within broader social and structural conditions. Second, recovery pathways were adapted to the organisation of addiction care in Spain by integrating previous recovery pathway classifications (Best, et al., 2018; Kelly, 2017; Moos, 2008) with the structure of the Spanish public addiction care network (Plan Nacional sobre Drogas, 2017). This network includes first-level services for initial support, information, detection, harm reduction and referral; second-level specialised outpatient services for assessment, treatment planning and referral; and third-level highly specialised resources such as inpatient detoxification units, therapeutic communities, supported housing and day centres. This structure informed the development of seven recovery pathway categories adapted to the Spanish context: 1) addiction-specialised primary care services, 2) specialist outpatient treatment, 3) residential and community-based treatment, 4) peer-based recovery support, 5) services for high-risk situations, 6) low-threshold and harm reduction services, and 7) natural recovery (see OSF 'Adaptation of Recovery Pathways to the Spanish Context').

Sensitive Contextual Questions and Participant Acceptability

Experts considered questions on migration status, sexual diversity, housing and economic situation relevant for understanding recovery in context. However, they noted that these questions could generate discomfort or distrust if presented at the beginning of the survey without a clear explanation of their purpose. Following expert consensus, these questions were relocated to a final section entitled Specific Context Information, and the instructions were expanded to explain their relevance and emphasise their voluntary nature.

Cognitive interviews supported this decision. Participants generally considered these questions appropriate, but confirmed that placing them at the end of the survey was more acceptable and reduced initial response burden. This change therefore improved contextual sensitivity and participant acceptability.

Reformulation of Addiction-Related and Recovery-Journey Questions

A central theme concerned the wording of core questions about addiction-related problems and recovery journeys. Experts identified ambiguity in the term problem, which could be interpreted in different ways. To improve conceptual precision, the question assessing the onset of addiction-related problems was reformulated to refer more explicitly to the point at which substance use began to cause negative consequences or significant disruption in life.

Cognitive interviews supported this change, as participants anchored their responses in moments when they began to lose control over substance use and when it started to affect their lives.

The review also highlighted the need to clarify the concept of relapse. Experts noted that relapse could be interpreted in different ways and recommended broadening the wording by using several related expressions to improve identification. However, cognitive interviews showed that the broader term setback was sometimes interpreted as referring to an exceptional or extreme life event rather than a return to substance use, generating confusion and limiting the question's ability to capture relapse experiences accurately. In response, the question was reformulated using language and synonyms employed by participants themselves.

Finally, the question assessing the beginning of recovery was revised to capture the subjective turning point of the recovery process rather than the start of formal treatment. Experts emphasised the importance of distinguishing when individuals considered their recovery to have begun, while acknowledging the role of memory in identifying this moment. Cognitive interviews confirmed that participants understood this distinction and described personally meaningful turning points, such as deciding to seek help, becoming aware of the need for change or reaching a critical point.

Recovery Non-Linearity and Self-Identified Recovery Stage

Cognitive interviews showed that participants often described recovery as an evolving process rather than a fixed status. Some did not frame themselves simply as "recovered" or "in recovery", but instead referred to how they currently felt within their process. Similarly, when asked whether recovery was perceived as complete or ongoing, participants often expressed ambivalent or evolving views. This led the research team to reconsider how to capture participants' own appraisal of their current recovery phase. As a result, a new question was developed to assess self-identified recovery stage, informed by the recovery stages proposed by Leamy, et al., (2011). This item was designed to complement existing indicators such as time in recovery and relapse history, and to better capture non-linear recovery processes.

Adaptation of recovery pathways

The recovery pathways section required substantial contextual adaptation. Experts considered the initial list too long and technical, especially for participants who might not know the formal names or administrative categories of the services they had used. They recommended grouping resources into broader and more intuitive categories, avoiding excessive detail, and focusing on main treatment modalities, resources for specific situations and mental health services.

Cognitive interviews confirmed this concern. Participants sometimes had difficulty distinguishing between service types, such as therapeutic communities and day centres. However, they were usually able to describe the activities, intensity and setting of the services they had attended. For this reason, pathway categories were simplified and accompanied by short explanatory descriptions. The final version included eight service modalities organised within the seven recovery pathways adapted to the Spanish context.

Cultural and Linguistic Refinement of SABRS Items

The SABRS items also required cultural and linguistic adaptation. During the expert review, some items were retained without modification, whereas others were shortened, reformulated, merged or removed because of redundancy, limited contextual relevance or unclear wording. This reduced the original 32-item version to a preliminary Spanish version of 26 items.

Cognitive interviews led to further refinement. Participants identified wording that could be simplified and items that overlapped conceptually. Six items were revised to improve clarity and contextual relevance, and four items were merged into two to reduce redundancy.

The final Spanish SABRS comprised 24 items (11 strengths and 13 barriers). This adaptation sought to preserve conceptual equivalence with the original 32-item instrument (15 strengths and 17 barriers) while improving cultural fit, comprehensibility and parsimony in the Spanish context.

Usability, Instructions, and Cognitive Burden

Cognitive interviews supported the usability, acceptability and response processes of the Spanish LiR survey. Participants generally considered it relevant and useful for reflecting on their recovery experiences, reported low to moderate cognitive burden, and perceived the survey length as appropriate. However, some instructions were considered too long or cognitively demanding, so they were rewritten using shorter and more direct wording, with response options and examples clarified where necessary. Participants also showed adequate memory access when answering time-based questions, including age, duration of substance use and time in recovery. Finally, following the principle of parsimony, questions about motives for beginning recovery and obstacles experienced during recovery were removed because they were insufficiently aligned with the study aims, increased survey length and were unlikely to provide sufficient depth. Overall, these refinements supported the acceptability, feasibility and user-friendliness of the Spanish LiR survey.

Discussion

This study provides a detailed account of the cultural adaptation of the LiR survey for use in Spain, following a collaborative, multistage process involving addiction experts and people in recovery. Although cultural adaptation is essential to ensure conceptual equivalence, contextual sensitivity and the appropriate assessment of recovery-oriented approaches, this process has been particularly neglected in the literature (Beaton, et al., 2000; Lira and Caballero, 2020). In this sense, the present study contributes by making explicit the decisions, revisions and sources of evidence that informed the development of the Spanish LiR survey.

The findings reinforce the idea that cross-cultural adaptation cannot be reduced to linguistic translation (Boateng, et al., 2018; Cynamon and Kulka, 2001). Translation was a necessary first step, but it mainly served to identify concepts that required deeper cultural, conceptual and experiential refinement. Terms such as recovery, active addiction, relapse and drug of choice were not only language problems, but also reflected different assumptions about how addiction and recovery are named, understood and experienced. This is particularly relevant in the Spanish context, where some people may describe their process in terms of change, improvement, stability or life reconstruction without necessarily identifying with recovery-based terminology. The adaptation process therefore required multiple types of modification, including rewording, relocation, deletion, simplification, description of response options and reformulation of key constructs.

The expert review was particularly useful for strengthening content validity evidence and identifying areas where conceptual or contextual fit required further discussion. Experts helped clarify which questions were relevant, unclear, culturally sensitive or insufficiently adapted to the Spanish addiction care system. One important decision concerned the assessment of recovery itself. Previous LiR surveys had included questions focused on definitions of recovery or self-identification as being "in recovery" or "recovered" (Best et al., 2015 (Best et al., , 2018 (Best et al., , 2020;; McQuaid et al., 2017). In contrast, experts in the present study recommended focusing more directly on key elements, trajectories and supports involved in recovery, regardless of the specific label used by participants. This is consistent with recent work suggesting that identifying core elements of recovery may offer greater specificity than relying exclusively on broad definitions of recovery (Zemore et al., 2023). It also responds to concerns that recovery research has often focused heavily on defining the concept, whereas greater attention may be needed to understand the supports, barriers and mechanisms that shape diverse recovery experiences (McDaniel et al., 2020). A second relevant contribution was the reformulation of questions related to recovery onset and time in recovery. Experts stressed the need to capture the key subjective moment when individuals consider their recovery began (decision to change, first serious attempt, turning point, or awareness of the need to change) and to validate the question by taking into account how people recall and access that information. Cognitive interviews supported this distinction, as participants were able to identify personally meaningful moments that marked the beginning of their recovery process.

The cognitive interviews also showed the value of involving people in recovery in the adaptation process. Participants identified wording problems, ambiguities, emotional reactions to sensitive questions, difficulties with service categories and areas where instructions could be simplified. These findings provided response-process evidence by showing how participants understood the questions, retrieved information and formulated their answers (Boateng, et al., 2018; Lecroy, 2019). One of the most relevant changes concerned the self-definition of recovery status. While previous surveys (Best, et al., 2015 (Best, et al., , 2018 (Best, et al., , 2020) ) had focused on how people described themselves in relation to their substance use problem (in recovery, recovered, etc.), users emphasized how they currently felt about their process, regardless of whether they considered themselves recovered or not. This led to the decision to include a new question with the aim of assessing participants' subjective perception of their current stage or phase of recovery. This change is a recent development in the field and aims to improve the examination of recovery journeys by acknowledging that recovery is a complex, dynamic, and non-linear process (Vanderplasschen and Best, 2021). The inclusion of this new question adds another component to the analysis, allowing for a more comprehensive exploration of the recovery process as a non-linear phenomenon (Time in recovery + Type of trajectory [with or without relapses] + Current phase of the process).

Furthermore, this research reinforces the value of gathering information from selected experts and users through standardized criteria that objectify expertise and ensure adequate heterogeneity (International Test Commission, 2017). The diversity of professional and user profiles enriched the discussions and provided a wide range of perspectives on the constructs.

In this sense, one of the most complex topics was determining the best strategy for capturing a wide range of recovery experiences without limiting the scope solely to abstinence-based perspectives. To achieve this, the focus was placed on the concept of "change" or "recovery" without explicitly referring to "quitting substance use." This finding emphasized the importance of establishing inclusion criteria with a wide range of synonyms to capture different profiles of people in recovery. One criticism of recovery research is that much of it is based primarily on samples of people in treatment, thereby limiting the variability of experiences and potentially introducing bias in the results (Vitellone, et al., 2022).

Another complex issue was the need to use synonyms across various concepts to ensure that different profiles could identify with them. It was also discovered that participants had difficulty identifying the services they attended and were unfamiliar with the therapeutic models or approaches. Therefore, it was deemed essential to describe recovery pathways in detail to aid identification. All these conclusions align with the findings of Bowen, et al., (2022), who reported high variability in responses to certain items depending on the individual's recovery journey.

Finally, the study illustrates the value of combining quantitative and qualitative strategies in cultural adaptation. The Item-level Content Validity Index provided a structured estimate of expert consensus, but qualitative analysis was necessary to interpret disagreement, identify conceptual concerns and guide specific revisions. In several cases, items with acceptable consensus scores were still modified after discussion or cognitive interviewing. In this regard, Morris, et al., (2017) emphasize the central role of qualitative analyses in instrument adaptation. It is therefore recommended to create dialogue spaces and integrate qualitative analyses into survey adaptation processes.

Strengths, Limitations and Future Research

This study has several strengths. First, the adaptation followed a systematic, multistage process informed by internationally recognised guidelines and combined quantitative and qualitative evidence across translation, contextual adaptation, expert review, and cognitive interviews. Second, the integration of multidisciplinary expert knowledge with the perspectives of people with lived experience of recovery enabled the evaluation of both conceptual relevance and how survey content was understood and interpreted by the target population. The inclusion of experts from different areas of the Spanish addiction care system also supported the contextual adaptation of recovery pathways and service-related content. Finally, the study provides a transparent account of the decisions made throughout the adaptation process, helping to distinguish linguistic translation from the broader conceptual, contextual, and functional changes required to adapt the LiR to the Spanish context.

Several limitations should also be considered. First, the findings provide validity evidence based on test content and response processes but do not constitute a full psychometric validation of the Spanish LiR survey. Future studies should examine its internal structure, reliability, measurement invariance, and associations with external variables in larger samples.

Second, although experts and cognitive interview participants were purposively selected to capture diversity in relevant characteristics and experiences, these samples cannot fully represent the broader population of people in recovery in Spain. Some recovery pathways, minority groups, gender identities, migration backgrounds, and experiences of natural recovery may therefore have been underrepresented. Finally, the adaptation was conducted within the specific Spanish sociocultural and addiction care context. Consequently, some adaptations may not be directly transferable to other countries or Spanish-speaking populations and would require further cultural and contextual evaluation before use.

Conclusion

The LiR survey has been adapted in several countries to examine addiction recovery journeys and life changes, but previous adaptations have rarely documented the cultural adaptation process in detail.
