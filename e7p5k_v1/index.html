Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. Some differences come from the ground truth: it lacks p-values with a correction name (“p Bonf = .061”), and counts partial eta squared written with its subscript after it (“η 2 p = .05”), “p = .05”, and some table notes and figure legends (“† p < .10, *p < .05”), which this module leaves out, as non-significant p-values. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. Abstract

Background. Research on neurodevelopmental conditions (NDCs) has traditionally emphasized challenges rather than strengths. Existing strengths research primarily covers on ADHD, autism, and Specific Learning Disorder (SLD), with limited attention to Developmental Coordination Disorder (DCD) and Developmental Language Disorder (DLD). Moreover, strengths are typically studied within single diagnostic groups, despite the frequent co-occurrence of NDCs, and most studies rely on adult self-reports or parent reports. Little is known about teachers' perspectives on strengths in children with NDCs, despite their unique opportunity to observe multiple children at once and in both academic and social contexts. Method. In this mixed-methods study, we explored teachers' perspectives on positive traits in children with NDCs. Sixteen teachers completed semi-structured interviews and the Signature Strengths Survey, indicating whether 24 character strengths were characteristic of specific NDCs. Qualitative data were analysed using inductive thematic analysis.

Results. Teachers identified 22 positive traits across children with NDCs, clustering into five domains: cognitive skills, self-regulation and efficacy, social and interpersonal skills, practical skills, and energy-related traits. Most traits were reported across diagnostic groups, some were reported uniquely in children with ADHD or autism. We described a conceptual framework on the development and use of positive traits comprising compensatory, promotive, protective, and double-edged mechanisms.

Conclusions. The findings highlight the diversity of positive traits identified by teachers and their context-, recipient-, and intensity dependent nature. By extending the limited literature across NDCs, this study contributes to a more balanced understanding of neurodevelopmental differences and may inform strengths-based educational and clinical approaches.

1.

Introduction

The American Psychiatric Association (2022) defines neurodevelopmental conditions (NDC) as a group of conditions with an onset in the developmental phase characterized by developmental deficits or differences in brain processes that produce impairments of personal, social, academic, and occupational functioning. Typically, these include, amongst others, Attention Deficit Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD), autism, Developmental Coordination Disorder (DCD), Developmental Language Disorder (DLD), and Specific Learning Disorder (SLD). These NDCs are common in children. Prevalences vary between 1% in autism (Zeidan et al., 2022) up to 5-6% in ADHD, DCD, DLD, and SLD (American Psychiatric Association, 2022; Calder et al., 2022; Polanczyk et al., 2007; Yang et al., 2022). There is also high co-occurrence amongst these NDCs.

For example, 60% out of a sample of 491 Belgian children with (features of) DCD (De Roubaix et al., 2025) and 66% out of a large sample of 1919 Italian children with ADHD (Reale et al., 2017) presented with more than one diagnosis. These prevalent conditions are traditionally described in terms of deficits, impairment and elevated support needs. Increasingly, concepts such as the Positive Youth Development Approach (Damon, 2004) and the neurodiversity paradigm (Chapman, 2020), urge us to focus not only on challenges but also on potentialities and strengths (to which we refer to as positive traits in this paper). Attention to positive traits has the potential to promote a more balanced perspective on NDCs (Shaw et al., 2021). Moreover, studies suggest that youth strength-based approaches, which focus on identifying and culturing positive aspects could positively influence children's self-confidence (Galloway et al., 2020; Krtek et al., 2022) and could help mitigate the negative effects of stigma (Visser et al., 2025). Likewise, in interviews, young adults with ADHD report that focussing on their strengths had helped them become more resilient (Dangmann et al., 2024). This potential benefit for well-being is especially important in children with NDCs, where low self-confidence is common (Betancourt et al., 2024; Clayton et al., 2026; O'Dea et al., 2021; Vieira et al., 2024; Wilmot et al., 2024). White et al. (2025) further argue that strength-based approaches have the potential to improve school experiences and educational outcomes. Aside from the possible direct positive effect on mental well-being and educational outcomes, a greater number of strengths in children seems to have cumulative positive effects on reduced support needs for mental health, social, and functioning support, both on the short and the longterm, demonstrating a positive developmental cascade effect (Altschuler et al., 2026). Therefore, identifying and building on strengths in-and outside the classroom appears to be worthwhile.

Research has begun identifying positive aspects in people with these neurodevelopmental conditions. Positive self-reports in adults with ADHD, autism, and dyslexia have been reported (Kannangara, 2018; Lampinen et al., 2026; Schippers et al., 2022), while lesser-known conditions such as DCD and DLD received less attention. The question arises whether these self-reported positive aspects in adulthood can also be traced back to childhood or that strengths develop with age. In childhood, studies examining strengths typically include parental reports (De Roubaix et al., 2025; Halder et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2025) and only to a lesser extent self-report (e.g., (Jones & Hesse, 2018; Mullally et al., 2026). As children spend considerable time at school, teachers could be considered important independent informants. Because teachers observe large numbers of same-aged children on a daily basis and have experience with NDCs in the classroom, they may be particularly well positioned to evaluate a child's strengths relative to age-related expectations. Yet, far fewer studies on strengths have included teacher's voices. In ADHD, teachers have highlighted strengths in creativity, humour, high energy, likability, spontaneity, openness, hard work, and physical education as strengths in children with ADHD, although identifying positive traits was not the primary objective of most of these studies (Krtek et al., 2022; McDougal et al., 2023; Ward et al., 2021). Similarly, a teacher study in autism has reported strengths related to kindness, self-regulation, friendliness, and perseverance, and other skills (including memory, being good at specific courses etc) (Mirenda et al., 2024). In DLD, teachers have reported strengths in cognitive skills, academic skills, creativity, sports, and social skills although again identifying positive traits was not the primary objective of most of these studies (Dockrell & Lindsay, 2001; Gibbons et al., 2023). To date, no studies have examined teachers' perspectives on strengths in children with DCD or SLD.

Because existing studies have focused on individual NDCs and often do not report co-occurring diagnoses, it remains unclear whether these strengths are specific to particular NDCs or reflect broader aspects of neurodivergence. Interpretation is further hampered by the sparse and uneven evidence base, with teacher perspectives examined only in ADHD, autism, and DLD, and not in DCD or SLD. As a result, it is currently difficult to determine which strengths may be shared across neurodevelopmental conditions and which may be more condition-specific.

Therefore, the primary objective of this study was to explore whether teachers perceive positive character traits or strengths in students aged 8-16 with ADHD, autism, DCD, DLD, or SLD, and to identify potential similarities and differences in the teacher-reported traits among children with these conditions. The following research question was addressed: How do teachers perceive and describe positive character traits or strengths in students aged 8-16 with ADHD, autism, SLD, DCD, or DLD?

2. Methods

This mixed method design study was carried out in accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. The local Medical Ethics Review Committee (METC East-Netherlands) determined that formal ethical approval was not required (reference number 2025-17988; 5 th of March 2025). The study was pre-registered on the Open Science Framework (https://osf.io/j2xf9). Prior to completing the recruitment questionnaire, all participants provided electronic informed consent. Teachers who mainly teach children with lower cognitive functioning (IQ below 85). The rationale of these specific eligibility choices are described in Supplementary file 1.

2.1.2. Participant selection

Using a purposeful maximum variation sampling technique (Patton, 2014), we defined three primary and three secondary variables to select participants allowing us to investigate the phenomenon from different angles enabling greater transferability. Candidates were selected to ensure maximum variation across these variables, prioritizing diversity in the primary variables, followed by consideration of the secondary variables.

The primary variables were: (1) Grade (primary / secondary school), (2) Experience with specific NDCs (ADHD, autism, DCD, DLD, or SLD), and (3) Country (the Netherlands or Belgium). The secondary variables were: (1) Course format (predominantly theoretical or practical), (2) Years of teaching experience (3-9y, 10-19y, or 20y or more), (3) School type (Mainstream or special education), and (4) Study field (specific content e.g. biology, arts). All variables were discussed with three researchers, three teachers, and three individuals with lived experience, who confirmed their relevance. The full rationale of these choices is described in Supplementary file 2.

2.1.3. Recruitment strategy

Our recruitment call was disseminated via social media platforms (Facebook and LinkedIn), as well as through the distribution of research flyers within the researchers' professional networks.

To minimise selection bias towards participants with particularly positive views, the study was described broadly as exploring teacher's experiences with NDCs and the character traits they observe. Later, targeted sampling was used to recruit underrepresented teachers, including those with experience with DCD and DLD and those teaching subjects such as art.

2.2. Data collection

Data were collected in three steps. First, candidates completed a short survey on eligibility criteria, sampling criteria, and contact details. Eligible participants were then selected using purposeful maximum variation sampling and invited to an in-person or online interview.

Interviews were recorded, transcribed and pseudonymized. Approximately two weeks later, participants received the transcript and a post-interview survey in Castor (Castor EDC, 2026). If necessary, three reminders were sent out via e-mail or telephone.

2.2.1. Semi-structured in-depth interview

Each selected teacher participated in a single semi-structured in-depth interview. The interview guide (Supplementary file 3) was developed by the first author and refined in consultation with three researchers and three people with lived experience. Following pilot testing with three teachers, minor wording adjustments were made. Data from pilot interviews are not included in the study. After each interview, a methodological report was created (Dierckx de Casterle et al., 2012).

2.2.2. Post-interview questionnaire

In a post interview questionnaire, , participants were first asked if they approved the transcript.

Next, teachers were asked to provide any thoughts they might have had after the interview. Lastly, they were asked to complete the "Signature Strengths Survey" (SSS) (McGrath, 2017). Although originally designed for self-report in adults, the survey was translated from English to Dutch and slightly adapted for use in this study. The SSS contains 24 descriptions of character strengths (Supplementary file 4) defined by Peterson and Seligman (2004) within the Virtues In Action (VIA) framework. For each description, teachers were shown a list of NDCs and were instructed to tick the box of any condition for which they felt the description was "absolutely essential to the condition", "defining of students diagnosed with the condition", or otherwise "part of the condition". They completed this task only for NDCs with which they had experience. Teachers had the opportunity to provide additional information in an open field.

To distinguish interview-derived positive traits from VIA character strengths in this study, the former are shown in single quotation marks (' '), whereas the latter are italicized and capitalized.

2.3. Data analyses

ATLAS.ti 24.0.0 software (ATLAS.ti Scientific Software Development GmbH, 2023) was used to facilitate qualitative data analyses. Several measures were taken to ensure adequate dependability, confirmability, credibility, and transferability including a.o. team debriefings, keeping an audit trial, and multiple member checks. A detailed description of these measures can be found in Supplementary file 5.

2.3.1. Analytical approach

The study adopts an inductive phenomenological thematic approach. The data was coded by two researchers (ADR and JdS). References to positive aspects, strengths, or talents were coded in parallel with separate codes identifying the NDC to which the comment referred. When teachers discussed positive traits in children with multiple NDCs, they were prompted to clarify to which NDCs they related the positive trait. Although challenges and negative aspects were also coded to provide contextual understanding of the data, these findings are not reported as they were outside the aim of the study.

After coding an interview, each coder wrote a narrative report (Dierckx de Casterle et al., 2012).

Both coders then compared and discussed their coding and narrative reports to gain a richer and more complete understanding. Using an iterative process of open, axial, and selective coding, the first author gradually merged similar codes where appropriate and progressively developed and refined overarching themes.

We first examined positive aspects across the entire sample before exploring patterns within specific NDCs. As not all teachers had experience with every condition, the amount of available data varied across conditions. For both the interview and SSS data, a positive trait was considered a recurring finding only when it was reported by more than one teacher. Responses mentioned by a single teacher were retained during analysis but are not reported as recurring themes in the results.

During the analysis, patterns were identified suggestive of underlying mechanisms of strength development and use. As these could not be captured by the coding described above, an additional exploratory thematic analysis was conducted on the same dataset to explore these patterns.

2.3.2. Data triangulation

Researcher triangulation involved two researchers analysing the data independently and discussing any differences. Methodological triangulation involved supplementing interview data with the standardized questionnaire SSS and inviting participants to provide written comments two weeks later to capture reflections or insights that emerged after the interview.

For theory triangulation, the inductively defined codes from the interview data were matched to the existing 24 character strengths from the VIA-framework. Results from the SSS can deepen the interpretation of our thematic results and could also identify character strengths that were not described in the interview. By first coding the data inductively, and only later examining the fit within character strengths defined in the VIA-framework, we enabled the emergence of innovative insights outside the existing VIA-framework.

2.4. Theoretical framework, research team, and reflexivity

>Theoretical framework, research team, and reflexivity

2.4.1. Theoretical framework

This study combines realist ontological assumptions about the neurobiological basis of NDCs with interpretivist and constructivist epistemologies. While we believe NDCs have a neurobiological basis that interacts with the environment (realism), these traits are perceived and understood differently depending on the teachers' social, professional, and cultural contexts and are therefore subjective in nature (interpretivism). This study aims to explore how teachers perceive and understand the positive aspects of NDCs, focusing on their lived experiences within the educational system. In this study, we acknowledge that these perceptions are co-constructed through the dynamic interaction between researcher and participant (constructivism).

2.4.2. Research team and reflexivity

The researcher team acknowledges that their own experiences and biases influence the research process. All interviews were conducted and coded by ADR, a female postdoctoral researcher with a background in paediatric physiotherapy and a research focus on DCD. The second coder, JdS, is a female PhD candidate with a background in clinical developmental psychology. Both coders are trained and experienced (8+ years and 2+ years respectively) in qualitative data analyses. Both have professional and familial experience with NDCs. LS is a female PhD candidate with a focus on strengths in ADHD, and grew up in a neurodivergent family.

Her background is in biomedical and cognitive neurosciences. She is trained and experienced in qualitative research (4+ years). MH is the principal investigator on the project, she has a background in psychology and 20 years of research experience in the field of ADHD.

All participants were unknown to the interviewer with no prior relationships. At the beginning of the interviews, participants were told that we were interested in learning not only about the things that were challenging for these children, but also things they were good at. They were also informed that the interviewer had a background in paediatric physiotherapy.

Secondary findings

While analysing the data, four distinct mechanisms through which positive and negative traits relate to each other were identified. Two mechanisms relate to the possible development of strengths (promotive and compensatory), one concerns a functional role in buffering challenges (protective), and one reflects the bidirectional nature of certain traits, whereby the same characteristic may result in both positive and negative outcomes (double-edged).

3.3.1. Promotive mechanisms

Promotive mechanisms can be understood as strengths that facilitate the development or use of other strengths. They might have the potential to initiate upward spiralling processes, in which initial positive characteristics generate further beneficial outcomes over time.

For example, according to teachers in this study, the strong interest-driven motivation often seen in students with autism may promote the development of advanced academic skills in areas that align with their interests. Similarly, in students with DCD or DLD, openness to seeking help and a sense of 'agency' may foster the development and use of 'gratitude' and promote prosocial behaviour such as helping others ('kindness'): "But they're also very grateful […] I'm thinking back right now to those same students I just mentioned [Pause]. Yes. That-the gratitude and the kindness-is definitely there in all of them, but maybe that's also because […] they realize that they're being helped." (Teacher 7 on students with DLD)

3.3.2. Compensatory mechanisms

Compensatory mechanisms appear to develop from challenges, whereby 'negative' experiences or challenges trigger the emergence of characteristics that are perceived positively by teachers.

For example, because students with DLD often face verbal communication difficulties, they may rely on alternative forms of expression, such as drawing, and develop creative ways of working around these challenges, thereby fostering divergent thinking. Moreover, in students with DCD and SLD, motor and learning difficulties respectively often result in slower progress and increased effort in task completion. Consequently, these students frequently seem to develop high levels of 'perseverance' or a 'positive and adaptive mindset'.

" [The] student with a learning disability might be able to put things into perspective by saying, "Yeah, but that's okay. I make mistakes, too. It's not my fault. We'll work through this together-or we'll figure out a strategy to solve it," because they have experience with that and are better able to put things into perspective." (Teacher 8 on students with SLD)

3.3.3. Double-edged mechanisms

Double-edged mechanisms refer to characteristics that can be interpreted as either positive or negative, depending on contextual factors, the recipient of the behaviour, and the intensity with which the behaviour is expressed.

First, the context in which a behaviour occurs plays a crucial role in how it is evaluated. A behaviour that is appropriate and valued in one situation may be perceived as inappropriate in another. For example, the 'sincerity' often observed in students with autism may be appreciated for its clarity and directness in 'communication'. However, in other situations, this same honesty may be perceived as inappropriate or tactless. Similarly, students with ADHD can draw on their creativity when many ideas are needed, but generating too many ideas may occasionally result in misinterpreting tasks.

"Someone with ADHD is a fountain of ideas about all the things they can do. […] though you do run the risk that they'll 'interpret' the assignment differently-that does happen, too." (Teacher 3 on students with ADHD)

Second, the interpretation of behaviour is influenced by the recipient. Not all individuals respond to the same behaviours in the same way. For instance, the impulsive behaviour in students with ADHD may be perceived as 'humorous' and 'energizing by peers, while teachers may experience it as disruptive or challenging to manage. Likewise, the honesty in students with ADHD an autism can be appreciated by the teacher but not by other students.

"That honesty. Yeah, they're-they sometimes don't even have that social filter yet, you know. Like today, the word 'sturdy' came up. "Miss, what does 'sturdy' mean?" I said, "Well, it's just a nice way of saying fat." "Oh, like you, then!" You know how it is. But for that kid, it was just an observation. […] But I also really appreciate that kids just say things as they are. […] [other kids] often find that really difficult! They think-they think these kids are too blunt or too rude." (Teacher 13 about students with autism)

Finally, the intensity of a behaviour further determines whether it is experienced as positive or negative. For example, students with SLD are often praised for their perseverance, yet excessive perseverance may have also been perceived as perfectionism by our participants. Likewise, students with DCD were often commended for seeking help when needed, yet frequent requests for support were sometimes viewed less positively when they exceed teachers' capacity to provide assistance.

"Yeah, the thing is, those kinds of people do tend to act a bit demanding toward the teacher sometimes.

Like they want to be kept "under your wing" […]." (Teacher 10 about students with DCD)

3.3.4. Protective mechanisms

Protective mechanisms refer to strengths that buffer or mitigate the impact of negative characteristics. This buffering effect can occur within the same condition, but may also be observed across co-occurring neurodevelopmental conditions, where a strength associated with one condition lightens challenges related to another.

For example, in students with ADHD, helpfulness and a tendency to take initiative may provide a constructive outlet for their high levels of activity, thereby reducing 'disruptive' expressions of hyperactivity.

They're often eager to offer help. […] But those are just little things that let them get up and move around a bit, while at the same time actually helping out with something. So they often get that-there are always exceptions, of course-but it often becomes second nature to them. They're taught from the start that "Ah, but if I want to help and I offer to, well, then I'm also-then I get to move around a bit. And I know that helps me not to stay sitting in that little chair for 50 minutes. (Teacher 11 on students with ADHD)

4. Discussion

In this study we explored whether teachers (n = 16) perceive positive traits in 8-16 year old students with ADHD, autism, DCD, DLD, or SLD and examined similarities and differences in positive traits across these conditions. Teachers reported 22 positive traits relating to cognitive skills, self-regulation and efficacy, social and interpersonal skills, practical skills, and energy trait. Across interview and survey data, most traits were shared across conditions, although some patterns were apparent. Social-energetic qualities appeared particularly salient in descriptions of students with ADHD, whereas cognitive strengths seemed especially prominent in descriptions of students with autism. Students with DCD and SLD, and to a lesser extent those with DLD, were described primarily in terms of interpersonal, self-regulatory and practical skills.

Teachers appeared to report a broader range of positive aspects in students with ADHD and autism than in students with DCD, DLD, or SLD. Exploratory analyses suggested four distinct mechanisms for strengths development and use.

4.1. Differences in positive traits across NDCs

Most positive traits reported by teachers were shared across conditions. However, some NDCs elicited a broader range of positive trait descriptions than others. Several factors may contribute to this variation, including experience with students with a given NDC, the visibility of NDC characteristics in the classroom, the extent of professional knowledge and awareness, the prominence of strength-based discourse surrounding particular NDCs, and the nature of the conditions themselves.

While most teachers had experience with ADHD, autism, and SLD, both ADHD and autism elicited more positive traits and endorsed character strengths than SLD. Therefore, teachers' experience with different NDCs is unlikely to fully explain the observed differences. However, the more limited experience with DCD and DLD may partly account for the narrower range of strengths reported for these conditions.

As both ADHD and autism are often characterised by more externalizing behaviour, students with these conditions were reportedly more 'visible' to teachers. In contrast, students with DCD, DLD, and SLD may be more likely to go unnoticed (Chepngeno et al., 2025; Rivard et al., 2007; Thompson et al., 2025), potentially limiting teacher's opportunities to recognize and describe their positive traits. Although ADHD and autism are also often considered 'invisible' conditions in the classroom, particularly when difficulties are masked or presented less stereotypically (Lynch & Davison, 2022; Putnam et al., 2025), the present findings may suggest that DCD, DLD, and SLD may be even less salient to teachers in everyday classroom interactions.

Students with DCD and DLD are often missed and underdiagnosed due to limited awareness and knowledge of these conditions (Klein et al., 2024; McGregor, 2020). This was also evident in the fact that only half of the teachers had experience with these conditions as compared to almost all teachers being experienced with the evenly prevalent ADHD and SLD. Hypothetically, greater awareness and understanding may provide teachers with richer frameworks for interpreting both challenges and strengths within a specific NDC. Consequently, strengths may be more readily identified in ADHD, autism, and SLD, than in DCD and DLD, where challenges may be more likely to dominate teacher's perceptions. This interpretation aligns with previous research reporting that without NDC knowledge, teachers often misinterpreted behaviour in negative ways (Dangmann et al., 2024).

There is also a relative lack of strengths-based narratives around DCD and DLD. Consequently, teachers may be less likely to describe and interpret positive characteristics in DCD or DLD than they are for conditions such as ADHD or autism. Strengths-based perspectives have received considerably more attention in the ADHD and autism literature, potentially making teachers more aware of, and attuned to, positive characteristics associated with these conditions.

However, this explanation does not fully account for the findings, as strengths-based discussions have long been present in the SLD literature, despite a comparatively smaller range of positive aspects being identified for SLD in the current study.

Some positive traits attributed to students with ADHD and autism appeared to reflect manifestations of core diagnostic characteristics. Traits such as high energy, enthusiasm, drive, and daringness may reflect hyperactivity and impulsivity in ADHD, while extensive knowledge, precision, calmness, and clear direct communication in autism may be linked to restricted interests and distinctive social-communication profiles. Several of these traits were uniquely associated with these conditions: 'high energy and enthusiasm' and Zest were reported only for ADHD, which was also the case in a study comparing parental perception of strengths in children with ADHD and autism (Schippers et al., 2026), whereas 'precision', 'intelligence and knowledge', and 'calmness' were reported only in students with autism in our study. Again, precision seemed more prominent in parent-reports in children with autism (Schippers et al., 2026). Comparable strength-based expressions of diagnostic features were less evident for DCD, DLD, and SLD.

4.2. Conceptual framework of strengths development and use

The dual nature of traits was also reflected in our framework for strengths development and use.

To our knowledge, this is the first conceptual framework describing how strengths may develop and be used in individuals with NDCs. While exploratory and requiring empirical validation, its proposed mechanisms are consistent with qualitative reports in previous studies.

Both adult autism and ADHD literature describe traits with dual expressions, such as impulsivity versus spontaneity in ADHD (Schippers et al., 2022) and rigidity versus above-average work ethic in autism (Bury et al., 2020). While ADHD research has primarily emphasized the role of context in shaping these outcomes (Nordby et al., 2023), autism research has additionally highlighted the importance of trait intensity and other's perceptions (Russell et al., 2019). This aligns with our findings, which suggest that whether a trait is experienced as positive or negative depends on the context, the recipient, and the intensity with which is it expressed. Our results extend findings from ADHD and autism adult research by suggesting that these mechanisms may also operate in children and across a broader range of NDCs.

Beyond such double-edged mechanisms, descriptions of promotive mechanisms are also evident in literature. For example, self-knowledge has been linked to resilience in young adults with ADHD (Dangmann et al., 2024), and helpfulness in adolescents with DCD (Khairati et al., 2024), while hyperfocus has been described as a source of creativity among neurodivergent individuals (Erin et al., 2026). By contrast, protective mechanisms, whereby a positive trait buffers the impact of an NDC-specific vulnerability or challenge directly related to the condition itself ('symptoms' of the NDC), are less explicitly described in the strengths literature. Instead, the available evidence primarily suggests that strengths may buffer against secondary consequences of NDCs. For example, recognizing and leveraging strengths may help reduce the negative impact of societal stigma among neurodivergent individuals (Erin et al., 2026) and has been associated with increased well-being (Hargitai et al., 2025), while prosocial behavior has been suggested to alleviate some difficulties experienced by adolescents with SLD or ADHD (Hysaj et al., 2025; Piko & Dudok, 2023). Thus, existing findings largely concern protection against adverse outcomes associated with NDCs rather than modification of their core characteristics.

Arguably, this focus aligns with neurodiversity perspectives, which emphasize supporting wellbeing, participation, and adaptation rather than conceptualizing neurodevelopmental differences as deficits that should necessarily be reduced.

Next, descriptions of compensatory mechanisms are widespread in literature. For example, persistent motor difficulties fostered the development of perseverance in adolescents with DCD (Khairati et al., 2024), while individuals with DLD have described becoming skilled at reading body language to compensate for language difficulties (Burnley et al., 2024). Likewise, communication difficulties have been linked to the development of creative forms of selfexpression in neurodivergent people (Erin et al., 2026).

The positive traits resulting from the compensatory mechanisms identified in this study were interpreted by teachers as adaptive strategies that help students navigate challenges associated with their NDC. However, from a lived-experience perspective, some of these behaviours may instead reflect masking, whereby children adopt learned behaviours to gain acceptance or meet societal expectations. Given that masking has been documented across all NDCs included in this study (Elsherif, 2025; Ford, 2027; Wilmot et al., 2024; Wurth et al., 2025), it is important to consider that some behaviours perceived as positive may reflect adaptation to neurotypical expectations rather than strengths experienced as such by the child. For example, teachers in our study identified self-confidence as a positive trait in students with ADHD and DLD, despite evidence that these groups often report lower self-confidence (Burnley et al., 2024; Massoodi et al., 2024). Students experiencing insecurity may use humour or adopt the role of the "class clown" to gain social acceptance (Gronneberg et al., 2023). Such behaviour may therefore be interpreted by teachers as confident, while serving as a coping response to underlying insecurity.

Therefore, future research should incorporate the perspectives of students with lived experience to better understand how these behaviours are experienced and interpreted.

4.3. Comparison with existing strength literature

The results in our study both confirm and extend the positive traits reported by teachers in previous studies. In children with ADHD, we could confirm teacher-reported strengths in divergent thinking, humor, social relationships, drive, positive and adaptive mindset, creative expression, strong motor skills, and high energy and enthusiasm (Krtek et al., 2022; McDougal et al., 2023; Ward et al., 2021), while in children with autism we could confirm strengths in intelligence and knowledge, kindness, social relationships, strong work-ethic, and academic skills (Mirenda et al., 2024). In children with DLD, we replicated teacher-reported strengths in social relationships, perseverance, creative expression and motor skills (Dockrell & Lindsay, 2001).

Not all previously teacher-reported positive traits were replicated in our study, including being hardworking in ADHD (McDougal et al., 2023), perseverance in autism (Mirenda et al., 2024), and a positive attitude, intelligence, and strong academic skills in DLD (Dockrell & Lindsay, 2001).

Given the limited evidence on teacher-reported positive traits and the heterogeneity in students with NDCs, some inconsistency across studies is unsurprising. Importantly, our results provide the first descriptions of teacher-reported positive traits in children with DCD and SLD.

Many of the positive traits identified by teachers in our study are consistent with those reported by parents and, where available, by children themselves. For example, high energy and enthusiasm have been described both by parents (Schippers et al., 2026) and by children with ADHD (Jones & Hesse, 2018; Ling & Diamond, 2025). Similarly, perseverance in children with DCD has been reported by parents (De Roubaix et al., 2025; Mandich et al., 2003; Tal-Saban & Weintraub, 2023) as well as by children with DCD (Khairati et al., 2024; Kirby et al., 2008). These parallels suggest that at least some strengths are recognised consistently across different informants and contexts.

At the same time, some discrepancies emerged between teacher and parent perspectives. A positive and adaptive mindset, a trait closely related to resilience, was identified by teachers of students with ADHD and SLDs but was largely absent from teacher reports concerning students with DCD, SLD, and autism. This contrasts with parent reports, in which resilience-related traits are frequently described in children with DLD (Burnley et al., 2024; Leitao et al., 2025; St Clair et al., 2023) and DCD (De Roubaix et al., 2025). One possible explanation is that teachers may be less aware of the considerable effort students invest in managing everyday challenges. This interpretation is consistent with parental accounts describing frustration that others often fail to recognize the difficulties underlying children's day-to-day functioning (De Roubaix et al., 2025; Leitao et al., 2025).

Conversely, some strengths identified by teachers may be particularly relevant in school settings and therefore less likely to emerge in parent reports. One example is agency, defined as recognising when support is needed and actively seeking, accepting, and using that support. This characteristic may be especially relevant in classrooms, where children are expected to manage learning demands more independently, whereas parents often coordinate and provide support at home. Consistent with this interpretation, agency or help-seeking is rarely highlighted in parental reports of strengths, with the study by McGregor et al. (2023) in children with DLD being an exception.

4.4. Clinical and research implications

Although this study reports positive traits identified by teachers within specific diagnostic groups, these findings should not be interpreted as defining condition-specific strength profiles.

Attempting to establish universal strengths-based equivalents of diagnostic criteria is neither feasible nor desirable, as it risks overlooking individual differences and reducing positive traits to another set of expectations against which children are evaluated. Rather, the findings demonstrate that students with NDCs, who are often characterised primarily in terms of their challenges, are recognised by teachers as possessing a wide range of positive characteristics within the classroom context. Therefore, the value of identifying these strengths lies not in creating checklists of desirable traits, but in broadening our understanding of children with NDCs and supporting more balanced, individualised perspectives that acknowledge both strengths and challenges.

During the final member-checking process, teachers suggested that the findings could help teachers adopt a more balanced view of students with NDCs by recognising their strengths alongside their challenges. Such a shift may be important given that teacher expectations influence students' educational experiences and outcomes (Gentrup et al., 2020). While diagnostic labels may, at times, contribute to lower expectations (Morte-Soriano et al., 2021), a strengths-based perspective may help counteract negative self-fulfilling prophecies and instead foster positive self-fulfilling prophecies, whereby teachers' expectations support the development of students' strengths. Supporting this possibility, teacher strength-spotting interventions have shown promising results (Quinlan et al., 2018).

The findings highlight that strengths are varying, dynamic, and shaped by context. Further research is needed to understand the factors that influence the expression, recognition, and development of strengths in students with NDCs. Additional analyses from the present study are underway to explore contextual influences. Future studies would also benefit from integrating the perspectives of children with lived experience, parents, teachers, and other stakeholders.

Triangulating these perspectives is essential for understanding not only which positive traits are recognised across contexts, but also whether these traits represent authentic strengths that support well-being and participation, or whether some may reflect masking behaviours that come at a personal cost to the child.

4.5. Strengths and limitations

The present study examined strengths across five neurodevelopmental conditions that frequently co-occur, providing a more ecologically valid perspective than studies focusing on a single diagnostic group. Moreover, it contributes to a limited body of strengths-based research on students with NDCs. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to explore teacherreported strengths in students with DCD and SLD, while also extending the evidence base for ADHD, autism, and DLD. Finally, the study incorporated the perspectives of individuals with lived experience in both the development of the interview guide and the interpretation of the findings, enhancing the credibility of the research.

The study focused exclusively on teachers' perspectives and therefore cannot provide insight into how strengths may be recognised or experienced by children themselves, parents, or other stakeholders. In addition, teachers varied in their familiarity with the different NDCs, which may have influenced the breadth and specificity with which positive traits were associated with some NDCs. At the same time, this pattern likely reflects the comparatively lower levels of awareness and recognition of DLD and DCD within educational settings.

5. Conclusion

In conclusion, teachers identified a wide range of positive traits in students with NDCs in their classrooms. Most of these strengths were reported across conditions, although some appeared to be more specific to ADHD and autism. The identified strengths clustered within five domains: cognitive skills, self-regulation and efficacy, social and interpersonal skills, practical skills, and energy-related traits. Moreover, a conceptual framework of strengths development and use is proposed. By adding to the limited literature on strengths in children with NDCs, this study contributes to a more balanced understanding of neurodevelopmental differences and may inform the development of strengths-based approaches in children with NDCs.
