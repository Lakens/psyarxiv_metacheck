Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

research_questions RQ1: Do accuracy and precision of eye tracking data differ between a webcam-based eye tracking system (Labvanced) and an infrared (IR) eye tracking system (Tobii Pro Spectrum), and do key eye tracking metrics derived from both systems differ systematically?

H1a: The IR system yields higher spatial accuracy than the webcam-based system, i.e., a smaller mean angular offset (in degrees of visual angle) between recorded gaze positions and target positions.

H1b: The IR system yields higher spatial precision than the webcam-based system, i.e., a smaller sample-to-sample root mean square deviation (RMS, in degrees of visual angle).

H1c: Despite the lower data quality of the webcam-based system, key eye tracking metrics derived from the classroom video vignettes (number of fixations, mean fixation duration, mean saccade length, and number of AOI hits on predefined student and teacher AOIs) differ only marginally between the two systems. Specifically, we expect the differences between systems to lie within equivalence bounds of d = ±0.30.

RQ2: Does the recording environment (laboratory vs. home) affect the data quality of webcam-based eye tracking?

H2a: Webcam-based recordings conducted at home show lower accuracy and lower precision than webcam-based recordings conducted in the laboratory.

H2b: Webcam-based recordings conducted at home show greater between-participant variability in eye tracking metrics (number of fixations, mean fixation duration, mean saccade length, number of AOI hits) than webcam-based recordings conducted in the laboratory.

H2c: The difference in data quality between the webcam-based and the IR system is larger for participants who completed the webcam recording at home than for participants who completed both recordings in the laboratory (interaction of eye tracking system × recording environment).

stimuli_description The main stimuli consist of five short video vignettes (approximately one minute each) showing authentic whole-class teaching situations from a secondary school mathematics lesson, in which a teacher teaches a class of 24 students about matrix computation. The vignettes were recorded in a previous DFG-funded project and have been used in several earlier eye tracking studies on teachers’ visual expertise, in which participants rated them as authentic and realistic. For these vignettes, areas of interest (AOIs) for individual students, the teacher, and the learning materials had already been defined in prior work. The same AOI definitions are used for both eye tracking systems. All vignettes were presented full screen, in identical form, and in the same sequence in both recording sessions. Participants were instructed to watch each video carefully as if they were observing a real classroom; no responses were required during viewing. In addition, participants completed a static graph interpretation task, which is analyzed exploratively and is not part of the confirmatory hypotheses. For the assessment of accuracy and precision, gaze data recorded while participants fixated known target positions during the system-specific calibration and validation procedures are used.

(2) Web-based eye tracking: Labvanced webcam-based eye tracking (Scicovery GmbH, Osnabrück, Germany), running in the Google Chrome browser. In the home condition, the participants’ own laptop or desktop webcams were used; in the laboratory condition, the webcam session took place on a laboratory computer. Gaze is estimated in real time from the webcam video stream; no video recordings of participants are stored. The effective sampling frequency of the webcam-based system depends on the webcam and computer hardware and is therefore variable across participants (typically around 30 Hz); the actual sampling frequency per recording will be reported.

Labvanced uses a virtual chin rest procedure that detects the participant’s head position and prompts the participant to reposition their head when it deviates from the calibrated position.

Variability in lighting is one of the factors expected to contribute to differences between home and laboratory recordings (RQ2).

calibration_points Both systems used a 9-point calibration, with calibration targets arranged in a 3 × 3 grid covering the screen (corners, edge midpoints, and center). In the infrared condition, the calibration was conducted in Tobii Pro Lab. In the webcam conditions, the built-in Labvanced calibration procedure was used, preceded by the Labvanced head position check. In the laboratory, the experimenter inspected the calibration results and repeated the calibration if it was insufficient; in the home condition, Labvanced prompted participants to repeat the calibration if it failed. area_of_interest_design AOIs are defined on the classroom video vignettes and were created in prior work with the same video material (Pappa et al., 2020). Each student visible in the classroom (24 students in total) is covered by an individual AOI. As the primary AOI type, we use rectangular student-area AOIs that enclose each student’s upper body, head, and desk area, since students are the central objects of attention in research on teachers’ visual expertise. Additional AOIs cover the teacher and the learning materials (e.g., blackboard). Because the students and the teacher move during the videos, AOIs are dynamic, i.e., their position and size are adjusted over time to follow the respective person. All remaining screen areas are treated as non-AOI area. Identical AOI definitions are applied to the gaze data of both eye tracking systems. AOIs for the graph interpretation task are not part of the confirmatory analyses.
size_of_aois Due to the perspective of the classroom camera, AOI sizes vary systematically with seating position: students in the front rows appear larger and are covered by larger AOIs than students in the back rows. The AOIs are comparatively large relative to the expected spatial error of both systems, which is the basis for our expectation in H1c that metrics based on these AOIs are robust to lower accuracy. The size of each AOI (mean, minimum, and maximum across the duration of each vignette) will be reported both in pixels and in degrees of visual angle for the laboratory setup.
distance_between_and_placement_of_aois Students are seated in rows and are therefore placed close to each other, so that neighboring student AOIs are directly adjacent and partly separated only by small gaps; in the back rows, student AOIs can partly overlap due to the camera perspective. AOIs are drawn so that they do not overlap; where students occlude each other, the boundary is drawn along the visible outline of the student in front. The teacher AOI is located at the front of the classroom and the AOIs for learning materials are located at the blackboard. Because of the small distances between neighboring student AOIs, inaccurate gaze data can lead to AOI hits being assigned to a neighboring student. To quantify this, we will additionally report, as a sensitivity analysis, AOI hits on the level of seating rows and classroom areas (front, middle, back) in addition to individual student AOIs.

Assignment was made by the research team before the first session; participants had no influence on their group assignment or the order of the sessions.

data_collection_procedures The target population consists of pre-service teachers, students of education-related study programs, and in-service teachers. sample_size_rationale The target sample size was determined by an a priori power analysis with G*Power 3.1.9.7 (Faul et al., 2007) for a repeated measures ANOVA with the within-subjects factor eye tracking system and the between-subjects factor recording environment, assuming a small-to-moderate effect size of f = 0.20, α = .05, and a power of 1 − β = .95. This analysis yielded a required sample size of 84 participants. The sample size was additionally constrained by the project budget (participant compensation for 84 participants). For the equivalence test of H1c, a sensitivity analysis shows that with N = 84 and equivalence bounds of d = ±0.30, the power to establish equivalence is approximately .73 if the true difference between the systems is zero. For the comparison of variances between the two recording environment groups (H2b), power is limited by the group sizes of about 42 participants each. We will therefore interpret non-significant results for these hypotheses with caution and report confidence intervals for all effect sizes.
stopping_rule Before data collection started, the experimental procedure was piloted internally with four test runs (two webcam-based and two infrared sessions) to test the technical setup, the calibration procedures, and the instructions. Data collection began after the final adjustments based on this pilot. Pilot data are not included in the analyses. Data collection was terminated once the target sample size of 84 participants had been reached; sessions that had already been scheduled at that point were still conducted, resulting in a final sample of 85 participants. All 85 participants will be included in the analyses, subject to the preregistered exclusion criteria. The number of participants was not adjusted on the basis of any interim inspection of the data.
measured_variables Independent variables are the eye tracking system (webcam-based vs. infrared; within subjects) and the recording environment (laboratory vs. home; between subjects). The following variables are measured.

Data quality measures (primary outcomes for H1a, H1b, H2a):
(1) Accuracy: the mean angular distance (in degrees of visual angle) between the recorded gaze position and the position of known target points, computed from the gaze samples recorded while participants fixated the validation targets. Lower values indicate higher accuracy, i.e., a smaller systematic offset between recorded and actual gaze position.
(2) Precision: the root mean square of the angular distances between successive gaze samples (RMS sample-to-sample, in degrees of visual angle) during the fixation of the validation targets. Lower values indicate higher precision, i.e., less noise in the recorded gaze signal.
(3) Data loss: the proportion of samples without valid gaze data during the presentation of the video vignettes. Data loss is used to describe data quality and as an exclusion criterion.
(4) Effective sampling frequency: the mean number of recorded gaze samples per second. This is reported descriptively, since it is fixed for the infrared system and varies across participants for the webcam-based system.

Eye tracking metrics derived from the video vignettes (outcomes for H1c and H2b), each computed per participant and system and aggregated across the five vignettes:
(5) Number of fixations: the total number of fixations per vignette, indicating how often gaze pauses on parts of the scene.
(6) Mean fixation duration (in milliseconds): the average duration of fixations, commonly interpreted as an indicator of the depth of processing of the fixated information.
(7) Mean saccade length (in degrees of visual angle): the average distance between consecutive fixations, indicating how far the gaze jumps while scanning the classroom.
(8) Number of AOI hits: the number of fixations landing within the student, teacher, and learning material AOIs, indicating which parts of the classroom receive attention. AOI hits are computed per AOI and summed across student AOIs.

Questionnaire variables: demographics (age, gender, study program or profession, study phase or years of teaching experience), experience with digital tools, perceived usability of the recording procedure, perceived authenticity of the classroom videos, and, for the home condition, feedback on technical problems during the session. These variables are used descriptively and in exploratory analyses.

Graph interpretation task: gaze data recorded during the graph interpretation task are analyzed exploratively and are not part of the confirmatory hypotheses.
indices All derived variables are computed per participant and per eye tracking system.

Conversion to visual angle: Distances on the screen are converted from pixels into degrees of visual angle with θ = 2 · arctan(s / (2 · D)) · 180/π, where s is the distance on the screen in cm (pixel distance multiplied by the physical pixel size) and D is the viewing distance in cm.

Accuracy: For each validation target j, accuracy is computed as the mean angular distance between the n gaze samples recorded during the fixation of the target and the target position: A_j = (1/n) · Σ θ_i, where θ_i is the angular distance between gaze sample i and the target. The accuracy score of a participant is the mean of A_j across all validation targets.

Precision: For each validation target j, precision is computed as the root mean square of the angular distances between successive gaze samples: RMS_j = √[(1/(n − 1)) · Σ θ_(i,i+1)²], where θ_(i,i+1) is the angular distance between samples i and i+1. The precision score of a participant is the mean of RMS_j across all validation targets.

Data loss: For each vignette, data loss is the proportion of the vignette duration that is not covered by valid gaze data. It is computed as the summed duration of all gaps longer than 75 ms (intervals between consecutive valid samples, including the intervals before the first and after the last valid sample) divided by the vignette duration. The data loss score of a participant is the mean across vignettes.

Effective sampling frequency: f = (n_samples − 1) / (t_last − t_first), computed per vignette from the timestamps of the recorded samples (in seconds) and averaged across vignettes.

Eye tracking metrics: For each vignette, the number of fixations, the mean fixation duration, the mean saccade length, and the number of AOI hits are computed. The number of AOI hits is the number of fixations whose position falls within any of the defined AOIs (students, teacher, learning materials); AOI hits per individual AOI and per AOI category are reported descriptively. For each metric, the participant’s score is the mean across all valid vignettes of the respective session.

Difference scores: For H2b and for the Bland-Altman analyses, a difference score is computed per participant and metric as Δ = metric(webcam-based) − metric(infrared). The standardized paired difference used for the equivalence tests in H1c is d_z = mean(Δ) / SD(Δ).

Questionnaire scales: Scale scores for usability, experience with digital tools, and perceived authenticity are computed as the mean of the respective items after reverse-coding negatively worded items. Scale scores are only computed for participants who answered at least 80% of the items of a scale.
eye_tracking_processing_algorithm Raw gaze data (timestamps and x/y screen coordinates) of both systems are exported and processed with the same custom Python pipeline. Fixations are detected with a dispersion-threshold identification algorithm (I-DT; Salvucci & Goldberg, 2000), which is suitable for both high-frequency infrared data and low-frequency, irregularly sampled webcam data. A fixation is identified when consecutive gaze samples stay within a maximum dispersion of 1.0° of visual angle (dispersion computed as the sum of the horizontal and vertical range) for at least the minimal fixation duration. Fixation position is defined as the mean position of all samples belonging to the fixation. Adjacent fixations are not merged. Saccades are defined as the transitions between consecutive fixations, and saccade length is computed as the angular distance between the positions of consecutive fixations. Fixations are assigned to an AOI if the fixation position lies within the AOI in the video frame displayed at the onset of the fixation. Pixel distances are converted into degrees of visual angle based on the physical pixel size and the viewing distance (65 cm in the laboratory; for the home condition, an assumed viewing distance of 65 cm combined with the screen properties reported by the browser). The same algorithm and parameters are applied to the data of both systems and both recording environments. As a sensitivity analysis, all confirmatory analyses for H1c are repeated after downsampling the infrared data to the mean effective sampling frequency of the webcam-based recordings, in order to separate effects of spatial data quality from effects of sampling frequency. Accuracy and precision are computed from the raw, unprocessed gaze samples recorded during the validation procedures.
minimal_fixation_duration The minimal fixation duration is 100 ms for both systems. At the typical sampling frequency of the webcam-based system of about 30 Hz, this corresponds to at least three consecutive gaze samples. Candidate fixations shorter than 100 ms are discarded and not counted as fixations.
upper_velocity_threshold Not applicable for the confirmatory analyses, because fixations are detected with a dispersion-based algorithm (I-DT) rather than a velocity-based algorithm. Velocity-based algorithms are not used because they cannot be applied reliably to the low and irregular sampling frequency of the webcam-based system. For comparability with prior work using the same video vignettes, the infrared data will additionally be processed exploratively with a velocity-threshold algorithm (I-VT) with a velocity threshold of 30°/s.
gap_fill_in_functions_or_noise_reduction_algorithms Gaps in the gaze data of both systems with a duration of up to 75 ms (e.g., caused by brief signal loss) are filled by linear interpolation between the last valid sample before and the first valid sample after the gap. Longer gaps are not interpolated and are treated as missing data. No additional noise reduction or smoothing filter is applied to the data of either system, because noise is itself an outcome of this study (precision) and smoothing would affect the two systems differently. Any filtering that is applied internally by the recording software of the respective system cannot be switched off and is part of the system comparison. Accuracy and precision are computed from the raw data before gap filling.

statistical_tests All analyses are conducted in R. For each participant, all outcome variables are computed per system and aggregated (averaged or, for counts, summed and divided by the number of valid vignettes) across the five video vignettes. Alpha is set to .05. Within each hypothesis, the multiple outcome variables are treated as one family, and p-values are corrected with the Holm-Bonferroni procedure within each family. Directional hypotheses are tested one-sided.

H1a and H1b (accuracy and precision): For each of the two outcomes, a 2 × 2 mixed ANOVA is conducted with eye tracking system (webcam-based vs. infrared) as within-subjects factor and recording environment (laboratory vs. home) as between-subjects factor. H1a and H1b are supported if the main effect of system is significant and the infrared system shows lower values (i.e., better accuracy and precision) than the webcam-based system. Effect sizes are reported as partial η² and as Cohen’s d_z for the paired difference, each with 95% confidence intervals.

H1c (eye tracking metrics): For each of the four metrics (number of fixations, mean fixation duration, mean saccade length, number of AOI hits), equivalence between the systems is tested with a paired two one-sided tests procedure (TOST) with equivalence bounds of d_z = ±0.30. Equivalence is concluded for a metric if both one-sided tests are significant after Holm-Bonferroni correction across the four metrics, i.e., if the 90% confidence interval of the standardized difference lies entirely within the bounds. H1c is fully supported if equivalence is established for all four metrics and partially supported if it is established for some of them. In addition, conventional paired t-tests are reported for each metric. As complementary measures of agreement between the systems, we report Bland-Altman analyses (mean difference and 95% limits of agreement) and intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC(2,1), absolute agreement) for each metric. The sensitivity analysis with downsampled infrared data repeats the TOST procedure.

H2a (data quality of the webcam system by environment): Accuracy and precision of the webcam-based recordings are compared between the home group and the laboratory group with one-sided Welch t-tests. H2a is supported if the home group shows significantly higher (i.e., worse) values than the laboratory group.

H2b (variability by environment): To separate variability caused by the recording environment from individual differences in gaze behavior, difference scores (webcam-based minus infrared) are computed for each of the four eye tracking metrics per participant. The variances of these difference scores are compared between the home group and the laboratory group with Brown-Forsythe tests (Levene’s test based on the median). H2b is supported for a metric if the variance is significantly larger in the home group after Holm-Bonferroni correction across the four metrics. In addition, the variances of the raw webcam-based metrics are compared between the groups in the same way.

H2c (interaction): H2c is tested by the system × environment interaction in the mixed ANOVAs on accuracy and precision described for H1a and H1b. H2c is supported if the interaction is significant and the difference between the systems is larger in the home group than in the laboratory group. Significant interactions are followed up with simple effects tests of system within each group (Holm-Bonferroni corrected).

Assumption checks and robustness: Normality of residuals is checked with Q-Q plots and skewness and kurtosis values. Because accuracy and precision values are typically right-skewed, robust alternatives are used if skewness exceeds |2| or kurtosis exceeds 7: in this case, the mixed ANOVA is replaced by a robust mixed ANOVA on 20% trimmed means (bwtrim, WRS2 package), and the t-tests are replaced by Yuen’s trimmed-means tests. Sphericity is not an issue because the within-subjects factor has only two levels. Homogeneity of variances between groups is not assumed for H2a (Welch tests). As a robustness check, all models for H1a, H1b, and H2c are repeated with session order (webcam first vs. infrared first) as an additional between-subjects factor; if order effects are significant, they will be reported and discussed.
data_exclusion_criteria Participant level: Participants are excluded from all analyses if they did not meet the inclusion criteria, or if they withdrew their consent. Participants are excluded from the analyses of a given system if the recording of that session was not saved due to a technical failure, if the calibration could not be completed, or, in the home condition, if the session was completed on a tablet or smartphone. Participants with valid data from only one session are retained for analyses that only require data from that session (e.g., H2a for webcam data) and are excluded from all paired comparisons between the systems.

Vignette level: A vignette is excluded from a participant’s session if data loss during that vignette exceeds 50%. For the paired comparisons of eye tracking metrics (H1c, H2b), only vignettes that are valid in both sessions of the participant are used, so that both systems are compared on the same stimulus material. Participants with fewer than three vignettes valid in both sessions are excluded from these analyses.

Data quality measures: Because accuracy and precision are outcome variables, no participant is excluded because of poor accuracy or precision values. Accuracy and precision are only treated as missing if valid gaze samples are available for fewer than five of the nine validation targets.

Outliers: No values are excluded as outliers, since extreme values in data quality are an expected and substantively relevant characteristic of webcam-based eye tracking. The influence of extreme values is handled by the robust procedures described in the statistical models (trimmed means if distributional criteria are violated). As a sensitivity analysis, all confirmatory analyses are repeated with a stricter vignette-level criterion (data loss above 25%).

The number of excluded participants and vignettes is reported separately for each system and recording environment, as the exclusion rates themselves provide information on the practical usability of webcam-based eye tracking.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. Introduction

Digital learning environments increasingly use process data to describe, predict and support learning. Eye tracking is a particularly direct process measure because it shows which parts of a learning material learners attend to. In research on graphs, gaze data have been used to predict whether learners will solve an item correctly (Authors, 2022; Authors, 2020b; Authors, 2024), to detect difficulties early enough for individual assistance (Authors, 2025b) and to prepare adaptive learning systems (Authors, 2025a). Gaze-reactive tutoring systems can improve learning (D'Mello et al., 2012), and multimodal learning analytics use gaze to model learning outcomes, task performance and engagement (Sharma & Giannakos, 2020). Webcam-based eye tracking, which estimates gaze in the learner's browser without special hardware, could turn these approaches into an infrastructure for learning research at scale, for example for online studies with large samples, attention tracking in online courses (Robal et al., 2018) or the detection of mind wandering during online reading (Hutt et al., 2024). This study asks what webcam-based eye tracking can and cannot do for such research, using graph interpretation as a demanding test case.

Graphs are central representations in science, mathematics and economics, and interpreting them is a well-known difficulty for learners, particularly when a question requires the area under a curve rather than its slope (Susac et al., 2018; Authors, 2019). Eye tracking has become a standard tool to study how learners process such items (for reviews, see Hahn & Klein, 2022; Strohmaier et al., 2020). Studies divide an item into areas of interest (AOIs), such as the graph line, the area under the line or the answer options, and often also divide the graph itself, for example into the axes and their labels (Authors, 2019) or into solution-relevant and irrelevant regions (Authors, 2022). Dwell on these AOIs has been compared between learners who answer correctly and incorrectly (Madsen et al., 2012), between learner groups (Susac et al., 2018; Authors, 2019; Authors, 2024) and between answer options (Authors, 2020a), and gaze transitions between AOIs have been used to confirm the objectives of test items (Authors, 2021). A review of 32 expert-novice studies found that most analyzed dwell time, fixation duration and fixation count on AOIs at the macro, meso and micro level of a graph, and that experts attended more to relevant and less to irrelevant parts of the graph (Authors, 2023). These studies used infrared (IR) eye trackers in the laboratory and usually included a few dozen participants. Such analyses depend on fine-grained AOIs within the graph, and this is what an online method would have to deliver.

Webcam-based eye tracking estimates gaze from the video stream of a standard webcam in the participant's browser and could make such studies possible online, with larger and more diverse samples (Semmelmann & Weigelt, 2018). Most studies have used the open-source library WebGazer (Papoutsaki et al., 2016); others have used commercial systems such as Labvanced (Kaduk et al., 2024). Webcam data are noisier and less accurate than IR data, and effects shrink when experiments move online (Kaduk et al., 2024; Slim et al., 2024; Van der Cruyssen et al., 2024; Yang & Krajbich, 2021). Webcam-based eye tracking has nonetheless replicated effects in visual world studies of language processing (Prystauka et al., 2024; Slim & Hartsuiker, 2023) and in looking paradigms with infants (Bánki et al., 2022; Steffan et al., 2024), but these paradigms mostly compare a few large regions, such as the quadrants of the screen. Van der Cruyssen et al. (2024) accordingly concluded that online webcam studies are feasible for relatively large effects and a small number of large regions of interest. Many applications in learning research require the opposite: they concern specific parts of a representation and often the individual learner. Graph items, which are static, text-heavy and contain several small regions in close proximity, therefore provide a demanding test case.

Accuracy, Precision and AOI Measures

Data quality is commonly described by accuracy, the systematic offset between measured and true gaze position, and precision, the variability of repeated measurements of the same gaze position (Holmqvist et al., 2012). The two affect AOI measures differently. Random noise mainly blurs gaze near AOI borders. A systematic offset shifts all samples of a participant in the same direction and can move gaze from one AOI into a neighboring one (Hessels et al., 2016; Orquin et al., 2016). If offsets differ between participants and persist across items, they also add person-specific variance that looks like stable individual differences in viewing. For IR eye trackers, these consequences are well known: the expected offset sets a lower bound for the size of AOIs, and Orquin and Holmqvist (2018) recommend AOIs of at least 3.2° even for a high-end laboratory system.

For webcam-based eye tracking, these consequences have rarely been examined. Validation studies typically report accuracy and precision averaged across participants and test whether experimental effects replicate at the group level (e.g., Kaduk et al., 2024; Semmelmann & Weigelt, 2018; Van der Cruyssen et al., 2024). It therefore remains unclear which component of webcam data quality limits AOI measures, whether offsets are specific to persons and stable across a task, and whether individual differences in webcam AOI measures agree with those obtained with an IR eye tracker. These questions matter whenever webcam data are used to characterize individual learners, for example to predict their answers (Authors, 2022; Authors, 2020b) or to adapt instruction to them (Authors, 2025a; Authors, 2025b). The contribution of the present study is to decompose webcam error at the person level and to test its consequences on AOIs that are identical for webcam and IR data.

The Present Study

Participants solved six multiple-choice graph items once with a commercial webcam-based system (Labvanced) and once with an IR eye tracker (Tobii Pro Spectrum). The graphs were identical in both sessions. This allowed us to define AOIs within the graph (function lines, x-axis, y-axis, plot area) in the coordinate system of the graph itself, so that they were identical for both systems and allowed a direct comparison. The webcam session took place in the laboratory or at home. We addressed three research questions: RQ1. How good is the quality of webcam data during self-paced work on static graph items, in the laboratory and at home? RQ2. Do webcam data resolve where learners look within a graph, and do they agree with IR data at the group and at the person level? RQ3. Which component of webcam data quality, sample noise or person-specific offset, limits these AOI measures, and can offsets be corrected without reference targets?

Method Participants and Design

Eighty-one participants, predominantly master's students in education-related programs, completed a webcam session and an IR session. We excluded four participants, among them one whose IR validation failed (validation accuracy 114.7 mm; median of all recordings 4.5 mm) and one participant with very little webcam data (436 valid samples across all items; median of all participants 3,660). The analyzed sample comprised 77 participants (mean age 24.9 years, SD = 3.3; 34 female, 43 male). All participants completed the IR session in the laboratory. Twenty participants completed the webcam session in the laboratory after the IR session, 25 completed the webcam session in the laboratory before the IR session, and 32 completed the webcam session at home after the IR session. Session order was thus crossed in the laboratory, whereas the home session was always the second session. The study was approved by the ethics committee of [institution blinded for review], and all participants gave written informed consent.

Materials

The task comprised six multiple-choice items on line graphs (see Figure 1). Each item consisted of a question, a line graph with one or two linear functions and four answer options, the last of which was "I don't know". Items asked for the slope of a graph or for the area under it in everyday contexts such as the number of new infections per week or the dosage of a medication.

The items were presented in the same fixed order in both sessions and were self-paced; a response ended the item. We number them 1 to 6 in the order of presentation.

Apparatus and Procedure

IR data were recorded with a Tobii Pro Spectrum at 120 Hz on a 23.8-inch monitor (1920 × 1080 pixels; median viewing distance 58 cm) after a 9-point calibration and validation (median validation accuracy 0.42°, IQR 0.31-0.60°; precision 0.18° RMS). Webcam data were recorded with Labvanced at the frame rate of the webcam (about 30 Hz) after a calibration with 55 points in four head poses, with a virtual chinrest, drift correction and recalibration every 120 s; the webcam protocol contained no validation targets. In both sessions, the graph items followed a video-viewing task that is not part of the present analyses. In the laboratory, Labvanced ran in Microsoft Edge on a Windows computer, with an external webcam (Logitech 720 Full HD); at home, participants used their own computers and browsers, mostly with integrated laptop cameras.

Data Preprocessing

Webcam coordinates were expressed relative to the Labvanced frame, which the item images filled completely; IR coordinates were expressed relative to the screen. Item boundaries in the IR recording were taken from the URL events of the web recording. Coordinates were converted to degrees of visual angle under laboratory viewing conditions (webcam frame: about 49° × 28°; IR screen: 52° × 30°). Data loss was the proportion of expected samples (trial duration × nominal sampling rate) without a valid gaze estimate. Trials with more than 50% data loss were excluded.

Areas of Interest

Calibration. Each graph image was calibrated against its own axes: the y-axis, the x-axis and the outermost labeled grid lines were detected automatically, and gaze was transformed into graph coordinates (u = 0 at the y-axis and 1 at the last labeled x grid line; v = 0 at the x-axis and 1 at the highest labeled y grid line). The function lines were then detected independently in both images. In graph coordinates, they agreed between sessions within .008 in slope and .004 in intercept, which confirms the calibration.

AOIs.

Within the graph, we defined four AOIs that were identical for both systems: the function lines (a band of ±6% of the plot height around each line), the x-axis and the y-axis including their tick labels, and the remaining plot area. The graphs were displayed larger in the webcam session (plot area 13.9-18.8° × 11.0-14.1°) than in the IR session (9.7-11.3° × 6.9-8.6°), so that the same AOIs were 1.4 to 1.7 times larger in degrees in the webcam data; the line band, for example, was 1.3-1.7° wide in the webcam session and 0.8-1.0° in the IR session. This difference favors the webcam.

Control Region.

As a control region, we used the function lines mirrored horizontally. The control region had the same shape, area (14.5% of the graph region on average) and vertical extent as the line band and a similar position (mean u = .51-.57 vs. .45-.50), but contained almost no graph content: dark pixels, which mark lines and labels, covered 0.5% of its area outside the line band, compared with 3.0% of the line band and 0.3% of the remaining plot area. Because mirrored lines cross the original lines, 12% of the control region overlapped the line band; we therefore repeated the comparison without this overlap.

Measures

Data Quality. We report data loss, the number of valid samples per second, the proportion of webcam trials recorded at half the nominal rate (median inter-sample interval above 50 ms) and precision as the root mean square of sample-to-sample distances (RMS-S2S). Precision was computed within fixations that were detected for both systems with the same dispersion-threshold algorithm (I-DT; 1.0°, 100 ms; Salvucci & Goldberg, 2000) on a common 30-Hz time base, and, as an upper bound that includes reading saccades, across sliding 200-ms windows.

AOI Measures.

Dwell share was the proportion of samples in the graph region that fell into an AOI. Enrichment was the dwell share divided by the area share of the AOI; an enrichment of 1 means that gaze fell into the AOI as often as expected if it were spread uniformly. Area shares refer to the graph region (the plot area together with the axes and their labels), and only samples within this region enter the shares; because the graph region and its AOIs were identical in both systems, these measures can be compared directly. The line signal was the enrichment of the lines minus that of the control region. We also report the share of all valid samples that fell into the graph region. To compare group-level gaze distributions on the graph, we pooled the laboratory participants of each system (each participant weighted equally) on a 12 × 12 grid over the graph region and correlated the resulting maps between systems.

Gaze Centroids.

To assess person-specific offsets without validation targets, we computed for each valid trial the median gaze position, subtracted the median across participants for that item and averaged these deviations across items, separately for each direction and system. If all participants view the same items, true differences in where they look should produce similar spreads of centroids in both systems; additional, reliable spread in webcam data indicates person-specific offsets.

Analyses

Measures were averaged per participant across valid items. Enrichment was tested against 1, and the lines against the control region, with Wilcoxon signed-rank tests. Systems were compared in laboratory participants with at least three valid items in both systems (n = 40) using paired t tests with dz and Pearson correlations. Reliability of individual differences was estimated by correlating means across two halves of the items (items 1, 3 and 5 vs. 2, 4 and 6), corrected with the Spearman-Brown formula; the reliability of group maps was estimated from random halves of participants (200 splits) in the same way.

Because session order was crossed in the laboratory, we separated system and repetition effects, assuming additive effects: the system difference contains the system effect plus the repetition effect when the IR session came first (n = 16 with paired data) and minus it when the webcam session came first (n = 24). Half the sum and half the difference of both differences estimate the system and the repetition effect, with 95% confidence intervals based on Welch standard errors.

Laboratory and home webcam data were compared with Welch t tests and with two onesided tests for equivalence (TOST; Lakens et al., 2018); data-quality measures were compared with Mann-Whitney tests. Because the graph task was not designed to test equivalence, we set the smallest effect size of interest to a medium difference of d = ±0.5 and report 90% confidence intervals, so that readers can judge which differences the data rule out.

For RQ3, we estimated the standard deviation of person-specific webcam offsets as the square root of the difference between the reliable (true-score) variances of the centroid deviations in webcam and IR data, that is, the variance multiplied by its split-half reliability. To check whether the different arrangement of the item pages could produce this difference, we predicted the centroid spread that the participants' IR viewing behavior would produce on the webcam pages, by weighting the centers of the question, graph and answer regions of the webcam pages with each participant's IR dwell shares on these regions. As a second estimate, we compared the median gaze positions of each participant in both systems directly in graph coordinates.

We then degraded the IR samples of each laboratory participant and recomputed the AOI measures. Errors were scaled to the display size of the graph in the webcam session, and we added offsets drawn from the estimated distribution (constant per participant across items) and slowly drifting noise (first-order autoregressive process with a time constant of 1 s), because slow drift does not average out as readily as independent noise; for comparison, we also added independent noise and drifting noise of 2°. Offset scenarios were repeated 10 times with new random offsets.

Outcomes were the share of graph gaze on the lines, the line signal and the correlation across participants between degraded and original measures. Finally, we tested a correction that uses only webcam data: each participant's samples on an item were shifted by that participant's mean centroid deviation on the other items (leave-one-item-out). Analyses were conducted in Python (pandas, SciPy, statsmodels, scikit-image). Processed data and analysis code are openly available (Authors, 2026).

Results

Data Quality (RQ1)

Data quality is summarized in Table 1. Webcam data loss on the graph items was low in both settings (median 8.7% in the laboratory and 5.4% at home), and webcam participants delivered a median of 28 (laboratory) and 29 (home) valid samples per second. Nevertheless, 13.5% of webcam trials recorded at home exceeded the exclusion criterion, compared with 0.4% in the laboratory (Mann-Whitney p = .005); at home, 36 of 192 trials were recorded at half the nominal rate, 18 of them in the 36 trials run in Microsoft Edge, whereas none of the 270 laboratory trials were affected.

In the laboratory, webcam precision within fixations was 2.5 times worse than IR precision (median 0.35° vs. 0.14°, dz = 2.71, n = 43); across sliding windows, which include reading saccades, the webcam median was 1.20°. Relative to the stimulus, webcam data were noisier at home than in the laboratory (median RMS-S2S within fixations 1.3% vs. 0.9% of the frame width, Welch t = 8.15, p < .001), as expected from smaller screens.

Where Learners Look Within the Graph (RQ2)

Within the identical graphs, the IR data showed a clear pattern (Table 2, Figure 1): gaze concentrated on the function lines (median enrichment 2.58; 95% of participants above 1), clearly more than on the mirrored control region (1.20; difference p < .001, positive for 98% of participants), whereas the axes (0.77 and 0.64) and the remaining plot area (0.69) received less gaze than their area would predict. The webcam data reproduced the direction of this pattern. Gaze concentrated on the lines (1.45; 80% of participants above 1, p < .001) more than on the control region (1.05; difference p < .001, positive for 73% of participants), and the axes received less gaze than expected (0.82 each, p = .024 and .042). The effect, however, was much weaker: the line signal was 0.41 in webcam and 1.37 in IR data, and the share of graph gaze on the lines was 21.4% compared with 37.7% (dz = -1.04). Webcam gaze fell on the graph region less often overall (28.4% vs. 36.4% of samples, dz = -0.95). At the group level, the webcam maps of gaze on the graph agreed well with the IR maps.

Their correlation ranged from .40 to .62 across items (Figure 1d), compared with a maximum of .68 to .86 expected from the reliability of both maps; corrected for reliability, the agreement was .59 to .79. The webcam maps were the limiting factor: their reliability with 44 participants was .53 to .78, compared with .80 to .95 for IR maps. At the person level, in contrast, the systems did not agree. The share of graph gaze on the lines was reliable within each system (split-half .56 webcam, .63 IR) but did not correlate between systems (r = -.10), and neither did any other AOI within the graph (r = -.23 to .23; Table 2).

Session Order and Repetition.

All participants worked on the items twice, and the second session took less than half as long as the first (laboratory: median 95 s vs. 219 s for all six items, p < .001). Repetition does not explain the weaker webcam signal. For the share of graph gaze on the lines, the repetition effect was -1.8 percentage points (95% CI [-7.2, 3.5]) and the repetitioncorrected system effect -16.7 points (95% CI [-22.0, -11.4]); even at the limit of its confidence interval, repetition could account for at most 44% of the 16.3-point difference between systems. The same held for the line signal (repetition -0.28, 95% CI [-0.58, 0.02]; system -1.04, 95% CI [-1.34, -0.74]). Repetition did reduce the share of samples on the graph region (-3.6 points, 95% CI [-6.2, -0.9]), consistent with less intensive viewing in the faster second session. Within-system tests and simulations compare the same recordings and are not affected by repetition.

Laboratory Versus Home.

No measure within the graph differed between settings (all p ≥ .48; laboratory n = 44, home n = 29). Within bounds of ±0.5 standard deviations, home and laboratory data were equivalent for the share of graph gaze on the lines (home minus laboratory: +0.5 percentage points, 90% CI [-2.8, 3.8], bounds ±4.2, p TOST = .033), the enrichment of the lines (+0.02, 90% CI [-0.20, 0.24], p TOST = .026) and the line signal (+0.04, 90% CI [-0.20, 0.27], p TOST = .043), but not for the share of samples on the graph region (-1.3 points, 90% CI [-4.3, 1.8], p TOST = .10).

Restricted to laboratory participants whose webcam session was also the second (n = 19), no measure differed (p ≥ .32), but equivalence could not be shown (p TOST ≥ .06). The data thus rule out medium-sized differences in the line measures, but not smaller ones. Only trial loss was clearly higher at home (Data Quality).

Noise Versus Offset (RQ3)

Gaze centroids revealed person-specific offsets in the webcam data. Across participants, the centroid deviations had a standard deviation of 4.1° horizontally and 2.9° vertically in webcam data, compared with 1.7° and 1.8° in IR data, and they were highly consistent across items in webcam data (split-half reliability .89 and .79; IR: .55 and .63). The additional reliable spread corresponds to person-specific offsets with a standard deviation of 3.6° horizontally and 2.2° vertically, that is, a mean offset magnitude of about 3.7°. The different arrangement of the item pages cannot explain this: the participants' IR viewing behavior, transferred to the webcam pages, would produce a spread of only 1.2° and 1.6°. The direct comparison of gaze positions on the identical graphs confirmed the estimate: the person-specific difference between webcam and IR positions had a standard deviation of 2.8° horizontally and 2.1° vertically (median magnitude 2.9°), which underestimates the offsets because the comparison was restricted to the region around the graph.

Webcam noise was considerably smaller: the RMS-S2S of 0.35° within fixations and 1.20° across windows corresponds to Gaussian noise with a standard deviation of 0.17° to 0.60° per axis.

Within the graph, a simulation with errors of this size reproduced the observed webcam data closely (Figure 1e). Independent or drifting noise of 0.6° reduced the line signal only from 1.37 to 1.13-1.14 and preserved individual differences (r = .93-.99). Offsets from the estimated distribution, with or without drifting noise, reduced the line signal to 0.32-0.35, the share of graph gaze on the lines to 21.1-21.3% and the correlation with the original data to .23-.26; the observed webcam values were 0.41, 21.4% and r = -.10 with the IR data. Only drifting noise of 2°, more than three times the upper-bound estimate, reduced the line signal substantially (0.60), but it still preserved individual differences (r = .69). Person-specific offsets are therefore sufficient to explain both the weaker signal and the lack of agreement in individual differences.

Correcting webcam data for person-specific centroid deviations did not improve agreement with IR data. The leave-one-item-out correction reduced the share of graph gaze on the lines from 21.4% to 19.6% (IR: 37.8%) and the line signal from 0.46 (0.41 with all 44 laboratory participants) to 0.24, and the person-level correlation with IR data remained close to zero (-.10 before and -.02 after correction; n = 40). A centroid-based correction cannot distinguish offsets from true differences in where participants look and therefore removes both.

Discussion

Because the graphs were identical in both sessions, we could compare a commercial webcam system and an IR eye tracker on the same AOIs within the graph. At the group level, webcam data captured the main pattern: learners looked at the function lines more than at a control region, and group gaze maps agreed with IR maps nearly as well as the webcam's own reliability allowed. The pattern was, however, much weaker, and individual differences did not agree between systems, although they were reliable within each system; repetition accounts for less than half of this difference. Person-specific offsets of about 3.7°, not noise, explain these limitations. Home and laboratory recordings did not differ detectably.

Accuracy, Not Precision, Limits AOI Analyses

Precision is the most frequently reported weakness of webcam-based eye tracking, and it clearly limits measures that depend on the segmentation of fixations. For dwell measures on static AOIs, however, noise largely averages out: even slowly drifting noise of the observed size left the line signal largely intact and preserved individual differences. Offsets, in contrast, move a participant's entire gaze distribution and thereby shift gaze from the lines into neighboring parts of the graph and off the graph. Because they differed between participants and persisted across items, they also created stable but spurious individual differences. This explains why webcam measures could be reliable and yet fail to converge with IR measures, although IR data resolved a line band of less than 1° and the same band was 1.3-1.7° wide in the webcam session.

Whether offsets of this size are a general property of webcam-based eye tracking or specific to our protocol cannot be decided from a single system. They are larger than the accuracy reported for the same system under optimal laboratory conditions (1.4°; Kaduk et al., 2024). Part of the error is probably generic: webcam-based systems estimate gaze from the appearance of the eyes and face, which differs between persons and depends on glasses, lighting and head position. Part may be specific to the protocol: without validation targets, poor calibrations could neither be detected nor repeated; recalibration every 120 s may have introduced new offsets; and head position may have drifted during the preceding video task. Studies that combine different calibration schedules with validation targets could separate these sources.

Offsets could not be corrected from the webcam data alone, because a correction based on each participant's gaze centroid removed true differences in viewing together with the offsets.

Validation targets avoid this problem in principle: the difference between measured and known target position reflects the error of the system alone, independent of where the learner chooses to look. As offsets were consistent across items (split-half reliability .79-.89), targets presented within or immediately before the task should capture them, and several targets across the graph would also capture position-dependent errors. Because offsets can change with recalibration, targets should be repeated. Our protocol contained no targets, so this correction remains to be tested.

What Webcam-Based Eye Tracking Can and Cannot Do for Learning Research

For educational technology research, the results lead to a simple decision rule: webcambased eye tracking is suitable when the research question concerns groups of learners and the AOIs are large relative to an expected offset of about 3.7°; it is not suitable, without per-learner validation, when the question concerns individual learners. Table 3 applies this rule to typical uses. Group-level questions are central to many uses of eye tracking in educational technology, such as comparing how learners attend to different designs of a graph or a learning material or whether instructional prompts redirect attention. Webcam data can answer such questions, also within a graph, provided that sample sizes account for the attenuation of effects to about 30% of the IR signal.

Individual-level questions are central to learning analytics and adaptive systems: predicting whether a learner will answer correctly (Authors, 2022; Authors, 2025a; Authors, 2020b; Authors, 2024), detecting difficulties for individual assistance (Authors, 2025b) or reacting to a learner's gaze in real time (D'Mello et al., 2012). For these uses, the results are a warning rather than a limitation: offsets produced individual differences that were reliable but unrelated to where learners actually looked, and a model trained on such data would treat them as characteristics of the learner.

Webcam-based gaze data should therefore inform decisions about individual learners only if accuracy has been validated for each learner, for example with validation targets embedded in the learning material.

Recommendations for Online Studies

Three recommendations complement Table 3. First, webcam-based studies should present validation targets within each task, so that accuracy can be estimated and corrected for each participant. Second, materials should be designed for the expected offset rather than the expected noise: AOIs should be large and separated by empty space, and graphs should be displayed as large as possible. Third, AOI measures should be reported with chance-level baselines and control regions, because dwell shares alone do not show whether an AOI is discriminated at all.

Limitations

Several limitations should be noted. First, we tested one commercial system with one calibration protocol (recalibration every 120 s, no validation targets); other systems or protocols may produce different offsets. Second, the offset estimate assumes that true differences in where participants look are similar in both sessions; it was supported by the predicted behavioral spread and by the direct comparison on the identical graphs, but it captures only the spread of offsets between participants. Third, all participants worked on the items twice; the crossed order limits the contribution of repetition to less than half of the system difference, assuming that repetition affects both systems equally. Fourth, the fixed item order confounds item position with item content; this does not affect the comparison of systems on the same item. Fifth, the home condition was not a genuine online scenario: participants came from the same population, were recruited for a laboratory study and had completed the IR session before, and the data were not crowdsourced.

The comparison therefore shows that a change of location did not detectably affect the data, not that webcam-based eye tracking scales to unsupervised, heterogeneous samples; moreover, equivalence bounds of ±0.5 standard deviations are wide relative to the attenuated webcam signal.

Conclusion

On graph items, a commercial webcam-based eye tracker can show where learners look as a group, also within the graph, but with a much weaker signal than IR eye tracking and without valid individual differences. The limiting factor is accuracy rather than precision. Webcam-based eye tracking can therefore already serve as an infrastructure for group-level learning research at scale, whereas individual diagnosis, response prediction and gaze-adaptive learning systems require accuracy to be validated for each learner.
