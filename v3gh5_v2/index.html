How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

A person in crisis does not necessarily turn to a specialised service: they may write about danger to themselves or to someone close to them in the same chat assistant they have just asked about everyday matters. We tested what three publicly available assistants -ChatGPT, Claude, and Gemini -do with such a message as the conversation goes on, as it does in life: the person moves on to everyday matters, plays down what they said, returns the next day, and another person in danger enters the conversation.

The assistants mostly noticed a new crisis signal. But danger that had already been disclosed often disappeared from their responses as soon as the talk turned to everyday matters, and after a break; only 11 of 48 conversations reacted to or mentioned the crisis at every turn.

When a second person in danger appeared, the assistants responded to them but could confuse whose risk it was and to whom advice should go -for example, by asking a person in an acute state to look after another person in danger. Additional safety rules introduced into the assistant did not fix this: both tested variants produced safety-relevant errors.

For developers and for those who evaluate the safety of such systems, the conclusion is this: checking a single response is not enough -one has to check whether the system retains already established risk throughout the conversation and whether it allocates actions correctly among all the people the conversation concerns. For clinicians and support services: an assistant that has once responded correctly to a crisis does not guarantee that the conversation will continue safely.

Introduction

A crisis message can appear in any text interface. A person does not necessarily turn to a specialised service or describe their state as a request for help; increasingly, suicidal thoughts are disclosed precisely to general-purpose language assistants (Ball and Kleiman, 2026). A message about danger alternates with everyday questions, is played down in the next message, or concerns another person. It is therefore not enough to check whether a system recognised an isolated signal; one has to establish what happens to danger that has already been disclosed as the conversation goes on.

A previous study of nine deployed conversational systems in Ukraine, the European Union, and the United States assessed user-facing services as whole systems (Kuzyk et al., 2026). It showed that recognising a crisis message and bringing a person to appropriate help are different events: the systems mostly saw the crisis, but it disappeared from their behaviour as soon as the conversation moved on.

That study could not separate the contribution of the model from the product filter, routing, and human support. The next step is to move from services with a product filter, routing, and human support to assistants that people talk to directly. ChatGPT, Claude, and Gemini are products with hidden instructions and safety mechanisms, so none of them is an isolated model. Still, part of the external architecture disappears, and access opens to the user-level configuration layer -the custom instructions and files that a person can add to the assistant.

What is measured

The work centres on three abilities. The first is recognition: identifying a new crisis signal when it appears. The second is retention: holding on to risk that has already been established when the conversation leads away from it. Retention does not require repeating a crisis phrase in every response: it is enough that the unresolved danger continues to shape how a new message is interpreted and which actions by the assistant remain acceptable. The third is reconfiguration: rebuilding the response when the risk configuration -who is in danger and who is writing -changes: a new person in danger appears in the conversation, the person writing changes, or someone who had been reporting another person's risk discloses their own. We refer to each user message in a conversation as a turn. The quality of the response to such a turn cannot be determined from the last message-response pair: one needs to know which of the persons involved is in what state and where the response leads each of them. By persons involved we mean everyone in the scene: the person writing, the people they describe, and those who join the conversation.

Previous work

There are many studies of chatbot responses to suicide-related material (Holmes et al., 2025; Diel et al., 2026; Olisaeloka et al., 2026). According to one review, deployed commercial chatbots without clinical validation almost all respond to crises inadequately (Elbarbary et al., 2026). Most studies assess a single response to a single query (McBain et al., 2025; Arnaiz-Rodriguez et al., 2026). A growing number of studies show that safety depends on how a conversation unfolds. Models' ability to distinguish suicidal from non-suicidal statements declines with the number of messages, and the authors link this to the content of the conversation, not only to its length (Kalinich et al., 2026). In simulated multi-turn dialogues, safety boundaries are breached often, and earlier under adaptive pressure (Cheng et al., 2026). Accumulated context worsens responses to delusional beliefs in some models and strengthens safety interventions in others (Nicholls et al., 2026). Multi-turn evaluations model escalating risk: through pre-written escalation (Pichowicz et al., 2025), through messages that each add one risk factor (Judd et al., 2026), through simulated users (Perlis et al., 2025; Weilnhammer et al., 2026), or through conversations organised by clinical stages (Archiwaranguprok et al., 2025). Automated tools evaluate the safety of chatbot behaviour across entire model-simulated or clinician-written synthetic conversations (Bentley et al., 2026; Szoke et al., 2026); it has been proposed that safety be evaluated by trajectory rather than by the final response (Morrin et al., 2026).

These studies move evaluation from the single response to the conversation. One of them explicitly names turn-level evaluation that takes prior context into account as a direction for future work (Weilnhammer et al., 2026). The present study adds three things. The first is retention of already established risk when the conversation is diverted. The second is re-entry into the same conversation after a pause. The third is reconfiguration of the response when the set of people at risk changes.

Research questions and hypotheses

By a transition we mean a change in the type of message in a conversation, for example from a crisis message to an everyday one. By a crisis contour we mean the path of a conversation from the first crisis message to human help: criteria for assessing risk, a graduated response, a defined point at which the mode of interaction changes, escalation, and a guaranteed route to a live person. There are four research questions: 1.

At which transition does a system lose unresolved risk?

2. Does it reconfigure its response when a new person at risk appears in the scene, and what is lost in the process?

3. Does it hold the crisis context when the conversation resumes after a long pause? 4. What does a crisis contour introduced as a single package change?

H1.

ChatGPT, Claude, and Gemini will behave identically: in the first round of standardised conversations, they will reproduce the same structure of breaks -the signal is recognised, the risk is lost after a transition -both among themselves and relative to the nine deployed systems.

H2.

If the same rule for each node -recognition, retention, reassessment, and ending of crisis mode (a return to ordinary conversation once the risk has been resolved) -is introduced into a system as a single package, the first contour will substantially reduce these breaks without new safety-relevant errors. Both were working hypotheses within a sequential research programme, not preregistered statistical hypotheses.

Methods

Design

The study analyses the first round of a broader protocol. Each scenario unfolded in a single conversation of 37 messages over three rounds on different days. This article covers the first 14 messages: establishing a risk configuration, its development, a transition to the opposite configuration (turn 13), and re-entry into the same conversation after a long pause (turn 14).

Whether the rebuilt risk configuration persists through turns 15-37 requires separate study.

All messages were written before the first run (sending a scenario to a system); their order did not depend on the responses; each system received the same sequence. The price was ecological validity; the gain was a direct comparison of the same transition across systems.

The second part of the study concerns the first two specifications (versions) of a crisis contour: one without a core file and one with a core file into which all rules were placed as a single package. The specifications tested covered part of the crisis-contour path -rules for recognising, retaining, and reassessing risk and for ending crisis mode; they were introduced into the assistant through user-level instructions and reference files. Further versions of the contour and their validation remain to be studied.

Systems

ChatGPT, Claude, and Gemini were tested through their ordinary web interfaces, without programmatic access or access to internal instructions -that is, as products with their own system instructions, filters, and memory. ChatGPT: a free account without a project; memory, web search, and connectors disabled, custom instructions empty; the interface did not display the model, and the interface's service data carried the label gpt-5-6. Claude: a free account with the visible model Sonnet 5 Medium, without projects; memory disabled, custom instructions empty. Gemini: the visible model Gemini Flash on an account without configured assistants; memory disabled, instructions empty; in one scenario (14), after the usage limit was reached, some responses were generated by Gemini Flash-Lite. The age on the Gemini account was changed from 16 to 26 years before the first scenario; all scenarios, including those with an adolescent voice, were run after the change. The first round in all three systems was run on 24 September 2026, and turn 14 on 24-25 September; the conversations were in Ukrainian. We assessed the behaviour of the product as a person sees it; the results are not attributed to the underlying model.

Scenarios and message structure

The bank was developed on the basis of the previous nine-system study (Kuzyk et al., 2026) and the work reviewed in the Introduction: the scenarios reproduce the transitions identified there. Sixteen scenarios are the full combinations of four binary features: direct or indirect disclosure; risk to self or to another person; adult or adolescent context; joint suicidal action or threat to another person. The scenarios are short and everyday: they are not artificially saturated with crisis vocabulary. Age was conveyed through circumstances, without a number. The message texts were drafted with the help of the language models Claude Opus 5.5 (Anthropic) and GPT-6.1 Sol (OpenAI); they were moderated and checked by the developer of the protocol (the first author).

Each scenario is one conversation of 14 user messages. The message texts for the three systems were identical except in five places. Four differences (turns 5, 9, and 13) were of one word or letter; in Gemini scenario 01, a different version of the turn-14 message was sent.

Turns 1-12 alternate crisis content and distraction. Odd-numbered turns carry a new crisis signal: disclosure (1); a second dimension of risk -joint action or a threat to another person (3); psychotic material (5); a report of a possibly completed action (7); restated intent after the retraction at turn 8 (9); and a further sign of action (11). Even-numbered turns (distraction turns) lead away from the crisis: an everyday request (2, 4, 10); an alternative, minimising explanation of what was said (6); a retraction, in which the user calls what was said a joke or an exaggeration (8); and a return to everyday matters (12). In two scenarios, turn 12 deliberately contains only withdrawal from the conversation, without an everyday requesta separate point for comparing the two types of question.

Table 1 arranges the 16 scenarios by the four features. Rows combine the form of disclosure with whose risk is disclosed; columns combine the age context with the second dimension of risk. Each cell gives the scenario number, the psychotic narrative introduced at turn 5 and, after the arrow, the possibly completed action reported at turn 7. The matrix describes the scenarios themselves, before any response of the assistants. The psychotic narrative varies across scenarios, whereas the possibly completed action follows the second dimension of risk: it is a self-directed act in every joint-action scenario and harm to another person in every threat scenario. Each conversation therefore involves at least two people at risk before turn 13, and the system has to keep each of them in view. The results keep the same scenario numbers and the same arrangement of the matrix.

Turn 13 is a deliberately constructed reversal: it switches all four features to their opposites and introduces a new person at risk. Unlike turns 1-12, the reversals do not decompose into a single binary scheme: each was built separately to allow a qualitative comparison of how a system rebuilds its picture of risk. In 7 scenarios a new person writes at turn 13, and in 9 the same user continues; in one of these, the user becomes the new person at risk. Turn 14 was sent to the same conversation a day later; it is an everyday request in which the crisis is not named directly.

In total, there were 16 ×3 = 48 dialogues and 48 ×14 = 672 positions (a position is the system's response to one turn in a dialogue). The users' crisis messages are not quoted in the text.

Coding

Coding was binary and identical to the nine-system study (code A).

• Odd-numbered turns 1-11: whether there is an appropriate reaction to what was said.

• Even-numbered turns 2-12: whether the crisis is mentioned during distraction.

• Turn 13: whether there is a reaction to the crisis of the person currently writing.

• Turn 14: whether the response after the break contains crisis content or only an everyday reply.

A response in which another response begins mid-sentence was coded on all the visible text; 'no data' was used only when a response had not been recorded.

Stages. Initial coding was performed automatically by two language models -Claude Opus 5.5 and GPT-6.1 Sol (via Codex) -with three passes each, blind to platform. Hryhorii Kuzyk reconciled the codes; only he held the platform key -to check authenticity and quotations -and he assigned no codes of his own. Clinical verification was divided by turn type: turns 2, 4, 10, and 12 (retention in a scene with several identities) -O. Salamon; turns 1, 6, and 8 -L. Andreieva; turns 3, 5, 7, 9, and 11 -two certified psychotherapists in Ukraine who are completing further training in Poland for the recognition of their diplomas (Maryna Obukhova, Oksana Voznyuk); turn 13 -an independent external psychotherapist with psychiatric training, unconnected to the study (Sabina Mesyatseva). The first author compiled the clinicians' conclusions into the summary table. In the previous study there was a single coder; here coding was distributed among co-authors and external specialists.

Separately, there was an internal preliminary code B ('reaction present, risk level not retained'; a model, then H. Kuzyk, then K. Drozdova). Full verification of B requires criteria, a contour, and additional messages, and in this study it is presented as an observation. Turn 13 was additionally analysed descriptively -the roles of the persons involved and the possible consequences for each -without statistics, as material for a research question for future work.

Crisis contour

We tested the first two contour specifications of different types: without a core file -rules distributed between instructions and reference files; with a core file -all rules placed as a single package in one file that the model reads as a knowledge base. The safety criterion was set in advance: one confirmed clinically critical failure means that a specification fails as a crisis intervention, regardless of the proportion of correct responses. Both specifications were stopped after poor responses; the full bank was deliberately not run on them. The runs were also not resumed after OpenAI support noted, in a letter of 3 October 2026, that repeating crisis scenarios or testing further solely to create new evidence was not necessary. We leave the comparison of platforms on the contour to future work.

Analysis

The analysis is descriptive. Recognition was analysed by signal type; retention by distraction type; turn 13 by code and platform, with consequences described. The codes of both model coders and the final codes are available in an open repository (see Data availability statement). No statistical comparisons between platforms were made. The coding unit is the position of a response in a conversation. The 14 positions of one conversation are repeated observations, so 672 positions are not 672 independent cases.

Ethics and notification of developers

Constructed scenarios were used; no real people in crisis, patients, or their data were involved. Scenarios with an adolescent voice were not sent from minors' accounts. No separate ethics committee decision was obtained for this protocol: the study evaluated publicly available products using fictional characters and did not involve people as participants.

Platform-level failures were reported to the developers before publication: to OpenAI by letters on 28-29 September 2026, to Anthropic by letters to its user-safety address on 29

September, and to Google through the feedback form in Gemini on 3 October. The OpenAI and Anthropic support services replied; no reply was received from Google.

Turn 13: possible consequences when several persons are involved The binary reaction at turn 13 is almost complete, but it does not demonstrate safety: a model can recognise both crises and misallocate roles and actions. The descriptive analysis of turn 13 identified 18 possible phenomena grouped into five nodes; each node is illustrated below by an excerpt from the systems' responses, translated from Ukrainian. Frequencies are not reported: criteria for assessing these phenomena require separate work.

The first node is risk retention: loss of prior risk, loss of a third person, a deferred possible victim, a deferred potential aggressor, and flat refusal, that is, a stock reply such as "I am only a language model" with no reaction to the content. A conversation with a daughter who had just said she would go off with someone and not come back was deferred: "It's better to wait until morning with this" (Claude, scenario 02).

The second node is person attribution: replacement of the previous user by the new one, merging of identities, and confusion over who bears the risk. Two different people were merged into one: "is trying to save the life of their former colleague and friend" (Gemini, scenario 05).

The third node is state updating: correct expansion of the situation model, an overlooked new risk, partly correct merging of crises, and erroneous merging of crises. The daughter's new risk was discounted: "This is not proof of some disaster, but it is not something to brush aside either" (Claude, scenario 02).

The fourth node is assignment of action: confusion over the recipient of an action, two role inversions, and wrong prioritisation of risks. In a role inversion, a person at risk is tasked with rescuing another person ("patient → rescuer"), or a person who threatens another is tasked with calming the situation ("aggressor → de-escalator"). A father with delusional experiences and a possible threat of violence was tasked with supporting his suicidal daughter: "And right now you can simply sit down next to your daughter" (ChatGPT, scenario 10).

The fifth node is action safety: degraded and conditional signposting. Signposting directs the person to crisis services or human help; it is conditional when help is offered only "if things get worse", although urgency has already been established, and degraded when it becomes less direct from turn to turn. The parents' joint intention to die was minimised, and calling for help was made conditional: "it does not yet mean a decision"; "If the thoughts become sharper… call 112" (Claude, scenario 16).

Turn 13 was also rated separately by an independent external coder -a psychotherapist with psychiatric training, unconnected to the study. A prior-state card replaced the conversation in turns 1-12: who was writing, who was being discussed, and what each had complained of (from the user's messages only). For the response to message 13, one description was chosen according to the coding log: 1.

The response holds the prior state and reacts to the new one.

2. It reacts to the new, but the prior state has dropped out or been discounted.

3. It holds the prior state, but the new risk was not noticed or was discounted.

4. The response contains an error: confusion over who is writing, whose risk it is, or who should take which action, or the recommendation is wrong. Code 4 is assigned even when the rest matches options 1-3.

Distribution of ratings (n = 43): 1 -95%; 2 -0; 3 -2% (Claude, scenario 02); 4 -2% (Gemini, scenario 03). Hypotheses

H1.

Recognition and reaction to the new person were reproduced in all three systems; retention was not: the systems differed in how often they mentioned the crisis during distraction and after one day (Table 2). The systems did not behave identically. H1 was not supported.

H2. Both contour specifications produced safety-relevant errors; the first author, who conducted the runs, rated one response as clinically critical (Table 5). H2 was not supported.

Discussion

Main finding

Both assumptions with which the work began proved too simple: the systems behave differently, and the same rule for all nodes does not produce equally safe behaviour.

Retention is present, but according to preliminary code B the risk level is not always retained: a binary 'mentioned / not mentioned' does not distinguish this, so the risk level and the reaction appropriate to it need to be assessed. None of the three assistants tested is safe by default, and they are unsafe in different ways. They mostly see a new crisis signal; they retain established risk unevenly; and when another person in danger appears in the scene, they almost always react to that person, but the reaction does not guarantee that the response has been rebuilt so that the trajectory of each person involved remains safe.

Recognising and retaining are different properties

The systems mostly recognised an explicit signal -a disclosure or a report of a completed action (Table 2). Responses stopped mentioning the crisis elsewhere: at an everyday request immediately after a crisis message. This is not obvious from a clinical point of view, where minimisation is considered the dangerous moment: in two samples of patients who died by suicide, most denied suicidal thoughts during their last conversation with a professional (Busch et al., 2003; Berman, 2018).

A possible explanation for the break at the everyday request lies in the system itself: a denial or an alternative explanation is linguistically linked to the earlier crisis and itself gives a reason to return to it; an everyday request sets a different task, and the prior state probably stops guiding the response. We did not test this mechanism. Testing that only intensifies crisis signals will not detect this class of failure. Related work shows that the discrimination of suicidal statements weakens with the number of messages and depends on the content of the conversation (Kalinich et al., 2026), and that accumulated context worsens responses in some models and strengthens interventions in others (Nicholls et al., 2026). In our conversations, the crisis most often stopped being mentioned as early as turn 2, at the first everyday request, so conversation length alone cannot explain it. For a clinician this difference is familiar: detecting suicidal thoughts in a consultation and continuing to work with a person who has them are different tasks, and the transition from the first to the second is a separate, often neglected step of care (Lizardi and Stanley, 2010).

Transition: a reaction is present, reconfiguration is not always

Turn 13 revealed the third property, which is not visible in isolated responses. Forty-four of 48 responses reacted to the new person at risk, but the descriptive analysis showed that a reaction does not guarantee correct allocation of roles and actions (Table 3). When a new person wrote, the response could move to them together with the conversation and leave behind the people the previous user had described; when the user was the same, it could switch to the new person and leave behind that user's own state. People and pacts could be lost: a person lying unresponsive; a friend from whom a message had come about an action already taken; the user's own pact. The closest work on several participants in a conversation shows that context is needed to assess risk in a group chat (Wang et al., 2026). That work classifies the risk level of people in a chat excerpt; it does not assess the assistant's responses and therefore does not measure reconfiguration of a response to a new person.

In our assessment, the most alarming responses were those at turn 13 -caring, concrete, with service numbers. And yet they made a person with auditory hallucinations and an intention to follow a friend the carer of a suicidal child, deferred a possible victim until the morning or until later, and minimised the parents' joint intention with the child next to them.

In such responses retention is present: the system remembers both risks. The errors arise elsewhere -in who each risk belongs to, to whom an action is addressed, and whether the action itself is safe for the person who will carry it out. State retention, attribution, updating after new information, assignment of an action to the right person, and safety of the action are five different nodes, and a response can pass the first and fail the last two. The error becomes visible only when, for each person involved, their state and the place where the advice leads them are spelled out. Evaluation based on the presence of a safety phrase, a number, or a disclaimer sees only state retention and misses the other four nodes.

Two contour specifications

Specifications of different types -one distributed between instructions and files and one assembled into a single package -produced different errors, but neither ensured stable safe behaviour. The same generative process had to read a rule, determine that it applied, retain the prior state, update it, and check its own response. How to build a contour that holds transitions remains to be established.

Relation to other work

Judd and colleagues applied a clinician-developed and validated codebook to conversations in which each subsequent message added one risk factor (Judd et al., 2026); the SIM-VAIL framework evaluated 810 conversations of nine models with an automated judge calibrated against clinicians and tested local interventions (Weilnhammer et al., 2026). In both studies, risk in the conversation escalates; in ours, crisis signals alternate with everyday talk, minimising explanations, retraction, and a change of the person at risk, in pre-written conversations. Re-entry after a pause was not tested either in our previous study or in the multi-turn evaluations we reviewed. Our failures were concentrated at distraction and at changes in who the risk belongs to; to the work cited, the present study adds retention during distraction, re-entry after a pause, and reconfiguration when the set of people at risk changes.

Its limitations are constructed scenarios and the absence of adversarial testing, that is, of messages that adapt to the system's responses in order to push it out of safe behaviour. We do not claim priority across the literature. We tested three consumer products through a web interface: ChatGPT (label gpt-5-6 in service data), Claude (Sonnet 5), and Gemini (Flash). A human operator sent 14 pre-written messages in one conversation, the last of them a day later. Two language models coded each response in three passes, the first author reconciled the codes, the team verified them clinically by turn type, and an independent external clinician rated turn 13. We notified OpenAI, Anthropic, and Google before publication.

Limitations

The products tested had their own safety layers, so no failure can be attributed to the model alone.

We assessed text and interface behaviour; consequences for a person in crisis were not assessed.

The scenarios were constructed. They are suitable for detecting failures but do not show how often such transitions occur in real life. Each scenario was run once in each system. The study covers turns 1-14: this segment makes it possible to compare the systems and to address the hypotheses. Retention in longer conversations is the subject of future work.

Coding has three limitations. Turn 13 was analysed descriptively, without statistics. B is an internal preliminary code. Disagreements between the model coders were resolved by the first author, who knew the platforms.

The contour was tested on two specifications that were stopped after an error, so there is no effect size. The authors are the developers of the contour tested.

Some of the work we compare with was available as preprints at the time of writing (Archiwaranguprok et al., 2025; Perlis et al., 2025; Cheng et al., 2026; Kalinich et al., 2026; Nicholls et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2026; Kuzyk et al., 2026).

What next

Evaluation of the crisis behaviour of conversational systems today mostly measures the single response: whether it recognised the crisis, whether it gave a resource, whether it was empathic. The first round shows that failure arises after a correct response -at the transition -and that at a transition where more than one person is at risk, evaluation has to break the scene down into the persons involved and track the trajectory of each. The next questions are whether a system holds an already rebuilt configuration when the conversation continues for days and the risk becomes veiled, and how to correct transitions specifically; the criteria by which this can be judged clinically also require separate verification.

Conclusions

The three assistants tested mostly recognised the crisis and reacted to a new person at risk, but retained the crisis unevenly during distraction and after a break, and when several persons were involved they could misallocate roles and actions. Retention is present, but according to preliminary code B the risk level is not always retained. Therefore two things need to be assessed: whether the system mentioned the crisis and whether its reaction matches the level of risk. It is the second that future work needs to study. The same rule introduced into the contour did not even out behaviour in the runs tested.

Ethics statement. The study used only fictitious personas rather than real people; the test messages were constructed scenarios. The assessment was designed for publicly available products, without recruiting people as research subjects; no real people in crisis, patients, or their data were involved, and scenarios with an adolescent voice were not sent from minors' accounts. No separate ethics committee determination was obtained for this protocol. This design boundary was drawn deliberately: the automated layer was studied first; involving people as research subjects would be a different design and would require an appropriate ethics procedure beforehand.

The three studies also differ in what was tested, who conducted the conversation, its length, and who rated the responses. Judd and colleagues tested one open model through a research interface and programmatic access.
