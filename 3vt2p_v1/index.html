Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. Abstract

Functional communication skills represent important measures in aphasia therapy. Assessing these skills, however, remains challenging. Mainstream pen-and-paper based instruments often elicit indirect rather than direct speech and fail to capture the complex communicative demands encountered in everyday communication situations, limiting their ecological validity and construct validity. The current study explored the feasibility of using immersive Virtual Reality (iVR) to assess functional communication in persons with aphasia. After developing a proof-ofconcept version -incorporating the needs and ideas of both speech and language therapists and persons with aphasia -we investigated whether (1) iVR enables a realistic simulation of everyday communication, including its technical feasibility and acceptability for the intended users, and (2) iVR simulation has added value for functional communication assessment by allowing manipulation of communicative complexity, the elicitation of direct speech, and both verbal and non-verbal aspects of communication to be assessed. The results suggest that it is technically feasible to create iVR environments for the assessment of functional communication in persons with aphasia that are acceptable to (end) users and that enhance the construct validity of functional communication tests. Despite remaining areas for improvement, iVR was found to have a clear added value for assessment purposes.

Introduction

Persons with aphasia (PWA) experience language difficulties due to acquired brain damage, such as a stroke. To plan effective aphasia therapy, it is relevant not only to assess the nature and severity of the language problems, but also to measure the extent to which PWA can communicate in daily life despite their language difficulties. Whereas the former goal of language assessment is typically achieved using impairment-based linguistic assessment tools, the latter is addressed through functional assessment tools (AARP, 2014). There is considerable consensus that these functional assessment tools are indicative of functional communication skills and that these skills represent important outcome measures in aphasia therapy (e.g., Dalton & Richardson, 2019); however, there is still ongoing debate about which linguistic-communicative components should -and should not -be considered part of functional communication (Barnes & Bloch, 2019; Doedens & Meteyard, 2022). In this article, we consider functional communication to comprise the skill to achieve goal-directed communication independently, effectively (e.g., Doedens & Meteyard, 2022; Wallace et al., 2017), and efficiently (Kong, 2009; Ruiter et al., 2011) in ecologically valid contexts, resembling daily life communication.

The mode of communication used, and the well-formedness of the utterances used are subordinate to the goal of achieving communicative success (Blomert et al., 1994; Frattali, 1992).

For functional communication assessment in Dutch-speaking PWA, there are two norm-based tests available: the Scenario Test (van der Meulen et al., 2008) and the Amsterdam-Nijmegen Everyday Language Test (ANELT; Blomert et al., 1995).

Although the former was found to be more appropriate for assessing functional communication in individuals with severe aphasia, the latter was better suited for individuals with mild to moderate aphasia (Schumacher et al., 2020). Both tests follow the same testing approach, which involves some sort of role-playing. The tester presents an everyday communication scenario orally, in the Scenario Test supported by pictures and in the ANELT -in some scenarios -with objects. The tester then prompts the PWA with questions such as 'What would you say?' to encourage them to respond verbally in the ANELT or either verbally or non-verbally in the Scenario Test.

For the resulting scores to be valid indicators of functional communication, participants must -among other things -comprehend and remember each orally presented scenario, imagine they are in a different physical space and role, switch to the required role (e.g., a customer buying a bouquet in a flower shop), maintain this perspective while generating their response in direct speech, and switch to the nonverbal communication channels (e.g., pointing or gesturing) when language difficulties are encountered. This suggests that responding to scenarios requires not only language processing (Wall et al., 2018), but also non-linguistic cognitive functions such as attention, working memory, and executive control. Clinical observations suggest that PWA who experience executive control difficulties typically struggle with role-playing (Garcia et al., 2007). An indication of difficulties in adopting a role is the frequently observed use of indirect speech (e.g., 'I would say that I would like to buy a bouquet with roses and lilies for 25 euros') rather than the required direct speech (e.g., 'Could you please put together a bouquet with roses and lilies for 25 euros').

Previous research indeed supports the claim that non-linguistic cognitive functions play a role in everyday language and communication abilities PWA (e.g., Dutta et al., 2023; Frankel et al., 2007; Hula & McNeil, 2008; Murray, 2012; Olsson et al., 2019; Wall et al., 2015). Schumacher et al. (2020), for example, found that non-linguistic cognition (i.e., attention and executive control) becomes crucial for performance on functional communication tasks when language production is impaired and nonverbal modes of communication are allowed. Scientific support for the role of non-linguistic cognitive functions in the pretend role-play required in pen-and-paper tests of functional communication comes from studies examining the relationship between executive control and behaviour in serious-gaming (van de Sande et al., 2015) and in children's pretend role-playing (Constien et al., 2026; Kelly et al., 2011). Since PWA commonly present with non-linguistic cognitive impairments (e.g., Akkad et al., 2023; Frankel et al., 2007; Murray, 2012), poorer performance on functional communication tasks may not solely reflect deficits in the target construct (functional communication), but may also arise from limitations in other underlying cognitive processes, a phenomenon referred to as task impurity.

In addition to concerns about task impurity, limitations in ecological validity -the extent to which test performance generalizes to real-world communicative behaviourmay also comprise construct validity (i.e., the extent to which the test measures what it is intended to measure). The traditional standardized, pen-and-paper tests of functional communication are typically administered in highly controlled and quiet clinical environments, in a role play setting with a (somewhat) familiar SLT. A test setup like this places different -and generally much lower -demands on personal, social, and environmental factors that are involved in everyday communication. In daily communication, people with aphasia are often less able to use their remaining language skills because they face additional non-linguistic cognitive challenges such as time pressure to communicate information quickly and the interference of background noise (Harmon, 2020; Harmon et al., 2024; Nelson et al., 2023). In these communicative settings, attentional demands increase. The behaviour of -especially unknownconversational partners may also increase communicative complexity. In a qualitative study on social participation (Dalemans et al., 2010), PWA indicated that their communication partner's knowledge about aphasia as well as their skills and willingness to use aphasia-friendly conversation strategies, influenced their level of engagement.

PWA indicated that an attitude of willingness, having patience, making an effort, and offering help without being overprotective made interactions easier. Reported barriers included, among other things, a lack of effort and a lack of patience on the part of communication partners. These personal, social, and environmental factors may hinder language use and the use of non-verbal communication skills (e.g., gestures, pointing) to compensate the language difficulties (Baylor et al., 2011; Doedens & Meteyard, 2022; Harmon et al., 2024; Rotherham et al., 2025; Stark, 2025).

Because the personal, social, and environmental demands discussed above are either absent or present to a much lesser degree in current standardized, pen-and-paper tests of functional communication than in everyday communication, outcome measures obtained from these tests may not be fully valid indicators of daily functioning (Pinto et al., 2025; Spreij et al., 2022). As naturalistic observations of PWA's daily communication lack the necessary experimental control and are typically too time-consuming and costly for clinical use, we hypothesized that immersive virtual reality may help overcome the limitation of traditional pen-and-paper tests of functional communication.

Virtual reality

Virtual Reality is a technology that enables users to experience highly realistic or otherwise credible simulations of, and interaction with, a computer-generated environment. Immersive VR (iVR) aims to envelop the user completely within this virtual environment, thereby creating a strong sense of presence-namely, the subjective experience of "being inside" the artificial world. In Head-Mounted Displays-based iVR, visual information is presented directly on the VR headset in front of the user's eyes, thereby minimizing external sensory input and enabling the user to focus exclusively on the virtual environment (Furht, 2008).

To date, there is no clear evidence regarding the added value of iVR for assessing functional communication in PWA, even though Garcia and colleagues already noted the potential of VR to this aim as early as 2007 (Garcia et al., 2007). In consultation with speech and language therapists (SLT), they recommended that VR-scenarios should be ecologically valid, such that they represent everyday activities that occur in a social context. They also suggested that the level of difficulty could be manipulated by introducing noisy environments or highly stressful situations that elicit more spontaneous reactions. Although immersion has been shown to enhance iVR-based word-learning outcomes in neurologically healthy adults and to reduce lexical errors in PWA (Franco et al., 2025), the potential of iVR for functional communication assessments in PWA hasto the best of our knowledge -not been studied. Nevertheless, several studies indicate that non-immersive and semi-immersive VR environments are feasible tools for training communication skills in this population (Amaya et al., 2018; Bryant et al., 2020; Devane et al., 2025; Giachero et al., 2020; Rasheva-Yordanova et al., 2025). Although inconsistent findings regarding the effectiveness of (i)VR-based training of functional communication are reported (Cao et al., 2021; Giachero et al., 2020; Kalinyak-Fliszar et al., 2015; Marshall et al., 2016), there is currently no clear evidence that such (i)VR-based interventions are more effective than other therapeutic approaches, such as face-to-face or computer-based therapy. iVR has also been used to assess non-linguistic cognitive functions in non-aphasic stroke patients through real-life scenarios (e.g., shopping in a virtual supermarket), indicating that immersive VR is not only feasible for this diagnostic purpose (Palmans et al., 2026; Spreij et al., 2022), but may also enhance ecological validity and provide multi-sensory input, such as immediate auditory feedback and a realistic simulated settings, to support optimal understanding of test instructions (Stark, 2025; Wall et al., 2018).

Research aims

The current study aimed to explore the feasibility of using iVR to assess functional communication in PWA. We hypothesised that iVR may be equipped to mitigate the task impurity and ecological validity issues discussed above. Immersion -defined as the psychological state in which individuals perceive themselves as being enveloped by, included in, and interacting with the VR-environment (Berkman & Akan, 2019) -could support PWA in assuming a role during the functional communication tests, thereby improving construct validity. In addition to the fact that iVR allows for the combination of high experimental control, high ecological validity, and reproducibility (Peeters, 2019), there are more reasons for assuming its benefits for language assessment. As indicated above, the virtual environment might support language comprehension during scenario introduction, help PWA to keep the scenario active in working memory, and allow for controlled manipulation of real-world communication demands, for example by adding environmental distractions, such as background noise.

Despite the promising potential of iVR for assessing functional communication, there are also some concerns. Cybersickness -an experience similar to motion sickness -may occur when using iVR via head-mounted displays, although only a small number of adverse events have been reported in neurological populations (Stam et al., 2025). The safe use of VR for persons with balance impairments, such as hemiparesis, which frequently occurs after stroke and may therefore accompany aphasia, must be guaranteed (Park & Lee, 2020). In addition, VR environments may pose a risk of overstimulation for individuals with acquired brain injury, as these persons often report increased sensitivity to auditory and visual stimuli, particularly when stimuli are multisensory (de Sain et al., 2024). This heightened sensitivity may contribute to fatigue, cognitive overload, low concentration, and less focus in stroke-induced populations (Saidi et al., 2026), which may introduce new forms of task impurity. It is therefore important to develop VR environments that are ecologically valid in terms of multisensory stimulation, while avoiding levels of sensory load that exceed those typically encountered in everyday situations. Expectations of iVR-based assessment of functional communication in aphasia are high, yet it remains unclear how feasible it is to use iVR for this goal. By developing a proof-of-concept iVR-based test of functional communication, we explored the feasibility of using iVR in this context, by following the focus areas of feasibility studies as specified by Bowen et al. (2009): acceptability, demand, and limited efficacy. In the development of the proof-of-concept version, we incorporated the needs and ideas of both SLT and PWA. These research aims led to the following two research questions and subquestions: 1.

Is a realistic simulation of daily communication feasible? This research question included sub-questions regarding both the technical feasibility of the simulation and its acceptance by the intended users, namely PWA and SLT.

2. Does iVR simulation provide the expected benefits for assessing functional communication? This research question included the following sub-questions:

Are non-verbal aspects of communication measurable within the simulation? Can construct validity be enhanced through greater presence, as indicated by participants producing only direct speech? And is it possible to adjust the test's complexity by varying the communication demands?

Materials and procedure

The final Phase, 'Testing', involved evaluation of the beta version of the POC iVR-based assessment instrument during individual user-centred test sessions with PWA and SLT.

The individual test sessions lasted between 1 and 1.5 hours on average. The sessions with the SLT were conducted at Radboud University or at the SLT's workplace, whereas the test sessions with the PWA were primarily carried out at the Siza aphasia centres in Arnhem and Tilburg iv . Each session was videotaped for transcription and was organised in the same standardised manner for both PWA and SLT. The only exception was that, as in the focus groups, an aphasia-friendly setting was created for the PWA. In addition, a 5-7-minute interview was conducted at the start of the test session to describe the participants' aphasia based on semi-spontaneous speech. PWA were prompted to describe their previous experience with iVR, their opinion on the digitalisation of daily life, and their experience with gaming. The resulting speech samples were later evaluated using the six spontaneous speech rating scales of the Dutch Aachen Aphasia Test (Graetz et al., 1992).

After receiving general instructions, fitting the Meta Quest 3 headset, and calibrating the iVR environment, participants were presented with the beta version of the iVR-based functional communication test, consisting of the two scenarios with three conditions each. That is, the six test items -comprising the three conditions (i.e., standard, noise, and impatient) for both the Child and Train Station scenarios -were presented in a pseudorandomised order. Sequential administration of the same condition across the two scenarios was avoided. In cases where a participant's response was not comprehensible, feedback cues were provided immediately. When responses were appropriate within the context of the scenarios, the cues were still presented at predefined, pseudorandomised moments across test items. For safety reasons, all participants completed the test session in a seated position, wore the headset no longer than 15 minutes continuously, and could take a break at any time. The Meta Quest 3 volume was set at 8 bars, providing a moderate to loud listening experience, but this could be adjusted to personal preferences.

Subsequently, key domains commonly evaluated in feasibility studies (Bowen et al., 2009) were assessed using both quantitative and qualitative approaches. The acceptability domain comprised visual analogues scale (VAS, range 0 to 10) responses regarding satisfaction or user-experience, including perceived appropriateness of the iVR environment, the feeling of being physically present inside the iVR environment (presence), and the (non)verbal behaviour of the virtual interlocutor. The questions were supported by written keywords and screen recordings of the iVR environment. The Mann-Whitney U test, the nonparametric equivalent of independent t test, was used to compare the VAS scores of the PWA and SLT. With Wilcoxon signed-ranks test, nonparametric equivalent of dependent t tests, the differences in responses per scenario were analysed.

The α level (two-sided) was set at 5% and Pearson's correlation coefficient, r, was used to indicate the strength of the effect. Cohen's criteria (1988 ( , 1992 ( , cited in Field, 2009) ) were used to differentiate between small (r = 0.1), medium (r = 0.3), and large (r = 0.5) effects.

The demand domain included a question for SLT regarding the degree to which they considered the iVR-based test better than the pen-and-paper based one. Limited efficacy was assessed through three lines of inquiry. Firstly, we observed participants' success or failure in carrying out the test and responding to the feedback cues. We observed whether non-verbal aspects of communication (e.g., pointing and gestures) were used to support or replace spoken language. Because the VR headset covered participants' eyes and forehead, other paralinguistic aspects of their responses, including facial expression and gaze direction, were not analysed. We also observed whether the PWA and SLT used direct speech exclusively in the iVR environment, which we interpreted as an indication for their sense of presence within the virtual scenario. Secondly, we assessed whether PWA and SLT perceived the noise and impatient conditions as more communicatively demanding than the standard condition, by asking them to indicate which condition they perceived as most difficult. Because no simple non-parametric repeated-measures test exists for more than two categories, we used Fisher's exact tests with a two-sided α level of 5% to assess differences in perceived communicative difficulty between conditions within each scenario. Odds ratios were used as an effect size. Thirdly, to further assess whether the iVR scenarios elicited only direct speech, we administered the iVR-based test to 40 neurologically healthy control participants with no professional background in speech and language therapy, and therefore unfamiliar with the ANELT. For 20 participants, the standard conditions of the Train Station and Child scenarios were presented in a counterbalanced order. In the remaining participants, all six test items (both scenarios with three conditions each) were presented in a counterbalanced order. In all 40 participants, either 6 or 10 scenarios from the adapted (Ruiter et al., 2011; Ruiter et al., 2023) pen-and-paper version of the Amsterdam-Nijmegen Everyday Language Test (ANELT; Blomert et al., 1995) were also administered in order to compare the use of direct speech across the two test formats.

Results

Phase 1: Understand

The online questionnaire on current practices and requirements among SLT revealed that the most frequently mentioned elements of respondents' definition of functional communication were communication (n = 26), daily situation (n = 21), transaction (n = 19), non-verbal (n = 8), verbal (n = 7), and effectiveness (n = 7). To capture functional communication as comprehensively as possible, respondents most frequently indicated that a functional communication test should assess the following modes of communication: gestures (n = 29), facial expressions (n = 20), written language production (n = 18), spoken language production (n = 17), and pointing (n = 16).

Regarding the subskills of functional communication to be assessed, (non-)verbal effectiveness (M = 4.9), (communicating effectively) in a range of everyday situations (M = 4.8), one-on-one communication (M = 4.7), and using various modes of communication (M = 4.6) received the highest ratings from the respondents on a 5-point scale.

Among the tests and tasks used to assess functional communication, the following -sometimes combined -were mentioned most frequently: a dynamic assessment approach (Hersh et al., 2013) et al., 1995) (n = 26). When asked to indicate the ecological validity of these test results, most respondents (n = 26) indicated them to be representative to a certain extent. The next most common response was that these results were only slightly ecologically valid (n = 9). Of the 38 SLT, 27 indicated that they administer the ANELT in clinical practice.

The other 11 were familiar with the ANELT but did not use it themselves. The most frequently mentioned limitations of the ANELT included: the omission to assess the skill to switch to other communication channels than spoken language (n = 21), the tendency of the scenarios to elicit more monologic than dialogic discourse (n = 18), the lack of anchor points for objectively scoring effectiveness (n = 18), and the absence of opportunities to assess lengthier conversations, that is those exceeding five minutes (n = 18).

When explicitly asked to rank the limitations of the ANELT and other assessment tools for functional communication in terms of priority for improvement in a new iVRbased assessment tool (with lower average scores indicating higher priority), respondents identified the following as most important: the ability to assess the skill to switch to other communication channels than spoken language (M = 3.1), anchor points for objectively scoring effectiveness (M = 3.4), as well as representative scenarios of daily conversation (M = 3.4). As a final question, respondents were asked which additional aspects of communication should be assessable in the new iVR-based test to provide a more comprehensive picture of functional communication. The extent of the support required from the conversation partner (14%), personalized scenarios (11%), and a dialogue involving one speaker and several interlocutors (7%) were most frequently mentioned.

Phase 2: Explore

In addition to the SLTs' responses to the online survey regarding current practices in functional communication assessment and their requirements for the development of the iVR-based functional communication test, the SLT who participated in the focus group noted that functional communication could also be defined by its social-interactional characteristics, rather than being solely transactional in nature. They also indicated that functional communication involves not only conveying information but also comprehending incoming messages. They emphasized the importance of several findings from Phase 1, including the need to assess non-verbal aspects of communication alongside verbal communication, and the ability to initiate other-initiated repair (i.e., the SLT prompts the speaker to repair their response) for within the iVR environment to determine the PWA's level of independence in functional communication.

Regarding the complexity of daily communication, PWA highlighted that stress negatively affected their functional communication skills. They explained that stress could arise when the conversation partner spoke quickly and allowed little time for the PWA to respond, as well as when they were unexpectedly confronted with a communicative situation and had to respond immediately (e.g., an incoming phone call).

PWA differed in the extent to which they felt that either non-verbal or verbal background noise negatively affected their communication skills, which in part seemed to be related to the fact that they wore earplugs regularly: Ik kan het een beetje rustig vertellen hier, kort. En ik kan … af en toe een beetje lawaai, een beetje moeilijk (wijst naar oren). Dus nu, ik ga altijd oordopjes hier.

Een beetje rustiger.

I can tell it a bit calmly here, briefly. And I can… sometimes a bit of noise, a bit difficult (points to ears). So now, I always go earplugs here. A bit quieter (PWA 8).

PWA's suggestions for improving the assessment of daily communication included incorporating the extent to which PWA inform their conversational partner about their aphasia, for example, by showing a card stating, 'I have aphasia'. They also emphasized the need to evaluate the influence of background noise, the degree of patience demonstrated by the interlocutor, the difficulty of the conversational topic, and the amount of information received on verbal effectiveness and efficiency. Furthermore, the difference between in-person conversation and telephone conversation was considered important to incorporate in the iVR-based test.

When asked to prioritize which aspects of functional communication should be evaluated in the iVR-based test, the PWA in focus group F2a (n = 6; see Table 1) reached consensus on the importance of including the following elements: telephone communication, informing the conversational partner about aphasia, one-to-one versus group communication, time pressure, varying degrees of interlocutor patience and support, and communication in urgent situations. The PWA in focus group F2b (n = 4; see Table 1) similarly highlighted the relevance of telephone communication, interlocutor patience and support, time pressure, and urgent communicative situations. In addition, unlike the first group, they emphasized the importance of the interlocutor's speech rate, the amount of information provided, and the conversational topic. None of the PWA indicated that background noise was essential to include in the iVR-based test; in both groups (F2a and F2b), this was classified under the category 'uncertainty regarding its importance'.

Phase 3: Materialize

Alpha version of the iVR-based functional communication test

Building on our previous research on the ANELT (Ruiter et al., 2011; Ruiter et al., 2023), we used, with permission, the Child scenario from the original pen-and-paper Dutch version of the ANELT (Blomert et al., 1995) and the Train Station scenario, taken from the Dutch and adapted version of this test (Ruiter et al., 2023). In the Child scenario, the situation is described in which a child has fallen, and the general practitioner needs to be called because the situation does not look good, followed by the question : 'What would you say?

The Train Station scenario goes as follows: 'You are at the train station. You need to go to Zwolle [a city in the Netherlands]. The train will depart from another platform. You are in quite a hurry, but you don't know where to go. You walk up to the train conductor and say…?' v.

These two scenarios were selected because we assumed that visually illustrating the context in the iVR-environment (i.e., seeing a child fall on the pavement in front of your living-room window) might support comprehension of the relative complex verbal instructions. In addition, we considered these scenarios unsuitable for being addressed solely through appropriately pointing to an object, for example to indicate the television you would like to buy in an electronics shop. This aligns with the aim of the ANTAT, which primarily measures verbal communication. Other reasons for selecting these scenarios were that the PWA had indicated that time pressure (i.e., having to make an emergency call to the general practitioner versus having to catch the train) hinders communication. They also had indicated that making a phone call was more challenging than communicating in person.

Drawing on the focus group discussions and prioritizations of factors that complicate everyday communication, three conditions were created for each scenario: a) a standard condition, in which the virtual interlocutor was patient and understanding and no background noise was present, b) a 'noise' condition, in which background noise was added (i.e., music containing pseudo-speech fragments in the Child scenario and travel announcements in the Train Station scenario), while the virtual interlocutor remained patient, c) an 'impatient' condition, in which there was no background noise, but the interlocutor responded impatiently (e.g., the train conductor who avoids friendly, smiling eye contact), as PWA had indicated that impatience evoked internal stress, which in turn hinders verbal communication.

The iVR test did not include any practice items; however, in line with standardised assessment procedures, the iVR-based ANELT scenarios were largely scripted (see Figure 1). Five seconds after being invited to explore the iVR-environment (by being prompted to look to their right and left), a voice-over automatically began introducing the scenario (e.g., 'You are at the train station. You need to go to Zwolle. The train departs from another platform. You are in quite a hurry, but you don't know where to go.

Fortunately, there is a train conductor'). Approximately five seconds after the voice-over ended, the virtual interlocutor automatically initiated the interaction (e.g., 'How can I help you?'). Participants were then given time to respond (non-)verbally. The test administrator could conclude the scenario by manually triggering the virtual interlocutor's response indicating comprehension (e.g., 'Okay. Please come with me.

We're going to help you'). If the test administrator did not fully understand the participant's message, they could manually trigger the virtual interlocutor to provide either implicit feedback (i.e., 'Sorry?)' or explicit feedback (i.e., 'Sorry, I really don't understand. Could you explain it in another way?). This consistency in scenario set-up was intended not only to improve testing standards, but also to facilitate PWA in understanding the task instructions (Galliers et al., 2011).

The two scenarios were developed on a proprietary iVR platform provided by The Simulation Crew, the software developer partnering in this research project. The platform is built in the Unity game engine and combines pre-scripted dialogue trees with a rulebased behaviour system that controls the virtual interlocutor's verbal and non-verbal responses. Audio-recordings were generated automatically, and Microsoft Azure Speech Service was used to create a rough verbatim transcript of the participant's response, including time stamps. The data logs also included the number of times explicit or implicit feedback was provided. As hand tracking was incorporated, participants did not need to hold controllers (Bryant et al., 2025; Galliers et al., 2011; Vaezipour et al., 2019) and could see their hands, allowing the tester to observe pointing and gestural communication.

The software ran on a Meta Quest 3 standalone headset, allowing user-based assessment to be carried out at the Siza aphasia centres. This headset was also selected because it is relatively lightweight, making it comfortable for participants to wear. Using a laptop, test administrators could monitor the participants' view within the iVR environment, select scenarios and conditions, (combined) replay of the voice-over and the virtual interlocutor's initiation of conversation, and conclude scenarios. As a result, participants did not have to remove their headset or use hand controllers to navigate the scenarios, allowing them to fully concentrate on their communication (Vaezipour et al., 2023). Test administrators did not have to speak as part of the assessment, which further improves test standardisation.

From alpha version to beta version

As indicated before, the alpha version was evaluated in a focus group with 3 PWA. They indicated that the test instructions (i.e., voice-over and text of virtual interlocutor) as well as the conclusion of the scenarios were very clear, but that the child's injuries should be depicted a bit more severely in the Child scenario to justify calling the general practitioner. We therefore adapted the software by presenting the child's leg after the fall in a more clearly distorted manner to make the injury appear more severe. Regarding the Train Station scenario, PWA indicated that the train conductor did not express impatience clearly enough, and the fact that he was standing in front of the participant immediately was not considered very realistic. They suggested that the conductor should first be located among fast-moving passengers on the platform. Unfortunately, however, realising visually convincing crowd behaviour within the performance constraints of the standalone headset -without compromising frame rate and thereby increasing the risk of cybersickness -would have required considerable additional development effort that fell outside the scope of this proof-of-concept. Instead, we modified the scenario by having the conductor look at his watch from time to time while participants formulated their response and having him walk slowly toward the participant rather than standing directly in front of them.

As part of the limited efficacy assessment, we observed PWA's and SLT's success or failure in carrying out the test and responding to the feedback cues. None of the participants needed prompts in the remaining test items. In addition, 2 out of 7 PWA started their response directly after the voice-over, before the virtual interlocutor had spoken. For 1 PWA this was during the first test item, for the other the fifth. Another PWA first described the iVR environment, before providing a response to the virtual interlocutor. Three of the 6 PWA asked for the (combined) repetition of the voice-over and the virtual interlocutor's initiation of conversation. Two of them made this request during the first test item. In all participants, both the explicit and implicit feedback cues prompted initiation of repair. All participants tolerated wearing the headset, with none reporting nausea, other physical discomfort, or requesting additional breaks.

Whereas indirect speech was observed in at least 30% of their responses to the pen-and-paper version of the ANELT, none of the participants produced indirect speech in the iVR-based test. Put differently, speakers unfamiliar with the ANELT produced direct speech exclusively in the iVR-based test, similarly to the PWA and SLT that participated in the user-based assessments.

Discussion and conclusion

The current study explored the feasibility of using iVR to assess functional communication in PWA. After developing a proof-of-concept version -incorporating the needs and ideas of both SLT and PWA -we first investigated whether iVR enables a realistic simulation of everyday communication, including its technical feasibility and acceptability for the intended users. Second, we analysed whether iVR simulation provides the expected benefits for assessing functional communication.

Regarding the first research question -whether iVR allows realistic simulation of everyday communication -both SLT and PWA generally perceived the environment as realistic, understood what was expected of them, and indicated that they would respond in a manner similar to how they do in everyday communicative situations. Nevertheless, the following points of attention can be distilled. In order to allow user-based assessment to be carried out at the Siza aphasia centres, we used a standalone VR-headset. Achieving realistic appearance and behaviour of multiple animated bystanders within the performance budget of such a mobile device -without framerate drops that increase the risk of cybersickness -requires substantial development effort. This optimisation work fell outside the scope of the present TRL 3 proof-of-concept, and we therefore did not populate the train platform with fellow passengers walking back and forth in the Train Station scenario, despite indications from PWA and SLT that this would enhance ecological validity and the sense of time pressure. Another consequence of using iVR in stroke-induced populations was that participants were less able to physically approach objects or other individuals, because, for safety reasons, they were required to remain seated. While safety was a primary concern, this may have negatively impacted ecological validity. Lastly, in the impatient condition of the Train Station scenario, the train conductor was generally not perceived as impatient but rather as grumpy. In the Child Scenario the difference in intonation of the doctor's assistant in the standard and impatient condition was too subtle. These results suggest that designing the non-verbal communicative behaviour of virtual interlocutors requires more fine-tuning than could be achieved within the time frame of this feasibility study.

The second question related to the degree to which iVR provided the expected benefits for assessing functional communication. Although SLT expected the iVR test to require at least as much administration time as the pen-and-paper based version, they perceived it as having clear advantages over the pen-and-paper version. The reported advantages included improved test standardisation, as the therapist more clearly adopted the role of test administrator rather than assuming a role within the scenario, the added value of effective feedback cues as also included in the Scenario Test (van der Meulen et al., 2008), and enhanced construct validity resulting from the use of direct speech style. This research question also included a sub-aim to investigate whether non-verbal aspects of the users' communicative behaviour are measurable. As indicated before, we selected scenarios from the original pen-and-paper Dutch version of the ANELT (Blomert et al., 1995) and the Dutch and adapted version of this test (Ruiter et al., 2023) that would not merely elicit a non-verbal response, such as pointing out which bouquet to order in a flower shop. The scenarios predominantly elicited verbal responses, and beat gestures were the only non-verbal behaviour observed. Although the potential added value of measuring gestures in iVR-based functional communication assessments requires further investigation, we would like to highlight that the beats captured within the current study suggest that hand tracking can support gesture analysis-provided that gestures are produced in front of the user to remain within the field of view of the headset cameras.

A secondary aim of this research question was to analyse direct speech production in the iVR-based functional communication test, based on the hypothesis that experienced presence in the iVR environment -and the resulting improvement in role-taking-would elicit more direct speech and thereby improve construct validity (compared to the penand-paper based assessments). In line with this hypothesis all participants, the PWA, SLT and neurologically healthy control participants with no professional background in speech and language pathology only used direct speech when providing responses appropriate to the scenarios, which suggests that using iVR instead of pen-and-paper for functional communication assessment improves construct validity. Additional evidence may be obtained in future research by conducting a direct comparison between scores from the pen-and-paper version of the ANTAT and those from the iVR version of the test.

A third aim of this research question was to determine the possibility of adjusting the test's complexity by varying the communication demands. The results indicate that despite the still suboptimal simulations of impatience in the virtual interlocutor across both scenarios -participants generally reported that the impatient condition of the Train Station scenario and the noise condition of the Child scenario were more difficult to respond to than the standard conditions. This suggests that, although further refinement of the virtual interlocutor's impatient behaviour is required, manipulating communicative complexity is technically feasible.

We observed considerable inter-individual variability among PWA in the extent to which a non-aphasia-friendly conversational partners or background noise are perceived to negatively affect their communicative behaviour. We emphasise that this variability does not reflect the degree to which spoken language is objectively compromised by these manipulations of communicative complexity, but rather how participant perceive their impact. These perceived differences may reflect premorbid and postmorbid variations in sensitivity to stimuli (Nelson et al., 2023); however, they mayat least in part -also reflect the degree to which self-monitoring abilities, often associated with executive control (see Mandal et al., 2020, for a discussion), are impaired. As noted in the Introduction, non-linguistic cognitive impairments, including deficits in selfmonitoring and executive control, are common in PWA (e.g., Akkad et al., 2023; Frankel et al., 2007; Murray, 2012). Since we did not assess these non-linguistic cognitive skills in the participants with aphasia, we cannot rule out that such cognitive impairments may have hindered some participants' ability to validly rate the extent to which these manipulations negatively influenced their functional communication skills, potentially leading to an underestimation of these effects. Further research is needed to determine when external factors such as background noise and an unfriendly conversation partner are obstructive to everyday communication, and for which PWA (Dalemans et al., 2010; Harmon, 2020; Harmon et al., 2024; Nelson et al., 2023).

Our study is limited by the fact that the proof-of-concept version of the iVR-based functional communication test did not include any practice items. As a result, some participants required a prompt from the tester to initiate their response after the voiceover and virtual interlocutor had finished, whereas others began responding immediately after the voice-over. Importantly, this issue typically occurred during the first test items presented, suggesting that the inclusion of practice items could effectively mitigate this lack of clarity.

Another limitation is that the manipulation of communicative demands was assessed qualitatively. That is, we analysed participants' perceived effects of background noise and an impatient interlocutor on their functional communication skills. As discussed above, these perceptions could have been negatively influenced (underestimated) by diminished executive control. In line with Nelson et al. (2023), a quantitative measure of verbal functional communication would have provided a more objective assessment. Although such methods are available for (non-)verbal effectiveness and efficiency (Ruiter et al., 2011; Ruiter et al., 2023), they could not be validly applied to the present data. This is because all test items (three conditions per scenario) were administered to all participants in a counterbalanced order, potentially resulting in learning effects due to repeated exposure.

Conclusion

The results obtained in the current study suggest that it is technically feasible to create iVR environments for the assessment of functional communication in persons with aphasia that are acceptable to (end) users and that enhance the construct validity of functional communication tests. Despite remaining areas for improvement-such as enhancing the sense of time pressure through more effective non-verbal representations of the virtual interlocutor's impatience and further exploration of the added value of ASR-iVR was found to have a clear added value for diagnostic purposes. In particular, the increased immersion in the scenarios, and the resulting consistent use of direct speech, appear to contribute to a more accurate measurement of functional communication skills.
