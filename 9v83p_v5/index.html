This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Dissociative experiences are common and transdiagnostic. Yet causal evidence for psychological mechanisms of dissociation remains scarce, particularly in youth. Our group has developed a translational cognitive-behavioural model of a subset of common dissociative experiences (FSA-dissociation), but causal relationships proposed within this model remain to be tested.

This study sought to experimentally test three causal relationships proposed in the FSAdissociation CBT model using an interventionist-causal paradigm. A benefit of this method is the opportunity it provides to simultaneously develop targeted therapeutic techniques whilst empirically testing the underlying theory.

We conducted a randomised multiple baseline experimental design study with nine NHS patients (aged 16 to 23), targeting three hypothesised mechanisms of change: cognitive appraisals of dissociation, perseverative thinking, and affect intolerance. The study was registered on ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT06054009). Each participant received four sessions of individual CBT with a clinical psychologist targeting one hypothesised mechanism.

Analysis indicated successful targeting of cognitive appraisals, and partial success for affect intolerance and perseverative thinking. Findings tentatively support cognitive appraisals and affect intolerance as candidate causal mechanisms in youth dissociation. Any effect of targeting perseverative thinking was better accounted for by reduced cognitive appraisals.

Although phase instability limited inference, this study offers novel causal insights into transdiagnostic dissociation and informs early-stage treatment development for this presentation. Namely, it suggests that future psychological interventions for transdiagnostic dissociation in youth should incorporate targeting of cognitive appraisals, but that further research is required to determine whether affect intolerance constitutes a therapeutic target in this context.

Testing causal mechanisms of transdiagnostic dissociation in 16-to-25year-olds: A randomised multiple baseline experimental design using brief targeted CBT.

Dissociation involves confusing and upsetting feelings of unreality, unfamiliarity, or disconnection in relation to your mind, body, or surroundings (Černis et al., 2020). For example, some people describe being detached from their own emotions, including affection for loved ones, or feeling profoundly 'strange', as though they are living life from behind a thick pane of glass.

In children, adolescents, and young adults, such experiences are prevalent: more so than in later stages of life (Černis et al., 2023). A recent meta-analysis (Kate et al., 2020) suggests 11.4% of undergraduate students meet the threshold for a dissociative disorder, with a still higher proportion self-reporting at least some level of dissociative experience (17%). These relatively high rates are of particular clinical importance, since dissociative experiences have been linked to self-harm and suicide (Černis et al., 2019; Foote et al., 2008; Vine et al., 2020), and with increased severity (La Mela et al., 2010) and incomplete treatment-response (Bae et al., 2016) of comorbid mental health diagnoses.

Crucially, dissociation may constitute a novel target for early intervention. Transdiagnostic dissociative experiences are increasingly being implicated in the development and maintenance of other mental health problems, such as anxiety, depression, and psychosis (Černis, Evans, et al., 2021; Černis, Molodynski, et al., 2022; Černis, Antonović, et al., 2025).

Given that the incidence of new mental health difficulties peaks in adolescence and early adulthood (Díaz-Caneja & Guloksuz, 2024), and that dissociation is also more common at this age, the development of a targeted intervention for transdiagnostic dissociation in young people is a clinical priority for the field.

Our previous work has laid the foundations of the translational process required to develop such an intervention (see Clark, 2004). We first identified a subtype of common dissociative experiences that are unified by a core phenomenological experience of a 'felt sense of anomaly' (FSA) (Černis, Beierl, et al., 2021; Černis et al., 2020), thus also incorporating depersonalisation and derealisation experiences, such as feelings of unreality, disconnection, and unfamiliarity of one's body, perceptions, and internal and external world. This subtype of dissociative experiences (FSA-dissociation) was then demonstrated to be common and transdiagnostic (Černis, Evans, et al., 2021; Černis, Molodynski, et al., 2022; Černis et al., 2026).

Having defined the core problem to be addressed, we then identified plausible mechanisms underlying FSA-dissociation, resulting in a theoretical cognitive model of the problem (Černis, Ehlers, et al., 2022; Černis, Johns, et al., 2025). Briefly, this model posits that FSAdissociation is interpreted as an internal threat and maintained by two mutually reinforcing feedback loops. A 'foreground' loop of negative cognitive appraisals and counterproductive coping strategies (including rumination) directly sustains the experience, whilst a 'background' loop of affect intolerance and low subjective self-efficacy provides the context for the initial and continued threat interpretation.

Whilst some observational support has been found for aspects of this theoretical model in young people (Černis et al., 2026; Esteban Serna et al., in prep.), to proceed with the translational intervention development process, the model must now be experimentally validated. That is, evidence is required to support the proposed causal relationships between the mechanisms outlined in the model and FSA-dissociation. Further, methods for targeting and ameliorating the mechanisms must be identified: if they are not amenable to psychological intervention, they cannot be targets in a model-driven therapy protocol.

The interventionist-causal paradigm (e.g., Kendler & Campbell, 2009) has proven useful in recent similar programmes of translational research (Freeman, Dunn, et al., 2015; Freeman, Waite, et al., 2015) as a method for pursuing both objectives simultaneously. In this paradigm, a therapeutic technique is used to selectively manipulate (ameliorate) a mechanism and determine its indirect 'downstream' effect on the problem of interest (i.e., in the present context, determining whether dissociation subsequently also improves). This method therefore requires two checks: first, that the manipulation (intervention) had an effect on the targeted mechanism; and second, whether that successful manipulation has any indirect effect on the clinical outcome.

At this early stage of the translational process, it is prudent to establish 'proof of concept' prior to larger-scale testing. For this purpose, single case experimental designs (SCEDs) are a valuable methodological choice (Krasny-Pacini & Evans, 2018), since with careful design such studies have high internal validity, enabling evaluation of causality despite small sample sizes (Lobo et al., 2017). SCED studies, or 'single-case experiments' (Morley, 2018), measure dependent variables repeatedly across phases where the intervention is delivered or withheld: effectively allowing each participant to act as their own control arm (Lobo et al., 2017). Multiple baseline designs are a form of SCED that retain these benefits and may be more appropriate in psychological intervention research where it is more practicable to demonstrate replication of effects across multiple participants, rather than through repeated application and withdrawal of the intervention.

The aim of this study, therefore, is to test via multiple baseline design (MBD) methodology the three largest psychological contributors to FSA-dissociation as identified in previous cross-sectional research.

For Intervention 1, the mechanism of interest was negative cognitive appraisals of the dissociative experiences. In network analyses of observational data, this proposed mechanism demonstrated a causal effect of 0.73 in a non-clinical adult group (Černis, Ehlers, et al., 2022) and 0.72 in a psychosis group (Černis, Molodynski, et al., 2022). Further, in an adolescent group, cognitive appraisals of dissociation were the dominant predictor of transdiagnostic dissociation in a hierarchical regression model (β=0.753; Lofthouse et al., 2023).

Intervention 2 aimed to target perseverative negative thinking (rumination), since this demonstrated a causal effect of 0.31 in a non-clinical group, and 0.52 in a psychosis group (Černis, Ehlers, et al., 2022; Černis, Molodynski, et al., 2022).

Affect intolerance (aversion to the experience of one's own emotions) was the focus of Intervention 3 (causal effect 0.26 non-clinically, 0.41 in psychosis; (Černis, Ehlers, et al., 2022; Černis, Molodynski, et al., 2022). Recent longitudinal research supports the relevance of this construct to FSA-dissociation in young people. Černis et al., (2026) report that avoidance and denial of affect (expressive suppression) was the only significant mediator between dissociation scores over one month in a group of 3076 adolescents.

In order to determine the success of each intervention in modifying its intended target, the primary outcomes in this study were measures of these three psychological factors. The secondary outcome was FSA-dissociation. Where an intervention was successful, subsequent reductions in transdiagnostic dissociation may indicate preliminary experimental support for the proposed causal role of the targeted mechanism. Such evidence is required to validate the theoretical model and refine treatment development efforts. Successful interventions that reduce FSA-dissociation would be candidates for inclusion in a future targeted multi-modular intervention for transdiagnostic dissociation. As such, the current study constitutes a 'proof of concept' study adopting an interventionist-causal paradigm to simultaneously test the theoretical model and inform early-stage treatment development.

Method

Ethics

This study received ethical approval from the North West -Greater Manchester Central NHS Research Ethics Committee (23/NW/0163) and was registered with ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT06054009). The study protocol was preregistered on Open Science Framework (OSF): https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/8VEZM.

The authors abided by the Ethical Principles of Psychologists and Code of Conduct as set out by the BABCP and BPS, and informed consent to participate and for the results to be published was obtained.

Design & Procedure

The study used a randomised multiple baseline experimental design, with an 'AB + Follow-Up' structure (i.e., baseline phase, intervention phase, follow-up phase with no active intervention or therapist contact).

All participants completed baseline, post-therapy, and one-month follow-up research assessments with a research assistant (MA or GS) who was not blind to allocation, but was independent from therapy delivery. In the final assessment, participants were invited to give verbal and/or written qualitative feedback about the therapy and study procedures.

For the baseline phase, each participant was randomly allocated to wait either one, two, three, or four weeks between the baseline assessment and the start of the intervention. Randomisation of the baseline length is a design feature recommended to improve the scientific credibility of the study (Kazdin, 2019; Kratochwill et al., 2022). Randomisation was carried out via the opening of a sealed envelope by the research assistant once all baseline measures had been fully completed. Envelopes were prepared by a colleague who was not involved in the study. The intervention phase was five weeks. In this time, participants received four 1:1 one-hour long psychological therapy sessions with a qualified clinical psychologist (EČ) (see Intervention, below). The follow-up phase was one month. In this phase, there was no further contact with the therapist.

See Figure 1 for the CONSORT diagram of the study illustrating its design and summarising participant recruitment and retention.

Participants

Participants were outpatients recruited from services for young people in Birmingham Women's and Children's NHS Foundation Trust (BWC) mental health services through clinician referral and self-referral via posters and leaflets displayed in BWC waiting rooms. Additionally, we circulated study information to the research interest email list of the University of Birmingham Centre for Human Brain Health.

Inclusion criteria were: aged 16 years or over; experiencing significant levels of 'felt sense of anomaly'-type dissociation defined as a score within the 'moderate severe' or 'severe' range on the ČEFSA-14 (i.e., score of 39 or above) (Černis et al., 2023).

Exclusion criteria were: confirmed diagnosis of an Axis II ("personality") disorder; primary diagnosis of alcohol/substance dependency, organic syndrome, or learning disability; presence of risk issues that would be a clinical priority above managing dissociative symptoms (e.g., severe self-harm; active suicidal behaviour; etc.); and current engagement in any other individual psychological therapy (or psychological therapy due to begin within the participation window for this study).

We aimed to recruit 4 participants to each of the three interventions, and for participants receiving the same intervention to be recruited soon after one another. This target was set since Kazdin, (2021) describes three or more baselines as typical for a multiple-baseline design. For Intervention 1, one person declined to participate on the day of the scheduled (concurrent) baseline, and another was admitted to inpatient care shortly beforehand, resulting in only two participants for this intervention. As a result, the methodology was amended to non-concurrent baselines for the following interventions. Recruitment for Intervention 2 was closed at three participants due to recruitment delays. Four participants were recruited for Intervention 3, as planned. Since Kazdin, (2021) states that 'a minimal [sic] of two [baselines] is possible but infrequently used' (p.58), the data for Intervention 1 were retained despite under-recruiting.

Measures

Two kinds of measures were employed in this study: 'standard' measures, which were selfreport validated psychometric measures completed at each of the three assessments -each measuring an element of the FSA-model -and 'target' measures (Morley, 2018).

Target measures were two visual analogue scales (VAS) completed daily (in baseline phase), or every other day (in intervention and follow-up phases). The frequency was reduced in the latter two phases to reduce participant burden and support participant retention. Target measures asked participants to rate from 0 to 100: 1) how much they have been bothered by the intervention-specific psychological factor (i.e., cognitive appraisals, perseverative thinking, or affect intolerance) since the last rating, and 2) the severity of the distress caused by their dissociative experiences since the last rating. Measures were completed on hardcopy forms, or sent via text or email to the research assistant, according to participant preference.

In addition to the standard measures described below, at each assessment point, participants answered one item asking the frequency of recent self-harming behaviours ('In the past two weeks, how often have you deliberately self-harmed (e.g. cutting, burning, etc.) without intending to die?'), with a Likert scale rating from 0 (never) to 4 (daily or almost daily).

At baseline only, participants also completed demographics questions and questions regarding their previous receipt of psychological therapy. At consent, participants gave permission for the research team to access their medical records to extract their recorded diagnosis and prescribed medication. This was done by EČ whilst participants were enrolled in the study.

Černis Felt Sense of Anomaly scale (short-form) (ČEFSA-14; Černis et al., 2023)

The ČEFSA-14 measures FSA-dissociation over the past two weeks on a Likert scale from 0 (never) to 4 (always). Higher scores indicate more frequent FSA-dissociation, with an indicative scoring guide available for interpretation. This scale has excellent psychometric properties (Cronbach's alpha=0.92), and demonstrates measure invariance across gender, clinical status, and age (from 13 to approximately 65 years) (Černis et al., 2023). (CAD-P; Černis, Bird, et al., 2021) The CAD-P assesses catastrophic cognitive appraisals of dissociation. It consists of 13 items, such as 'I can't trust my own mind', rated on a 5-point Likert scale (0 never to 4 always). The total sum score ranges between 0-52, with higher scores reflecting greater frequency of negative cognitive appraisals of dissociation. This scale is psychometrically robust, with Cronbach's alpha 0.92 in two large groups of adolescents (Lofthouse et al., 2023; Shipp et al., 2023).

Cognitive Appraisals of Dissociation in Psychosis measure

Responses to Dissociation scale (RTD; Černis, Ehlers, et al., 2022) Using six items (e.g., 'I try to focus on something else'), this scale measures the use of common coping strategies (safety behaviours) when experiencing dissociation. Higher scores reflect greater use of coping strategies, rated from 0 (never) to 4 (always). The scale has demonstrated good internal reliability (Cronbach's alpha=0.67 in a non-clinical group; 0.69 in a large psychosis group; Černis, Ehlers, et al., 2022; Černis, Molodynski, et al., 2022).

Perseverative Thinking Questionnaire (PTQ; Ehring et al., 2011) The PTQ measures how respondents typically engage in negative ruminative thinking, with higher scores indicating more perseverative thinking. Fifteen items scored from 1 (never) to 5 (always) include items such as 'I keep asking myself questions without finding an answer'. This is a well-validated scale and has demonstrated high internal reliability in a large adolescent group (n=1209; Cronbach's alpha=0.94) (Lofthouse et al., 2023).

Online Alexithymia Scale (amended) (OAS; Černis, Ehlers, et al., 2022) Validated by Černis, Ehlers, et al., (2022), this version of the OAS (Thompson, 2007) focuses on difficulties identifying and describing one's feelings (e.g., 'When asked which emotion I'm feeling, I frequently don't know the answer). All 11 items are rated from 1 (strongly agree) to 5 (strongly disagree) with one reverse-coded item. Higher scores indicate lower alexithymia. This version of the scale has demonstrated high internal reliability in a large general population group (n=6161; Cronbach's alpha=0.84) (Černis, Ehlers, et al., 2022).

Affect Intolerance Scale (AIS; Stapinski et al., 2014)

Across 30 items (rated 1 strongly disagree to 6 strongly agree), the AIS measures beliefs and perceptions of negative emotions (e.g., 'I fear that negative feelings put my body under a lot of stress'). Higher scores indicate stronger affect intolerance. This scale demonstrated excellent internal reliability (Cronbach's alpha=0.95; Černis, Ehlers, et al., 2022).

General Self Efficacy scale (GSE; Schwarzer & Jerusalem, 2012)

Higher scores on the 10-item GSE indicate greater subjective coping ability (self-efficacy). Items are rated 1 (not at all true) to 4 (exactly true), and include 'If I am in trouble, I can usually think of a solution'. This scale has demonstrated high internal reliability (Cronbach's alpha=0.92) (Černis, Ehlers, et al., 2022)

Intervention

Participants received four one-hour sessions of individual therapy with a qualified clinical psychologist with expertise in dissociative difficulties (EČ). Sessions took place in-person at the University of Birmingham or the participant's home, according to their preference. Therapy was provided in addition to usual care. Procedural fidelity was not independently assessed.

In each intervention, the first session included socialisation to the therapy session format, and psychoeducation and formulation to describe how the targeted mechanism relates to the participant's dissociative experiences. Sessions 1 to 3 included learning and practicing a targeted active change technique, and the final session focused on consolidation of new information and planning for the participant to continue therapeutic change independently.

The key change techniques were: cognitive restructuring (e.g., Beck, 2021) (Intervention 1), and worry postponement (e.g., Wells, 1999) (Intervention 2). For Intervention 3, following psychoeducation (emotion literacy), Socratic dialogue was used to identify and challenge negative beliefs about emotions. Motivational interviewing was then used to explore participants' readiness to change their relationship with their emotions, and tailored suggestions of relevant DBT skills were offered where appropriate, alongside supported practice to incrementally approach emotions. Fuller description and justification of the interventions is included in Supplementary Materials.

Analysis

For each intervention, standard and target measure data were analysed to determine whether the mechanism of interest (i.e., cognitive appraisals, perseverative thinking, affect intolerance) was effectively targeted by the intervention. Indication of significant reduction in the intended mechanism was taken as a successful manipulation of the mechanism. Next, for participants where the intervention successfully manipulated the targeted mechanism, standard and target measure data for dissociation were analysed. Given our interest in causal mechanisms of dissociation, the remainder of the standard measure data was subject to exploratory analysis and used to inform interpretation of the results (see Supplementary Material).

To determine whether changes in standard measure scores were meaningful, data were analysed using the Jacobson and Truax method for evaluating reliable change (Morley & Dowzer, 2014). Reliable change thresholds for each scale were calculated using reliability coefficients and standard deviations from peer-reviewed published studies (see Supplementary Material). Changes were considered reliable where they exceeded 1.96 (corresponding to the 95% confidence interval of the standard normal distribution) times the standard error of difference for the scale. Clinically significant change could not be calculated, as none of the scales had established clinical cut-offs or normative comparison data. Note that this constitutes an additional analysis not presented in the preregistered protocol: https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/8VEZM.

Target measure data are presented graphically, and visual and statistical analysis was used to assess within-and between-phase data patterns, as per the recommendations for SCEDs (Lobo et al., 2017). Within-phase analysis included determining: level (mean, median, and standard deviation); trend (visual analysis); and stability (whether 80-90% of datapoints fall within 15% of the median; Lobo et al., 2017). Between-phase analysis included visual analysis of immediacy of effect and consistency of data patterns, and calculation of a nonparametric effect size statistic (Tau-U; Parker et al., 2011) to quantify the magnitude and direction of change relative to baseline phase data. Tau-U calculations were performed using a web-based calculator (Vannest et al., 2016).

Results

The demographic characteristics of participants are shown in Table 1. The mean age of participants was 19.8 years. The majority (4 participants; 44%) were White, 2 (22%) Black, 2 (22%) mixed/multiple backgrounds, and 1 (11%) Asian. 8 of the 9 participants had previously received psychological therapy before, but only 1 had received therapy specifically for dissociation. The mean ČEFSA score at baseline was 44.2 (SD=6.6), which is in the 'moderately severe' range. Only one participant was not prescribed any psychiatric medication (P6). All other participants were prescribed an SSRI (escitalopram 20mg; fluoxetine 30-40mg; sertraline 25-100mg). Additionally, two participants were prescribed antipsychotic medication (P1 and P4; aripiprazole 15mg and olanzapine 10mg); P4 was prescribed lamotrigine, and P5 melatonin.

Intervention 1: Cognitive appraisals

Primary objective: Confirmation of successful manipulation

Standard measure data: Inspection of the CAD-P scores via across time-points indicated that both participants' catastrophic cognitive appraisals of dissociation reduced post-therapy and remained lower than baseline at follow-up (reliable change; Supplementary Material, Table 2).

Target measure data: Within-phase analysis showed low levels of stability, but visual analysis suggested a downward trend in P2's their therapy phase (Figure 2). Between phases, both participants' scores imply a downward trend. This was supported by Tau-U analysis, with significant moderate or large effect compared to baseline for both participants (P1: followup: Tau-U=-0.663, 90% confidence intervals (CI)= -1--0.213, p=0.0152; P2: intervention: Tau-U=-0.950, 90% CI= -1 --0.549, p<0.001, follow-up: Tau-U=-0.827, 90% CI= -1 --0.403, p=0.0014).

Therefore, we considered the data to confirm that there had been a successful manipulation of cognitive appraisals of dissociation for both participants, and continued to analyse the secondary objective.

Secondary objective: Indication of causal relationship

Standard measure data: ČEFSA-14 scores also significantly decreased for both participants at the post-therapy and follow-up timepoints (Supplementary Material, Table 2).

Target measure data: Visual analysis indicated a stable decrease in P2's therapy phase (Figure 2), with no other notable within-phase trends. Between phase, both participants' data suggested decreased scores between baseline and subsequent phases. This interpretation was supported by Tau-U analysis: both participants showed moderate or large effects between baseline and follow-up phases. However, this only reached a trend level for P1 (Supplementary Material, Table 2), but was significant for P2 (intervention: Tau-U=-1, 90% CI= -1 --0.599, p<0.001; follow-up: Tau-U=-0.973, 90% CI= -1 --0.548, p<0.001).

Intervention 2: Perseverative thinking ("worry")

Primary objective: Confirmation of successful manipulation Standard measure data: This data suggested mixed results for the manipulation of perseverative thinking as measured by the PTQ (Supplementary Material, Table 3). P3 showed no reduction post-therapy but had a reliable reduction in PTQ score from baseline to follow-up; P4 showed no reliable change in PTQ scores at any timepoint; and P5 showed a reliable reduction from baseline at both post-therapy and follow-up.

Target measure data: Only P5's data showed stability in all three phases (Figure 3). Visual inspection between-phases suggested slight improvements in worry ratings for P3 and P5, and slight worsening for P4. This interpretation was supported by Tau-U calculations (Supplementary Material, Table 3), which indicated significant improvement between baseline and follow-up phase for P3 (only) with moderate effect size (Tau-U= -0.529, 90% CI= -0.894 --0.165, p=0.0168).

We therefore considered the manipulation of perseverative thinking successful for P3 and P5, and not successful for P4.

Secondary objective: Indication of causal relationship

Standard measure data: For both P3 and P5, there were no reliable reductions in ČEFSA-14 post-therapy, but both showed significant reductions at follow-up (Supplementary Material, Table 3).

Of note, exploratory analysis of the other standard measures (Supplementary Material) found significant CAD-P reductions for P3 at follow-up and for P5 at post-therapy.

Target measure data: For P5, two of three phases were stable, and visual analysis detected no within-phase trends for any phase (Figure 3). Between-phases, ratings appeared stable, with a possible decrease in scores below baseline in the follow-up phase. This was supported by a significant large effect from baseline to follow-up in the Tau-U analysis (Tau-U= -0.813, 90% CI= -1 --0.350, p=0.0039). However, for P3, the median for all three phases were identical, visual analysis indicated no trends within-or between-phases, and Tau-U analysis found no significant results.

Intervention 3: Affect intolerance

Primary objective: Confirmation of successful manipulation

Standard measure data: Analysis found no reliable change in AIS scores for any participant at any timepoint (Supplementary Material, Table 4).

Target measure data: Only P7's data demonstrated within-phase stability (Figure 4).

Between-phases, the data suggested mixed results. P7's median scores showed no betweenphase changes, and P8's suggests potentially worsening scores across phases. Visual analysis was supported by Tau-U analysis, which showed no significant effect sizes for P7 and significant worsening between baseline and follow-up phases for P8 (Tau-U=0.581, 90% CI=0.237-0.924, p<0.0054).

P6 and P9's median scores suggested a reduction between baseline and therapy that is maintained at follow-up. Whilst visual analysis of P6's data was hampered by very low stability, this pattern was clearer in P9's data. Tau-U analysis supports this interpretation of P9's scores (only), with a significant maximum improvement both between baseline and intervention (Tau-U= -1, 90% CI= -1 --0.429, p=0.0039), and between baseline and follow-up phases (Tau-U= -1, 90% CI= -1 --0.452, p=0.0027).

We therefore considered the manipulation of affect intolerance successful for P9 only. Since the visual and Tau-U analysis indicated a significant worsening in the targeted mechanism for P8, this was considered a significant manipulation of the targeted mechanism (in the opposite manner to that intended), and P8 was therefore included in analysis of the secondary objective to nevertheless evaluate the causal relationship between mechanism and dissociation.

Secondary objective: Indication of causal relationship

Standard measure data: Neither P8 nor P9's standard measure data (Supplementary Material, Table 4) showed reliable changes in ČEFSA-14 scores at any timepoint.

Target measure data: For P9, between-phase visual analysis of target measure data (Figure 4) indicated a reduction in scores between baseline phase and therapy phase that was maintained at follow-up. This interpretation is supported by the Tau-U analysis at a trend level only with a moderate effect size (Supplementary Material, Table 4).

For P8, phases were marked by high levels of instability, with no obvious within-or between-phase trends. Tau-U analysis also found no significant effects.

Discussion

The aim of this study was to test the proposed theoretical model of transdiagnostic dissociation whilst simultaneously identifying effective therapeutic techniques to be incorporated into a targeted intervention in future. To the best of the authors' knowledge, this is the first study in the field of dissociation to address this particular translational objective. Given the mixed results across the interventions, our results indicate both a need and direction for further treatment development.

The first intervention indicated that it is possible to target negative cognitive appraisals of transdiagnostic dissociation and that doing so is associated with reduced dissociative symptoms and distress about these, consistent with a causal relationship. It is striking that with only four sessions, both participants' dissociation scores reduced below the study entry criterion. Such efficacy is perhaps unsurprising, given that this technique is a core component of CBT interventions across the spectrum of mental health presentations, including within CBT for Depersonalisation-Derealisation Disorder (CBT-f-DDD) (Hunter et al., 2018).

Evidence for targeting perseverative negative thinking in this context was mixed, however. A plausible interpretation of the results from the second intervention is that any causal effect of rumination on dissociation is better accounted for by cognitive appraisals. Reliable change in perseverative thinking was always accompanied by reliable change in cognitive appraisals, and any reductions in dissociation scores were seen concurrently or following those. More work is required, therefore, to determine whether focusing on the process of rumination in a targeted intervention for transdiagnostic dissociation offers any additional benefits above and beyond addressing the content of negative cognitive appraisals. Clients may find the worry management strategy helpful if they regularly become entrenched in their negative cognitive appraisals. However, on the basis of this study, there is insufficient evidence to recommend that an intervention tailored to transdiagnostic dissociation should target the process of rumination specifically.

The proposal that affect intolerance maintains dissociative experiences is a distinct feature of our theoretical model compared to the CBT model for Depersonalisation-Derealisation Disorder (Hunter et al., 2003). Though, notably, Vancappel and El-Hage's (2023) model of dissociation, proposed on the basis of existing literature, includes a similar mechanism in the form of negative beliefs about emotion. In the third intervention, the manipulation of this target mechanism was not reliable, and as a result, it is not possible to make firm conclusions about causality. Where some evidence of successful manipulation was present (i.e., for P9), there was mixed evidence for causality: visual analysis suggested a reduction, but this did not reach significance thresholds. Further, the increased subjective distress related to affect (in)tolerance reported by P8 ("How much my relationship with my emotions has bothered me") demonstrates the need to refine the affect intolerance intervention delivered here in order to appropriately and ethically manage intervention-related symptom fluctuations, and mitigate against iatrogenic harm.

Qualitative feedback from participants provides an explanation for the mixed effects of the affect intolerance intervention: all participants highlighted the number of sessions (four) as insufficient. This was perhaps particularly limiting when targeting affect intolerance, as it is likely that graded and repeated experiential work is required to produce meaningful change (Pascual-Leone & Greenberg, 2007). Thus, a third recommendation for future treatment development work is to re-test the affect intolerance intervention using an increased number of sessions. On the basis of this study, it is still too early to draw conclusions about whether affect intolerance is a psychological target amenable to modification in this group, and whether doing so would successfully reduce FSA-dissociation.

It is important to note that confidence in the conclusions from this study are limited by the low levels of within-phase stability within the majority of participants' data, and high between-participant heterogeneity. Lack of stability reduces internal validity and makes it difficult to determine whether the intervention has truly produced a change in scores. Lobo et al., (2017, p. 3) state that 'it is recommended' that stability is established in the data before the intervention is introduced. However, this is challenging to operationalise where the study design incorporates a randomised length of baseline in a multiple baseline design, and where practical considerations for booking therapy session appointments may reduce participant and research team ability to respond dynamically to 'live' phase-stability data.

Nevertheless, future research testing the hypotheses generated here should seek to address these challenges wherever possible.

No formal therapist competency or fidelity checks were conducted in the current study. This project was an unfunded pilot study delivered by an experienced clinical psychologist with established training and routine practice in CBT with dissociation and young people.

Interventions were brief, manual-guided, and targeted at specific mechanisms, using established therapeutic techniques, which helped standardise delivery. However, the absence of independent fidelity or competency assessment is acknowledged as a limitation and should be addressed in future, funded work.

This study therefore represents a preliminary, but critical, first step in the empirical testing of our theoretical model of FSA-dissociation, and the development of a translational targeted psychological intervention. The findings are consistent with the proposed model, and tentatively support the use of CBT strategies addressing negative cognitive appraisals in the treatment of FSA-dissociation. They also emphasise the need for further exploration of affect intolerance as a potentially amenable therapeutic target in this context. Consideration of data stability and heterogeneity, therapy fidelity, and intervention 'dose' will ensure greater confidence in the validity of the findings.

Supplementary material

A) Methods Intervention

In each intervention, the first session included socialisation to the therapy session format, and psychoeducation and formulation to describe how the targeted mechanism relates to the participant's dissociative experiences. Sessions 1 to 3 included learning and practicing a targeted active change technique (see below), and the final session focused on consolidation of new information and planning for the participant to continue therapeutic change independently.

Following advice from lived experience advisors consulted during the design of this study, each session ended with a 'review and recap' to support participants to remember the session despite potential dissociation-related memory difficulties. The review questions prompted participants to identify a key learning point and to explicitly plan how to remember this between sessions. Participants were invited to record the review in any way they would find helpful, including audio or video 'selfie' recordings.

Intervention 1: Negative Cognitive Appraisals of Dissociative Experiences ('your thoughts and beliefs about dissociation')

The key therapeutic technique delivered was cognitive restructuring, drawn from classical cognitive-behavioural therapy (CBT; e.g., Beck, 2021). The aim was for participants to become aware of the automatic appraisals they make of their dissociative experiences and replace catastrophic interpretations with more balanced judgements.

Intervention 2: Perseverative Thinking ('worry')

The key technique in this intervention was the restriction and management of worry via the use of 'worry time'. Participants were supported to identify their positive beliefs facilitating worry as well as the negative effects of worry: this built the rationale for introducing the worry time technique. In contrast to Intervention 1, the content of the worrying thoughts was not addressed or reappraised.

This technique has been thoroughly tested in interventionist-causal paradigms in the context of psychosis (Foster et al., 2010; Freeman et al., 2015 Freeman et al., , 2021)), where it has been demonstrated to be effective in reducing worry, and thus, paranoia. It was therefore anticipated to have a similar effect in the context of FSA-dissociation.

Intervention 3: Affect Intolerance ('your relationship with your emotions')

This intervention incorporated psychoeducation about emotions (emotion literacy) and identification of participants' own beliefs about emotions. The latter was facilitated by using selected items from the AIS (Stapinski et al., 2014) and common 'emotion myths' (Leahy et al., 2011, p.232; e.g., 'being emotional means being out of control') as starting points for discussion in session 1.

In session 2, participants' beliefs about emotion were further explored via Socratic dialogue with a CBT cognitive restructuring worksheet providing scaffolding for this discussion. If indicated, participants were encouraged to design a behavioural experiment to further challenge their beliefs.

Session 3 used a motivational interviewing framework to explore participants' readiness to change their relationship with their emotions. If indicated, they were then introduced to selected dialectical behaviour therapy (DBT) techniques and concepts, alongside supported practice to incrementally approach emotions (e.g., purposefully engaging fully with a sad song, with a plan for how to emotionally repair afterwards). This discussion was tailored to participants: thus, no participant engaged in every strategy.

Analysis

B) Results

Results: Intervention 1 (Cognitive Appraisals)
