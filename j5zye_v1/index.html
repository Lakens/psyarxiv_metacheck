Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 128 papers with 246 instances of power statements, 203 were correctly detected (true positives), 22 were missed (false negatives) and 21 were incorrectly detected (false positives). Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Marginal Significance works

List all sentences that describe an effect as ‘marginally significant’.

The marginal module searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The list of terms is a subset of those listed in a blog post by Matthew Hankins. The module returns all sentences that match terms describing ‘marginally significant’ results.

Some of the terms identified might not be problematic in some contexts, and there are ways to describe ‘marginal significance’ that are not detected by the module.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 51 papers with 87 statements, this module correctly identified 38 statements (true positives) and incorrectly flagged 22 statements (false positives). It failed to detect 27 statements. Thus, among all statements flagged by the module, 63% were genuine cases (positive predictive value). However, the module missed 42% of all true statements (false negative rate). This validation was made with an earlier, looser pattern; the pattern now requires “significant” or “significance” to follow the trigger word closely (#695).

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

Abstract

Aging is associated with changes in large-scale functional brain organization, such as reductions in network segregation, which have been interpreted as a feature of age-related neural dedifferentiation. However, functional network organization may may also reflect accumulated life experiences. Bilingualism, in particular, has been linked to experience-dependent variation in cognitive and neural systems supporting language processing and executive control. Whether bilingualism is associated with network segregation across adulthood, and how these associations vary with language experience, remains unclear. To address this question, the present multisite study examines resting-state functional connectivity (RSFC) among the Language network, the Default Mode (DMN), and the Multiple Demand (MD) (which support language processing, internally directed cognition, and domain-general cognitive control, respectively). The sample comprised N= 605 adults spanning younger to older adulthood, drawn from eight independent datasets across four sociolinguistic contexts. Older age was associated with less segregation across all three network pairs. Notably, bilingual adults showed greater Language-DMN, Language-MD, and DMN-MD segregation than monolingual adults. Immersion-defined contrasts further revealed variation across linguistic environments such that both immersed and non-immersed bilinguals showed greater DMN-MD segregation than monolinguals, while greater Language-MD segregation was observed primarily in immersed bilinguals compared to monolinguals. In contrast, continuous measures of bilingual experience were not significantly associated with segregation and did not modulate age-related differences. Together, these findings highlight age-related reductions in network segregation and demonstrate that bilingual language experience is associated with systemic differences in large-scale functional network organization across adulthood.

Healthy aging has been associated with changes in the organization of large-scale functional brain networks that support cognition, including reduced within-network connectivity and increased cross-network coupling, commonly referred to as reduced network segregation (Chan et al., 2014). Importantly, these age-related differences in functional network organization have been associated with variability in cognitive performance, including language outcomes (Nakamura et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2026; Zhang & Diaz, 2023a). Together, this work suggests that reduced segregation may represent a functionally meaningful feature of age-related brain reorganization rather than simply a correlate of chronological age. Additionally, resting-state functional network organization has been shown to be shaped not only by aging, but also by accumulated experience, as intrinsic connectivity remains sensitive to experience-dependent plasticity throughout adulthood (Ferreira & Busatto, 2013; Guerra-Carrillo et al., 2014). Bilingualism may be one such experience, given that repeated use and management of multiple languages engage both language-specific and domain-general control systems (Green & Abutalebi, 2013; Pliatsikas & Luk, 2016). Moreover, individual differences in bilingual experience such as language-use diversity, age of acquisition, proficiency, and frequency of use, have been associated with variability in resting-state connectivity within and between these systems (Gullifer et al., 2018; Jiang et al., 2026a; Sulpizio et al., 2020). Thus, while aging and bilingual experience have each been linked to functional network organization, their joint effects remain unclear. Using a large-scale, multicenter dataset (N = 605) spanning multiple linguistic environments, the present study examines whether categorical and/or distinct dimensions of bilingual experience are associated with distinct patterns of network segregation among the default mode network (DMN), language network, and multiple demand (MD) network, and whether bilingual experience moderates age-related differences in network segregation.

1.1 Age-Related Differences in Functional Network Organization

Resting-State Functional Connectivity (RSFC) provides a means of characterizing the intrinsic organization of large-scale neural systems in the absence of explicit task demands. Across the adult lifespan, this organization becomes less differentiated, consistent with the broader concept of neural dedifferentiation i.e., an age-related reduction in the specificity of neural representations and systems (https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?RxraZ0. Li et al., 2001; Park et al., 2012). Within contemporary network neuroscience, dedifferentiation can be examined through network segregation, which captures the relative balance between functional connectivity among regions belonging to different networks. Highly segregated networks exhibit comparatively stronger within-network connectivity and weaker between-network connectivity, while reduced segregation reflects weaker within-network cohesion and great cross-network coupling (Chan et al., 2014).

Aging has been consistently associated with reductions in functional segregation across the brain (Betzel et al., 2014; Chan et al., 2014; Malagurski et al., 2020). These differences are especially pronounced in higher-order association networks that support complex cognitive functions (Chan et al., 2014). More recent lifespan evidence further suggests that declines in segregation are associated with poorer behavioral performance, including declines in global cognition and language outcomes (Zhang et al., 2021). Together, these findings suggest that network dedifferentiation may have meaningful behavioral consequences rather than simply reflecting chronological age (Malagurski et al., 2020; Pedersen et al., 2021).

The DMN and MD networks may be especially relevant to age-related changes in largescale functional brain organization. The DMN has been theorized to contribute to internally directed and self-generated cognition, including conceptual processing and mind wandering. Aging has also been associated with reduced functional cohesion within this network (Andrews-Hanna, 2012; Damoiseaux, 2017). The MD network is widely considered to support domaingeneral processes including cognitive control, working memory, attentional allocation, and interference resolution (Duncan et al., 2020). Similar to the DMN, aging has also been associated with reduced functional differentiation of the MD network from other systems (Billot et al., 2026; Grady et al., 2010; Hoffman & Morcom, 2018). In contrast, the core Language network may show comparatively greater preservation across adulthood. For examplehttps://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?uoBnVP language-specific frontotemporal systems have been shown to maintain substantial functional specificity despite agerelated structural change (https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?uoBnVP Campbell et al., 2016; Samu et al., 2017) , while more recent work similarly demonstrates maintained functional selectivity of the Language network in later life (Billot et al., 2024). Although within-network connectivity and functional selectivity are not equivalent to network segregation, both are consistent with the possibility that the Language network retains greater functional specificity with age than some domain-general systems. Consistent with this interpretation, left-hemisphere Language network segregation appears to decline more slowly than whole brain segregation (Zhang & Diaz, 2023a , 2023b).

Importantly, age-related differences may emerge not only within individual networks, but also in how language-specific and domain general systems interact. In prior work, segregation among Language, DMN, and MD networks has been associated with age-related differences in speech production and disfluency, suggesting that the broader organization of these networks may be relevant for language functioning across adulthood (Nakamura et al., 2026). Together, these findings raise the possibility that some age-related language difficulties may reflect changes in the broader functional architecture supporting coordination between language-specific and domaingeneral systems rather than widespread deterioration of the core Language network itself. This motivates a closer examination of age-related differences in segregation among the Language, DMN, MD networks.

1.2 Bilingual Experience and Functional Connectivity

Beyond the effects of aging, functional network organization remains sensitive to accumulated experience throughout adulthood, with resting-state connectivity reflecting experience-dependent plasticity across sensory, motor, and cognitive domains (https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?PGNP38 Guerra-Carrillo et al., 2014). Bilingualism is one such experience that may be especially relevant in this context. Herehttps://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?IU9gDK bilingualism is defined as the regular use of two or more languages in everyday life (Kroll et al., 2015) , while recognizing that it is a multidimensional experience characterized by substantial variation in, e.g., age of acquisition, proficiency, frequency and context of language use, language balance, and switching practices (DeLuca et al., 2019; Gullifer & Titone, 2020; Luk, 2015; Luk & Bialystok, 2013). A central feature of bilingual language use is that both languages remain active during listening, reading, and speech planning, allowing the non-target language to influence processing even when it is not intentionally being used (Dijkstra, 2005; Kroll et al., 2006; Marian & Spivey, 2003). This parallel activation creates the potential for cross-language competition and repeatedly engages cognitive processes involved in monitoring, language selection, inhibition, interference control, and flexible switching between languages (Abutalebi & Green, 2007; Green & Abutalebi, 2013). It has been posited that the sustained recruitment of these processes may contribute to experience-dependent differences in the intrinsic connectivity of language and domain-general control systems. In line with this view, recent RSFC studies have reported bilingualism-related differences in the coordination of language and cognitive control regions including the dorsal anterior cingulate cortex, caudate nucleus, inferior frontal gyrus, and other regions implicated in language selection and monitoring (Becker et al., 2016; Berken et al., 2016; L. Li et al., 2015). Differences have also been observed in the coupling of control-related regions with broader association systems, including the DMN and Salience network (Cai et al., 2021; Grady et al., 2015; X. Li et al., 2021).

Importantly, these effects are not uniform in direction. For examplehttps://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?sKLRce earlier secondlanguage (L2) acquisition and greater diversity of language use have been associated with stronger connectivity among frontal, striatal, and language-related regions (Berken et al., 2016; Gullifer et al., 2018; Kousaie et al., 2017) , whereas higher L2 proficiency has been associated with weaker connectivity involving the left anterior cingulate cortex and right midfrontal gyrus relative to lower-proficiency bilinguals, suggesting that greater bilingual experience does not necessarily correspond to uniformly stronger connectivity (Sun et al., 2019). Additionallyhttps://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?dj096S diversity of language use across social contexts, often referred to as language entropy (Gullifer & Titone, 2020) , has also emerged as an important factor in considering bilingual neural adaptations. Gullifer et al., (2018a), for example, demonstrated that greater social diversity of language use was associated with greater connectivity between the anterior cingulate cortex and bilateral putamen, as well as between the caudate and superior temporal regions. Collectively, these findings have contributed to a broader shift away from categorical comparisons of bilingual and monolingual speakers. Notably, studies such as Sulpizio et al., (2020) characterized bilingualism as a continuous gradient incorporating age of acquisition, proficiency, and language use. The results revealed that different combinations of these experiences predicted distinct patterns of connectivity within and between language and executive-control networks. Thus, variability across RSFC findings may partly reflect heterogeneity in the bilingual populations samples, including differences in language history, proficiency, patterns of language use, and sociolinguistic contexts (Jafari et al., 2021; Jiang et al., 2026b).

Crucially, much of the existing work has focused on connectivity between selected regions or networks involved primarily in language control. Less is known about whether diverse dimensions of bilingual experience are associated with the broader functional organization of language-specific and domain-general systems. Specifically, it remains unclear whether bilingual experience relates to the degree to which the Language, DMN, and MD networks remain functionally differentiated from one another across adulthood.

1.3 Bilingualism, Aging, and Neural Resilience

Together, the evidence reviewed above raises an important question at the intersection of bilingualism and aging, i.e., if aging is associated with reduced differentiation of large-scale functional networks, and bilingual experience is associated with differences in the organization and coordination of these same systems, might language experience contribute to age-related functional network organization? Bilingualism has been long hypothesized to contribute greater neurocognitive longevity in older age, with much of this literature framed within the cognitive reserve hypothesis (Bialystok, 2021; Stern, 2002). Some neuroimaging findings are in fact consistent with the possibility that bilingual experience is associated with differences in how the brain responds to aging. For example, older bilinguals have demonstrated cognitive performance comparable to that of monolingual counterparts despite showing greater age-related structural brain differences, suggesting that bilingual experience may support performance in the presence of neural decline (Gold, 2015; Schweizer et al., 2012). Functional studies have also reported bilingualism-related differences in the recruitment and coordination of control networks in older adulthood. For instancehttps://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?xOJeZA bilingual older adults have shown stronger connectivity within frontoparietal and default mode networks than monolingual older adults (Grady et al., 2015) , while other work has identified differences in the recruitment of fronto-striatal systems that have been interpreted as potentially reflecting greater neural efficiency (Anderson et al., 2021). More recently, preserved intrinsic functional network organization has been proposed as one mechanism through which bilingual experience may relate to neural aging despite evidence of more advanced neuroanatomical aging (Stevens et al., 2023). Nevertheless, the specific functional adaptations associated with bilingual experience in older adulthood remain unresolved, and existing studies do not point to a single, uniform pattern of connectivity differences across neural systems.

One important source of this variability may be the substantial heterogeneity of bilingual experience itself. Bilingual speakers differ across multiple, partially independent dimensions, including age of acquisition, proficiency, frequency of use, language dominance, switching practices, and the diversity of contexts in which each language is used (Gullifer et al., 2021; Kałamała et al., 2023). These experiences are also embedded within distinct geographic, sociocultural, and linguistic environments that shape opportunities for language use and the control demands associated with managing multiple languages (Titone & Tiv, 2023a , 2023b https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?PtfCZt. Immersion, for example, involves the sustained exposure to and use of a second language within an environment where that language is the dominant community language (Linck et al., 2009). Such environments can alter both the frequency and contexts of L2 use and, consequently, the demands placed on language selection and control (Green & Abutalebi, 2013). Consistent with this view, neurocognitive adaptations associated with bilingualism has found differences between immersed and non-immersed speakers, making immersion yet another important dimension to consider in the broader linguistic context (DeLuca et al., 2019; Pliatsikas et al., 2017; Stein et al., 2014).

How bilingualism is operationalized is therefore especially consequential in studies of aging. Detailed characterization of language history and use can strengthen categorical comparisons by providing greater confidence that bilingual groups reflect meaningful and comparable patterns of experience, while also allowing variation within those groups to be examined. Yet, the vast majority of bilingual neuroimaging studies do not examine multiple linguistic environments within the same study, making it difficult to determine whether observed relationships between bilingual experience and functional brain organization generalize across linguistic contexts. This issue is particularly relevant to aging research, where education, socioeconomic status, and occupational demands may additionally covary with both bilingual experience and age-related cognitive or neural outcomes (Chapko et al., 2018; Stern, 2012).

These distinctions are theoretically important because dimensions of bilingual experience may relate differentially to functional network organization. In other words, experiences that place sustained demands on language control could support greater differentiation among functional systems, whereas frequent coordination between languages and control processes could also promote greater cross-network connectivity. Therefore, the direction of bilingualism-related differences in age-related segregation may depend on both the aspect of bilingual experience measured as well as the networks examined and the functional metric used. This possibility also presents a logistical challenge. Although bilingualism is increasingly understood as multidimensional, no single questionnaire captures all relevant dimensions, and commonly used questionnaires differ substantially in their content, response scales, and scoring criteria (Dass et al., 2024; Yu et al., 2026). Consequently, it remains unclear whether relationships between bilingual experience, age, and network segregation generalize across samples and measurement approaches. To push the field forward, multiple bilingual populations must be examined within the same study to evaluate whether comparable patterns of functional network organization emerge across distinct geographic, linguistic, and measurement contexts.

1.4 The Present Study

The present study takes this next step by examining a large multisite sample, investigating whether age and bilingual experience are associated with functional network segregation among the Language, DMN, and MD networks. Data were integrated across eight samples collected in separate geographic and linguistic environments (Macau SAR China, The United Kingdom, The Czech Republic, and Northeastern USA). Bilingual experience was assessed using two different measurement toolshttps://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?KsDokQ the Language History Questionnaire 3.0 (LHQ3; https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?Q13SNp. Li et al., 2020) and The Language and Social Background Questionnaire (LSBQ; Anderson et al., 2018) , both of which capture overlapping but non-equivalent aspects of language proficiency, history, and use (see full details in Methods). We first tested whether older age was associated with reduced network segregation among the three networks (Language, DMN, and MD), predicting lower network segregation with older age. We then examined whether network segregation differed between monolingual and bilingual participants, including contrasts with immersed and nonimmersed bilinguals to capture broad differences in linguistic environment. We predicted that bilingual participants would show greater network segregation than monolingual counterparts across all three network pairs. Finally, we tested whether continuous measures of bilingual experience were associated with distinct patterns of network segregation and whether these relationships varied across adulthood. We predicted greater bilingual engagement to be associated with greater network segregation among all three networks of interest and to attenuate age-related reductions in segregation.

2.2 Bilingual Experience Measures

Bilingual experience was assessed using two complimentary instruments, the LHQ (https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?HX44mQ. Li et al., 2020) and LSBQ (Anderson et al., 2018). The Macau sample completed the LHQ, whereas the UK and Czech samples completed the LSBQ. Because these tools differ in item content, response scales, and scoring procedures, questionnaire-specific measures were analyzed separately rather than transformed onto a common scale (see details in, e.g., Dass et al., 2024). Across analyses, bilingual experience was characterized primarily with respect to participants' first and second languages (L1 and L2). Accordingly, for participants who reported using more than two languages, these measures may not fully capture their broader multilingual experience.

For the LHQ sample, bilingual experience was characterized using the Multilingual Language Diversity (MLD) index. The MLD index reflects language dominance (use and proficiency). For the LSBQ samples, bilingual experience was characterized using the LSBQ composite score, which reflects multiple aspects of bilingual language experience and use.

Language-use entropy was additionally calculated separately within each of the four bilingual datasets following a Shannon entropy framework (Gullifer & Titone, 2020). Entropy values were calculated using the languageEntropy package in R according (Gullifer & Titone, 2020). Higher entropy values indicate a more balanced distribution of multiple languages within a given communicative context, whereas lower values indicate greater dominance of one language. Because the LHQ and LSBQ differ in the number, format, and response structure of the contexts assessed, questionnaire-specific procedures were used to derive the language-use portions entered into the entropy calculation.

For the LHQ sample, language-use experience was assessed across 17 communicative contexts, including social media, reading, writing, interactions with different interlocutors, and self-directed language activities. Participants reported the amount of time spent using each language within these contexts as well as the frequency of language activities on a 7-point scale.

For Likert-based responses, ratings were re-baselined by subtracting 1 such that 0 represented no language use, and the resulting values were divided by the summed ratings within each context to derive proportional use of each language. These context-specific language-use proportions were then used to calculate Shannon entropy for each available context with the langaugeEntropy package. Entropy was calculated separately within each available context and then averaged across contexts to obtain a participant-level entropy score.

For the LSBQ samples, entropy was derived from the Community Language Use Behavior section across 24 available sub-contexts, including interactions with parents, siblings, relatives, friends, roommates, and partners, as well as language use at home, school, work, social activities, shopping, health care, reading, electronic communication, media use, and other everyday contexts. The Grandparents context included in the full LSBQ was unavailable across all datasets and was therefore excluded to maintain a common set of contexts. LSBQ responses were recorded on a five-point scale ranging from 0 (All English) to 4 (Only the other language). To calculate entropy, each response was converted into complementary proportions of English and non-English use such that 0 corresponded to 1.00 English/0.00 non-English, 1 to .75/.25, 2 to .50/.50, 3 to .25/.75, and 4 to 0.00/1.00. Shannon entropy (base 2) was then calculated separately for each sub-context using the languageEntropy package in R (Gullifer & Titone, 2020). Entropy values ranged from 0 (indicating exclusive use of one language within a context) to 1 (indicating equally balanced use of English and the non-English language). For participants with missing responses in a given context, entropy for that context was treated as missing. A participant-level entropy score was therefore calculated as the mean entropy across all available contexts, excluding missing contexts.

2.3.2 Preprocessing Pipeline

For all datasets, anatomical and functional data were visually inspected for artifacts and signal drop-out. Functional data quality was more formally assessed using the fBIRN QA tool, measuring the number of potentially clipped voxels, mean signal fluctuation-to-noise ratio (SFNR), and per-slice variation (Glover et al., 2012). Comparable site-specific quality control procedures were used for the remaining datasets prior to the preprocessing procedures. Preprocessing analyses were carried out using the CONN functional connectivity toolbox (22 v.2407) in MATLAB (Whitfield-Gabrieli & Nieto-Castanon, 2012). Preprocessing steps included functional realignment and unwarping to estimate and correct for participant motion, distortion correction based on the field map, and a slice-timing correction which corrected for maturation of the BOLD signal over time (Huettel et al., 2004). Additionally, functional outliers were detected with an ART (Artifact Detection Tools) based identification method (https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?fuFkUB in which outliers were defined using a conservative threshold (i.e., 97th percentile) and subsequently removed. All anatomical and functional images were normalized into standard Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) space. The anatomical images were segmented into gray matter, white matter, and cerebral spinal fluid (CSF) tissue classes with CONN which uses SPM's unified segmentation and normalization procedure. These masks were then applied to the functional images (Ashburner & Friston, 2005). During registration, functional images were aligned to anatomical images, and both were normalized to standard space. A smoothing kernel of 6 mm was used to increase the signal-to-noise ratio, as well as to reduce spurious activations of single voxels. During denoising, the representative noise signal from white matter (5 components) and CSF (5 components) was extracted, and any signal correlated with these components was removed from the BOLD signal. The noise removal used the CompCor approach, which extracts multiple signals from CSF and white matter areas to capture motion and physiological artifacts while excluding neural signals, which avoids introducing artifactual negative correlations in the connectivity measures (Chai et al., 2012; Liu et al., 2017). To eliminate frequencies of less interest, a band-pass filter (0.008, 0.09 Hz) was used (Davey et al., 2013; Gohel & Biswal, 2015; Hallquist et al., 2013). The effects of the following quality assurance parameters were controlled during data analysis: number of outlier and non-outlier scans (outlier threshold = 0.5 mm), max and mean motion, and max and mean global BOLD signal changes (outlier threshold = global signal z-value of 3). Additional nuisance regressors accounted for head motion and artifact-related signal fluctuations, including volumes identified as outliers using a motion threshold of 0.5 mm and a global BOLD signal change threshold of z = 3. The analyses removing variance associated with the variables described above occurred in a single linear regression step, and the residualized BOLD signal was used for further statistical analyses.

2.4 Node Definition and Network Measures

Regions of Interest (ROIs) were defined a priori for the three functional networks; DMN, Language network, and MD network. DMN and MD nodes were derived from the (Power et al., 2011) functional parcellation, with 6mm-radius spherical ROIs centered on the MNI coordinates. Language network ROIs were defined based on (Fedorenko et al., 2010). Although these regions were originally characterized in the left hemisphere, corresponding right-hemisphere homologues were included here to characterize bilateral network organization because both bilingual language experience and aging have been associated with variability in hemispheric lateralization and greater bilateral recruitment (e.g., Hull and Vaid, 2006; Cabeza 2002). All ROIs were constructed in MNI space and visually inspected for anatomical placement and spatial overlap. Two DMN ROIs that overlapped with the language network were excluded. See Table 2 for details. Functional time series and ROI-to-ROI connectivity matrices were obtained using the CONN Toolbox (Whitfield-Gabrieli & Nieto-Castanon, 2012).

For each participant, self-correlations along the matrix diagonal were excluded prior to calculating network-level measures. Negative correlations were set to zero because of uncertainty regarding their interpretation in resting-state functional connectivity analyses (Hallquist et al., 2013). The same network-measure pipeline was applied independently to each contributing dataset.

The primary RSFC outcomes were symmetric pairwise segregation measures for the Language-DMN, Language-MD, and DMN-MD network pairs. Whole-network within-network connectivity was first calculated separately for the DMN, Language, and MD networks as the mean connectivity across all ROI pairs belonging to each network, incorporating both left-and right-hemisphere nodes. Between-network connectivity was calculated separately for each network pair as the mean connectivity between all ROIs belonging to one network and all ROIs belonging to the other network. Thus, separate between-network measures were derived for Language-DMN, Language-MD, and DMN-MD connectivity. 3.1 Age Effects on Network Segregation

First, to examine whether age was associated with pairwise network segregation, separate baseline models were fitted for each network pair with sex, education, and dataset included as covariates. Older age was significantly associated with lower segregation across all three network pairs, replicating patterns found in (Nakamura et al., 2026). Specifically, older age was associated with decreases in Language-DMN segregation (b = -0.00121, SE = 0.00036, t = -3.42, p < .001), decreases in Language-MD segregation (b = -0.00176, SE = 0.00039, t = -4.52, p < .001), and decreases in DMN-MD segregation (b = -0.00100, SE = 0.00041, t = -2.44, p = .015). These results indicate that older age was overall associated with reduced functional segregation among the DMN, Language, and MD networks. Full model outputs are provided in the Supplementary Table S1. As an additional sensitivity check, we also tested age-related differences in network segregation within just the bilingual group. Older age was again associated with decreases in Language-DMN segregation (b = -0.00130, SE = 0.00049, t = -2.65, p = .008); Language-MD networks (b = -0.00248, SE = 0.00059, t = -4.17, p < .001); and the DMN and MD networks (b = -0.00154, SE = 0.00063, t = -2.45, p = .015). These findings indicate that negative associations between age and network segregation were also observed when analyses were restricted to bilingual participants.

3.2 Categorical Group Differences: Bilingual vs Monolingual

Next, categorical group-comparison models were conducted to examine whether network segregation differed between monolingual and bilingual participants with age, sex, and education included as covariates. Bilingual participants overall showed significantly greater segregation compared to monolinguals, across all three network pairs. Specifically, bilingual participants showed greater Language-DMN segregation (b = 0.0260, SE = 0.0130, t = 2.00, p < .05), greater Language-MD segregation (b = 0.0637, SE = 0.0184, t = 3.46, p < .001), and greater DMN-MD segregation (b = 0.0881, SE = 0.0189, t = 4.67, p < .001) compared to monolingual counterparts.

Planned contrasts further examined immersed and non-immersed bilingual participants relative to monolingual participants. Relative to monolinguals, immersed bilinguals showed significantly greater Language-MD segregation (b = 0.108, SE = 0.025, t = 4.27, p < .001) and DMN-MD segregation (b = 0.140, SE = 0.027, t = 5.16, p < .001), whereas the difference in the Language-DMN segregation did not reach significance (b = 0.0361, SE = 0.0197, t = 1.83, p = .067). Non-immersed bilinguals also showed significantly greater DMN-MD segregation than monolinguals (b = 0.0634, SE = 0.0217, t = 2.92, p = .011), but differences in Language-DMN segregation (b = 0.0214, SE = 0.0146, t = 1.47, p = .142) and Language-MD segregation (b = 0.0394, SE = 0.0213, t = 1.85, p = .097) were not significant. Full model summary tables are provided in the Supplementary Materials Tables S2 -S4.

4. Discussion

The present study examined whether age and bilingual language experience were associated with large-scale functional network organization, focusing on segregation among the Language, Default Mode (DMN), and Multiple Demand (MD) networks. Across seven independent datasets from distinct geographic and sociolinguistic contexts, older age was associated with reduced segregation across all three network pairs. At the group level, bilingual adults showed greater Language-DMN, Language-MD, and DMN-MD segregation than monolingual adults. Further analyses distinguishing bilinguals by immersion status revealed that both immersed and non-immersed bilinguals exhibited greater DMN-MD segregation relative to monolinguals, whereas greater Language-MD segregation was observed only among immersed bilinguals. However, the continuous dimensions of language experience examined here, including multilingual language diversity, language-use entropy, and the LSBQ composite factor score, and entropy, were not significantly associated with network segregation and did not moderate agerelated differences. Together, these findings identify two robust patterns. First, age-related reductions in large-scale network differentiation, and second, greater network segregation among bilingual relative to monolingual adults. The continuous within-bilingual analyses provide a complementary but more qualified picture of which specific dimensions of bilingual experience may contribute to these group-level differences.

4.1 Age-Related Differences in Network Segregation

Older age was consistently associated with lower segregation across the Language-DMN, Language-MD, and DMN-MD network pairs. This finding is consistent with broader evidence that aging is accompanied by reduced network segregation among large-scale functional networks (Chan et al., 2014; Zhang et al., 2026; Zhang & Diaz, 2023a). Importantly, present findings also converge with previous work showing age-related differences in functional organization among the DMN, Language, and MD networks (Nakamura et al., 2026). Taken together, these results support the hypothesis that reduced segregation is a robust feature of age-related functional reorganization and that these effects are not restricted to a single network or cognitive domain.

4.2 Bilingualism and Large-Scale Network Organization

A second major finding is that bilingual adults showed greater segregation than monolingual adults across all three network pairs, even when age, sex, and education were accounted for. These results extend prior work linking bilingual language experience to differences in intrinsic functional connectivity (Gullifer et al., 2018; Jiang et al., 2026b; Sulpizio et al., 2020) by demonstrating differences at the level of border network differentiation. Whereas much of the existing previous work has examined connectivity between selected regions or within specific language and control systems, the present findings suggest that bilingualism is also associated with the relative functional separation of the DMN, Language, and MD networks. This finding is theoretically meaningful because bilingual language use places recurring demands on both language-specific and domain-general systems (e.g., Green & Abutalebi, 2013; Pliatsikas, 2020). Managing more than one language involves coordinating linguistic representations while flexibly recruiting control processes according to communicative demands. Repeated engagement of these systems across adulthood may therefore be associated with differences in how strongly large-scale networks maintain their functional differentiation. In this sense, greater segregation among bilingual adults is consistent with experience-dependent accounts in which sustained cognitive and linguistic demands are associated with adaptations in functional brain organization (Bialystok, 2017; Green & Abutalebi, 2013; Grundy et al., 2017; Pliatsikas, 2020). The present findings are also compatible with broader cognitive-reserve and neural resilience perspectives, insofar as greater differentiation of functional systems may represent one way in which accumulated experience is associated with variation in brain organization across adulthood.

The immersion-defined contrasts further suggest that the broader linguistic environment may be a relevant factor to these group differences. Both immersed and non-immersed bilinguals showed greater DMN-MD segregation relative to monolinguals, whereas greater Language-MD segregation was observed only among immersed bilinguals.. One possibility is that sustained use of an L2 in an environment where it is the dominant community language places greater demands on the coordination of language-specific and domain-general control systems, including language selection and switching across communicative contexts (Green & Abutalebi, 2013). Greater differentiation between the Language and MD networks may therefore reflect adaptation to these recurring control demands. These patterns are consistent with the possibility that the demands of a given language environment may give rise to neuroadaptive changes in language-control related systems (Bialystok, 2016 (Bialystok, , 2025;; DeLuca et al., 2019; Green & Abutalebi, 2013; Pliatsikas, 2020).

4.3 Continuous Dimensions of Bilingual Experience

The continuous within-bilingual analyses addressed a different question by examining whether individual variation in specific dimensions of bilingual experience explained variation in network segregation. Contrary to our predictions, neither the LHQ MLD index nor the LSBQ composite score, or language-use entropy was significantly associated with segregation, and these measures did not moderate age-related differences in segregation. These null effects should not, however, be treated as directly contradicting categorical bilingual-monolingual or immersiondefined findings described above.

The discrepancy between the categorical and continuous findings warrants consideration. Bilingualism encompasses partially separable dimensions including age and context of acquisition, proficiency, patterns of language use, switching behavior, and interactional context, different measures capture distinct aspects of this experience rather than a common underlying construct (Kałamała et al., 2023; Luk & Bialystok, 2013; Surrain & Luk, 2019). Although categorical bilingual-monolingual comparisons distinguish groups with different language experiences, they do not identify which dimensions of those experiences account for the observed neural differences. Likewise, the absence of significant associations with MLD, entropy, or the LSBQ composite does not establish that other dimensions of bilingual experience are unrelated to segregation. The present findings therefore support a distinction between group-level differences in functional network organization and the specific experiential dimensions that may contribute to those differences. Identifying those dimensions will require analyses that consider individual languageexperience characteristics alongside the broader sociolinguistic contexts in which they occur.

For example, previous work has found that specific dimensions such as age of L2 acquisition is associated with greater interhemispheric frontal connectivity, whereas greater social diversity of language use is independently associated with connectivity involving the anterior cingulate cortex and bilateral putamen (Gullifer et al., 2018). Similarly, age of acquisition, proficiency, and language use have been associated with distinct patterns of connectivity within and between language and control networks (https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?HMeVjL Sulpizio et al., 2020). These distinctions must therefore be considered when relating self-reported experience to neural organization. The present results may speak to this complexity. Our results may indicate that categorical bilingualmonolingual contrast captures the cumulative influence of multiple correlated experiences and linguistic environment. Individual questionnaire scores, on the other hand, isolate only a subset of those experiences. As such, individuals with similar questionnaire scores may arrive at those scores through qualitatively different language experiences. This very issue may be amplified in multisite studies in which questionnaires, linguistic environments, and participant populations differ across samples. Thus, the absence of one-to-one associations between the continuous measures examined in the present study and network segregation does not necessarily demonstrate that individual dimensional factors of language experience are unrelated to functional brain organization. Rather, it highlights the challenge of identifying which dimensions, combinations of dimensions, and measurement approaches most effectively characterize the experiences relevant to large-scale network organization.

4.4. Limitations and Future Directions

Several features of the present study should inform interpretation of the present findings. The multisite design provided a large and heterogenous sample spanning a broad range of adulthood and linguistic environments, but this heterogeneity also introduced differences in sample structure and measurement across datasets. In particular, the continuous bilingualexperience analyses were based on smaller subsets of the overall sample. The LHQ analyses included approximately 125 participants and the LSBQ analyses approximately 199 participants, compared to with the larger multisite sample for the categorical group comparisons. The withinbilingual analyses therefore have reduced statistical power to detect these associations, particularly if effects of individual experiential dimensions are modest. Restricted variability may have further reduced sensitivity in some measures. For example, within the Macau sample, younger adults were relatively homogenous in MLD and language-use entropy, whereas older adults showed substantially greater variability. Such differences in range may have attenuated associations between continuous predictors and neural outcomes and may have been especially consequential for interaction analyses.

Continuous bilingual experience measures were also analyzed within questionnaire frameworks rather than across the full sample because the LHQ and LSBQ characterize overlapping but non-equivalent dimensions of language experience. The contributing datasets further differed in their distributions of age and language environment. This was evident in the Macau sample, which contained distinct younger and older groups rather than continuous representations across middle adulthood, and in the association between immersion status and dataset. These features inherently limit the extent to which age, immersion, and geographic context can be fully disentangled.

At the same time, the multisite design is a notable strength of the present study because it allowed bilingual experience and network organization to be examined across a broader range of linguistic and sociocultural contexts than is typically possible within a single sample. The challenge for future work will be to retain this breadth while improving comparability across sites. Recent efforts such as the MULTIDGEN protocol provide a useful framework for this approach by emphasizing standardized characterization of multilingual experience and neuroimaging outcomes across linguistic and cultural contexts to facilitate cross-site data pooling (Pliatsikas et al., 2025). Prospectively designed multisite studies that adopt common measures while deliberately sampling a wide range of relevant experiential dimensions will be particularly valuable.

5 Conclusions

The present findings provide evidence for two robust features of large-scale functional network organization across adulthood. First, older age was associated with reduced functional network segregation across the Language, DMN, and MD networks, extending previous evidence for age-related reductions in network segregation (Campbell et al., 2013; Chan et al., 2014; Nakamura et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2021; Zhang & Diaz, 2023a). Second, bilingual adults showed greater segregation than monolingual adults across all three network pairs, extending prior work on bilingual differences in intrinsic organization to a systems-level measure of functional network connectivity (Gullifer et al., 2018; Jiang et al., 2026b; Sulpizio et al., 2020). Immersion defined contrasts further suggested that these group differences may vary across linguistic environments, with DMN-MD differences observed across both immersed and non-immersed bilinguals and Language-MD differences more evident among immersed bilinguals. Continuous measures of bilingual experience did not explain corresponding individual variation in network segregation, although these analyses were constrained by smaller subsamples, heterogeneous measures, and differences in the range of bilingual experience represented across datasets. Taken together, the present findings suggest that both age and bilingual language experience are associated with largescale functional network re-organization, while the specific experiential and contextual factors underlying bilingual-related differences remain an important target for future research.
