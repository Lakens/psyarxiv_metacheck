Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Abstract

Why does consciousness feel like something, and why is experience unified rather than fragmented? We propose that these properties arise from an entangled trefoil of emotion, interference-based inference and hierarchical orchestration, shaped by the thermodynamic constraint that living systems must remain far from equilibrium using limited energy. Emotion compresses actions into valenced signals of what matters, while a pleasure cycle schedules competing biological needs through predictive wanting and satiety organised around the true hedonic outcome of liking. Hierarchical orchestrating regions integrate and broadcast information through interference between classical coupled oscillators, shaped by the spectral gaps of the connectome driven by long-range exceptions to local cortical wiring. The architecture of the Entangled Loop consists of three inseparable strands, each continually constraining the other two, with the one operator linking computational possibilities to the arrow of time and energetic cost of running them. Evidence suggests that the same three signatures emerge independently in large language models trained on human language, but as shadows of the generating architecture rather than evidence of consciousness. The trefoil predicts a distinctive human vulnerability because our abundance of rare long-range connections promotes more computation for less energy while making the architecture more fragile. Neuropsychiatric disorders could therefore arise from perturbations entering through any strand and propagating through the others, while the more robust architecture of other species may help explain why animal models reproduce human psychiatric disorders poorly. Sustaining and restoring flexible entanglement in the Entangled Loop may provide mechanistic routes towards flourishing.

Introduction

Theories of consciousness have tended to treat emotion as content the conscious mind happens to represent rather than as part of its architecture. The authoritative recent survey by Seth and Bayne (2022) maps a field organised around Global Workspace theory (Baars, 1988; Dehaene et al., 1998; Mashour et al., 2020), Integrated Information Theory (Oizumi et al., 2014; Tononi, 2004 Tononi, , 2008 Tononi, , 2016;; Tononi et al., 1994), Higher-Order Theories (Brown et al., 2019; Lau and Rosenthal, 2011) and the predictive processing or active inference family (Clark, 2016; Friston, 2010; Parr et al., 2022), of which the Beautiful Loop is the most complete recent synthesis (Laukkonen et al., 2025). A smaller but substantial divergent tradition has argued for affective primacy from different starting points. Panksepp (1998) has argued from comparative neuroscience, Solms and Friston (2018) and Solms (2021) from psychoanalytic and brainstem-centred formulations, Damasio (1994 Damasio ( , 2010 Damasio ( , 2018) from the somatic-marker and homeostatic approach, and Craig (2015) from interoception. We position the present perspective within this second tradition whilst extending it through a formal thermodynamic and computational argument that the affective-primacy literature has not developed systematically.

We argue that emotion is the computational solution to the intractability of survival, the evolutionary reason brains became conscious, and the architectural ground from which the rest of the world model takes its shape. Emotion is not only a fast compression into valence but a continuous scheduler, realised through the pleasure cycle through predictive wanting and satiety organised around the true hedonic outcome of liking, which allows competing biological needs to take turns. The felt quality of experience is what this valenced architecture becomes when its content is bound through interference and broadcast through orchestration, not a further fact added on top of neutral representation.

The perspective develops the argument in four steps. First, we develop the argument that thermodynamic constraint favours emotion as a natural compression of the decision space of adaptive behaviour and, through the pleasure cycle, as a continuous scheduler of competing needs. We review the affective neuroscience that identifies the distributed hedonic network as directly tractable to causal manipulation. Second, we show that the same thermodynamic pressure for more computation for less energy has selected not only for emotion but also for hierarchical orchestration of information through interference-based inference and turbulent neural dynamics. Third, we ask whether emotion is really an architectural building block rather than merely content represented by an otherwise neutral system. Supporting evidence comes from recent mechanistic interpretability work on large language models showing that the best model of human linguistic output is organised around functional emotions (Sofroniew et al., 2026). Given the ubiquity of emotion in human language, this may not seem a surprising finding to some, yet the work shows that these functional emotions are not just content but important for the model computation. Equally, other work has shown that large language models exhibit hierarchical (Proca et al., 2024; Urbina-Rodriguez et al., 2026) and quantum-like computational regimes (Riechers et al., 2025) not dissimilar to those found in the biological brain. Fourth, we draw out the consequences of treating the three strands as an entangled architecture for theories of consciousness, empirical prediction, neuropsychiatric illness and the restoration of flourishing.

The resulting theory we call the Entangled Loop. Brains are under severe thermodynamic constraint to decide quickly what matters using very little energy, combine distributed information into coherent interpretations and make selected content rapidly available across the system. Emotion addresses the first problem through valenced compression and the continuous scheduling of competing needs, interference addresses the second by allowing distributed signals to reinforce or cancel one another, and hierarchical orchestration addresses the third through integration, ignition and broadcast. The mathematical description of the interference regime is quantum-like, but the proposed substrate is entirely classical, consisting of coupled neural dynamics constrained by the structural connectome (Deco et al., 2026b; Scholes, 2024 Scholes, , 2026)). The term quantum-like interference therefore describes a form of probability and inference rather than quantum biology. Crucially, the three operations are not independent modules or successive stages. What matters changes what competes for binding and broadcast, interference changes the coherent content available to orchestration, and orchestration changes which contents can redirect the continuing emotional cycle. Their mutual constraint is what makes the architecture a loop and their vulnerability to one another is what makes it entangled (see Figure 1).

The architecture of consciousness is represented as a trefoil whose three lobes correspond to emotion, interference-based quantum-like inference and hierarchical orchestration. This paper is one of three companion preprints that together set out a mathematical unification of brain dynamics and its implications for the architecture of conscious experience. The single-operator unification in the One Operator preprint (Kringelbach and Deco, 2026) demonstrates that connectome harmonics, the turbulence smoothing kernel and the complex harmonics form are three readings of one self-adjoint operator under the functional calculus, an identity tested with an LSD perturbation in which a single scalar coupling parameter predicts shifts in two mathematically independent functional domains. The present paper sets out the architectural theory this unification scaffolds, in which the primacy of emotion, the quantum-like character of binding inference and the hierarchical orchestration that coordinates them are derived from a single thermodynamic constraint. A first application of the framework, the Canary in the Mind preprint (Deco et al., 2026c), shows that a signature of entanglement derived from the same operator carries predictive information about adolescent depression and anxiety at one-year follow-up, with external replication identified as the immediate next step.

Thermodynamics, emotion and the affective brain

Biological organisms are open systems that maintain themselves far from thermodynamic equilibrium. The fundamental constraint on any such system is to remain in its characteristic regime of order while expending as little energy as possible. We elaborated the principles in our Thermodynamics of Mind framework (Kringelbach et al., 2024a), from which the key elements of emotion, turbulent dynamics, quantum-like inference and hierarchical orchestration can all be derived.

Consider the computational problem faced by an animal needing to survive. At every moment, innumerable possible actions are available; each with uncertain consequences. The cost of computing expected utility over the full action space is prohibitive, and the time available is limited. Evolution's solution was not to compute better but to compress. Valence, the reduction of any situation to a graded signal of good-for-me or bad-for-me, turns an intractable optimisation into a rapidly evaluable approximation. Emotion is this fast, time-critical compression in a distributed hedonic network, which eventually gives rise to conscious feeling, i.e., what it feels like from the inside.

Contemporary emotion theory, as scoped by Kringelbach and Phillips (2014) in a synthesis of the field, organises this compression across several complementary traditions. The dimensional approach describes emotional states along continuous axes of valence and arousal (Barrett, 2017; Russell, 1980). The basic-emotions tradition posits discrete evolved categories with dedicated neural circuits (Ekman, 1992; Panksepp, 1998). The appraisal tradition centres the evaluation of events relative to goals (Lazarus, 1991; Scherer, 2009). The constructionist tradition treats emotions as categorisations of core affect (Barrett, 2017; Russell, 2003). The somatic-feedback tradition from James (1884) and Lange (1887) through Nauta (1971) and Damasio (1994 Damasio ( , 2010) ) grounds emotion in the registration of body states. Kringelbach and Berridge synthesise these around the repeating pleasure cycle, comprising anticipatory wanting, true consummatory liking and predictive satiety, which is the elementary unit of affective function (Kringelbach and Berridge, 2009). As shown in Figure 2, the Entangled Loop inherits this synthesis: The pleasure cycle is the empirical manifestation of emotion's evolutionary function in adaptive decision-making, and the distributed hedonic network is the anatomical substrate of the compression the thermodynamic argument requires.

The three phases also differ in their relation to the reward and, together, provide a continuous scheduler for behaviour (Georgiadis and Kringelbach, 2012; Kringelbach et al., 2012). Wanting is predictive because it draws the animal towards a reward before it is obtained, while satiety is likewise predictive because it forecasts when enough has been obtained for the current pursuit to yield to another before the slower bodily consequences are fully available. Liking is different because it is the hedonic read-out when the reward is actually obtained, providing the momentary truth that drives learning throughout the cycle, including the subsequent predictions of both wanting and satiety. The cycle can therefore move among competing needs without reducing them to a single reward value: Wanting opens a pursuit, liking registers what was actually obtained, and satiety progressively closes that pursuit so that another can become dominant. Selective satiety is thus the scheduling operation by which emotion continuously organises what the organism should do next (Berridge and Kringelbach, 2015; Kringelbach, 2005; Kringelbach and Berridge, 2009; Kringelbach et al., 2003; Rolls et al., 1989).

Figure 2. Evidence from emotion research.

A) The pleasure cycle is a continuous scheduler for competing biological priorities. Wanting is predictive, drawing the organism towards a reward before it is obtained; liking is the true hedonic read-out when the reward is obtained and drives learning throughout the cycle; and satiety is predictive, forecasting when enough has been obtained for the current pursuit to yield to another. Learning can therefore take place throughout the cycle rather than belonging to any single phase. Selective satiety allows competing rewards to take turns, so that emotion does not merely assign value but continuously organises what the organism should do next. Wanting and liking are dissociable, with dopaminergic and opioidergic manipulations differentially altering the two components, establishing that the hedonic architecture is not a monolithic reward signal (Berridge and Robinson, 1998; Smith et al., 2011). Selective satiety changes the priority of a particular reward with current motivational state rather than suppressing reward globally, providing the empirical basis for the scheduler (Kringelbach et al., 2003; Kringelbach et al., 2012). Satiation and satiety operate over different timescales, allowing an ongoing pursuit to end while controlling when it becomes eligible again (Blundell and Burley, 1987). B) The liking phase depends on specific hedonic hotspots (blue) and coldspots (red) that can enhance or suppress pleasure and are found in regions of rodent brains such as the ventral pallidum (VP), parabrachial nucleus (PBN), orbitofrontal cortex (OFC), insula cortex and nucleus accumbens (NAc) (Berridge and Kringelbach, 2015). The medial shell of the NAc contains a map of hotspots and coldspots established with opioid agonist microinjections. Human homologues form part of a distributed hedonic network in which direct causal intervention, including deep brain stimulation, can predictably alter affective experience.

The affective neuroscience of this network is well characterised in humans and other animals. In particular, Berridge's programme of optogenetic activation, pharmacological microinjection and selective lesion in rodents has established that wanting and liking can be directly and predictably dissociated by intervention (Berridge and Kringelbach, 2015). The research has identified the hedonic hotspots generating hedonic impact in subcortical (nucleus accumbens shell, ventral pallidum and parabrachial nucleus) and cortical regions (orbitofrontal, anterior cingulate and insula cortices), where the latter have been shown to extend and contextualise evaluation (Berridge and Kringelbach, 2008, 2015; Kringelbach and Berridge, 2009; Morales and Berridge, 2026). This has revealed the hierarchical organising principle in mammals between sensory and evaluation regions, selective satiety experiments demonstrating that hedonic evaluation is computed in the orbitofrontal cortex relative to current motivational state (Kringelbach, 2005), while primary sensory representation remains stable (Kringelbach et al., 2003; Rolls et al., 1989).

In humans, deep brain stimulation provides complementary interventional evidence. Kringelbach and colleagues provide an overview of the mechanistic principles including the ability to rebalance emotional circuits through specific interventions including deep brain stimulation of the periaqueductal grey for chronic pain which, with correct electrode placement, at low frequencies (20-40Hz) can lead to pain relief or severe pain at high frequencies (over 100 Hz) (Kringelbach et al., 2010; Kringelbach et al., 2007). In this vein, Bijanki and colleagues showed that direct stimulation of a specific region of the anterior cingulate cortex can evoke marked positive affect and anxiolysis, showing that direct perturbation of this circuitry can change reported and observed affective state (Bijanki et al., 2019). Other examples include how deep brain stimulation for chronic pain in the anterior cingulate cortex demonstrates the same affective tractability (Boccard et al., 2013), and how closed-loop deep brain stimulation systems using amygdala activity as a feedback signal produced rapid symptom relief in an individual with treatment-resistant depression (Scangos et al., 2021). Together these interventions show that direct perturbation of affective circuitry can alter pain, positive affect and depressive symptoms, extending correlational neuroimaging evidence with causal leverage over components of felt experience.

Further, support for fast valence compression taking place before the well-known conscious bottleneck after around 250 ms (Dehaene and Changeux, 2011), comes from research mapping the temporal dynamics of responses to evolutionarily important infant stimuli (Kringelbach et al., 2016). A study using local field potential recordings from deep brain electrodes in the periaqueductal grey in four chronic pain patients showed differences in response to infant vocalisations compared to constructed control sounds as early as 49 ms after stimulus onset (Parsons et al., 2014). Compared to natural control sounds (adult cries and animal distress sounds), significant differences were observed at 86 ms. Studies using magnetoencephalography found that visual and auditory infant stimuli ignite activity in the orbitofrontal cortex at around 130 ms, ensuring privileged access to both the sight and sound of infants facilitating the caregiving needed for the survival of the species (Kringelbach et al., 2008; Young et al., 2016). This is the first empirical anchor of the perspective. We interpret this evidence within the thermodynamic argument as suggesting why emotion became evolutionarily indispensable: Valence compresses the survival problem into a form that biological tissue can evaluate within its limited metabolic budget. And directly manipulating this emotional machinery leads to predictable changes in feeling state.

From thermodynamic pressure to turbulence, interference and quantum-like inference

The thermodynamic imperative does more than select for valenced heuristics at the behavioural level. It also shapes how neural populations can combine information. Unlike a Turing machine, a brain does not have sufficient energy to evaluate every signal independently and then exhaustively compare every possible interpretation. Oscillatory systems provide a candidate physical interference mechanism for bringing distributed activity together within the dynamics themselves. When coupled oscillations meet, their activity can reinforce or cancel other activity according to their relative phase. Unlike a simple sum of independent signals, the resulting state depends on relationships among the signals themselves. Mammalian brains operate through coupled oscillatory dynamics, including turbulent-like dynamics that support long-range information processing (Deco and Kringelbach, 2020; Deco et al., 2026a; Deco et al., 2021a). Rare long-range anatomical connections violating the common exponential distance rule of brain wiring are particularly important for this distributed coordination (Deco et al., 2021a; Ercsey-Ravasz et al., 2013).

Two everyday objects show the difference. Throw two dice and count the routes to a total, and more routes can only ever mean more of that total, because probabilities are never negative and can only add. Drop two stones into still water and watch where the ripples meet, and where crest meets trough the surface lies flat. Nobody computed that flatness, which is simply what phase does, and anything that has phase gets it free, whether the colour of a butterfly's wing without any pigment or the silence a noise-cancelling headphone makes by addition.

Now imagine a thousand oscillators with random phases, one of which is wired against the rest. Nothing in their amplitudes betrays it and their phases look like noise. A machine that finds it by testing must generate, hold and reject candidates, with each physical erasure carrying a thermodynamic cost. Switch on the coupling instead and the random phases wash each other out into a single synchronised wave, while the oscillator wired against the field cannot join and is left standing in antiphase, in plain sight. Nothing was generated, nothing was tested and nothing was rejected, since the wrong candidates cancelled one another rather than being represented, tested and discarded by the machine. The answer is what is left standing. This is what it means for interference to perform binding that classical inference cannot achieve at the same cost, and it is why the law of total probability, which only ever adds, cannot describe it. The price is instead paid largely in building and maintaining a medium coherent enough to carry the collective wave, which in the brain is supported by the rare long-range wiring that reshapes the spectrum. Evolution pays for the architecture and gains a cheaper way of letting the dynamics settle on an answer.

Classical and interference-based inference differ in how alternatives are combined. Classical probability assumes that mutually exclusive alternatives combine additively according to the law of total probability, whereas interference allows alternatives to enhance or suppress one another before a probability is read out. When these relationships require a vector description in Hilbert space and exhibit contextuality, order effects or violations of the law of total probability, their mathematical form is the same as quantum probability (Busemeyer and Bruza, 2012; Khrennikov, 2010). Scholes and colleagues have proposed a mechanism consisting of classical coupled oscillators whose collective dynamics can support a quantum-like probability structure (Amati and Scholes, 2025; Scholes, 2024 Scholes, , 2026)). Importantly, the resulting regime is therefore called quantum-like, although nothing in this description requires microscopic quantum coherence or quantum events in neural tissue.

The long-range connections in the brain provide a candidate structural condition for sustaining this regime. The eigenvalue spectrum of a connectivity network can contain spectral gaps that separate a small set of dominant collective modes from the remaining spectrum (Deco et al., 2026b). Work by Scholes and colleagues shows how such gaps can stabilise interference-supporting states in networks of classical coupled oscillators (Amati and Scholes, 2025; Scholes, 2024 Scholes, , 2026)). A spectral gap can therefore provide the structural condition for interference, but the gap is not itself evidence that interference has changed the probability structure. For this, interference must be measured in the dynamics. Växjö interference provides such a measure by quantifying departures from the classical law of total probability (Khrennikov, 2010), and therefore tests whether the cancellation made possible by the spectral architecture has entered the inference itself. The human structural connectome contains the anatomical ingredients needed to ask whether this principle operates at whole-brain scale. In a recent proof-of-principle article, whole-brain models constructed in a quantum-like regime fitted empirical resting-state neuroimaging data better than matched non-quantum-like models and had a lower model-derived thermodynamic cost (Deco et al., 2026b). Across the whole-brain models, Växjö interference tracked the spectral-gap structure (Deco et al., 2026b; Deco et al., 2026c). These findings establish that the quantum-like description is empirically useful in the tested whole-brain models. They are consistent with the present biological hypothesis that the brain uses quantum-like inference for cognition.

Importantly, the framework proposed here distinguishes the physical mechanism of interference from the computational function of inference. Distributed oscillatory states can combine constructively and destructively as activity evolves across the connectome, while the resulting interference can contribute to updating the organism's world model by combining evidence carried by many signals. Quantum-like inference describes the regime in which this interference produces a non-classical probability structure, so that the same process can be described at the physical level by the interaction of oscillatory states and at the computational level by the inference those interactions make possible. The proposal is therefore not that the brain is a quantum computer, but that classical brain dynamics may exploit interference to support a form of inference whose probability laws extend beyond classical Bayesian addition (Busemeyer and Bruza, 2012; Deco et al., 2026b; Khrennikov, 2010).

Supporting evidence of why quantum-like inference from interference matters comes from behavioural studies. As an example, in the classic conjunction fallacy of the so-called Linda problem, participants judge that Linda is a bank teller and active in the feminist movement is more probable than Linda is a bank teller alone, a direct violation of classical probability (Tversky and Kahneman, 1983). To account for this, classical probability accounts have to invoke additional mechanisms including representativeness heuristic (Tversky and Kahneman, 1983), conversational implicature (Dulany and Hilton, 1991) and probability substitution (Kahneman and Frederick, 2002). In contrast, quantum-probability models naturally account for such judgements because the context in which alternatives are considered changes how they combine through interference (Aerts, 2009; Pothos and Busemeyer, 2009, 2013; Wang and Busemeyer, 2013). As such, the conjunction fallacy demonstrates that the observed judgement cannot be represented by unmodified additive classical probability. Additionally, the brain's severe energetic constraints are difficult to reconcile with the energetic cost of exhaustive Bayesian updating over the enormous space of possible interpretations. Interference offers a parsimonious route by which alternatives can be combined directly within the dynamics of the system (Deco et al., 2026b).

Distributed information must also be coordinated so that selected content can influence the rest of the system. The brain exhibits precisely such hierarchical coordination. A large reservoir of specialised regions is coupled to a smaller set of orchestrating regions, an organisation that sits naturally within the Global Neuronal Workspace tradition developed originally by Baars as a cognitive framework (Baars, 1988), given neuronal form by Jean-Pierre Changeux and his student Stanislas Dehaene (Dehaene et al., 1998) and since consolidated in empirical findings (Mashour et al., 2020). The three central concepts of this tradition are 1) the workspace as the shared integrative stage, 2) ignition as the non-linear threshold transition at which local content gains global access, and 3) broadcast as its distribution across the brain. The dynamical description of the orchestrating workspace has been described using whole-brain modelling and directed transfer entropy (Deco et al., 2021b; Kringelbach and Deco, 2024). In the Entangled Loop, hierarchy therefore provides the coordination through which valenced, interference-shaped content can become globally available.

This orchestration is itself dynamic. Work on the salience and midcingulo-insular systems has shown how behaviourally relevant information can recruit control and coordinate switching among large-scale networks, while cognitive and behavioural flexibility depends on interactions among frontoparietal, mid-cingulo-insular and frontostriatal systems (Uddin, 2015 (Uddin, , 2021)). The Entangled Loop extends this principle by proposing the gains of this flexible access is continually shaped by affective scheduling and interference rather than by orchestration alone.

Thermodynamic pressure favours rapid and economical integration. Coupled brain dynamics provide a plausible substrate for interference, the structural spectrum of the connectome can stabilise the relevant collective modes, and hierarchical orchestration can distribute the resulting content. Recent whole-brain modelling shows that this quantum-like regime is a promising description of large-scale brain dynamics, but its role in biological inference remains a testable hypothesis rather than an established fact (Deco et al., 2026b). The Entangled Loop proposes that interference in coupled oscillators across the spectral gaps performs the binding that classical inference cannot achieve at the necessary low biological energetic cost.

The companion One Operator preprint provides a deeper mathematical account of why these dynamical features may covary (Kringelbach and Deco, 2026). It shows that connectome harmonics, the turbulence smoothing kernel and the complex-harmonics interference propagator can be written as different functions of one self-adjoint connectome operator. This unification matters here because it suggests that hierarchy, turbulent propagation and interference are not three unrelated additions to the theory. The operator account proposes that they are different dynamical readings of the same anatomical organisation. We used a global perturbation to test this unification, and as predicted by the theory found that a single coupling parameter predicts changes in two mathematically distinct functional domains (Kringelbach and Deco, 2026). The operator formulation is therefore the mathematical foundation of the Entangled Loop, but the biological claim developed here is more accessible, where one anatomical architecture may jointly shape how information propagates, interferes and becomes hierarchically integrated.

There is a complementary thermodynamic reading of the same operator that is central to the Entangled Loop. Its symmetric, self-adjoint part specifies the reversible structure of the computation, including the spectral hierarchy and the interference that the connectome can support, but this structure by itself carries no arrow of time and therefore no irreversible cost. Running the computation in time introduces directed, non-equilibrium dynamics, whose departure from detailed balance can be quantified through entropy production and related to the cost of cognition (Deco et al., 2025; Kringelbach et al., 2024a). In the operator formulation developed here, this irreversible contribution is carried by the antisymmetric face of the dynamics. The distinction is therefore between the symmetric face of the operator specifying what computation is possible and the antisymmetric face of the operator specifying what it costs to run that computation in time. Importantly, both are governed by the same spectral architecture, so that rare long-range connections are associated with enhanced information processing (Deco et al., 2021a), while the spectral architecture provides a route to lower model-derived thermodynamic cost (Deco et al., 2025; Kringelbach and Deco, 2026). This more-forless relation completes the Entangled Loop thermodynamically because emotion, interference and hierarchy are not only supported by one architecture but must be continuously run along an arrow of time whose energetic price is written into the other face of the same operator.

The deeper consequence is that the spectral gaps do not serve a single computational function. Rare long-range exceptions reshape the spectrum into separated bands of collective modes, and this spectral-gap structure simultaneously constrains the hierarchical organisation available for orchestration, the phase relationships through which distributed dynamics can interfere and the cost and speed with which the resulting computation can be run. The same anatomical exceptions therefore buy several properties at once rather than separately optimising hierarchy, interference and energetic efficiency. This provides the architectural basis of the more-for-less principle, while also creating its vulnerability, since perturbing the sparse long-range connections can simultaneously degrade several functions that depend on the same spectral organisation.

Is emotion really the building block? Evidence from large language models

As shown, the thermodynamic argument places emotion at the ground of the adaptive computation. But a sceptic may object that this confuses content with architecture. Valenced processing might be one among many outputs of an otherwise neutral computational machinery, and the feeling of pleasure or pain might accompany certain representations without constituting the architectural ground from which representation proceeds.

Intriguing cross-substrate evidence from large language models provides a different way of testing whether emotion is merely represented content or whether it leaves a deeper computational signature. Large language models are trained on human linguistic output and therefore provide unusually powerful statistical models of structures that human minds repeatedly express in language. Mechanistic interpretability is beginning to reveal how these structures are represented internally, building on scaling monosemanticity (Templeton et al., 2024) and circuit tracing (Lindsey et al., 2025). Sofroniew and colleagues identified 171 internal representations of functional emotion concepts in Claude Sonnet 4.5, whose geometry recovers valence as the principal axis and arousal as the secondary axis, while steering these representations causally changes the model's preferences and behaviour (Sofroniew et al., 2026). The important question for the Entangled Loop is therefore not simply whether an artificial system contains emotion concepts, since a monolithic representation of reward would not recover the biological architecture, but whether optimisation over human language begins to separate the functions that the pleasure cycle keeps distinct.

The significance of this result increases because emotion is not the only signature of the Entangled Loop recovered independently in large language models. Riechers and colleagues showed that neural networks, including transformers, can spontaneously discover quantum-like generative representations of structured training data (Riechers et al., 2025), while Urbina-Rodriguez and colleagues (Urbina-Rodriguez et al., 2026), using the partial information decomposition framework developed by Rosas and colleagues (2020), identified a synergistic core in which middle layers carry information available only through their collective activity. This resembles the synergistic organisation found in the human brain (Luppi et al., 2022), and damaging these synergistic components produces disproportionate changes in behaviour and performance. Thus three features that arise from very different literatures, namely affective geometry, quantum-like representation and synergistic hierarchy, are independently recovered when artificial systems become sufficiently powerful models of human linguistic output. Their convergence matters because the models were not designed to instantiate the Entangled Loop, nor were these three properties imposed as a common architectural objective. Instead, optimisation over the statistical structure of human language recovers features that are also central to the functional organisation we propose for the biological system that generated that language.

The biological architecture operates under a metabolic budget and must continually maintain itself far from equilibrium, while its dynamics unfold through a physical connectome whose spectral organisation couples hierarchy, propagation and interference. A transformer instead acquires its emotion geometry, quantum-like representations and synergistic hierarchy through optimisation over language, without a physical connectome whose spectral gaps jointly generate these properties. Nor does it contain the self-renewing pleasure cycle of wanting, liking and satiety through which biological valuation continually changes what matters to the organism. The distinction is therefore architectural rather than taxonomic. Similar computational signatures can occur in both systems, while only the living brain contains the thermodynamically constrained dynamical loop in which the three signatures are physically coupled over time.

The cross-substrate convergence provides a third, independent constraint on the perspective because it approaches the proposed architecture from the opposite direction. The affective and wholebrain evidence begins with biological organisation and asks what computation it supports, whereas mechanistic interpretability begins with a system trained on the products of human cognition and asks what internal organisation is required to reproduce them. That the optimisation independently recovers functional emotion geometry, quantum-like representations and synergistic hierarchy makes these features unlikely to be arbitrary additions to the biological theory. Yet the comparison also exposes what a language model lacks. Recovering valence or an emotion vector is not equivalent to possessing the pleasure cycle, because the biological architecture separates predictive wanting, true liking and predictive satiety within a continuously turning scheduler whose learning is driven across the cycle by what is actually liked. This is also why reinforcement learning has the wrong shape for the biological problem: By collapsing reward into a monolithic value to be maximised, it removes the distinction between prediction, experienced outcome and satiety, whereas the pleasure cycle exists precisely because competing biological needs must take turns rather than be reduced to one scalar. The stronger cross-substrate prediction is therefore that increasingly faithful models of human cognition should reveal functional differentiation corresponding to the cycle, rather than merely a richer vocabulary of affect. Finding such differentiation would strengthen the claim that human linguistic output carries a trace of the architecture that generated it, while its absence would mark a principled boundary between statistical emotion representation and the affective scheduler proposed here.

Towards a stronger theory of consciousness

Most theories of consciousness isolate one of the operations that the Entangled Loop treats as inseparable. Global Neuronal Workspace theory (Baars, 1988; Dehaene et al., 1998; Mashour et al., 2020) explains how selected content ignites and is broadcast across a hierarchical workspace, Integrated Information Theory (Oizumi et al., 2014; Tononi, 2004 Tononi, , 2008 Tononi, , 2016;; Tononi et al., 1994) asks how causal integration gives a system unity, Higher-Order Theories (Brown et al., 2019; Lau and Rosenthal, 2011) place the decisive step in meta-representation, and predictive processing and active inference (Clark, 2016; Friston, 2010; Friston et al., 2017; Friston et al., 2006; Friston et al., 2020; Parr et al., 2022; Rao and Ballard, 1999) explain perception and action through inference within a generative world model. Northoff's Temporo-Spatial Theory adds the important insight that consciousness depends on the intrinsic organisation of brain dynamics across space and time (Northoff and Huang, 2017). The Entangled Loop shares this dynamical starting point but asks how this is constrained by emotion, interference and hierarchy. Each theory captures an important property of conscious processing, but none begins with the biological problem that precedes them all, namely how a living system continually decides what matters, binds the distributed information bearing on that decision and gives the resulting content control of one body.

The affective-primacy tradition begins closer to this problem. Panksepp (1998) placed primaryprocess emotional systems at the evolutionary foundation of consciousness, Solms and Friston (2018) and Solms (2021) locate its primitive form in brainstem affective arousal, Damasio (1994 Damasio ( , 2010 Damasio ( , 2018) ) builds conscious experience from homeostatic body-state registration, and Craig (2015) from interoception. This raises an important anatomical divide. Higher-order accounts place conscious emotional experience in cortical networks (LeDoux and Brown, 2017), whereas Panksepp and Solms allow more elementary consciousness to arise subcortically. Merker reaches a similar conclusion from a different direction, arguing from comparative evidence and observations of children with hydranencephaly that upper brainstem systems may support basic conscious experience (Aleman and Merker, 2014; Merker, 2007). The Entangled Loop does not identify consciousness with cortex itself. Instead, cortex may greatly enlarge the repertoire, flexibility and contents available to the more fundamental entanglement of emotion, interference and orchestration.

The Entangled Loop shares the commitment of the affective-primacy theories that affect is constitutively prior to neutral cognition but gives that primacy a more specific computational form. Emotion compresses the survival problem into valence and the pleasure cycle continuously schedules competing needs, with predictive wanting and satiety organised around the true hedonic read-out of liking and with what is liked driving learning throughout the cycle (Berridge and Kringelbach, 2015; Kringelbach and Berridge, 2009). Affective primacy is therefore evolutionary and architectural, but it is not affective exclusivity.

The distinction matters because emotion alone cannot make a conscious world. Valenced scheduling must be bound with distributed sensory, memory and bodily information, and the resulting content must gain sufficient hierarchical access to influence the organism as a whole. The Entangled Loop assigns these operations to interference and orchestration. Interference between coupled brain dynamics provides a candidate mechanism for combining distributed signals into context-sensitive, quantum-like inference (Deco et al., 2026b; Scholes, 2024 Scholes, , 2026)), while the orchestrating regions provide the hierarchical integration, ignition and broadcast described by the global neuronal workspace (Deco et al., 2021b; Kringelbach and Deco, 2024). Emotion determines what matters for survival, interference shapes how the evidence bearing on those alternatives is combined, and orchestration determines what gains global access and can redirect the continuing cycle.

This is why the trefoil knot in Figure 1 is more than an illustration. The three strands are entangled because each constrains the operation of the other two. Just as the trefoil is the simplest non-trivial knot, none can be cut away while leaving the same system intact. A disturbance of affective scheduling changes what enters interference and orchestration; a disturbance of interference compromises the binding of otherwise intact valenced information and therefore what can be orchestrated; and a disturbance of orchestration prevents otherwise intact valenced and bound content from being appropriately selected and broadcast. Emotion has evolutionary primacy because it answers what matters to the organism, but conscious experience belongs to the whole trefoil, whose operation begins to unravel when any strand is sufficiently perturbed.

This also clarifies how the Entangled Loop extends the Beautiful Loop (Laukkonen et al., 2025). The Beautiful Loop captures the recursive self-evidencing structure by which epistemic states are sustained within active inference, whereas the Entangled Loop asks what gives such a loop biological direction, content and priority. The pleasure cycle supplies the continuous scheduling of what matters, interference supplies a physical route by which distributed evidence can be bound without reducing inference to sequential classical Bayesian updating, and the orchestrating hierarchy supplies the metalevel integration through which selected content gains global access. The result is not an alternative loop laid beside the Beautiful Loop but a proposal for the biological architecture that makes a selfevidencing conscious loop possible in a brain.

This architecture changes the framing of the hard problem. Panksepp, Solms and Damasio have all argued that Chalmers (1995 Chalmers ( , 1996) ) framed the explanatory gap identified by Levine (1983) from the wrong starting point, since neutral perceptual representation has no intrinsic reason to feel like anything while affect is already concerned with how things are going for the organism. The Entangled Loop sharpens this argument by placing felt quality within a continuously turning architecture rather than in a monolithic reward signal. Wanting and satiety predict, liking is the true hedonic read-out, and what is liked drives learning throughout the cycle; interference binds this valenced process with the wider contents of the moment, while hierarchical orchestration determines which of those bound contents are ignited and broadcast. Feeling is therefore not added to a completed neutral representation, but arises when the valenced cycle is bound into a coherent field and made globally available through the other two strands.

The three strands do not deductively prove an identity between this architecture and phenomenal experience, and arguably no theory of consciousness can do so (Kirk, 2019; Kripke, 1980). They do, however, specify a biological process with the properties an identity claim requires, including why experience has valenced quality, why it is unified rather than fragmented and why only some ongoing affective processing becomes consciously felt. The familiar comparison with peripheral fibres makes the point. C-fibres carry nociceptive information and C-tactile fibres preferentially signal gentle affiliative touch (Löken et al., 2009; McGlone et al., 2014), but neither peripheral signal contains the phenomenal quality of pain or pleasure. That quality belongs to what the entangled architecture does with the signal as it enters the cycle, is bound with the rest of the moment and gains hierarchical broadcast. The conceivability of a zombie hedonic system consequently loses force, not because hedonia alone is consciousness, but because removing felt quality while preserving the complete operation of the entangled architecture requires preserving the very valenced, binding and globally orchestrated process whose subjective aspect is at issue.

Empirical predictions

The trefoil architecture of the Entangled Loop yields a stronger class of falsifiable predictions than three independent mechanisms would provide. If emotion, interference and orchestration are truly entangled, a sufficiently strong perturbation entering through any one strand should propagate into measurable changes in the other two, while different primary points of failure can converge on overlapping conscious and clinical phenotypes. The decisive tests therefore concern both the local effect of a perturbation and the pattern of cross-strand propagation that follows.

Global Neuronal Workspace theory (Dehaene et al., 1998; Mashour et al., 2020) predicts that failures of consciousness reflect failures of ignition and broadcast. The Entangled Loop retains this essential role for orchestration but predicts that a primary workspace perturbation will not remain confined to global availability. Loss of orchestrating coherence should alter which valenced pursuits can dominate the pleasure cycle scheduler and should change the interference structure through which distributed content is bound. Conversely, perturbations beginning in affective scheduling or interference should propagate into directed transfer entropy organisation of the orchestrating ensemble (Deco et al., 2021b; Kringelbach and Deco, 2024). The temporal order of these changes provides a direct way to distinguish the strand through which a disturbance first entered the trefoil.

Integrated Information Theory (Oizumi et al., 2014; Tononi, 2004 Tononi, , 2008 Tononi, , 2016;; Tononi et al., 1994) predicts that reductions in consciousness reflect reductions in integrated information and disruption of the cause-effect repertoire. The Entangled Loop predicts a more structured failure. Integration can deteriorate because affective scheduling has become rigid, because interference no longer binds distributed information effectively or because orchestration no longer makes the resulting content globally available, and these routes need not produce the same trajectory through state space. Whole-brain perturbation should therefore reveal distinct initial failure modes followed by convergence as disruption spreads through the trefoil, rather than a uniform loss of integration alone (Kringelbach and Deco, 2026).

Higher-Order Theories (Brown et al., 2019; Lau and Rosenthal, 2011) place the decisive impairment at the metacognitive level. The Entangled Loop predicts that metacognitive failure is only one possible entry point and need not be primary. A patient may retain metacognitive capacity while the pleasure-cycle scheduler becomes impoverished, as in anhedonic states, or may retain hedonic reactions while interference or orchestration fails to bind and broadcast them appropriately. The theory therefore predicts dissociations not simply between first-order and higher-order representation but among intact liking, predictive wanting and satiety, interference-based binding and orchestrating access, with the pattern of secondary changes revealing their entanglement.

Predictive processing and active inference (Clark, 2016; Friston, 2010; Parr et al., 2022), and in particular the Beautiful Loop (Laukkonen et al., 2025), predict that conscious processing depends on recursively integrated inference within a generative model. The Entangled Loop adds that the relevant biological object is not a monolithic preference or reward function but a directed cycle that continuously schedules competing needs. Perturbing wanting, liking or satiety should therefore alter not merely the probability assigned to an outcome but the temporal ordering and eligibility of subsequent pursuits, with secondary changes in interference and orchestration. The Warlow-Berridge paradoxical experiment shows unusually clearly what happens when the normal cycle is shortcircuited (Warlow et al., 2020). Warlow and colleagues paired encounters with an electrified rod with optogenetic activation of the central amygdala, amplifying pursuit without making the shock pleasurable. The animals remained aversive to the shock yet repeatedly returned to the rod while stimulation was present, with normal avoidance returning when it ceased. Optogenetic stimulation therefore holds wanting open while liking remains negative, bypassing the normal cycle through which experienced outcomes drive subsequent learning. The paradox is not wholly unfamiliar from human life, where people can sometimes pursue experiences that remain painful. Crucially, the experiment does not turn something bad into something good but makes the animal continue to want something that remains bad.

The classical temporal-difference account formalises reward prediction within reinforcement learning (Schultz et al., 1997), but the Warlow-Berridge paradox exposes a structural impossibility for a monolithic scalar value. A value representing the aversive outcome must remain negative and cannot sustain approach, whereas making it positive enough to sustain approach means that it no longer represents the outcome the animal experiences. Reproducing the behaviour therefore requires a separately manipulable motivational quantity, abandoning the monolithic reward architecture. Selective satiety reveals the complementary requirement of normal scheduling, since a reward can remain pleasurable while its priority falls once enough has been obtained, allowing another need to take control (Kringelbach et al., 2003; Kringelbach et al., 2012). The pleasure cycle accommodates both cases because wanting, liking and satiety remain separate components of a continuously turning scheduler rather than different readings of one scalar value.

The affective-primacy tradition predicts that sufficiently large disruption of brainstem or interoceptive affective circuitry can abolish primitive sentience (Craig, 2015; Damasio, 2010 Damasio, , 2018;; Panksepp, 1998; Solms, 2021; Solms and Friston, 2018). The Entangled Loop agrees but predicts that neuropsychiatric illness need not begin in emotion. Anhedonia and avolition can arise through disturbances of liking and wanting (Husain and Roiser, 2018; Pizzagalli, 2022; Rømer Thomsen et al., 2015; Treadway and Zald, 2011), but pathological rigidity can also begin in the interference dynamics or in the orchestrating ensemble and subsequently deform affective scheduling. Similar symptoms may therefore arise from different primary failures, while different symptoms may share a common downstream collapse of the trefoil. This provides a mechanistic account of clinical heterogeneity rather than treating every disorder as a different lesion of a single affective variable.

At the computational level, the Entangled Loop predicts that quantum-like whole-brain models should fit neuroimaging data better than appropriately matched classical alternatives across independent datasets and brain states. It also predicts that the thermodynamic advantage quantified by the COCO framework (Deco et al., 2026b; Deco et al., 2025) will covary with the integrity of the spectral architecture. More specifically, perturbations that degrade interference should alter orchestrating coherence and the flexibility of affective scheduling even when mean activity and conventional functional connectivity remain similar, while interventions that restore the spectral architecture should recover these other strands together. Cross-strand recovery, rather than improvement in one measure alone, is the characteristic prediction of an entangled architecture.

The same architecture predicts a distinctive human vulnerability. Rare long-range connections are most developed in the human connectome and support the wider spectral gaps associated with enhanced information processing at lower thermodynamic cost (Deco et al., 2025; Deco et al., 2021a). Yet this computational advantage is inseparable from fragility, since disruption of these sparse connections degrades the spectral-gap structure and can propagate through interference, orchestration and emotion because the three strands are entangled. This provides a structural reason why animal models can reproduce particular circuits or symptoms while remaining poor models of many human neuropsychiatric disorders. Brains with less developed long-range architecture do not possess the same flexible repertoire and therefore cannot express all of the failure modes of the human trefoil. The architecture that enables more computation for less is also the architecture that creates a distinctively human vulnerability.

The same principle sharpens the cross-substrate prediction. Many things become possible for large language models that are not constrained by time-critical behaviour on a small energy budget. It has been shown that larger and more capable language models recover emotion geometry (Sofroniew et al., 2026), quantum-like representations (Riechers et al., 2025) and synergistic hierarchy (Urbina-Rodriguez et al., 2026), but the critical test is whether these remain independent signatures. At present there is no evidence that these signatures are functionally entangled in a loop. The recovered valence geometry does not yet amount to a pleasure-cycle scheduler separating wanting, liking and satiety. Mechanistic intervention should therefore ask whether distinct representations corresponding to anticipation, experienced hedonic outcome and enough can be dissociated, whether actual outcomes drive learning across those functions and whether perturbing one propagates into the model's synergistic and quantum-like organisation. Recovering such cross-component dependence would be stronger evidence for the linguistic trace of the Entangled Loop than valence-arousal geometry alone (Lindsey et al., 2025; Sofroniew et al., 2026; Templeton et al., 2024).

Toward flourishing

If the Entangled Loop is a trefoil, neuropsychiatric disorders are not disorders of emotion alone but failures of a powerful, yet fragile architecture whose three strands continually constrain one another. A disturbance can enter through affective scheduling, interference or orchestration and then propagate, so that the symptoms observed clinically may reflect both the primary fault and the secondary reorganisation of the remaining strands. Mental health is correspondingly not the optimisation of a single hedonic variable but the capacity of the whole architecture to keep valenced priorities flexible, bind them coherently and orchestrate their passage through time (Kringelbach and Berridge, 2009; Kringelbach et al., 2024a; Kringelbach et al., 2024b).

The first empirical signal of this clinical agenda is reported in the companion Canary in the Mind preprint (Deco et al., 2026c), in which Växjö Interference Connectivity derived from the same operator predicts depression and anxiety symptoms one year later in adolescents. The importance of this result for the Entangled Loop is precisely that the predictive marker is not itself an emotion measure. It reads the interference architecture, yet predicts later affective symptoms, which is consistent with, although it does not establish, the prediction that disturbance in one strand can have consequences expressed in another. Two adolescents with similar mean activity, conventional functional connectivity and static anatomy may therefore differ in architectural tuning before their clinical trajectories diverge, making cross-strand vulnerability detectable before symptoms consolidate.

Whole-brain modelling provides a way to distinguish these failure modes and to test how repair propagates through the architecture (Deco and Kringelbach, 2025). A depressive phenotype may begin with impoverished liking, maladaptive wanting or satiety, with rigid interference dynamics, or with impaired orchestration, and these different starting points should respond differently to perturbation even when their clinical presentation overlaps. Psilocybin can flatten and open hierarchy (Carhart-Harris et al., 2016; Carhart-Harris and Friston, 2019; Carhart-Harris et al., 2014; Daws et al., 2022; Deco et al., 2024), SSRIs modulate cognitive-affective bias (Harmer et al., 2017), ketamine can rapidly disrupt maladaptive dynamics (Krystal et al., 2019; Murrough et al., 2017), and deep brain stimulation directly perturbs selected circuits (Bijanki et al., 2019; Kringelbach et al., 2010; Kringelbach et al., 2007; Mayberg et al., 2005; Scangos et al., 2021). The Entangled Loop predicts that an effective intervention need not act first on the strand in which symptoms are most visible. Successful treatment should instead be recognisable by restoration spreading across the trefoil, with improved affective scheduling, interference and orchestration becoming coupled consequences of rebalancing the architecture.

The aim of such interventions need not be the reduction of neuropsychiatric symptoms alone. The ancient Aristotelian concept of eudaimonia, flourishing, or the exercise of distinctively human capacities at their best (Aristotle, 1998), provides the philosophical ancestor for what the Entangled Loop proposes, though with three important modifications. First, classical eudaimonia is an end-oflife achievement, the stable excellence of a whole life lived in accordance with virtue, assessable only in retrospect. The Entangled Loop's sense is in-the-moment, a dynamical state of the brain occurring now in lived time. Second, the content of this flourishing is meaningful pleasure, the integration of anticipatory wanting, consummatory liking and the higher-order sense of significance (Kringelbach and Berridge, 2009; Kringelbach et al., 2024b), rather than the exercise of virtue in Aristotle's sense. Third, unlike classical eudaimonia conceived as a relatively stable achievement, the Entangled Loop's flourishing is intrinsically unstable because the brain's dynamics are intrinsically unstable. On this account flourishing should be achievable when the orchestrating regions and hedonic network can be returned towards coordinated function, but it is not a plateau one reaches and occupies. It is a dynamical state that must be continually renewed, lost and recovered, and this instability is not a failure of flourishing but its condition. The clinical and personal task is therefore not to secure flourishing permanently but to develop the capacity for its repeated achievement.

This reformulation connects affective neuroscience to contemporary positive psychology. Ryan and Deci (2001), Keyes (2002) and Fredrickson (2013) have developed empirical operationalisations of eudaimonic wellbeing, and Huta and Waterman (2014) surveyed the conceptual landscape. The Entangled Loop makes flourishing more neurobiologically specific without reducing it to pleasure alone. Meaningful pleasure depends on the full pleasure cycle (Kringelbach and Berridge, 2009; Kringelbach et al., 2024b), but flourishing requires that this scheduler remain flexibly coupled to interference and orchestration so that what matters can be coherently bound, selected and renewed. Flourishing is therefore a property of the functioning trefoil loop rather than of any single strand, which is why restoring it may require intervention at a point different from the one at which suffering is most apparent.

Conclusion

We have proposed that consciousness in mammalian brains emerges from an entangled architecture of emotion, interference and hierarchical orchestration, shaped by the common thermodynamic demand to remain far from equilibrium while computing quickly and economically. Emotion has evolutionary primacy because it compresses the intractable problem of survival into valence and, through the pleasure cycle, continuously schedules competing needs. Yet affective primacy is not affective sufficiency. The valenced content of the cycle must be bound with distributed information through interference and selected, integrated and broadcast through the orchestrating hierarchy before it can contribute to the unified field of conscious experience. The three strands therefore do not form a sequence of independent mechanisms but a trefoil loop in which each strand continually constrains the other two, so that breaking any strand progressively changes the operation of the whole.

The same architecture has two inseparable mathematical faces. The symmetric face of the operator specifies what computation the connectome makes possible, including its hierarchy and interference, while its antisymmetric face carries the irreversible traversal of those possibilities through time and, in the thermodynamic formulation, the cost of running the computation. The spectral-gap structure links these faces, concentrating useful dynamics into collective modal bands while providing the architecture through which information-processing capacity, traversal speed and thermodynamic cost become coupled (Deco et al., 2025; Kringelbach and Deco, 2026). The pleasure cycle gives this arrow of time its biological purpose, since a scheduler must move from one priority to another and later return, rather than merely assign each a value. Consciousness on the Entangled Loop account is therefore not a static representation or a monolithic reward but the continuing operation of an affective scheduler whose contents are bound and orchestrated as the organism moves through time.

This entanglement also changes how neuropsychiatric illness can be understood. Pathology need not begin with emotion, since a primary disturbance of affective scheduling, interference or orchestration can propagate through the other strands and eventually produce overlapping symptoms. The resulting heterogeneity is expected rather than anomalous, because similar clinical states can be reached from different points of entry into the trefoil loop. Conversely, effective intervention may restore the architecture by acting on a strand other than the one in which symptoms are most obvious. The clinical aim is consequently not simply to suppress pathological affect but to restore the flexible entanglement through which wanting, liking and satiety can schedule meaningful pursuits, distributed information can remain coherently bound and orchestrating regions can keep the resulting repertoire available for adaptive behaviour and flourishing.

The Entangled Loop thereby makes a stronger claim than that emotion, interference and hierarchy are all important for consciousness. Their mutual dependence and entanglement are the architecture. The theory generates falsifiable predictions of cross-strand propagation across perturbations, brain states and disorders, and it gives cross-substrate findings in large language models a sharper test, since recovering emotion geometry or a monolithic reward representation is not enough without the cyclic differentiation and entanglement proposed here for biological brains. The felt quality of experience may therefore not have been an afterthought of evolution but part of the solution by which brains learned what matters, scheduled what to do next and kept that changing world coherently available as they acted within it.

Glossary

Active inference. The framework in which perception and action are jointly explained as minimisation of variational free energy, with the brain treated as a generative model that evidences itself through coordinated inference and behaviour. Affective primacy. The claim that emotion is evolutionarily and architecturally prior in establishing what matters for conscious organisms. In the Entangled Loop this does not imply affective sufficiency, since valenced content becomes conscious only through its entanglement with interference and hierarchical orchestration.

COCO (Cost of Cognition).

The framework of Deco and colleagues (Deco et al., 2025) that derives an analytical expression linking energy cost, non-equilibrium departure and information processing for measurable brain states. Conscious feeling. The phenomenological expression of valenced content when the continuing affective cycle is bound with distributed information through interference and gains global access through orchestration. Feeling is therefore not added to a neutral representation but arises within the functioning trefoil. Consciousness. On the Entangled Loop account, the sustained operation of an entangled architecture in which emotion continuously schedules what matters, interference binds distributed content and hierarchical orchestration integrates and broadcasts it. In the most primitive sense, conscious experience retains the affective character of things going well or badly, while elaborated consciousness requires the three strands to remain dynamically coupled.

Directed transfer entropy.

A measure of the directed flow of information between brain regions, used to identify the orchestrating ensemble that integrates and broadcasts valenced content across conscious states. Emotion. Evolution's fast valenced compression of the intractable decision space of survival and, through the pleasure cycle, a continuous scheduler that allows competing biological needs to take turns. Emotion is evolutionarily primary within the Entangled Loop but operates as one strand of an architecture entangled with interference and orchestration. Eudaimonia. Flourishing or living well. Classically (Aristotle, Nicomachean ethics) the stable excellence of a whole life lived in accordance with virtue, assessable only in retrospect. On the Entangled Loop account, flourishing is an in-the-moment dynamical state in which affective scheduling, interference and orchestration remain flexibly coupled in the service of meaningful pleasure.

Far-from-equilibrium.

A thermodynamic regime in which a system maintains itself in an ordered configuration through continuous energy exchange with its environment rather than decaying toward equilibrium. All living systems are far-from-equilibrium; the brain is paradigmatically so.

Global neuronal workspace.

A framework originating with Baars (Baars, 1988) and given neuronal form by Dehaene, Kerszberg and Changeux (Dehaene et al., 1998), in which consciousness arises through ignition and broadcast of content across a distributed workspace. Hedonia. Pleasure understood in its immediate, in-the-moment character, comprising the full pleasure cycle of wanting, liking and satiety. In contemporary affective neuroscience the distinction from eudaimonia is less sharp than often supposed: Hedonia and eudaimonia are integrated through meaningful pleasure.

Hedonic hotspots. Anatomically precise small regions in the nucleus accumbens shell, ventral pallidum, parabrachial nucleus and certain cortical sites where pharmacological or optogenetic manipulation specifically amplifies liking without affecting wanting (Berridge and Kringelbach, 2015). Hedonic network. The full distributed system underlying hedonic evaluation, comprising subcortical hotspots and cortical nodes including the orbitofrontal cortex, anterior cingulate and insula. Distinct from the hotspots, which are specific loci within the broader network. Homeostasis and allostasis. Homeostasis is the maintenance of internal physiological variables within narrow ranges around fixed set-points; allostasis is the broader process of maintaining stability through change, adjusting set-points in anticipation of demand. Both are central to contemporary interoceptive and predictive processing accounts of affect. Ignition. In Global Workspace theory, the non-linear threshold transition at which local content gains global access across the workspace and becomes available for broadcast. Interoception. The sense of the physiological state of the body, including signals of hunger, temperature, cardiac activity and visceral tension. Central to somatic-marker and active-inference accounts of affect (Craig, 2015; Seth and Bayne, 2022). Meaningful pleasure. The integration of anticipatory wanting, consummatory liking and the higherorder sense of significance into a coordinated state; the neuroscientific formulation that the Entangled Loop's account of flourishing builds on (Kringelbach et al., 2024b).

Orchestrating regions.

The core integrative hub of the global neuronal workspace, identified empirically through directed transfer entropy analysis (Deco et al., 2021b; Kringelbach and Deco, 2024). At one level of coarse-graining they comprise the precuneus, nucleus accumbens, putamen, posterior cingulate cortex, hippocampus, amygdala and isthmus cingulate cortex. The size and composition of the ensemble vary with coarse-graining level whilst the functional role is stable.

Partial information decomposition.

The mathematical framework of Williams and Beer, extended by Rosas and colleagues, decomposing the information multiple sources carry about a target into unique, redundant and synergistic components. Perturbation (in whole-brain modelling). Systematically altering parameters of a fitted whole-brain model to observe the resulting dynamical consequences; the computational counterpart to interventional experiments in empirical neuroscience. Pleasure cycle. The continuous scheduler formed by a cycle of anticipatory wanting, consummatory liking, predictive satiety that organises motivational behaviour across mammalian species (Georgiadis and Kringelbach, 2012; Kringelbach and Berridge, 2009; Kringelbach et al., 2012). Wanting and liking are dissociable components rather than expressions of one reward quantity (Berridge and Robinson, 1998; Smith et al., 2011). Selective satiety changes the priority of a particular reward with motivational state (Kringelbach et al., 2003; Kringelbach et al., 2012), while satiation and satiety provide distinct temporal processes for ending a bout and suppressing renewed pursuit (Blundell and Burley, 1987). Quantum-like inference. The computational regime in which interference between coupled classical oscillators contributes to inference in ways that can depart from additive classical probability. Interference is the candidate physical mechanism, while inference is the computational function served when those interactions update and bind the world model. When the resulting probability structure exhibits contextuality, order effects or violations of the law of total probability, its mathematical form is quantum-like without requiring microscopic quantum processes (Deco et al., 2026b; Khrennikov, 2010; Scholes, 2024 Scholes, , 2026)). Its biological role remains a testable hypothesis.

Self-evidencing inference. The process by which a generative world model supports itself through coordinated inference producing evidence that confirms the model's predictions. Central to active inference and the Beautiful Loop. Spectral gaps. Separations within the ordered spectrum of the connectome operator that divide its eigenmodes into distinct collective bands. In the Entangled Loop, this spectral-gap structure constrains hierarchical organisation and interference on the reversible face of the dynamics, while also coupling these computational possibilities to the speed and thermodynamic cost of their directed traversal. Steering (in mechanistic interpretability). Direct intervention on an internal representation in a trained neural network to observe the causal effect on the model's output; establishes that the intervened representation is functionally active rather than merely correlated with behaviour. Synergy. Information carried by the collective behaviour of a system that is absent in any of its components considered separately. The informational signature of integrated rather than merely distributed processing.

Thermodynamics of Mind.

The framework in which the brain is treated as a system under the dual constraint of remaining far from equilibrium while computing at low energetic cost (Kringelbach et al., 2024a). In the Entangled Loop, this pressure favours valenced compression and scheduling, interference-based integration and hierarchical orchestration, while the irreversible cost of running these dynamics is carried by the directed face of the operator. Turbulence. The dynamical regime of the mammalian brain characterised by irreversible, far-fromequilibrium operation with long-range influence propagating through rare long-range cortical connections that violate the exponential distance rule. Type-identity theory. The philosophical thesis that mental states are identical with particular physical states. Contrasted with functionalism and dualism; the Entangled Loop commits to a typeidentity claim with respect to felt quality and affective architecture. Valence. The graded good-for-me or bad-for-me quantity through which emotion compresses complex situations into rapidly evaluable priorities. Valence supplies the content of the pleasurecycle scheduler rather than a single monolithic reward value. Wanting, liking and satiety. Dissociable components of the pleasure cycle, where wanting predicts the value of obtaining a reward, liking is the true hedonic read-out when it is obtained and drives learning throughout the cycle, and satiety predicts when enough has been obtained for the current pursuit to yield to another. Wanting and liking can be dissociated experimentally (Berridge and Robinson, 1998; Smith et al., 2011), while selective satiety provides the reward-specific mechanism by which current motivational state changes priority (Kringelbach et al., 2003; Kringelbach et al., 2012). The distinction between satiation and satiety supplies the different timescales of ending and renewed eligibility (Blundell and Burley, 1987).

Warlow-Berridge paradox.

A key experiment in rodents producing an extreme dissociation in which wanting drives pursuit despite an aversive outcome (Warlow et al., 2020). This is a strong challenge to monolithic reward-learning paradigms including temporal-difference learning. Whole-brain modelling. The methodology of fitting biophysically plausible dynamical models to empirical neuroimaging data from large participant samples, allowing systematic perturbation to reveal causal mechanisms. See Deco and Kringelbach (2025), Whole-brain modelling: Cartography of the dynamics of mind, for the comprehensive reference.

Zombie (philosophical).

A hypothetical being physically and functionally identical to a conscious organism but lacking phenomenal experience. The conceivability of zombies has been used as an argument against physicalist theories of consciousness (Chalmers, 1995; Kirk, 2019). The Entangled Loop argues the zombie argument loses force for hedonic machinery, whose function is the production of states with felt positive or negative character.
