What we found

We found 14 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Effect Sizes in t-tests and F-tests works

The Effect Size module checks if effect sizes are correctly reported in t-tests and F-tests.

The Effect Size check searches for regular expressions that match typical ways in which effect sizes are reported. This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1 RAs) are widely used for obesity and type 2 diabetes, supporting weight loss and glycaemic control through effects on appetite and ingestive behaviour. The psychobiological mechanisms underlying these effects remain unclear. We reviewed evidence for candidate mechanisms and analysed quantitative and qualitative self-reported changes in appetite-related experiences from 704 people using GLP-1 RAs. Participants reported multiple changes that appeared distinct for food versus alcohol, with reductions in food urges and cravings largely independent of changes in alcohol use. Enhanced awareness and utilisation of hunger and satiety signals were frequently described, suggesting potential changes in interoceptive processes. Changes in enjoyment of eating were largely independent of reductions in food cravings, urges to eat, and gastrointestinal side effects. Improved ability to eat in a goal-directed manner was also frequently reported. Results support the presence of multiple cognitive and behavioural processes contributing to reduced food intake and provide testable hypotheses to explore in experimental work designed to isolate effects of GLP-1 RAs on goal-directed control, interoceptive awareness, attention, memory, and executive function.

1. Introduc on

Glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists (RAs) have quickly become the leading therapeutic tool for managing type 2 diabetes and obesity through their suppressive effects on appetite and metabolic regulation (Moiz et al., 2025). Recent evidence suggests that GLP-1 RAs also have the potential to reduce problematic drug and alcohol use, slow neurogenerative disease progression, reduce inflammation and treat various cancers (Fang et al., 2025; Gao et al., 2026; Z. Zheng et al., 2024), highlighting the widespread biological effects of GLP-1 receptor signalling beyond appetite regulation and metabolic control.

Despite wide clinical uptake, the mechanisms underlying the effects of GLP-1 RAs on ea ng behaviour remain incompletely understood (S. E. Chang et al., 2026; Cheney et al., 2025; West et al., 2025). To complement preclinical and clinical studies of their effects, qualitative approaches are valuable for identifying patterns in subjective experience that can help distinguish between potential mechanistic accounts and inform specific, testable hypotheses (Kyzar & Denfield, 2023; Willig, 2019). Many patients taking GLP-1 RAs describe profound changes in their relationship with food and experience of eating and other consummatory behaviours, often reporting reduced cravings for highly palatable foods and alcohol, and a diminished preoccupation with eating ("food noise"). Such reports suggest that GLP-1 RAs may alter cognitive processes underlying food motivation and reward in ways that are not easily measurable in preclinical research. In the current study we therefore sought to characterise theoretically informed domains of consumers' experience and examine how they co-occur, to generate more specific, testable hypotheses about the processes through which GLP-1 RAs suppress food intake, promote weight loss, and affect other appetitive behaviours (e.g., alcohol use) in humans. We take a review-informed approach, first evaluating the candidate mechanisms that could underlie the effects of GLP-1 RAs on eating behaviour and the existing preclinical and clinical evidence for each. We then use qualitative and quantitative analyses of patients' subjective experiences to identify patterns that may inform and distinguish between mechanistic accounts.

1.1. GLP-1 RA therapy

Following the synthesis and subsequent approval of exenatide as a treatment for type 2 diabetes in 2005, a wave of research has developed other compounds to mimic the effects of endogenous GLP-1 (Darwish et al., 2025). Endogenous GLP-1 plays a multifaceted role in appetite regulation, integrating peripheral satiety signals with central homeostatic and hedonic mechanisms to influence food intake (see Box 1). The contribution of GLP-1 signalling to feeding behaviour has consequently been a longstanding focus of research, with studies in animal models spanning more than two decades (Alhadeff & Grill, 2014; Larsen et al., 2001; Reidelberger et al., 2011; Richard et al., 2015; Scott & Moran, 2007; Steinert et al., 2016; Williams et al., 2009). This work has demonstrated that peripherallyadministered GLP-1 RAs slow gastric emptying and prolong satiety (Näslund et al., 1998). In addition, because GLP-1 receptors are widely expressed throughout the central nervous system (CNS), GLP-1 RAs are thought to influence activity within hypothalamic circuits that regulate energy balance and feeding behaviour (Gabery et al., 2020; Kanoski et al., 2016; Martins et al., 2023) as well as mesolimbic reward circuitry, including the VTA and nucleus accumbens (NaC) (X.-F. Wang et al., 2015). This provides a mechanism through which physiological energy signals can influence the rewarding and motivational properties of food and other substances. Furthermore, the expression of GLP-1 receptors within the hippocampus, together with evidence linking GLP-1 signalling to learning and memory (Begg & Woods, 2013; Yaribeygi et al., 2021), suggests an additional pathway through which these pharmacotherapies may alter consummatory behaviour.

It should be noted that in humans, direct evidence that GLP-1 RAs are 49 simultaneously acting on all these neural pathways to reduce appetite, is currently lacking 50 (West et al., 2025). Despite the widespread presence of GLP-1 receptors throughout the 51 CNS, it is not clear whether CNS penetration is critical for the appetite suppressing effects of 52 GLP-1 RAs or whether these effects can be explained largely by peripheral mechanisms that 53 subsequently influence central circuits regulating different aspects of appetite (S. E. Chang 54 et al., 2026; West et al., 2025). This ambiguity has driven renewed interest in studying 55 patient experiences as a means of understanding how GLP-1 RAs suppress food intake and 56 promote weight loss. 57

Satiety refers to the cascade of post-prandial gastrointestinal processes that suppress eating between meals. Neural and endocrine influences on appetite are complex (Begg & Woods, 2013; Hopkins et al., 2022). Environment

Homeostatic signals of appetite interact with (and are easily overridden by) other factors such as availability of food, time of day, social contexts, memory of past eating experiences, stress and mood (Parent et al., 2022; Plassmann et al., 2022; Stevenson et al., 2024; H. Food choice and memory

Food is a potent reinforcer (Volkow et al., 2011; G.-J. 1.2. Proposed Mechanisms of Ac on of GLP-1 RA

GLP-1 RAs act on diverse aspects of appetite, which can be broadly separated into the urge to eat (i.e. hunger drives), food choice and decisions about what to eat, and the inhibition of eating (satiation and satiety: Gibbons et al., 2019). Below we briefly review evidence for mechanisms of reduced food intake in preclinical models and humans following GLP-1 RA therapy (see Figure 1).

1.2.1. Reduced hunger drive

A large literature in rodents has elucidated how both peripheral and central administration of GLP-1 RAs reduce hunger and food intake (for review, see: Barrera et al., 2011; Steinert et al., 2016). In rodents, a single peripheral injection of GLP-1 RA (Exendin-4) prolonged the interval between standard chow meals relative to saline (Williams et al., 2009), suggesting general suppression of hunger. Across 7 weeks of daily injections (liraglutide), obese mini-pigs were also reported to reduce frequency of meal initiation (Raun et al., 2007; but see : Cawthon et al., 2023). A study using optogenetics and calcium imaging showed that stimulation of a subpopulation of GLP-1 neurons in the dorsomedial hypothalamus promotes so-called 'pre-ingestive' satiation (Kim et al., 2024).

In humans, extended GLP-1 RA administration is consistently reported to suppress hunger between meals and reduce general urges to eat (Näslund et al., 1998; van Can et al., 2014; Blundell et al., 2017; Bettadapura et al., 2025). However, these effects are surprisingly small in controlled laboratory conditions (Rutigliani et al., 2026). Many factors likely contribute to the general effects of GLP-1 RA administration on human hunger and between-meal satiety such as the population studied (e.g., with or without diabetes), the dose, type and frequency of GLP-1 RA administration and the types of meals provided to participants in the lab (Aldawsari et al., 2023; Rutigliani et al., 2026; Steinert et al., 2016). An additional, often overlooked issue is that assessing appetite and eating behaviour in the lab is fraught with difficulty, with social desirability effects and unfamiliar contexts likely to influence self-report and consumption (Best et al., 2018; Stevenson et al., 2024). These effects could introduce noise to the data and possibly obscure meaningful differences between groups in GLP-1 RA vs. placebo comparisons of general appetite suppression.

1.2.2. Improved sa a on and interocep ve signalling

One of the most reliable effects of peripheral GLP-1 RA administration in animals (both acute and chronic) is reduced meal size (Cawthon et al., 2023; Scott & Moran, 2007; Williams et al., 2009). Similarly in humans, meal duration was reported to be shorter following 4-week treatment of GLP-1 RA (Horowitz et al., 2012). Patients surveyed before and after starting GLP-1 RAs reported being significantly less likely to eat past the point of being full and less likely to eat in response to emotional or situational cues (e.g., boredom, stress or availability of food: Cheney et al., 2025). This data could suggest that GLP-1 RAs improve interoception -the interpretation of the body's internal physical and emotional states. This could be occurring via enhanced signalling of internal hunger, fullness and emotional states, and/or improved sensitivity to, or accuracy of these signals. This is not something that can be asked of animal subjects and has not yet been investigated as a potential mechanism in humans. Nonetheless, gastrointestinal interoception is increasingly recognised as playing an important role in the development and maintenance of obesity (L. Chang et al., 2025).

1.2.2.1. Dampening of reward circuitry -food cravings and beyond

Unlike general hunger and urges to eat, food cravings are an intense desire or urge to eat a particular food that are often triggered by conditioned food cues such as advertisements (Havermans, 2013). Cravings are correlated with actual food intake, particularly eating in the absence of homeostatic hunger, and therefore weight gain (Boswell & Kober, 2016). Typically, food cravings are for palatable, energy-dense foods (Gilhooly et al., 2007; Massicotte et al., 2019) indicating that they are closely linked to the motivational and rewarding value of food rather than homeostatic energy requirements.

Cravings for alcohol and other drugs are also a core feature of addiction and relapse (Sinha, 2013; Stohs et al., 2019; Vafaie & Kober, 2022).

Although measuring cravings in animals is difficult, studies directly stimulating GLP-1 receptors in the VTA and NaC show shifts in food preference and reduced willingness to work for highly palatable foods (Alhadeff et al., 2012; Dickson et al., 2012; Richard et al., 2015; but see : Cawthon et al., 2023; S. E. Chang et al., 2026). For example, Richard et al., (2015) reported that Exendin-4 injected directly into the NaC suppressed consumption of palatable foods (and chow, if only chow was offered), reduced lever presses for sugar pellets in a progressive ratio paradigm and reduced preference for a chamber previously paired with palatable food in a conditioned place preference test. Similar reductions in conditioned place preference for alcohol and nicotine following central administration of GLP-1 RA have also been reported, suggestive of a mechanism that is non-specific to food and appetite regulation (Egecioglu et al., 2013; Shirazi et al., 2013). We note however that acute increases in preference and motivation to work for palatable foods (alongside overall reduction in food intake) have been reported by two recent animal studies that used a chronic systemic GLP-1 RA treatment regimen to better mimic therapeutic use in humans (Cawthon et al., 2023; S. E. Chang et al., 2026). This highlights the complexity of the effects of GLP-1 RAs on food-related motivation and reward.

Patients taking GLP-1 RAs often report reductions in cravings for specific foods (Blundell et al., 2017; Cheney et al., 2025; Gibbons et al., 2021) and for other substances such as alcohol or tobacco (Budzyński et al., 2024; Hendershot et al., 2025). It is generally assumed that GLP-1 RAs act on general reward circuitry to reduce cravings for (and consumption of) food, drugs and alcohol (Badulescu et al., 2024; Hayes & Schmidt, 2016; Volkow et al., 2026). Evidence for this comes primarily from neuroimaging studies showing GLP-1 RA-associated decreases in reward-related neural activation when viewing images of food (Bae et al., 2019; van Bloemendaal et al., 2014) or alcohol (Klausen et al., 2022).

However, it should be noted that sample sizes are often small, hypothesis-driven region-ofinterest approaches (rather than whole-brain analyses) are common, and suitable control conditions (i.e. other reward cues from another category) are often lacking (Dang et al., 2026). There is a dearth of data from human behavioural studies examining changes in the reward value of food or drugs following GLP-1 RA administration. One study used green tea and fibre supplements to increase circulating GLP-1 levels and found that, relative to placebo, supplementation reduced attentional bias for alcohol images (relative to soft drink images) but had no effect on reported cravings for alcohol (Bremmer et al., 2025). Further research in this area is needed to provide support for the hypothesis that general dampening of reward-related circuitry is the key mechanism driving shifts in food preferences and reducing cravings for food, alcohol and other rewards. An alternative, but not mutually-exclusive, hypothesis is that the value of food and alcohol is reduced due to dampening of taste receptors following GLP-1 RA administration (Khan & Doty, 2025) or due to post-ingestive gastrointestinal effects (e.g., nausea, diarrhoea). Adverse gastrointestinal side effects are commonly reported by patients (Bettge et al., 2017) and exacerbated by foods high in fat and alcohol (Gentinetta et al., 2024). Repeated pairings of specific foods or alcohol with unpleasant post-ingestive consequences could therefore produce conditioned taste aversions, reducing both the anticipated reward value of these substances and the desire to consume them in the future. Pre-clinical studies suggest that GLP-1 RA can lead to robust taste aversions, depending on the type and route of administration (Decarie-Spain & Kanoski, 2021; Kanoski et al., 2012). 1.2.3. Reduced Cogni ve Preoccupa on

Another effect of GLP-1 RAs that cannot be studied in pre-clinical work is the substantial reduction in "food noise" that began to make news headlines following the widespread clinical use of GLP-1 RAs (Hayashi et al., 2023). This cognitive preoccupation with food had been previously studied within frameworks of dietary restraint, food deprivation and eating disorders (N. Jones & Rogers, 2003; Lydecker et al., 2022; Shaw & Tiggemann, 2004). However, emerging reports from individuals using GLP-1 medications demonstrate that food-related cognitive preoccupation is more common than previously appreciated in people with obesity, regardless of restrictive dietary practices. This is a phenomenon that has received relatively limited direct empirical attention (Dhurandhar et al., 2025; Naveed et al., 2025).

It is not clear whether reduced cognitive preoccupation with food following GLP-1 RA administration is a direct effect of GLP-1 RAs or simply a cognitive by-product of other candidate mechanisms of action. For example, it has been recently proposed that food noise reflects food cue reactivity and associated neural responses to food-related stimuli (Hayashi et al., 2023; Janik et al., 2026), implying that reductions in the reward value of food are the primary driver of reduced food-related cognitive preoccupation. Although Janik et al. (2026) go so far as to suggest that hypothalamic AgRP/NPY neurons are "the main generators of food noise" (p.6), this hypothesis remains largely speculative in the absence of direct evidence linking these neural systems to self-reported cognitive preoccupation with food.

One case-study recording neuronal activity directly in the human NaC reported that deltatheta band power was correlated with the onset of severe food preoccupation (Choi et al., 2025). Although this study should be interpreted with caution given the absence of an experimental design, it aligns with the idea that food noise is linked to activity in mesolimbic reward circuitry.

An alternative complementary explanation is that GLP-1 RAs reduce cognitive preoccupation through direct effects on cognitive circuits underlying attention, executive, or memory processes, rather than solely through reductions in food reward. Consistent with this possibility, emerging evidence suggests that GLP-1 RAs may improve several aspects of cognitive functioning, including learning, memory and executive function (Hanssen et al., 2023; Monney et al., 2023; Yaribeygi et al., 2021). Under this account, reductions in food noise may reflect broader changes in cognitive processing, rather than simply a reduction in the rewarding properties of food. One candidate is hippocampal-dependent episodic memory, which is argued to play a key role in hunger and appetite regulation (Higgs, 2002; Higgs & Spetter, 2018; Parent et al., 2022; Stevenson et al., 2024). GLP-1 RAs have been shown to improve hippocampal-dependent spatial learning and memory in rodents (During et al., 2003; Seo et al., 2023), although strong clinical evidence in humans is currently lacking.

1.2.4. Improved Goal-Directed Control

As noted previously, GLP-1 RAs may directly modulate prefrontal cognitive circuits and improve aspects of executive functioning (Hanssen et al., 2023; Monney et al., 2023; Yaribeygi et al., 2021). This raises the possibility that GLP-1 RAs are reducing intake of unhealthy food, alcohol and other substances through strengthening of top-down control processes, rather than simply bottom-up reductions in their associated reward value. In line with this, one study demonstrated that after 12-days of peripheral GLP1-RA administration, rats showed enhanced inhibition of responding in the presence of an inhibitory cue, but no change in appetitive behaviour in the presence of a cue signalling food reward (S. Jones et al., 2019). Generally, however, the effect of GLP-1 RAs on formal tests of goal-directed control and impulsive decision making has not yet been examined (in either animals or humans). Some support for the idea that GLP-1 RAs may improve learning processes underlying goal-directed behaviour and control comes from a study showing that GLP-1 RA administration improved adaptive updating of choice behaviour during a probabilistic learning task in individuals with low insulin sensitivity (Hanssen et al., 2023). Further highlighting the importance of cognitive control processes in shaping GLP-1 RA treatment response, two studies have reported greater weight loss in adults reporting higher levels of pre-treatment eating restraint (de Boer et al., 2016; van Ruiten et al., 2022).

1.3. The Current Study

Above we have outlined candidate mechanisms by which GLP-1 RAs reduce consumption of food and other substances in humans (see summary in Figure 1). However, cognitive, reward-based and interoceptive accounts have yet to be effectively integrated into the still-fragmented literature on GLP-1 RAs. It is also unclear whether the effects of GLP-1 RAs arise through different mechanisms across patients, and how frequently distinct patterns of effects occur. In this regard, user reports are a particularly valuable source of information to refine theoretical models of the mechanistic effects of GLP-1 RAs on consummatory behaviour. The current study therefore presents a secondary analysis of qualitative data collected as part of a broader survey investigating patients' motivations for initiating and discontinuing GLP-1 RA therapy (Griffiths et al., 2026). We analysed patient reports describing changes in eating urges, cravings, cognitive preoccupation, alcohol use, and related experiences following GLP-1 RA therapy. We also conducted a structured thematic analysis to identify mechanistically relevant patterns in these reports and analyse the co-occurrence of themes.

Our analyses were guided by several questions emerging from the existing experimental literature, including: 1) Are reductions in food and alcohol consumption reported by the same participants, and do they appear to be associated with similar changes in subjective experience? This addresses whether reductions across different consummatory behaviours might reflect a common underlying mechanism (i.e. general reductions in appetitive reward). 2) Do changes in appetite, food interest, craving, cognitive preoccupation, and enjoyment tend to cluster together within individuals, or do participants show distinct patterns of change across these domains? This begins to address whether GLP-1 RAs produce a common change in eating-related motivation or affect different aspects of eating through partially distinct mechanisms. 3) Is reduced enjoyment from eating particularly prevalent in people reporting more gastrointestinal side effects? This assesses whether reduced food reward might be secondary to aversive post-ingestion experiences. 4) Does reduced enjoyment from eating occur particularly among those reporting reduced urges to eat? This examines whether changes in the motivational urge to eat and the hedonic enjoyment of food tend to co-occur following GLP-1 RA treatment, or whether they change independently. 5) Does reduced cognitive preoccupation with food co-occur with reduced urges to eat and/or reduced cravings? This assesses whether reduced food-related thoughts necessarily reflect a broader reduction in the rewarding value of food, or whether these experiences can occur independently. 6) Do reports suggest greater goal-directed control over eating in the absence of reductions in enjoyment of food or urges to eat? If participants describe changes in their ability to regulate eating despite continuing to experience food as enjoyable and continuing to experience urges to eat, this could be indicative of changes in top-down behavioural control that are separable from changes in food reward.

2. Methods

>Methods

2.1. Par cipants

Participants (n=829) completed an online survey (administered via REDCap) asking about experiences with GLP-1 RA medications. The study was approved by the Sydney Local Health District Human Research Ethics Committee (2024/ETH00676). Participants were recruited internationally through social media, including GLP-1RA-focused Reddit communities and paid Facebook advertisements. No reimbursement was provided to participants. Eligible participants were aged 18 years or older and reported current or previous use of any GLP-1RA medication. The present analyses excluded 124 participants who reported taking more than one type of GLP-1 RA medication. These participants were excluded because many responded to questions about changes in appetite relative to their previous medication rather than relative to pre-treatment. In addition, one participant did not respond to any questions regarding changes in appetite and was therefore excluded from all analyses. After excluding these 125 participants, 704 participants remained for analysis.

2.2. Survey

Details of survey development and methodology have been reported elsewhere (Griffiths et al., 2026). Briefly, the survey collected demographic information, medicationrelated experiences, and perceived changes in eating behaviour, substance use, lifestyle, and mental health following GLP-1RA treatment. On average the survey took 23.9 minutes to complete (SD = 101.2 minutes) and included numerous open-text responses. The current analysis focused on responses to a subset of questions asking about changes in food and alcohol use behaviour. We focused on questions targeted at aspects of appetite for food and alcohol, gastrointestinal side effects to capture aversive experiences that may drive food-related decisions, and joy from eating to assess whether GLP1-RA treatment altered the hedonic aspects of food consumption. Specifically, the following variables were assessed:

2.2.1. Appe te-Related Change Variables

Participants were asked to select from the options 'reduced', 'no change', 'increased', or 'unsure' in response to four questions about changes in desire for specific foods (e.g., sweet or fatty foods), preoccupation with food, urges to eat and general interest in food after commencing GLP-1 RA treatment. Participants were also asked whether they drank alcohol. Those who answered yes, were then asked to indicate, using the same response options, whether they had experienced changes in their alcohol use following GLP-1 RA treatment.

2.2.2. Gastrointes nal side effects

Participants were asked to indicate whether they experienced any unpleasant side effects while taking GLP-1 RA medication. Those who indicated that they experienced nausea, acid reflux, diarrhoea or constipation were categorised as having experienced gastrointestinal side effects.

2.2.3. Joy from ea ng

Participants were asked to indicate the joy they experienced from eating, on a Likert scale ranging from 1-10.

2.2.4. Open ended ques ons

Participants endorsing a decrease in desire for specific foods, preoccupation with food, urges to eat, general interest in food, or alcohol use were prompted to "describe the changes in [question]" via open-ended text responses.

2.2.5. Ea ng Disorders Examina on Ques onnaire (EDE-Q)

The restraint subscale of the EDE-Q was used in the current study (Fairburn & Beglin, 1994). The EDE-Q restraint subscale assesses the extent to which an individual has attempted to restrict their food intake over the previous 28 days. It consists of five items rated on a 7-point scale, with higher scores indicating greater dietary restraint. The mean of the five items is calculated to produce the restraint score, ranging from 0 to 6. Good internal consistency has been previously reported for the restraint subscale (Luce & Crowther, 1999; Peterson et al., 2007)

2.3. Data Pre-processing and Analysis

Several features of the study may have reduced the likelihood of bot participation, including the considerable length of the survey, the absence of financial reimbursement, and the inclusion of numerous open-text response fields requiring substantive written answers. Nonetheless, following data collection, responses were screened for indicators of automated or non-human participation, including obviously nonsensical or irrelevant opentext responses and other readily apparent signs of bot activity. No responses were identified as clearly indicative of bot participation, and no data were excluded on this basis.

For the appetite-related change variables we recoded responses of "decrease" as -1, "no change" as 0 and "increase" as +1 to enable correlations assessing associations between appetite-related change variables for food and alcohol across participants. Participants indicating "unsure" were excluded from correlational analyses. Due to the ordinal nature and skewed distribution of the response categories, Spearman's rank-order correlations were used.

We then used independent samples t-tests and Chi-squared tests to examine whether those who did and did not experience gastrointestinal side effects differed in ratings of joy experienced from eating, or likelihood of endorsing an increase, decrease or no change in appetite-related change variables.

We then used joy from eating as a dependent variable in a series of independent samples t-tests comparing those who reported reduced vs. no change in: 1) desire for specific foods; 2) urges to eat; 3) preoccupation with food; and 4) general interest in food (the number of participants who endorsed increasing on these metrics was deemed too small for analysis).

Exploratory comparisons between those assigned female or male sex at birth were conducted for appetite-related change variables, experiences of gastrointestinal side-effects and joy experienced from eating (See Supplemental Tables S1 and S4).

For the open-text responses, an inductive coding approach was used to identify themes potentially relevant to underlying mechanisms of change. The first author and a second member of the research team independently reviewed the raw responses and generated an initial set of candidate themes for the three questions pertaining to reduced desire for specific foods, reduced general appetite for food (including urges to eat and cognitive preoccupation), and reduced alcohol use. Together the emergent themes were agreed upon, before all responses were independently coded. Responses that were ambiguous mechanistically (e.g., pertaining to "less binges", "reduced obsession") or merely describing shifts in behaviour ("drinking less alcohol", "reduced portions", "less emotional eating") were ignored. Each participant response could be assigned multiple codes. Interrater reliability was assessed using Cohen's kappa for each theme. Excellent agreement between coders on the presence or absence of each thematic code was observed, with kappa values ranging from .771 -.937 for desire for specific foods, .815 -.886 for reduced general appetite for food and .832 -.944 for alcohol use.

Where possible, follow-up analyses examined whether different themes tended to co-occur within participants. For example, we explored whether participants who reported reduced cognitive preoccupation with food also tended to report reduced cravings.

However, this approach was limited by the fact that some themes were directly prompted by specific survey questions and were therefore explicitly endorsed by up to 80% of participants, resulting in differential base rates of endorsement across themes. The exception to this was the alcohol-related item which simply asked participants to describe the "changes in their drinking habits", without prompting specific mechanisms of change.

For this item, Chi-squared tests of independence and odds ratios were used to examine cooccurrence between mechanistic themes emerging from participants' responses.

Exploratory comparisons of qualitative theme endorsement by sex assigned at birth were conducted using Fisher's exact tests (See Supplemental Tables S2-S4).

Given the exploratory and hypothesis-generating nature of the study and the number of statistical tests conducted, the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure (Benjamini & Hochberg, 1995) was used to control the false discovery rate (BH-FDR). Tests were grouped into families according to the conceptual structure of the analyses and the distinct research questions they addressed, rather than being specified a priori.

2.4. Researcher reflexivity

The qualita ve analysis was informed by the authors' exis ng knowledge of the literature on GLP-1 receptor agonists and ea ng behaviour, including specific hypotheses and ques ons regarding poten al mechanisms of ac on. Accordingly, the analysis was not conducted from a purely induc ve or theore cally neutral posi on. The domains examined were informed by ques ons arising from the exis ng experimental literature, although coding was based on par cipants' reported experiences rather than on the assump on that par cular mechanisms were present. 4. Discussion

Despite increasing use of GLP-1 RAs to manage obesity and type 2 diabetes, an integrated understanding of their effects on cognitive control, interoception and foodrelated reward remains unclear. In this study, we used survey data to generate novel hypotheses regarding the mechanisms through which GLP-1 RAs suppress food intake, promote weight loss, and influence other appetitive behaviours. To this end, we integrated evidence from the existing literature with qualitative and quantitative analyses of patients' subjective experiences to evaluate patterns that may help identify candidate mechanisms and clarify areas of overlap between them. Our key findings are outlined below.

4.1. Mul ple Parallel Mechanisms of Ac on

We asked first whether reductions in food and alcohol consumption were reported by the same participants, and if so, whether they appeared to be associated with similar changes in subjective experience. Most participants indicated reductions in general urges to eat, cravings for specific foods and cognitive preoccupation following GLP-1 RA therapy.

Although these variables were coded as simple directional change scores (-1, 0, +1) and, conceptually, seem closely related, the modest correlations between domains (r = .145-.333) suggest that they were only partially overlapping experiences, with some individuals reporting changes in one domain but not others. Likewise, changes in alcohol use behaviour (reduced, no change or increased) was not correlated meaningfully with changes in general urges to eat, cravings for specific foods or cognitive preoccupation with food. Exploratory comparisons between males and females (sex assigned at birth) suggested minimal sex differences in reported experiences of GLP1-RA therapy. Overall, these data suggest that, in humans, GLP-1 RAs are unlikely to be simply dampening general reward-circuitry leading to reductions in consummatory behaviours (Badulescu et al., 2024; Hayes & Schmidt, 2016; Volkow et al., 2026). Instead, they point to multiple parallel mechanisms of action that are distinct for alcohol versus food.

We were interested in examining whether reduced enjoyment from eating would cooccur with reduced urges to eat. Two recent animal studies using chronic peripheral GLP-1 RA administration to better mimic therapeutic use in humans reported that changes in the incentive value of food and overall food intake do not always operate in parallel (Cawthon et al., 2023; S. E. Chang et al., 2026). Although GLP-1 RA-treated animals showed preserved (or even enhanced) responding for palatable food rewards, they nevertheless consumed less food and lost more weight than vehicle-treated controls, suggesting that reduced food intake and metabolic effects can occur independently of reductions in food reward. Similarly in our data, those with reduced eating urges (and reduced preoccupation with food) did not report significantly less joy of eating compared to those reporting "no change" on these appetite-related metrics. Although speculative, these findings are consistent with the possibility that reductions in food-related thoughts and urges can occur in the absence of substantial changes in the enjoyment of eating, suggesting partially dissociable effects on motivational and hedonic aspects of food reward (Cawthon et al., 2023; S. E. Chang et al., 2026).

4.2. Improved interocep on following GLP-1 RA therapy

One of the most significant findings from the thematic analysis were clear descriptions of improved interoceptive awareness following GLP-1 RA therapy, which has to date received little empirical focus. Specifically, participants frequently reported having a better understanding of what hunger "actually felt like" (e.g., relative to boredom, anxiety or emotional eating). They also reported a shift towards craving nutritious foods, feeling content after one or two bites and not feeling the need to finish meals unnecessarily. This data could suggest that internal homeostatic cues of hunger and fullness are more robust following GLP-1 RA therapy and that these internal signals are better able to override external or situational cues. While gastrointestinal interoception is increasingly recognised as playing a role in the development and maintenance of obesity (L. Chang et al., 2025), our data suggest that the potential effects of GLP-1 RAs on interoceptive processes warrant greater empirical attention. Future research could move beyond self-report measures to examine whether GLP-1 RA therapy alters the accuracy, sensitivity, or behavioural utilisation of hunger and satiety signals, and whether any such effects are specific to gastrointestinal sensations or generalise to broader domains of bodily awareness. Unsurprisingly, given that alcohol consumption is not homeostatically regulated in the same way as food, having "enough" alcohol was reported by participants to be less about physiological need and more about reward (or aversion), social norms and intoxication thresholds. This again highlights that there are at least partially distinct mechanisms of action of GLP-1 RA on reducing food intake vs. alcohol intake. The findings also challenge the view that the therapeutic effects of GLP-1 RAs can be understood primarily in terms of reduced reward processing. While reward-related changes may contribute, participants' descriptions of altered perceptions of hunger, satiety and emotional states suggest that effects on homeostatic and interoceptive processes may be equally important for understanding reductions in food intake.

4.3. Improved goal-directed control: direct effect or side effect?

Another theme emerging spontaneously from qualitative data for both food and alcohol consumption was an increased capacity to exert goal-directed control over behaviour. Some participants described being better able to eat for nutritional rather than hedonic reasons and making more deliberate and informed choices regarding both food and alcohol intake. One interpretation is that GLP-1 RA therapy directly enhances cognitive control processes relevant to self-regulation. However, an alternative explanation is that these experiences are secondary to reductions in appetite, craving, or food-related preoccupation, which may reduce the frequency or intensity of situations requiring selfcontrol. Our analysis had limited capacity to disentangle increased control from reduced urges, although we note that in the alcohol data, no participant endorsed both themes simultaneously. Identifying whether GLP-1 RA therapy enhances cognitive control or reduces the need to exert cognitive control is an important future direction. The limited existing literature provides some suggestion that GLP-1 RAs may influence processes relevant to goal-directed behaviour. For example, GLP-1 RA administration has been associated with enhanced inhibitory responding in rodents, improved adaptive learning in humans, and greater weight loss among individuals with higher levels of pre-treatment eating restraint (S. Jones et al., 2019; Hanssen et al., 2023; de Boer et al., 2016; van Ruiten et al., 2022). While numerous studies report using the "control of eating questionnaire" in research on GLP-1 RAs (e.g., Blundell et al., 2017; Gibbons et al., 2021; Wharton et al., 2023) this questionnaire measures self-reported craving rather than control per se. There is therefore an urgent need for both animal and human research investigating whether GLP-1 RAs influence goal-directed control and reduce habitual and impulsive responding for food (and other) rewards, using established behavioural methods.

One final consideration is that changes in diet composition following GLP-1RA administration may themselves contribute to changes in cognitive functioning. In particular, GLP-1RA use is frequently associated with reductions in consumption of energy-dense foods, including foods high in sugar and fat. Evidence from animal models suggests that diets high in sugar and/or fat can adversely affect cognitive functioning, whereas reducing exposure to these foods can improve cognitive performance (Kendig et al., 2021 (Kendig et al., , 2023;; Rehn et al., 2026). Thus, some of the cognitive changes reported following GLP-1RA use may reflect, at least in part, changes in dietary composition rather than direct effects of GLP-1RAs on cognition. Disentangling these possibilities will be an important avenue for future research, particularly given that changes in appetite, food choice, dietary composition, and cognitive control are likely to occur concurrently following GLP-1RA treatment.

4.4. Cogni ve Preoccupa on is dis nct from Cravings

The concept of "food noise" has emerged relatively recently, and speculation remains as to what exactly this cognitive preoccupation with food represents. A prominent explanation for food noise is that it reflects reward-related responses to food cues, with reductions in the incentive value of food proposed as the primary mechanism through which GLP-1 RAs reduce food-related cognitive preoccupation (Hayashi et al., 2023; Janik et al., 2026). This view has gained traction despite relatively limited direct evidence linking reward-related neural activity to the subjective experience of food noise. Our correlational data shows that while reduced cognitive preoccupation with food was reported as a common experience following GLP-1 RA therapy, it was only modestly associated with reductions in urges to eat and food cravings. This indicates that these phenomena are not simply interchangeable manifestations of reduced food reward, but instead reflect partially distinct processes that warrant separate consideration in mechanistic accounts of GLP-1 RA action. Support for this interpretation comes from the thematic analysis of the alcohol-use data. When asked to describe reductions in alcohol consumption, participants who spontaneously reported decreases in alcohol-related cognitive preoccupation were notably less likely to describe reductions in alcohol craving, and vice versa, confirmed through a significant inverse association between reporting these experiences. This pattern suggests that cognitive preoccupation and craving may represent dissociable aspects of appetitive experience rather than interchangeable manifestations of a single reward-related process, a hypothesis which should be examined using more robust experimental measures.

As discussed in the Introduction, an alternative explanation is that GLP-1 RA therapies reduce cognitive preoccupation with food via direct effects on cognitive circuits underlying attention, executive, or memory processes (Hanssen et al., 2023; Monney et al., 2023; Yaribeygi et al., 2021). More broadly, the relationship between eating behaviour and prefrontal cognitive systems is underscored by pharmacological evidence that targeting attentional and executive circuits can alter appetite and binge eating (Griffiths et al., 2024; Heal & Smith, 2022). For example, Lisdexamfetamine (LDX), originally developed for attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder, has since been shown to reduce binge eating behaviour and is now approved for the treatment of binge eating disorder in several countries (Heal & Smith, 2022). Its neural effects appear to be distributed across multiple large-scale networks, including but not limited to reward-related circuitry, with changes in functional connectivity across prefrontal, limbic, and subcortical systems associated with clinical improvement in binge eating symptomology (Griffiths et al., 2024). It is therefore premature to exclude the possibility that GLP-1 RAs directly modulate cognitive pathways, leading to reduced cognitive preoccupation with food and food intake (and possibly increases in executive functioning). Future research should assess the effects of GLP-1 RA on diverse aspects of food cognition including, for example, food memory (Higgs, 2002; Higgs & Spetter, 2018), the cognitive representation or salience of food-related information (Chae et al., 2026; Lim & Kim, 2026) and executive functions more broadly.

4.5. Condi oned Taste Aversions

As expected, based on previous reports (Bettge et al., 2017; Gentinetta et al., 2024), reports of gastrointestinal side effects were common. We were interested to examine whether there was any evidence of conditioned taste aversions driving reductions in food and alcohol intake, whereby repeated pairings of specific foods or alcohol with unpleasant post-ingestive consequences could reduce both the anticipated reward value of these substances and the desire to consume them in the future. However, we found no evidence that those reporting gastrointestinal side effects experienced reduced enjoyment/joy from eating or were more likely to endorse reduced alcohol use relative to those who reported no gastrointestinal side effects. The comparable levels of eating enjoyment reported across those with and without gastrointestinal side effects are therefore consistent with the notion that GLP-1 RAs do not primarily reduce food intake through nausea or other gastrointestinal side effects, as has sometimes been speculated (Douros et al., 2025). Our data aligns with a previous study reporting that GLP-1 RA-induced weight loss occurred independently of gastrointestinal adverse events (Wharton et al., 2022). In our thematic analysis, however, we identified that those who experienced gastrointestinal side effects were significantly more likely to describe potential over-suppression effects or unintended effects of the GLP-1 RA medication such as regularly forgetting to eat or having to force oneself to eat. This is concerning from a nutritional perspective and suggests that gastrointestinal side effects could be a useful indicator for monitoring patients who might be at risk of inadequate nutrition.

4.6. Limita ons

Our study had a number of limitations. Although the lengthy, unreimbursed survey may have discouraged bot participation, and responses were screened for obvious signs of automated activity, undetected bot participation cannot be ruled out. Accordingly, the findings should be considered exploratory and replicated using in-person data collection.

The study relied on retrospective self-report data, which means we cannot draw firm conclusions or casual influences regarding the mechanisms underlying reported changes in eating behaviour. Although participants described changes following GLP-1 RA initiation, we cannot determine whether these experiences were caused directly by the medication or arose indirectly through associated changes in eating behaviour, weight loss, health status, or other psychological factors. Unfortunately, the wording of some survey questions likely influenced participants' responses. In particular, prompts relating to reductions in food intake explicitly referenced cognitive preoccupation, urges to eat, cravings and general interest in food, limiting our ability to examine the independence of these constructs in the food-related data. Finally, we could have asked more details about dosage and frequency of side effects, to be able to conduct more detailed analyses with these factors.

4.7. Conclusions

Qualitative findings from patient self-report identified several significant patterns with implications for future experimental research. Collectively, the findings suggest that current models emphasising reduced food reward as the primary mechanism of GLP-1 RA action may be incomplete. Instead, our data generate several testable hypotheses (as outlined in Table 8): that GLP-1 RAs may 1) enhance sensitivity to hunger and satiety signals and improve interoceptive awareness more broadly; 2) improve goal-directed control over consummatory behaviours; 3) reduce food-related cognitive preoccupation through effects on broader cognitive systems involved in attention, memory, and executive function; and 4) influence consummatory behaviour through multiple partially independent pathways rather than a single common mechanism. The findings also suggest that reductions in alcohol consumption and other appetitive behaviours may not be explained solely by reduced reward valuation, raising the possibility that cognitive and self-regulatory processes contribute to these effects.
