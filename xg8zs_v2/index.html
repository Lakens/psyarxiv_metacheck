Power Analysis Check

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Abstract

When shopping online consumers are frequently confronted with fake reviews. The consequences for the consumers are purchasing flawed, low-quality or even dangerous products. Therefore, to develop effective countermeasures, we need to understand how consumers evaluate the veracity of reviews when making product choices. Thus far, attempts to develop theories about how consumers evaluate review veracity have been based on laboratory or retrospective studies. Therefore, we used thinking-aloud methodology with interactive researcher prompting to more directly observe consumer decision-making regarding the evaluation of consumer reviews. We observed that participants use consumer reviews as a source of information about products. We identified three interrelated judgements in participants' evaluation of online reviews: review relevance, reviewer credibility, and perceived review veracity. During each judgement participants alternated between heuristic and more systematic processing of deception cues, such as wording, depending on the level of suspicion they trigger. If an evaluation indicated that a review is irrelevant, unbelievable, or untrue, it was not used in the purchase decision process. We integrated our findings from the directly observed decision-making process with existing theory to create an overarching model of human perception of review veracity within the context of an authentic shopping simulation.

1. Introduction

The increase in online shopping has changed both what goods consumers buy and how they decide which products to buy. In particular, online reviews are integral to how consumers make informed purchase decisions about material (purchases with the intention to acquire a possession) and experiential purchases (purchases with the intention to acquire a life experience) (Dai et al., 2020; Gu et al., 2021). However, research found that for experiential purchases consumer reviews had less influence on the purchase decision because consumers perceive reviews to be less reflective of the experiences' objective quality than for material goods, which is why in this study we focus on reviews about material products (Dai et al., 2020). Research indicated that the usefulness and trustworthiness of online consumer reviews (OCRs) is impeded by criminals creating fake reviews (FRs) to distort the picture of product quality (He et al., 2022; Salminen et al., 2022). These FRs are often bought by sellers on Amazon and other websites via social media networks such as Facebook, to quickly increase the average rating of their products and so increase their sales (He et al., 2021 (He et al., , 2022)). As the majority of FRs are written for lower quality products (He et al., 2022), the impact on consumers can be considerable, particularly in the case of products pertaining to health or safety.

Despite increasing experience with OCRs, consumers' detection of FRs remains around chance level (Salminen et al., 2022). Therefore, as a protective measure, larger platforms such as Amazon, use automated fake review detection to delete FRs. However, research shows that automated systems are only around 90% accurate at detecting FRs when used on data sets consisting of FRs written by study participants (Plotkina et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2016). Additionally, Mukherjee et al. (2013) found when authentic FRs, crawled from platforms, were analysed by these automated systems, the detection rate was only about 80%.

Since then, there has been negligible improvement in the accuracy rates because fraudulent PrePrint reviewers are continuously modifying their methods to evade detection. Consequently, automated FR detection as a countermeasure must undergo constant refinement; as such, it is perpetually one step behind (Paul & Nikolaev, 2021; Salminen et al., 2022). Additionally, not all platforms use automated fake review detection, making alternative methods to protect consumers essential (Paul & Nikolaev, 2021).

One of these alternatives is to enhance consumers' capabilities in detecting FRs by providing training. Costa Filho et al. (2023) showed that providing participants with persuasive knowledge concerning detection cues, can improve the detection rate of participants in controlled laboratory settings when the main task was to identify FRs.

However, this does not align well with how veracity evaluations are performed outside of the laboratory where they are embedded within a wider purchasing decision (Walther et al., 2025). Additionally, there are still shortcomings in understanding how people make decisions about the veracity of OCRs, preventing interventions to reach their full effectiveness (Walther et al., 2023). Most studies have either used existing frameworks and adopted a deductive method to confirm their hypotheses in laboratory settings or they used inductive retrospective methods to gather insights into the decision-making process (Walther et al., 2023). Both deductive and retrospective approaches are useful in testing trainings. However, understanding the processes undergone by consumers to detect FR's is essential to instil longterm behavioural changes. While research indicates which factors consumers use to evaluate online reviews, we understand less about how these factors are integrated into judgements, how different judgements relate to one another, and under which circumstances consumers actually evaluate the veracity of review content (Walther et al., 2023). Therefore, there remains a need to investigate how consumers perceive and evaluate review veracity and how the reviews inform the decision process in the shopping experience, to ensure the development of effective training with long term intervention benefits outside of the PrePrint laboratory (Brown, 2002; Naderbagi et al., 2024). To gather this information, we use a thinking-aloud method with interviewer prompts to analyse the decision-making processes of consumers in a simulation of a shopping process.

1.1. Theoretical Background

There are three different types of FRs (Jindal & Liu, 2008). First, untruthful opinions portray false opinions of products. Second, reviews prioritizing the brand over the product itself, offering minimal guidance to consumers regarding a specific item. Finally, non-reviews feature advertisements for various products or services, along with other unrelated content, without providing any actual experiences with the product (Jindal & Liu, 2008). The present study focuses on untruthful opinions, as these are most relevant to understanding how consumers evaluate deceptive reviews. Unlike the other two categories, untruthful opinions involve a deliberate misrepresentation of information, making the evaluation of review veracity central to their detection.

Despite the growing importance of online consumer reviews (OCRs) in purchase decisions, relatively little research has examined how consumers perceive and judge review veracity. A recent systematic literature review by (Walther et al., 2023) identified two main strands of research: a) studies investigating the cues used to indicate the veracity of a review and b) studies examining how those cues are processed to judge veracity. These strands have drawn on a diverse range of theoretical perspectives (Ansari et al., 2018; Filieri, 2016; Walther et al., 2023), resulting in a fragmented understanding of how consumers identify and evaluate potentially deceptive reviews. For example, uncertainty reduction theory, speech act theory, credibility theory, and language expectancy theory, have been used to identify cues to veracity (Ansari & Gupta, 2021; Jensen et al., 2013; Racherla et al., 2012). In contrast, warranting theory, the elaboration likelihood model (ELM), and cognitive dissonance theory PrePrint have been used to explain how these cues are processed to arrive at judgements (Ansari et al., 2018; Chatterjee et al., 2023; Luo et al., 2013; Román et al., 2019).

Although these theories provide valuable insights into individual aspects of review evaluation, their application to the context of fake review detection also presents two disadvantages. First, applying an existing framework on a new problem might mean that important aspects distinguishing the new problem from the original research context are overlooked (Dohn, 2021). Second, most of the research was conducted in laboratory settings that did not consider the context of making a purchase decision. Therefore, the context in which the veracity judgements were made might differ from the actual process through which consumers usually make such veracity judgments. This might limit the practical application of the findings (Naumann et al., 2022). In particular, understanding how consumers evaluate the veracity of reviews requires considering not only the cues they use and how they process them, but also how these evaluations are embedded in the broader online shopping process (Walther et al., 2023).

Taken together, existing research provides substantial knowledge about which cues consumers use to evaluate reviews and several explanations of how these cues may be processed. However, three important uncertainties remain. First, it remains unclear how different review judgements are related to one another during actual review evaluation. For example, wider deception research has shown that veracity judgements can be biased by heuristic evaluations of trustworthiness (O'Sullivan, 2003), and that evaluations of credibility, competence and veracity can all mutually influence one-another (Dunleavy et al., 2010).

Second, existing theories provide limited insight into whether judgements emerge sequentially, simultaneously, or conditionally depending on consumer goals and information needs. Third, most evidence originates from laboratory tasks or retrospective accounts, making it difficult to understand how review evaluation unfolds during ongoing purchase PrePrint decisions. As a result, much prior research provides limited understanding of how consumers integrate multiple review judgements in authentic shopping situations.

Three attempts to build a more integrative theory of how consumers perceive deception in consumer reviews have been made by Filieri (2016), Román et al. (2019) and Walther et al. (2025). Although these studies approach review evaluation from different theoretical perspectives, they share the objective of synthesizing cue identification with description of theorized processes for how consumers form judgements about OCRs. However, they differ in their focus, with Filieri (2016) examining perceived trustworthiness, Román et al. (2019) examining perceived deception, and Walther et al. (2025) providing a broader account of how consumers evaluate reviews within the online shopping process, incorporating usefulness, trustworthiness and veracity. Filieri (2016) focused on how consumers determine the perceived trustworthiness of reviews. Drawing on credibility theory, Filieri (2016) identified several factors, including source and message trustworthiness, review valence, and review patterns that influence perceived trustworthiness. These effects are moderated by consumer experience and website type. For example, the presence of patterns in the publication dates or the repetition of wording can diminish the perceived trustworthiness of a given text. Conversely, when such reviews align with the preceding consumer experience, the perceived trustworthiness increases. This trustworthiness then affects the review's persuasive power (Filieri, 2016).

Thus, Filieri's (2016) framework demonstrates that consumers do not evaluate reviews in isolation but also consider characteristics of the source, patterns across reviews, and their own previous experiences when determining whether a review can be trusted. Román et al. (2019) focused on how consumers perceive deception, rather than trustworthiness, in OCRs. Their Perceived Deception in Online Consumer Reviews (PDOCR) model builds on ELM and cognitive dissonance theory (Román et al., 2019). Using PrePrint interviews, Román et al. (2019) identified antecedents (e.g., initial expectations, elaboration motivation/ability) influenced perceived deception in OCRs, which in turn influenced consumers' self-protective behaviours and public and revenge behaviours (consequences). In other words, an individual's initial expectations regarding a product serve as a critical factor in determining the perceived deceptiveness of a review. This perceived deceptiveness, in turn, influences the consumer's decision to employ protective measures against FRs, such as ignoring or down-voting them. Furthermore, this perceived deceptiveness affects the consumer's interaction with the product, including the purchase decision. Filieri (2016) explains how consumers make decisions about the credibility of reviewers and Román et al. (2019) supplement this with a naturalistic description of how consumers perceive deception within review content and how this perception influences their subsequent behaviour. However, while the determination of perceived trustworthiness (Filieri, 2016) and the perception of deceptive OCRs (Román et al., 2019) rely on different processes and cues (Burgoon et al., 2003; Castelfranchi & Tan, 2002), the judgements are related to and can influence one another (Dunleavy et al., 2010). Therefore, since the two frameworks focus on different aspects of review evaluation, neither fully captures the underlying processes involved in consumers' veracity judgements. This suggests the need for a more comprehensive theoretical framework that considers the different types of judgements consumers make and how these are embedded in the broader purchasing process.

Addressing this need, Walther et al. (2025) used grounded theory methodology to gain a more comprehensive understanding of human perception of review veracity, including the cognitive processes, knowledge and the cues used, and how these are embedded in the online shopping process. In contrast to the previous frameworks, which focused primarily on particular aspects of review evaluation, Walther et al. (2025) aimed to capture the broader process through which consumers evaluate OCRs and incorporate these evaluations into their PrePrint purchase decisions. Their theoretical framework states that a consumers' online purchase process consists of two decision processes in which there are different decision loops (Walther et al., 2025). In the first decision process consumers choose what type of product to buy (e.g. deciding between a vacuum cleaner and a vacuum robot), while in the second consumers choose a specific product to buy (e.g. deciding between different brands). Within both decision processes the information gathered about the product, which usually incorporates OCRs heavily, is evaluated to arrive at an informed decision. Additionally, after each review evaluation, consumers decide whether they have sufficient information or if they need additional information (again, commonly additional OCRs), in relation to their perceived value of the product, to make a purchase decision (Walther et al., 2025).

This part of the process indicates that the impact of undetected FRs can increase with lower value products. Since participants read reviews to reach information sufficiency and are likely to have a lower information sufficiency threshold for lower value products, they may read fewer reviews for these lower value products and may do so less critically. Consequently, individual reviews may carry greater relative weight in the final decision. This makes it particularly important to understand how consumers evaluate individual reviews and whether their scrutiny differs depending on the perceived value of the product. Credibility refers to the extent to which the review appears grounded in genuine product experience and therefore provides a plausible account of product use. Although Walther et al. (2025) propose these concepts are analytically distinct and propose they are each analysed sequentially, this claim currently lacks strong empirical backing. Since Walther et al. (2025) developed their model of OCR use from interviews rather than observations of behaviour, their model is at risk of post hoc rationalisation and from consumers having insufficient insight into their own cognitive processes (Schreurs et al., 2025). Moreover, participants often used the terms interchangeably while also articulating these judgements as separate decisions (Walther et al., 2025). That is, while the researchers classified distinct evaluations of trustworthiness, credibility and veracity, the consumers themselves did not use these terms consistently themselves. While this may reflect normal imprecision in lay use of terms, it may also indicate that for participants the separation between these judgements was less clear cut than Walther et al.'s (2025) model implies.

Therefore, one objective of the present study is to examine whether consumers distinguish these judgements during ongoing review evaluation or whether they emerge as overlapping aspects of a broader evaluation process when observed concurrently with shopping behaviour.

Of particular relevance to this paper are the moments in the shopping process where consumers incorporate OCRs and decide how and if to factor these into the purchase decision, and whether these are genuinely sequential and distinct.

Taken together, the frameworks proposed by Filieri (2016), Román et al. (2019) and Walther et al. (2025) provide an increasingly comprehensive understanding of how consumers evaluate OCRs. While the first two frameworks identify different aspects of review evaluation and their consequences, Walther et al. (2025) integrate these aspects into the broader online purchase process. Nevertheless, due to the retrospective nature of the studies, it remains unclear how these judgements unfold during ongoing review evaluation, whether they occur sequentially or overlap, and how the cues identified in previous research are processed to arrive at these judgements.

Understanding these distinctions is important not only to advance the theoretical understanding of consumer veracity judgements but also to develop interventions that improve consumer resilience to FRs. For example, research shows that human deception detection is poor even after receiving training (Plotkina et al., 2020). However, approaches based on the verifiability of information or source credibility have been shown to be effective in unmasking liars in interrogations (Sitaula et al., 2020) and identifying fake news (Nahari et al., 2014). Therefore, understanding whether consumers rely on distinguishable processes when evaluating review trustworthiness, credibility, and veracity could help identify specific aspects of review evaluation that could be targeted through training. For example, training consumers to verify information and assess source credibility might be effective in increasing their resilience to FRs.

Additionally, since online consumer reviews are used for both high-and low-value products, and previous research (Walther et al., 2025) shows that consumers read different numbers of reviews depending on their perceived value of the product, it is important to take this into account when further exploring veracity judgements on OCRs. In particular, examining how consumers evaluate reviews in different purchase contexts may help establish whether the perceived value of a product influences the extent to which consumers scrutinise individual reviews and the cues they use to assess review veracity. In particular, while previous literature on review evaluation has considered value as motivation for more thorough evaluation of reviews following the ELM, Walther et al. (2025) instead found that value was evaluated alongside existing expertise. Therefore, rather than increasing value simply biasing consumers toward greater review, they found that consumers instead only engaged in evaluative effort that was necessary to achieve information sufficiency. That is, higher value products do not necessarily lead to more extensive or careful evaluation of reviews when consumers feel they have sufficient information to make an informed decision about whether PrePrint a purchase is considered worthwhile given the associated risks. This emphasis on information sufficiency was proposed to indicate review processing depth was better explained through the Heuristic-Systematic Model (HSM, Chaiken & Ledgerwood, 2012) than the ELM (Walther et al., 2025). Walther et al.'s (2025) use of the HSM also allowed for heuristic and systematic evaluations to directly influence one another, but precise analysis of how these two processing types impact on one another is not possible to observe from retrospective interviews.

Therefore, evaluating claims made in Walther et al. (2025) such as that when systematic processing is triggered, only content cues such as argument quality or detailedness are processed systematically currently lack empirical support and so require verification.

Collectively, the existing literature suggests that consumers evaluate review trustworthiness, credibility, and veracity, but it remains unclear how these judgements interact during ongoing shopping activity. The present study addresses this gap by directly observing review evaluation behaviour in real time. Rather than focusing on isolated cues or retrospective accounts of decision-making, we investigate how review judgements emerge, overlap, and contribute to purchase decisions as consumers interact with online reviews.

The present study therefore builds on and seeks to refine the framework proposed by Walther et al. (2025) by addressing the vulnerabilities to the validity of their account emergent from their use of retrospective data collection methods. Rather than developing an entirely new theoretical account, we aim to examine how consumers evaluate review veracity during ongoing online shopping and how judgements of trustworthiness, credibility, and veracity relate to one another. Using concurrent thinking-aloud protocols during simulated online shopping, we investigate the relationships between different judgements, the cues informing them, and the conditions under which consumers scrutinize perceived review veracity. In PrePrint doing so, we aim to clarify how consumers process review information and how their veracity judgements are embedded in the online purchase decision process. This paper uses the grounded theory on how consumers identify fake online reviews provided by Walther et al. (2025) as the focal lens for explaining how consumers evaluate online product reviews during online shopping. The theory combines different theories and adopts the HSM (Chaiken & Ledgerwood, 2012) as a primary decision process. The theory fulfils the IMPACT criteria (Hollebeek et al., 2025) providing a theoretical perspective on human perception of review veracity to explain when consumers rely on heuristics and when systematic scrutiny occurs, instead of a cue list perspective (Interestingness). It matches (Matching) because of the model's sufficiency principle and how triggers are processed in the observed sequence of judgements that the results document. Parsimony is accounted by focussing on a small set of process stages and triggers rather than using a long set of loosely connected predictors identified by previous literature. The platform design and consumer training are able to target the stage where processing of information occurs rather than providing generic advice to consumers (Applicability). Conceptual rigor follows from a clear distinction between relevance judgements, source judgements, and veracity judgements while providing boundary conditions that are tied to the perceived product value and consumer knowledge. Testability stems from the process claims that generate observable predictions about judgement order, trigger conditions, and reliance on review information.

Existing research in fake review detection is largely focused on identifying deception cues and providing frameworks for perceived deception and perceived trustworthiness of reviews. However, the central theoretical uncertainty of how the veracity evaluation process is embedded within the shopping process remains unresolved. Research suggests that in real shopping decisions, the review evaluation is embedded within an information sufficiency seeking process, implying that veracity judgements may be rather conditional than constant.

Interventions that target to improve fake review detection risk to be less effective when assuming that consumers actively and routinely scrutinize content truthfulness during online shopping. Therefore, in this study we use thinking-aloud method with reviewer prompting to triangulate previous results, asking the research question: At which point and how do consumers judge the veracity of consumer reviews when purchasing a product online?

In line with theory development typologies proposed by (Lim, 2026b), this paper offers a process-based explanation that specifies the ordering of stages and the conditions that trigger movement between them. This contribution reflects theoretical integration and theoretical clarification, rather than merely extending existing cue taxonomies. Its novelty lies in demonstrating how review evaluation unfolds through three recurring and interrelated judgements, how these judgements may overlap or be abbreviated in practice, and how information sufficiency, perceived product value, consumer knowledge, and trigger cues shape the extent of review scrutiny.

The paper highlights a counterintuitive pattern: consumers use source credibility as a heuristic for truthfulness, potentially allowing content-level deception to pass unnoticed, even among individuals who are aware that fake reviews are prevalent.

2. Methods

We adopted a thinking-aloud methodology in an interactive/concurrent verbal-protocol procedure with researcher prompting to gain insights into how consumers determine the veracity of OCRs (Boren & Ramey, 2000; Van Someren et al., 1994). This method is optimal to address our research goals because it allows us to observe real-time decisions while reducing memory loss and retrospective reconstruction compared with traditional interviews and while still acknowledging the influence of researcher prompting (Eccles & Arsal, 2017).

2.1. Sampling and Participants

The sample was a convenience sample acquired via the participation system for (business) psychology students at a German University of Applied Sciences and a Dutch University. We aimed at collecting data from adults (at least 18 years old) living in the Netherlands or Germany that have done online shopping at least twice within the last year. Since research (Charters, 2003) and pre-tests (web appendices C and D) showed that thinking-aloud is cognitively less complex and less disruptive to participants' thought processes when done in their native language, participation was limited to native speakers of English, German or Dutch. These three languages were understood by the researchers. We also made the survey available in all three languages.

A total of 20 students participated in this study. After 18 participants our initial inductively generated themes were no longer changing and themes were sufficiently clearly conceptualised indicating a theoretical saturation at the level of theme (Braun & Clarke, 2021; Mason, 2010). However, we continued the data collection to allow for more robust results, based on suggested sample sizes (Guest et al., 2006; Mason, 2010; Morgan et al., 2002).

During analysis, we sought disconfirming cases to ensure the robustness of our results, by actively searching for parts in the interviews that did not correspond with the developing PrePrint themes or process, for example looking for participants that did not look at reviews before making a decision and analysing why they may differ from the majority of participants (Guest et al., 2006; Lim, 2026a).

Participants were aged between 19 and 28 years (M = 21.95 years; SD = 2.42). Their net income per month ranged from €200 to €2000 (M = €1062.71; SD = €544.71). Table 1 gives an overview of the sample's socio-demographic characteristics.

2.2. Procedure

Sixteen participants took part in-person and four participants took part online. For in-person sessions, participants used the researcher's laptop for the study and the researcher sat behind them to be able to see their screen. The online sessions were facilitated by a shared screen feature of the Videocall App used (Microsoft Teams). In both settings the researcher asked participants to elaborate on their behaviour when they did not verbalize it. The study took between 30 and 100 minutes per participant (M = 64 minutes, 21 seconds; SD = 19 minutes, 46 seconds).

Due to technical issues, one participant received the wrong product as test product (a pink charger instead of the water bottle), and another's audio was lost. When the charger was shown again later, it was dismissed by the participant and therefore for this participant we only used the data of 6 products. The missing audio was replaced with a participant-confirmed summary that was written by the researcher from their notes and memory directly after the study and was adjusted and confirmed by the participant on the same day.

The study was conducted as an online survey using the Qualtrics platform. First, the researcher talked participants through the informed consent, entailing the procedure and asking for consent to audio recordings. If participants had any questions, they were answered in full before agreeing to consent. (For the full survey see https://osf.io/acy2h/?view_only=02e4e923f37b4d47b4b3608fe6d151b3).

For the thinking-aloud part of the study participants were presented seven products on live webpages from Amazon, meaning the exact reviews participants were exposed to could vary per participant (Wirth et al., 2004). Each participation was recorded via MS Teams meeting, without camera. Participants looked at the seven products and decided whether they would want to buy the product while verbalizing their thoughts out loud. The first product that participants were exposed to, was a water bottle so that participants could test out thinking-PrePrint aloud. The other six products were shown to the participants in a randomized order to avoid primacy and recency effects (Kite & Whitley, 2018). The task description was written according to the guidelines by Van Someren et al. (1994). After the first product, the researcher asked participants if they felt comfortable, instructed them if they could improve the verbalization of their thoughts (Boren & Ramey, 2000; Charters, 2003), and asked participants if they wanted to continue. Following each individual product, participants were asked to answer multiple questions about the perceived veracity of the OCRs and about the perceived value of the product. These questions were always displayed in the same order after the products. Lastly, participants were asked to indicate their demographics, such as age and gender, silently, via the survey.

The researcher avoided it as much as possible, but gently reminded participants of the task when they fell silent, leading to a more interactive character, as described in literature (Boren & Ramey, 2000; Oh & Wildemuth, 2016; Van Someren et al., 1994). For example, when a participant stopped talking but the researcher could see, that they were scrolling they said 'So, uh, you were scrolling, um, just now and just looked at the reviews. Why?' or when they shared their opinion about something they read without saying it aloud 'So you said now that it's [the average rating] pretty high, but you didn't say the number. Could you maybe say that as well?'. Additionally, after the first test product the researcher instructed participants on how to improve their thinking-aloud, by for example saying, 'if you say a little bit more like I'm scrolling now'. These instructions were written down by the researcher beforehand in all three languages to keep them as simple and as similar as possible across participants (Van Someren et al., 1994).

2.5. Analysis

The voice-recordings of the participants were, in accordance with participant's consent and GDPR, automatically transcribed by the transcription service Amberscript (https://www.amberscript.com/en/). Transcripts were analysed using an inductively developed code schema in five steps (Braun & Clarke, 2006; Clarke & Braun, 2017), using the software MAXQDA (Manufacturer VERBI GmbH, Version 24.5.1, https://www.maxqda.de/). To protect the privacy of the participants in this study we will refer to them with 'P#'.

Before the analysis, we checked transcripts' quality against the audio and identified and removed irrelevant parts of the text for anonymization and to facilitate familiarization (Backman & Kyngäs, 1999; Braun & Clarke, 2006 , 2022). Three people (the first researcher and two student assistants) produced verbatim transcripts (anonymizing potentially identifiable information; deleting parts irrelevant to the research question) and enriched the transcripts with indications of the participant's behaviour during the study (e.g. scrolling through the website; marking whenever reviews were read) for analysis.

Our analyses are based on 19 transcripts and one participant confirmed summary. The summary captured the overall behaviour of the participant, e.g. that they first scanned the newest reviews for key words to indicate their relevance and if they weren't found, the PrePrint reviews were discarded, and cues they used to make judgements. However, since the confirmed summary did not include specific quotes, it could not provide disconfirming evidence and therefore only supported our observations of higher-level process. If the summary would be excluded, the conclusions would not change.

Our analysis consisted of a three-stage process whereby we moved from inductive, to deductive and finally abductive inferencing. First, we inductively generated initial codes by systematically coding interesting features of the data. These initial codes were adapted iteratively during the coding process as thematic overlaps between individual parts of the data became clear. To ensure an unbiased analysis the second author, trained in thematic analysis but naïve to this research field and existing theories, and the first author independently performed initial coding. During the analysis phase both researchers frequently discussed the analysis and developed codes to coordinate their perspectives into an agreed set of initial codes (Lim, 2025). Initial codes were then refined focusing on identifying how and at which points participants judge the veracity of consumer reviews. The first and second author then developed initial codes into preliminary themes grounded within the empirical data.

Next, all researchers reviewed each theme and discussed if the themes were sufficiently explicative of the data and captured participants' perspectives and processes regarding review evaluations, generating the thematic map (Figure 2). This form of discussing the themes follows best practice for codebook thematic analysis compared to using intercoder-agreement because it reflects the aim of developing themes that capture meaning and process rather than developing objective classification taxonomies (Braun & Clarke, 2023).

In the final stage of our inductive analysis, the themes were clearly defined and named during an ongoing analysis and refinement of the themes through discussion and constant validation against the data. Importantly, the framework proposed by Walther et al. (2025) was not used as a predefined coding framework during this initial inductive analysis but was PrePrint introduced alongside other extant theoretical work in the next step to build an integrative theory.

Our second deductive phase of the analysis refined our initial thematic framework by triangulating between our inductively generated themes and extant theoretical explanations of how and when consumers make veracity judgements. In particular, we drew from Walther et al.'s (2025) grounded theory as the most complete theory of how deception judgements are made within the shopping process. We then tested this developing theory against our data through constant comparison to ensure our integrated theory was validated against empirical observation (Whittemore & Knafl, 2005).

Our final abductive analysis contrasts our findings with observations from the broader deception literature to develop testable hypotheses about the proposed decision-making processes which can in turn inform suggestions for effective intervention design. We present the outcomes of this final stage within the discussion section. In this way we move from our empirical data to an integrative theory, and finally to a clear plan of action to support resilience to the harms caused by fake consumer reviews.

3.2. Thematic Analysis

To answer our research question -when and how OCRs are judged -we used thinking-aloud methodology with interactive researcher prompting to gather real-time observations. We first inductively analysed the transcripts and the summary using thematic analysis and subsequently used the grounded theory by Walther et al. (2025) as a sensitising framework to triangulate the results and refine the theoretical framework. This resulted in the Consumer Review Evaluation Model (CREM), an integrated framework that refines and extends existing theorization of online consumer review evaluation.

The transcripts indicate that participants used product information to form a first impression of the product and then engaged with OCRs when additional information was required to support a purchase decision. Both sources of information were either heuristically or more systematically evaluated by the participants depending on features of the information.

For the purpose of the present analysis, heuristic evaluation was inferred from observable behavioural indicators in the think-aloud protocols rather than from direct observation of cognitive states. Reviews were classified as being processed heuristically when participants rapidly skimmed content, focused on isolated keywords, provided top-level impressions, or moved between reviews without extensive justification. More deliberate evaluation was inferred when participants reread reviews, compared information across reviews, verified claims against product descriptions, explicitly reasoned about inconsistencies, or verbalized uncertainty about their judgements. These distinctions were used as analytic indicators of processing depth rather than direct evidence of heuristic or systematic cognition. Analysis of the transcripts and summary identified three recurring judgements through which participants evaluated OCRs, although the cues informing each judgement varied. In contrast to a strictly sequential interpretation, the findings suggest that these judgements were applied conditionally and with varying degrees of overlap depending on the review and the PrePrint information needs of the participant. Figure 2 shows the thematic map of this observed process.

The data showed that participants typically formed a first impression of reviews to determine whether they appeared relevant, that is, whether they contained sufficient useful information for a purchase decision. This initial assessment often resulted in either discarding the OCR due to irrelevance or engaging with the OCR in more detail. Reviews that appeared relevant were then subject to further evaluation. Depending on whether trigger cues were present, this evaluation remained heuristic or became more systematic. During this process participants assessed both the reviewer and the review content. Judgements concerning reviewer credibility and review veracity could occur either in parallel or sequentially, depending on the nature of the cues encountered. While all three judgements were identifiable in the transcripts, the process was not observed as an invariant sequence. Rather, the judgements served as analytical dimensions that were activated to varying degrees as participants examined an OCR. Negative judgements at any point could lead participants to discard the OCR or assign less weight to it in their purchase decision.

3.2.1. Using Reviews to Inform Product Choice

Participants' initial interest in purchasing a product was shaped by a heuristic evaluation of its attractiveness, primarily based on design. If the product appeared unattractive, participants dismissed it without seeking further information. For example, participant 4 thought the bottle was ugly, and so did not look at individual reviews and only looked at the overall reviews before making a negative purchase decision 'Yes, the consumer reviews are 4.6 out of five and above, so I'm actually quite happy with that. I'm not going to look at outliers because this isn't really the sort of product where you sometimes run into technical issues. I do think the bottle is, well, ugly, so I wouldn't buy it (P4).' 3.2.2. Process Of Evaluating Individual Reviews

The protocols show that participants who felt unable to make purchase decisions based on their own expertise and product descriptions relied on OCRs. These reviews were initially evaluated by skimming through them, and then more systematically, if the initial evaluation identified specific causes of concern, or if participant indicated that the product was high value and the participant expressed the need to gather more information about the product.

The initial skimming through was reflected by participants not reading every single word of the review aloud, only verbalizing keywords. These behavioural indicators formed the basis for distinguishing between more heuristic and more deliberate forms of evaluation throughout the analysis. If participants got stuck on a keyword, they expressed their concern and read the review aloud more carefully. This often went hand in hand with verbalizations such as P06 had when a review said that the laptop did not have Bluetooth 'I'd scroll back up a bit now, because I thought there has been something about Bluetooth'. At this point the participant started to systematically evaluate the reviewers' competence and verify the content of the review. These expressions of behaviour indicated that the evaluation of specific reviews followed a certain process. In detail, three recurring forms of judgement were identified when participants evaluated individual reviews and weighed their information into a purchase decision. The judgements could be made in parallel, partially overlap, or occur sequentially and were 'relevance', 'reviewers' credibility', and 'veracity' and were identified as the subthemes of the 'review process'. If one of the judgements was clearly negative, participants PrePrint often discarded the review or substantially discounted its importance. Reviews that were positively evaluated across these judgements were likely to be used in the purchase decision.

3.2.2.1. Relevance

Relevance referred to whether a review contains information that is useful to informing a purchase decision and was first heuristically evaluated via review structure and content.

Reviews which were too long (e.g. P09, P14) or in a foreign language (e.g. P11, P18) were directly skipped, whereas reviews that contained keywords to details were perceived as more relevant and therefore evaluated more systematically. In which case, participants read the content more carefully to determine if it could help the participant's purchase decision (e.g. P05, P07). For example, P12 described that because the reviewer was 'talking about used [product], and I'm actively looking to buy new ones. That's why I've written off this review for myself.' in this example the review was describing a different version of the product than the participant was interested in, and so information about the quality of second-hand goods was considered irrelevant and the review disregarded. Additionally, if the review mentioned negative points about the product without elaborating them (review is too short) or was outdated (e.g. P11: '…that's somehow not such a positive assessment, but it's also a bit older"), the review was systematically evaluated on relevance by evaluating the content of the review. If the review was deemed relevant, participants often engaged in additional evaluations of the reviewer and/or the review content. Furthermore, it is important to notice, when participants read reviews and did not see anything wrong with it, so as to heuristically evaluate and positively judge the reviews, they only skimmed through them without engaging in a detailed evaluation of all three judgements, until they find a review that has relevant information for them.

Participant 19 verbalized this process particularly clearly 'Now I'm reading another new review here -it's spot on. I'm just skimming through [next review] -it's PrePrint a nice keyboard. Lovely design -oh, someone's written [next review] that they're disappointed. But as I've found out that it doesn't have a fan, I don't really mind. My main concern is whether I can use it for work? After all, [next review] says it's not for gaming, but for the office.' 3.2.2.2. Reviewers' Credibility

This theme captured the ways in which participants determined if the author of a review was a competent and trustworthy source of information. In the grounded theory (Walther et al., 2025), the researchers make a distinction between trustworthiness and credibility.

Trustworthiness seemed to reflect whether the reviewer was honest and knowledgeable while credibility seems to reflect whether the description of the product in the OCR seems to reflect the actual experience of the reviewer with the product. This means that the reviewer not only used the product once, but multiple times and correctly. However, in the think-aloud protocols we found that participants seemed to evaluate the credibility of the reviewer as a whole, their honesty, knowledge and correct use of the product as one, instead of distinguishing between trustworthiness and credibility. For this reason, we use the broader label reviewer credibility throughout the remainder of the manuscript to capture this combined assessment of honesty, competence, knowledge, and product experience. This was verbalized by for example laughing about the reviewer if their competence was questioned (P05) and by intertwining the judgement about the reviewer's knowledge and experience with the product. Reviewers were evaluated two-fold. On the one hand, the reviewer was evaluated based on the individual review in terms of their perceived competence via their wording and language use. On the other hand, the reviewer was judged by comparison with other reviews.

3.2.2.2.1. Reviewer Judgement Based on Individual Review PrePrint

The initial judgement about reviewers' competence was made heuristically when the language matched the expectations of the participants (e.g. details, concision, supporting media such as photographs). However, if they differed, for example if the wording seemed unnatural, the logic of the content or the grammar was dubious, a more deliberate evaluation was triggered (e.g. P09, P13). Participant 11 put this very directly: 'I think it somehow sounds the weird here. I don't know, something doesn't sound quite so natural to me…'.

The competence of the reviewer was also perceived to be in question when there was a mismatch between the review and the consumers' knowledge, experience or perception of the product. For example, if the reviewer criticized the product for a malfunction that the participant attributed to a defective product, the reviewer's expectations and competence were questioned. The reviewer was perceived to be authentic when the review was linked to a personal experience, based on realistic expectations, or included a comparison to a different product. For example, P13 states 'I think that comes across as authentic too, definitely. [...] Because she's talking about her own… well, because she's talking about a real-life, everyday situation here. It's sort of… I don't know, it sounds a bit specific, somehow.'. Indicating that for this participant, if reviewers are able to describe a clear experience, they must be sufficiently familiar with the product.

If the reviewers used a language that was too broad or too emotional, a more systematic evaluation is triggered and the reviewer is perceived as questionable, because they were perceived as less objective. More careful and deliberate evaluations were also triggered when participants saw inconsistencies in the review. For example, when participants saw a mismatch between media -such as photo or video -and review content. Then participants would more carefully read the review to identify the cause of the mismatch. For example, when the media indicated complaints within a review were due to incompetence on the part of the reviewer, the review was discarded.

Participant 15 described it as '(the reviewer) PrePrint presented (the problem with the product) in the video as if that was the product. But it's obviously not the product, the product has just got wet or broken, or he has even destroyed it himself". Afterwards, the reviewer is judged based on comparison with other reviews. Participant 13 describes the transition from judging the individual reviewer to judging them in comparison with other reviews 'I have to say, when I read this review, I wonder whether it was really written by that person, because I find it very… Well, generally speaking, there are loads of positive reviews, so I'll have a look at the others, but I just think -well, obviously, maybe it's someone who's really into this sort of thing -but personally, I wouldn't think of writing a review saying that having two versions is particularly handy, that the two-metre cable does this and the one-metre cable does that; it comes across as a bit, well, scripted, I think. But now, at first glance, at least the fact that it's got four stars out of five looks a bit better, at least.' 3.2.2.2.2. Reviewer Judgement Based on Comparison with other Reviews

Additionally, reviews were heuristically compared to previously read reviews, and this informed an initial judgement of reviewer competence when the content aligns with previously read reviews or the participant's own knowledge. For example, if a problem was mentioned by multiple reviews participants thought it to be accurate, P07 indicated that what 'convinced me earlier is now completely irrelevant, because so many people are writing about the same [negative] experience.' However, if the information provided by the review contradicted other reviews a more deliberate evaluation is triggered to determine whether the difference is due to reviewer incompetence, superior expertise, or different experience than other reviewers. Additionally, a deliberate evaluation of the reviewer's competence is triggered if the language is too formal (e.g. P13) compared to other reviews. If the heuristic or systematic evaluation of the reviewer clearly indicated low reviewer competence the review PrePrint was discarded. If the difference could not be clearly attributed to low competence, participants engaged in further scrutiny. During this process, considerations of reviewer credibility and review veracity frequently informed one another and were sometimes evaluated simultaneously. Participant 5 verbalizes shortly how they switch between the credibility and veracity judgement based on a review they compare to other reviews 'It's difficult, because the few positive reviews I've read stood in stark contrast to the negative ones I've read.

Whether that makes them any less true is another matter. I think I'd tend to agree. Perhaps some people were just lucky, whilst others might simply be better at using computers.'

3.2.2.3. Veracity

Veracity evaluations directly addressed whether participants believed the information in a review was deliberately misleading and so contrast with reviewer evaluations where misinformation is allocated to incompetence, not deliberate manipulation. The initial heuristic evaluation of the veracity of the review content was therefore judged both partially simultaneously and partially sequentially with the reviewer judgement, as sometimes a deliberate distinction was necessary to determine whether incongruous information was accurate or not, and then if inaccuracies were deliberate or not. However, when a more systematic evaluation was triggered, participants often prioritised assessing reviewer credibility before undertaking a more elaborate evaluation of veracity. Nevertheless, the findings suggest that these evaluations were not strictly separated and could partially overlap.

In many cases, reviewer competence appeared to receive greater attention because information from an apparently incompetent reviewer was often discounted. However, this pattern was not universal and some initial veracity assessments occurred alongside judgements of reviewer competence.

The features used to determine review veracity emphasized how verifiable the review content was. If the review mentions details that are easily verifiable, mentions pros and cons of a product that other reviews mention and are in sync with the product descriptions and the participants' experiences and expectations then veracity evaluations are often processed heuristically. However, as with reviewer evaluations, if the review content differed from participants' expectations, more systematic evaluation was triggered. During a systematic evaluation participants consider if the inconsistencies (e.g. contradicting information, imbalance between positive and negative reviews, or mismatches between the product description and the review) in the review content can be attributed to either dishonesty of the reviewer or a legitimate other cause, for example they are honestly reviewing a previous iteration of the product. If the inconsistency was attributed to dishonesty, then the review was discarded. If it was perceived to be honest, the review was used to inform the purchase decision and the process either started anew with a new review or the process ended with a purchase decision. The example from participant 6 clearly describes how the participant struggles to define whether the review is mistaken and incompetent, because they have the facts (whether or not the laptop has Bluetooth) wrong, or whether the reviewer actively lied 'But that would definitely be something that's important for me to check, and I'm particularly interested in that right now.

3.3. Defining the Three Judgements

During the answering of the questions participants were further asked to verbalize their thoughts. Therefore, when asked to judge the relevance, trustworthiness, credibility and veracity of the reviews, their thought processes were documented. Throughout answering PrePrint whether participants thought the reviews were credible and trustworthy participants mostly gave the same arguments to justify their answers, for example participant 7 argued 'The reviews seemed to be spot on; er, yes, I'd agree with that too, simply because they described the problem in great detail. The reviews I read seemed, for the most part, to be trustworthy. I'd tick exactly the same box for the same reasons.' This supports our interpretation that the reviewer credibility, so their trustworthiness, as in knowledge, and credibility, as in experience, are determined in an intertwined judgement. While for arguing their veracity judgement, participants mostly gave verifiability reasons, such as participant 10 'I think the review of this product was largely accurate. I'd tend to agree, because people have uploaded videos and pictures of it as well.' This judgement is about deciding if the reviewer is deceiving the participant on purpose or if the reviewer is incompetent. This distinction also helps explain why participants often devoted less effort to evaluating veracity when a reviewer was already perceived as incompetent. In such cases, a detailed veracity evaluation was frequently abbreviated or deemed unnecessary. Another example that shows directly how veracity is judged more based on facts and credibility and trustworthiness are judged with the same reasoning concerning the reviewers' credibility was made by participant 2 'I think the reviews for this product were largely true. Yes. [The reviewers] make it clear that it was intended for light tasks, and they also mentioned that it worked well for those tasks. That's something I'd expected, so that seems to be true. 4. Discussion

We used thinking-aloud interviews with researcher prompting to answer the research question 'At which point and how do consumers judge the veracity of consumer reviews when purchasing a product online?'. The findings suggested that participants tend to prioritize evaluating reviewer credibility over the truthfulness of review content, unless they have a high need for information and the claims are specific. Furthermore, when participants engaged in veracity assessment, they typically started with heuristic cues related to verifiability (e.g. specificity and consistency), followed by more analytical comparisons with available evidence.

Interestingly, evaluating the reviewer often served as a heuristic for truthfulness: when a reviewer appeared trustworthy and competent, participants assumed the information was accurate. This heuristic approach highlights the importance of trustworthiness cues in online environments.

From a practical standpoint, this insight offers clear guidance for platform design.

Enhancing and clearly signalling reviewer reliability may support consumer decision-making.

However, our findings suggest that credibility indicators should ideally be accompanied by mechanisms that promote content verification, as consumers may otherwise rely too strongly on reviewer credibility as a proxy for truthfulness.

4.1. Developing a Synthesized Theoretical Framework for Consumers Perception of Review Veracity

Most existing research has adapted a multitude of theories to the human fake review detection context (Paul & Nikolaev, 2021; Walther et al., 2023) identifying cues to veracity (e.g. Ansari & Gupta, 2021; Jensen et al., 2013), inspecting the processing of cues (e.g. Chatterjee et al., 2023; Román et al., 2019) or doing both using retrospection (Walther et al. 2025). By using The present study extends existing research by directly observing review evaluation behaviour during ongoing shopping decisions. Whereas previous work relied primarily on retrospective accounts, our findings provide evidence of how review judgements unfold in real time and under which conditions consumers engage in more elaborate scrutiny.

Our findings indicate that participants initially engaged in rapid heuristic screening of OCRs and only moved towards more elaborate evaluation when trigger cues, inconsistencies, or unmet information needs emerged. The data shows that review relevance is shaped by both review characteristics (e.g. content, length) and the perceived need to gather more information about the product. If a review meets this need, participants evaluate the reviewer's competence-initially through language, then by comparing it with other reviews. A key finding of the present study is that participants rarely treated reviewer evaluations and veracity evaluations as fully separate processes. Instead, these judgements frequently overlapped and informed one another during review evaluation. This observation refines existing conceptualisations of review evaluation by demonstrating that the judgements are less clearly separated and less strictly sequential than previously assumed. Instead, participants appeared to move flexibly between judgements depending on the information available and the level of scrutiny triggered by a review. Which in turn means that educating and training consumers with specific cues could improve overall scrutiny.

Reviews that were positively evaluated across the three judgments were more likely to inform the purchase decision, whereas strongly negative evaluations often led participants to discard or discount the review (see Figure 3). Participants' use of cues such as the amount of details and information in the review also aligns with previous research that found fake PrePrint reviewers relied on semantic (general) memory to write a review, while genuine reviewers used episodic (specific) memory. This difference results in fake reviews containing less concrete and timely structured details than genuine reviews (Kronrod et al., 2023).

4.2. Dual Processing Models in Purchase and Veracity Decisions

The CREM suggests that participants engaged in deeper evaluation primarily when they encountered expectation violations or had a heightened desire for more information. These triggered default heuristic processing and led to more deliberate scrutiny of review content, supporting a dual-processing approach to information evaluation.

This dynamic aligns closely with the Heuristic-Systematic Model (HSM) which emphasizes that individuals shift from heuristic to systematic (or critical) processing when seeking informational sufficiency or questioning the confidence in their judgement (Chaiken & Ledgerwood, 2012). In contrast, ELM posits that motivation and ability determine whether individuals process information via the central (systematic) or peripheral (heuristic) route PrePrint (Petty & Cacioppo, 1986). While ELM has been applied to fake review detection, the dynamic interplay between processing modes as seen in our study, with heuristic processing as the default and systematic processing activated by specific cognitive triggers, aligns more precisely with HSM's predictions. This also resonates with Walther et al's (2025) grounded theory which suggested that the triggers for deeper, systematic processing align more closely with HSM's emphasis on confidence about the review information and information sufficiency to make a purchase decision (Chaiken & Ledgerwood, 2012; Petty & Cacioppo, 1986).

Our findings show that participants used the number of reviews as a heuristic cue for credibility. A moderate-to-high number of reviews increased trust in both the average rating and individual reviews, suggesting a belief in reviewer consensus and low risk of manipulation-consistent with Park et al. (2007). However, when the number of reviews seemed disproportionately high relative to product sales or market age, it triggered suspicion and more deliberate evaluation. This finding stands in contrast to earlier research that suggested consumers fail to adjust their perception of the average rating according to the total number of reviews (de Langhe et al., 2016). However, in our study consumers do not mention how other aspects influence the average rating which is consistent with previous research (de Langhe et al., 2016).

Participants also paid closer attention to extreme reviews, often viewing them as less credible but more useful -especially negative ones -in helping form a strong first impression and supporting or challenging their initial product choice. This supports Park and Nicolau's (2015) research that showed extreme ratings are more engaging. While moderate reviews were seen as more reliable, they required more cognitive effort and were typically considered later in the decision process, possibly explaining why they were rated as less enjoyable in prior studies (Park & Nicolau, 2015).

Thus, while both models offer valuable insights, our data suggests the conclusion that HSM provides a nuanced and context-sensitive framework for understanding how individuals assess the credibility of OCRs in real time, accounting for the default reliance on heuristic cues and the conditions under which systematic processing is triggered, resulting in most overlap with the CREM and a robust explanation for the dynamic nature of OCR evaluation.

4.3. Alignment With Models of Deception Detection

The CREM indicates the direct relevance of wider work on deception detection that has not received much attention in the fake review literature. Participants, despite being aware of FRs, seem to presume that reviews are honest and only engaged in deeper processing of veracity when triggered by a violation of their expectations, demonstrating a truth bias (Stiff et al., 1992; Vrij & Baxter, 1999). There is debate about whether the truth bias is better explained by the truth-default theory (TDT) (Levine, 2024) or the Adaptive Lie Detector (ALIED) model (Street, 2015).

TDT states that people tend to presume that other people communicate honestly most of the time, to enable efficient communication and cooperation (Levine, 2024). However, when the other seems to have an obvious motive for deception, or when people are primed to expect deception, people become suspicious of others (Levine, 2024). The ALIED model, on the other hand, states that people use adaptive reasoning approaches, using available information and weigh it against each other, to make lie-truth judgements, without differentiating in the processes between the lie and truth judgement (Levine & Street, 2024; Street, 2015).

Our participants became suspicious when the communicated content was either selfcontradictory or inconsistent with known facts, for example the product description or in contrast to the rest of the OCRs, which suggests that TDT suffices as an explanation for our PrePrint data (Levine, 2024). However, participants also deliberately evaluate the competence of a reviewer using cues and available information to satisfy their need for information, which indicates that the ALIED model is a more accurate fit for explaining our data (Street, 2015).

Future research should disambiguate the two theories to potentially improve fake review detection by priming people to believe there are more vs. fewer FRs. This could give insights into whether the truth default prevails, and whether making people aware of the number of FRs improves resilience, even if only by reducing the truth bias.

4.4. Reputation Management and Verifiability Approach

Our data shows that when people engage in deeper analysis for veracity, they do so by checking claims against evidence within the review content. Once deliberate analysis for veracity is triggered, participants compare information from the reviews to their own knowledge, product information and other reviews as well as look for theoretically checkable facts. This behaviour can be explained by participants unconsciously applying verifiability approach to review judgements. Verifiability approach is about exploiting the fact that truth tellers tend to provide more verifiable details than liars (Nahari et al., 2014; Palena et al., 2021). Additionally, participants' deliberate evaluation of a review is triggered by inconsistencies between the review, their own knowledge or other available information, which mimics the strategic use of evidence in interrogations of professional interviewers (Oleszkiewicz & Watson, 2021; Verschuere et al., 2021). The strategic use of evidence by professionals is characterized by withholding evidence from a suspect to see if their account of the events will contradict the evidence and might provide them with new information (Oleszkiewicz & Watson, 2021). Future research could use this insight to inform interventions by telling participants to look for verifiable information and inconsistencies to effectively spot incorrect claims.

Participants' behaviour to judge the reviewer's competence underscores that the source of a review and the quality of the review have the most influence on how credible a review is perceived, aligning with earlier research on how credibility weighs into the use of reviews to inform purchase decisions (Chakraborty & Bhat, 2018; Shalev et al., 2025). Furthermore, this behaviour aligns with previous research indicating that people base trust decisions on reputation and that this reputation does not have to be observed or experienced directly but can also be heard about (Milinski, 2016; Tennie et al., 2010).

In the online world this reputation system can lead to reviewers being incentivized to give accurate accounts of their experience because others are watching, improving transparency and decreasing information asymmetry between sellers and consumers (Tadelis, 2016). Additionally, this supports that reviews designated as 'verified purchases' are regarded as having been composed by reviewers who are perceived to possess enhanced benevolence, competence and integrity compared to reviews that are not marked as such or that are marked as 'incentivized reviews' (Nguyen et al., 2024).

This highlights practical implications: On the one hand, educational initiatives focused on source evaluation may enhance consumers' scrutiny in review evaluation because consumers seem to naturally prioritize source evaluations over veracity judgements. Working with people's heuristics is often more effective than teaching them something new (Mertens et al., 2022). However, reviewers with strong credibility cues could be less scrutinized if education only focusses on credibility cues. Therefore, our results rather support interventions

4.5. Limitations

Our thinking-aloud methodology with interactive researcher prompting allowed us to gather real-time data providing direct evidence of when and how students make veracity decisions about OCRs within the shopping process. Nonetheless, our methods do have some limitations.

While we ensured that participants could feel as free as possible to exclaim their thoughts by providing materials in their native tongue and making sure they felt comfortable during the study, it is a skill to verbalize complex thought without simplifying or distorting it (Charters, 2003; Pressley & Afflerbach, 1995 /2009). By giving the participants a test object and giving them feedback afterwards, we tried to overcome this bottleneck of slowing down the thought process (Charters, 2003). We cannot be sure that the verbalization of thoughts did not influence the thought process to comply with social norms or whether it influenced their perceptions of the reviews, for example researcher prompting could have led participants to elaborate information that would otherwise have remained unattended. Research found that when message delivery of advertisements is interrupted, the advertisement can have a bigger influence on the consumers' processing, than when it is delivered uninterrupted (Kupor & Tormala, 2015). In our case the interruptions could be that participants had to be reminded of thinking-aloud, which could have increased the message processing and lead to an even more deliberate evaluation of the reviews. This increase could have distorted our results by increasing the systematic evaluations of reviews.

Moreover, we combined in-person and online participation. While the researcher used the same protocols and prompts during the participation, it could be that the different modes of delivery could impact on the data provided by participants. However, prior research has PrePrint shown that despite concerns about deploying think-aloud protocols online, the main risks pertain to safeguarding and the potential disruption to cognitions from poor internet connectivity and not from impacts on communication or rapport (Alhejaili et al., 2022).

Similarly, we observed no meaningful distinction in the reports of participants via our online or in person protocol.

Following evidence that even highly proficient second language speakers process text differently to native speakers (Pérez et al., 2018) and because reading information in a second language changes how that information is processed and reported within think-loud protocols (Davis & Bistodeau, 1993), participants performed the study in their own language. However, this does mean that we cannot be sure that our process fully captures the (very common) experience of people having to perform online shopping in a non-native language. How second language users evaluate reviews is not widely researched, but there are reasons to expect there may be some differences in how information is processed. For example, the impact of reduced processing fluency on the ability to recognise or integrate cues to credibility or veracity is unclear (Morishima, 2013). Prior research has also shown that hotel reviews tend to be perceived as more positive when read in a second language (Lopes & Avramova, 2026), possibly because of a tendency for second language learners to respond more emotively to positive over negative words (Sheikh & Titone, 2016). In contrast, nonnative speakers have previously been shown to be better at detecting deception, though primarily due to a reduced tendency to assume truthfulness (Da Silva & Leach, 2013; Evans & Michael, 2014). Therefore, there is a need to better understand how well our model captures the processing of OCR by non-native speakers.

Additionally, one participant's audio recording was lost and replaced with a participant-confirmed summary and one participant was shown the wrong test product. This means that for the conclusions we used the transcripts of 18 participants with each 7 products, PrePrint one participant with 6 products and one confirmed summary. However, if the summary or the participant with 6 products would be excluded, the conclusions would not change. This is because we reached theoretical saturation after 18 participants (including the participants with technical issues) and the last two participants did not produce any disconfirming cases that would further develop our initial inductive analysis (Braun & Clarke, 2021). Therefore, theoretical saturation was reached on both a thematic level and on the overall process level after 18 participants.

We used live webpages of Amazon as product stimuli, which led to slight changes in prices and newly added reviews per participant. Since we had no control over which reviews the participants looked at, we cannot determine what the specific effects of each review were.

However, since in this study we were interested in the perceived value of the products and how the reviews are processed by consumers in the context of a simulated purchase decision, the influence of each review was not our goal. Instead, having more variance in the reviews allowed us to have more heterogeneity and improve the generalizability of our findings beyond the scope of a specific subsample of reviews. Additionally, fourteen participants took part in the study within a two-day timeframe, decreasing the chances of fundamental changes in the reviews and prices of the products. Our findings triangulate existing studies using static websites (e.g. Ansari & Gupta, 2021) but allow us to go beyond the influence of specific cues to describe the wider process more holistically.

In contrast to not controlling which reviews are displayed to participants, we asked participants to evaluate seven predefined products and indicate whether they would want to purchase them. With this we intended to produce an authentic purchase situation. However, no actual purchase decision with financial consequence occurred. Therefore, our authentic purchase context is more accurately described as a realistic shopping simulation using live webpages, not a real shopping experience.

Moreover, the think-aloud procedure potentially creates demand characteristics and sensitisation effects, especially when it is necessary to prompt participants to articulate their thoughts (Hertzum et al., 2009). Participants completed seven product evaluations, after each, they were explicitly asked to indicate their perceived veracity of the reviews. Repeatedly asking participants to evaluate review veracity may have increased their attention to potentially deceptive reviews during subsequent product evaluations. Although this sensitization may primarily have influenced how frequently or extensively participants scrutinized reviews, rather than the underlying evaluation process itself, we cannot rule out that the process was also affected. Future research should aim at eliminating these demand characteristics and sensitisation effects.

Additionally, the relatively homogeneous sample consisting of 20 students aged 19 to 28 recruited from (business) psychology programmes in Germany and the Netherlands further limits the generalizability of the conclusions. The homogeneity of the sample in combination with the simulation of the purchase decision could have influenced the participants' information search, since central variables of CREM, such as product knowledge, purchasing experience, financial resources, perceived value, and susceptibility to review information, are likely to differ substantially across age and consumer groups.

In spite of these limitations, our results triangulate well with existing lab studies and retrospective inductive studies (Ansari & Gupta, 2021; Filieri, 2016; Walther et al., 2025) and therefore allowed us to directly evidence the described processes by comparing them to authentic real time behaviour.

5. Conclusion

This study developed the Consumer Review Evaluation Model (CREM), which builds on previous research to explain how consumers evaluate the perceived veracity of online reviews PrePrint during purchase decision-making. The model highlights how judgements of review relevance, reviewer credibility, and perceived veracity interact, and how consumers' information needs may shape the extent to which they engage in more deliberate evaluation.

Our results indicate that participants primarily focus on assessing the credibility of the reviewer rather than scrutinizing the veracity of the review-except when their need for information sufficiency is heightened, for example due to a higher value of the product, and the claims presented in the reviews are detailed. Under these conditions, participants seem to shift toward a more intentional and reflective evaluation of truthfulness. The veracity judgement begins with initial indicators of verifiability, such as linguistic precision and internal coherence, and progresses to a more systematic comparison with other reviews or existing knowledge about the product.
