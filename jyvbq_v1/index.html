To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 2 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 128 papers with 246 instances of power statements, 203 were correctly detected (true positives), 22 were missed (false negatives) and 21 were incorrectly detected (false positives). Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Marginal Significance works

List all sentences that describe an effect as ‘marginally significant’.

The marginal module searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The list of terms is a subset of those listed in a blog post by Matthew Hankins. The module returns all sentences that match terms describing ‘marginally significant’ results.

Some of the terms identified might not be problematic in some contexts, and there are ways to describe ‘marginal significance’ that are not detected by the module.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 51 papers with 87 statements, this module correctly identified 38 statements (true positives) and incorrectly flagged 22 statements (false positives). It failed to detect 27 statements. Thus, among all statements flagged by the module, 63% were genuine cases (positive predictive value). However, the module missed 42% of all true statements (false negative rate).

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

This updated meta-analysis evaluated evidence for direct and indirect mental interactions with random binary event generators (RNGs) in studies published after the meta-analysis by Bösch, Steinkamp, and Boller (2006). The aim was to estimate the overall magnitude and precision of reported effects, assess between-study heterogeneity and potential publication bias, and compare findings across direct intention and indirect field-based protocols.

Studies were identified through database searches, specialist journals and conference proceedings, an AI-assisted literature search, and contact with researchers in the field. Forty-nine eligible studies were included: 18 direct-interaction studies, in which participants intentionally attempted to influence RNG outputs, and 28 indirect-interaction studies, in which RNG activity was examined in relation to collective human attention.

Direct interaction studies showed a small positive overall per trial effect size d = 0.0017, 95% CI [0.0001, 0.0033], p = .041; I² = 0%. Indirect interaction studies showed a larger but statistically nonsignificant and less precise effect size estimate, d = 0.0006, 95% CI [-0.00001-0.0012], p = .064; I² = 25.2%).

Moderator analyses of direct studies suggested larger effects among selected participants and in bidirectional-intention tasks, although these estimates were imprecise and based on few studies.

Selection-model, excess-significance, and proportion-of-significant-results analyses indicated possible publication bias in both research paradigms.

Overall, the findings suggest small deviations from chance in direct RNG-interaction studies, whereas evidence for indirect effects remains uncertain because of lower precision. Potential selective reporting and the limited number of studies constrain firm conclusions, highlighting the need for preregistered, adequately powered, and independently replicated investigations.

Introduction

Whether human mental states can exert a systematic, statistically detectable influence on the behavior of certified physical random systems is one of the most empirically demanding questions in anomalous cognition research. The experimental program addressing this question spans more than six decades, involves hundreds of laboratories across multiple continents, and has generated a literature of several thousand published reports. Yet, no consensus has emerged, not because the data are sparse, but because the data are deeply contested along methodological, statistical, and theoretical lines that resist easy resolution.

The present meta-analysis enters this debate with the specific aim of providing the most comprehensive and methodologically transparent quantitative synthesis to date, covering both direct protocols in which individuals or groups consciously attempt to bias an RNG output, and "field" (indirect) protocols in which distributed RNG networks are monitored for coherence anomalies during periods of large-scale collective human attention, encompassing both localized non-intentional/implicit group or healing settings (FieldREG) and global collective attention events (GCP).

Historical Context and the Emergence of the RNG Paradigm

The use of electronic random number generators as targets for putative mind-matter interactions (MMI) emerged from the earlier dice-throwing tradition. Helmut Schmidt's mid-20th-century work, conducted with apparatus based on quantum-level radioactive decay and electronic noise sources, established the foundational design logic: if a device whose outputs are determined by processes that are, in principle, immune to classical perturbation shows systematic deviations correlated with the stated intention of a human participant, conventional explanations must be excluded before an anomalous conclusion can be entertained. Schmidt's early electronic PK studies (Schmidt, 1970; 2017) remain among the most frequently cited primary sources in this literature, with the apparatus design using a quantum noise source driving a binary display setting the template adopted by nearly all subsequent direct interaction researchers. The paradigm was institutionalized and scaled through the Princeton Engineering Anomalies Research (PEAR) laboratory, where Robert Jahn and Brenda Dunne (Dunne & Jahn, 1995) conducted what would become the benchmark dataset: a twelve-year program involving 91 volunteer participants generating approximately 2.5 million RNG trials, within which HI (intend-high) and LO (intend-low) conditions were compared against a baseline (BL) control.

A parallel tradition of pre-recorded target experiments, in which participants attempted to influence RNG output that had been generated and stored before the intention session, extended the paradigm into time-reversed causal models that remain theoretically unresolved (Schmidt, 1993).

Independently, the field (or indirect) paradigm emerged from the observation that certain spontaneous community events seemed to correlate with anomalous variance in nearby RNG devices.

This observation led to the development of the FieldREG methodology and, eventually, to the Global Consciousness Project (GCP), initiated in 1998 by Roger Nelson ((Nelson et al., 1998) which established a world-spanning network of physical RNGs recording 200-bit samples every second, 24 hours a day, from nodes distributed globally. The field paradigm inverts the causal logic of direct interaction: no participant targets the device intentionally; instead, the network is monitored for correlated deviations during naturally occurring events predicted to generate large-scale coherent human attention, e.g., global disasters, major ceremonies, and mass media broadcasts (Nelson, 2024).

Prior Meta-Analytic Evidence

The most methodologically rigorous and widely cited of these quantitative syntheses is Bösch, Steinkamp, and Boller (2006). This meta-analysis of 380 experimental studies carried out up to 2004, found a per-bit standardized effect size d=0.00007 (Holmberg and Cardeña, 2026) with a high level of heterogeneity and signs of publication bias.

Bösch et al. estimated that approximately 1,544 unreported null experiments would be required to nullify the observed meta-analytic signal through a file-drawer effect alone, a figure that has been contested by both proponents arguing that it is an overestimate and skeptics arguing that it is insufficient to rule out selective reporting (Kugel, 2011; Williams, 2022).

For the field paradigm, the GCP's formal experiment has been synthesized at intervals by Nelson, Bancel, and collaborators. Bancel and Nelson (2008), reporting on 236 world events accumulated over nine years of continuous operation, found that "the cumulative significance across all events favors the hypothesis by more than 4.5 standard deviations," with secondary analyses suggesting the result is driven by correlations across global RNG network distances, an architecturally non-trivial finding that distinguishes genuine network coherence from simple univariate deviation. By the formal experiment's completion across 500 pre-specified events covering the period 1998-2015, Nelson (2024) reports that the accumulated departure exceeds 7 standard deviations.

Because the GCP constitutes a single continuously running experiment rather than a series of independent replications, the critical assessment focuses less on study-to-study consistency and more on analytical validity within that single protocol, event selection operationalization, temporal window specification, post-hoc secondary analyses, and the adequacy of pre-specification, given that events are identified from news reports after the fact.

Theoretical Frameworks and the Direct-Field Distinction

The theoretical interpretation for both paradigm families is underdeveloped relative to the accumulated data. Direct micro-PK accounts range from decision augmentation theory (May, Utts, & Spottiswoode, 1995), which locates the anomaly in precognitive information use at decision points rather than physical biasing, to von Lucadou's Correlation Matrix Method model (von Lucadou, Römer, & Walach, 2007), which frames psi effects as non-local correlations governed by organizational closure constraints to quantum-measurement observer-collapse models that attribute RNG deviations to the act of conscious observation (Maier, Dechamps and Rabeyron, 2022; Radin and Cline, 2026).

None of these frameworks has achieved sufficient specificity to generate differential predictions that could adjudicate between competing accounts using the existing data. For the field paradigm, the dominant theoretical commitment is to some form of "consciousness field", the hypothesis that largescale coherence of human attention constitutes a physically detectable state that correlates with anomalous structure in physically distributed random systems.

Holmberg's (2026) Cognitive Entropy Shift Model, which suggests that distinct cognitive states modulate probabilistic systems by reducing entropy, represents a more recent theoretical candidate, although its empirical status is preliminary.

The Present Meta-Analysis: Rationale, Scope, and Design

The present meta-analysis is an update from the Bösch, Steinkamp, and Boller (2006) metaanalysis through 2026 for direct and indirect interaction experimental protocols. Effect sizes are expressed as per-trial standardized effect sizes (denoted as d) to permit cross-paradigm comparison. . The goal is not to adjudicate the ontological claim of whether mind-matter interaction in the sense of anomalous RNG biasing exists as a genuine physical phenomenon.

The meta-analysis can determine what the current experimental database, taken as a whole and evaluated by contemporary standards of methodological transparency, does and does not support and what experimental designs would be required to generate stronger evidence in either direction.

Method

Studies retrieval

A search with https://scholar.google.com/ , the more comprehensive database of scientific materials, with the keywords "random number generator AND human intention OR mental interaction" from 2004 (included) to September 2026 yielded 17800 results.

A more efficient strategy was to check all specialised journals that publish such a type of phenomenon Furthermore, in September 2026, a search was carried out using the app.undermind.ai, a modern AI tool that offers the possibility to retrieve scientific references for a review by using the following prompt: "List all studies related to the interaction with random number generators, completed after Bösch, Steinkamp, and Boller, (2006) meta-analysis, up to date".

Again, authors who were known to be involved in such investigations were directly contacted via email.

The overall search produced 46 papers related to both direct and indirect interactions with random number generators; see the reference list of all included and excluded studies and the PRISMA flowchart in the Supplementary Material.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

A study was included if it satisfied all these conditions: a. The independent variable must be a human direct or indirect intention to reduce the randomness of the binary events either directional, that is, enhancing only the number of 0 events or vice versa, or both type of events. As direct intention, we defined a condition in which the participants were instructed to voluntarily try to modify the randomness of the events. As indirect, we defined a condition in which the participants were not informed that their mental activity was correlated with the outputs of the binary events. b. the dependent variable must be the number of binary events, 0 or 1 generated by random number generators, or similar electronic devices, e.g., Zener diodes.

c. included descriptive or inferential statistics related to the comparison between mental interactions with control or expected random events, sufficient to estimate standardized effect sizes and the corresponding variances; d. published in English in a scientific journal, in conference proceedings, and PhD dissertations, that is studies with a minimum of independent control; e. not included in the Bösch, Steinkamp and Boller (2006) meta-analysis.

According to criterion a), we excluded studies with non-human participants, for example, Bedford et al. (2005). Furthermore, we excluded studies that used random binary events as the dependent variable, but the authors used their outputs to detect specific non-randomness thresholds, for example, a standardized deviation of z = 1.65 (corresponding to a one-tailed p = .05; Tressoldi et al.

2014; Tressoldi et al., 2020).

We also excluded all studies carried out by Maier and Deschamps (Deschamps, 2025), who tested the hypothesis that the RNG should mainly be affected by unconscious-related states of mind of the participants.

According to criterion b), we excluded studies using different electronic outputs as dependent variables, e.g. photons (Radin, 2025; Tressoldi et al. 2016).

Data extraction

For each selected study, we extracted the following information: authors of the study, year of publication, type of intention: direct (voluntary) vs indirect (field effect) mind-matter interaction, aim of intention: unidirectional, increase or decrease 0s or 1s, or bidirectional, number of participants, selection or non-selection of participants, number of trials, bits/sec of RNGs, total number of bits, and type of statistic. This extraction was carried out by the two authors and verified using Gemini 3.1 Pro, extended thinking, and Z AI, GLM-5.3, with the following prompt: "Estimate the per-trial and per-bit effect sizes and their variances from the experiment described in the uploaded paper, extract the number of trials, and justify these values". Any discrepancy was solved rechecking the original paper.

Effect size estimation

Effect sizes were estimated with the formula: z normal deviate/√number of trials; variance with the formula: 1/Number of trials.

Data analyses

For the estimation of the overall effect size we applied a frequentist random effects model using the metafor v.5.2 (Viechtbauer, 2010).

For the frequentist random model, following the recommendations of Langan et al. (2019) we used the restricted maximum likelihood (REML) approach to estimate the heterogeneity variance and the Hartung's profile likelihood method for deriving the average effect size estimate and its confidence interval (Rubio-Aparicio et al. 2018).

Identical analyses were applied separately to both the direct and the indirect interaction.

Discussion

This updated meta-analysis synthesized post-2004 studies examining direct and indirect mental interaction with random number generators (RNGs). Overall, the findings indicate small positive pooled effects for both paradigms. Direct-intention studies showed a statistically significant mean effect size (d = 0.0017, p = .04), whereas indirect or field-based studies showed a larger nominal effect estimate (d = 0.0006) but with lower precision and no conventional statistical significance (p = .064).

Thus, the evidence provides limited support for deviations from expected RNG behavior associated with human mental activity, but the magnitude of these effects is extremely small and their interpretation remains uncertain.

The direct-interaction results are notable because they were associated with very low overall heterogeneity (I² = 0.1%). This suggests that the included studies produced relatively consistent effectsize estimates despite differences in participants, apparatuses, protocols, and intention instructions.

However, homogeneity alone does not establish the validity of an anomalous effect, particularly given the indications of publication bias. The selection-model effect size estimates were non-significant for both direct and indirect studies, and the PSST and TESS analyses suggested an excess of statistically significant findings.

The direct-interaction studies produced a nominal effect approximately three times larger than that observed in indirect-intention studies. Nevertheless, this finding should not be interpreted as evidence that field effects are stronger. The indirect estimate was less precise, and the reported confidence interval indicates substantial uncertainty around the pooled effect. In addition, indirect studies differ fundamentally from direct studies in their sampling, event definition, temporal structure, data volume, and analytic flexibility. Field protocols may involve very large numbers of trials and naturally occurring events, but these features also increase the importance of transparent eventselection criteria, preregistration of analysis windows, correction for multiple testing, and clear separation between confirmatory and exploratory analyses.

The moderator analyses provide tentative evidence that selected participants and bidirectional tasks may be associated with larger effects in direct-intention studies. Selected participants showed an approximately five fold larger mean effect size than unselected participants, and bidirectional intention tasks showed a larger mean effect than unidirectional tasks. However, neither the selected-participant nor the bidirectional-task subgroup reached conventional statistical significance, and heterogeneity was substantial in these subgroups. These results should therefore be considered hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory. They may reflect genuine differences in participant characteristics or task demands, but they could also arise from small study numbers, selective reporting, or differences in experimental quality.

Strengths

A major strength of this review is its inclusion of both direct and indirect RNG-interaction paradigms. Most previous quantitative syntheses have focused primarily on direct intentional influence, whereas the present analysis allows a structured comparison between individual intentional protocols and field-based designs. This broader scope helps clarify that the two paradigms should not be treated as interchangeable: they involve different theoretical assumptions, operational definitions, and methodological vulnerabilities.

A second strength is the use of contemporary random-effects meta-analytic methods, including restricted maximum likelihood estimation and Hartung-type confidence intervals. These methods are appropriate when effect sizes may vary across studies and provide more conservative uncertainty estimates than simpler fixed-effect approaches. The use of multiple publication-bias assessments is also a strength because no single test can reliably identify selective reporting under all conditions.

This meta-analysis further extends the evidence base beyond the Bösch, Steinkamp, and Boller meta-analysis by incorporating studies published from 2004 through 2026. The search strategy combined database searching, examination of specialist journals and conference proceedings, AIassisted reference identification, and direct contact with researchers in the field. This multi-source approach was intended to reduce the likelihood that relevant studies were overlooked.

Limitations

Several limitations constrain the interpretation of the present findings. First, the pooled effects are extremely small. Even where statistically significant, effect sizes of this magnitude are highly vulnerable to minor methodological artifacts, imperfect randomization, data-processing decisions, optional stopping, selective reporting, or unrecognized dependencies among observations. Statistical significance should therefore not be equated with practical or theoretical significance.

Second, the number of studies available for several analyses was limited, particularly in moderator subgroups. This reduces statistical power, broadens confidence intervals, and makes estimates of heterogeneity unstable. The high heterogeneity observed among selected-participant studies further limits confidence in the corresponding subgroup estimate.

Third, the operationalization of effect sizes as the reported statistic divided by the square root of the number of trials assumes comparability across diverse experimental designs and statistical procedures.

Fourth, the operationalization of effect sizes as the reported statistic divided by the square root of the number of trials assumes comparability across diverse experimental designs and statistical procedures. Differences in RNG hardware, trial definition, aggregation methods, pre-processing, baseline conditions, and inferential tests may affect the comparability of standardized estimates. Future meta-analyses would benefit from detailed coding of these design features and from sensitivity analyses using alternative effect-size metrics.

Fifth, the indirect or field-based literature presents distinctive challenges. These studies often rely on naturally occurring events and large-scale datasets, where event inclusion, event timing, timewindow selection, outcome specification, and secondary analyses may be difficult to standardize fully. Consequently, even a large volume of recorded RNG data cannot compensate for insufficiently constrained hypotheses or post hoc analytic flexibility.

Finally, the present meta-analysis evaluates statistical patterns rather than the underlying mechanism. The findings cannot distinguish between a genuine mind-matter interaction, methodological artifacts, expectancy effects, selective reporting, statistical misspecification, or alternative theoretical accounts such as decision augmentation. Stronger evidence will require designs capable of differentiating among these competing explanations.

Suggestions for Future Investigations

Future direct-intention studies should use preregistered protocols that specify the primary outcome, direction of the predicted effect, stopping rule, number of trials, exclusion criteria, statistical model, and treatment of missing or corrupted data. Studies should also employ automated data capture, tamper-evident logs, independent monitoring of RNG performance, and public sharing of raw data and analysis code.

Multi-laboratory collaborations are especially important. Large, preregistered replication projects using identical hardware and analysis plans could determine whether small effects are reproducible across independent laboratories. Such studies should include both selected and unselected participants, allowing a prospectively specified test of whether participant characteristics moderate outcomes. Recruitment, screening, and selection procedures should be reported transparently.

Direct studies should also compare unidirectional and bidirectional intention tasks within the same experimental framework. This would clarify whether the larger point estimate observed for bidirectional tasks reflects a genuine task-related difference or differences in study design and reporting. Including sham conditions, blinded experimenters, automated randomization, and negativecontrol analyses would further strengthen causal inference.

For indirect or field-based studies, future protocols should define event-selection criteria, observation windows, primary outcomes, and statistical analyses before events occur whenever possible. Event registries, timestamped predictions, independent event adjudication, and transparent rules for handling overlapping or unexpected events would reduce concerns about retrospective selection. Analyses should distinguish clearly between confirmatory tests and exploratory follow-up analyses.

Finally, future meta-analyses should include prospectively registered null studies, and more detailed methodological coding. Sensitivity analyses for reporting bias, and assessment of study quality could provide a more informative estimate of the evidential value of the literature. Until such evidence is available, the present findings should be regarded as suggestive but insufficient to establish that human mental intention produces reliable physical deviations in RNG output.
