Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Effect Sizes in t-tests and F-tests works

The Effect Size module checks if effect sizes are correctly reported in t-tests and F-tests.

The Effect Size check searches for regular expressions that match typical ways in which effect sizes are reported. It subsequently checks different ways in which Cohen’s d, g, ηp2, and ωp2 can be computed against the reported value. If effects are missing, or might be incorrect, you the module provides a warning. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

While AI-conversational agents are increasingly integrated into behavioral and mental health interventions, standardized instruments to assess their performance remain scarce. To address this gap, we developed an instrument to measure user-subjective experience with AI-driven agents in the healthcare domain. We focused on user perception due to its direct contribution to intervention effects, and on healthcare given that the rapid adoption of AIagents in this domain continues to outpace the development of validated evaluation instruments.

Across four studies, we developed and tested the Chatbot User Evaluation instrument (CUE-Chat). First, we identified relevant assessment domains and items through literature review, existing instruments, input from AI-driven agents, and the research team's domain expertise. Following this, we requested feedback on draft subscales, definitions, and items from expert and laypeople panels. We then tested the structure of the instrument's concept version. Finally, we conducted a psychometric evaluation and finalized the instrument.

CUE-Chat consists of four factors: Perceived Efficacy (4 items), Usability (4 items), Intelligence (4 items), and Safety and Risk Management (4 items).

Reliability and validity of the total score and each subscale was good to excellent (Cronbach's α = .73-.92; Ω = .74-.93). Predictive validity was strong with all scales predictive of users' overall rating of an AI-agent's performance.

We conclude that CUE-Chat's strong psychometric properties are promising, and that it is particularly useful for researchers integrating AIdriven agents into behavioral and mental health interventions in both developmental and evaluative stages.

Introduction

The rapid development in Artificial Intelligence (AI) has catalyzed the use of AI-driven applications in behavioral and mental health promotion. As a result, AI-driven conversational agents are increasingly being integrated into behavioral and mental health interventions (Fu et al., 2026). These agents operate on Large Language Models (LLMs) built on deep learning architecture, through which they can interpret and generate messages in a conversational manner (Chen et al., 2025). Where previous agents were typically rule-based and operated on simple if-then logic, AI-driven agents can be developed based on therapeutic and behavior change techniques, such as motivational interviewing, goal setting, monitoring, and psychoeducation (Chen et al., 2025; Fu et al., 2026). The current research project aimed to develop an instrument that can assess how users experience AI-driven conversational agents implemented in the healthcare domain.

Assessing user perception is pivotal, because positive appraisal contributes to intervention effects and performance evaluations of user-agent interaction are important predictors of psychological outcomes. For example, when people interacted with an AI-driven agent representing their future self, users' subjective experiences, such as persuasiveness, realism and engagement, predicted psychological outcomes over more objective features, like visual embodiment or voice synthesis (Albrecht et al., 2025). Conversely, AI-agents are frequently criticized for delivering shallow, confusing, or brief interactions that occasionally generate inappropriate responses or fail to identify mental health crises, resulting in pronounced user dissatisfaction (Chen et al., 2025).

While the integration of AI-driven agents into health interventions continues to grow exponentially, standardized tools to assess user perception of these agents have not kept pace (Parks et al., 2025). Consequently, there is a gap in our understanding of users' perspectives on AI-agents in digital interventions regarding their subjective experience, preferences, and satisfaction. Bridging this gap allows for a deeper understanding of the user perspective and tailoring these agents to those in need of behavioral and/or mental support and, ultimately, for improving accessibility and effectiveness of digital mental healthcare (Chaudhry & Debi, 2024). To this end, we developed and validated a standardized instrument to assess user-subjective experiences with AI-agents integrated in behavioral and mental health interventions.

Why Standardized Assessment of User Experience of AI-Agents Matters

The lack of standardized instruments to evaluate user-subjective experiences of AI-agents creates both methodological and practical challenges.

Non-standardized evaluation tools produce disparate evaluations across studies making it difficult to compare agents and hindering knowledge synthesis through meta-analyses (Fitrianie et al., 2025; Hua et al., 2025). In the absence of validated tools, researchers and developers must rely on their intuition and ad-hoc feedback to guide iterative agent refinement (Mertens & Van Gelder, 2025). Validated tools are needed to detect counterproductive, inaccurate, or unsafe agent development prior to large-scale deployment, particularly among vulnerable populations.

Existing Frameworks for Evaluation of AI-agents

Research to date has mainly focused on agent design, technical performance, and operational effectiveness, often determined by expert opinion and intuition (Hua et al., 2025; Parks et al., 2025). For example, Radziwill and Benton's (2017) influential framework for evaluating the quality of AI-driven agents identifies three attributes: Efficiency (i.e., agent's performance), Effectiveness (i.e., agent's functionality and humanity), and Satisfaction (i.e., agent's level of affect, ethical behavior, and accessibility).

While these are important aspects that contribute to agent quality, they evaluate AI-agents purely from a technological perspective (Denecke et al., 2021). Especially in the healthcare domain-where there are often no 'correct' answers and success relies on evaluating thoughts and feelings rather than objective truths-perceived meaningfulness of an interaction and evaluation by users are also important to factor in when assessing the performance of agents (Parks et al., 2025).

Recently, initiatives were undertaken to develop frameworks specifically created for evaluating AI-agents in healthcare. These frameworks do take the user perspective into account. For example, the Health Care AI Chatbot Evaluation Framework (HAICEF; Hua et al., 2025) recognizes evaluation on the level of design and operational effectiveness, and adds the levels 1) trustworthiness and usefulness and 2) safety, privacy, and fairness.

Existing Instruments for Assessment of AI-agents

While evaluation frameworks like HAICEF call for domain-specific instruments to assess AI-driven agents, a validated tool specific to healthcare does not exist yet. The few existing questionnaires that aim to evaluate employed AI-agents were designed for use across domains, for application in domains other than healthcare, or for extensive assessments to gain a complete evaluation of an agent. More specifically, the Chatbot Usability Questionnaire (CUQ; Holmes et al., 2023) is a generic questionnaire that can be used across domains, but misses aspects important to healthcare, such as perceived usefulness and safety. The Chatbot Usability Scale (BUS-11) (Borsci & Schmettow, 2024) and the Questionnaire to Evaluate User Satisfaction in AI-Chatbots (Møller et al., 2025) were created to assess applications in the customer-support domain, resulting in a mismatch between item content and the healthcare domain, such as "the chatbot function was easily detectable" (BUS-11; Borsci & Schmettow, 2024) and "using the AI chatbot allows me to be more productive" (Questionnaire to Evaluate User Satisfaction in AI-Chatbots; Møller et al., 2025). Lastly, the Artificial Social Agents Questionnaire (ASAQ; Fitrianie et al., 2025) is also not focused on healthcare and contains 90 items to assess 24 different constructs. While it provides a thorough assessment of the agent, it is difficult to integrate lengthy questionnaires into an intervention evaluation study -even when it is considered a primary outcome. Although a shorter version of the ASAQ (24 items) exists, its reliance on single items to assess each of its 24 constructs brings challenges in terms of content validity and reliability.

Consequently, developing a standardized and brief instrument to evaluate AI-driven conversational agents embedded in digital healthcare tools is both necessary and timely. Healthcare-focused evaluation frameworks highlight the need for domain-specific assessment instruments for AIapplications in this context (Chen et al., 2025) with a specific focus on user perception (Parks et al., 2025). To address this gap, the current study presents the development and evaluation of a tailored evaluation instrument optimized for healthcare AI-agents, with a specific focus on user perceptions in behavioral and mental health interventions.

The Present Project

The goal of the present research project was to develop a brief psychometrically validated instrument to assess subjective experiences with AI-driven conversational agents in behavioral and mental health care. We specifically focused on 1) user perception, given its impact on intervention effects, and 2) the healthcare domain, where the exponential growth in the use of AI-agents has outpaced the development of validated assessment tools. Such a user-based and domain-specific approach fits well with the context of potentially vulnerable populations and sensitive topics for which the instrument might be used. Furthermore, this project focused on AI-driven chatbots that can be considered as a specific class of conversational agents activated by natural language input, even though the terms chatbot and conversational agents are often used interchangeably in the literature (Borsci et al., 2022; Radziwill & Benton, 2017).

The instrument, the Chatbot User Evaluation instrument (CUE-Chat), was developed across four studies. In Study 1, we identified relevant dimensions and formulated items based on a review of the literature and related instruments, input from AI-driven agents, and the research team's domain expertise. In Study 2, we refined the dimensions, definitions, and items based on feedback from expert and laypeople panels. In Study 3, we tested the structure of the concept version of the instrument in a general population sample after participants interacted with a chatbot. Finally, in Study 4, we psychometrically evaluated CUE-Chat in a general population sample after participants interacted with one of three chatbots.

CUE-Chat can be used in multiple stages of agent development, for example during user testing to assess user acceptance, during intervention piloting to identify areas for improvement, during intervention evaluation to gain insight into the perceived added value of the agent by users, and for comparing different agents.

Transparency and Openness

For all studies, the related data, analysis codes, research materials, and detailed information regarding the used AI-chatbots are available at the Open Science Framework (OSF; https://osf.io/hnv2d). The four studies were preregistered jointly (AsPredicted #261292; https://aspredicted.org/qx3jr4.pdf ). The collected information was analyzed using deductive thematic analysis (Proudfoot, 2023) to uncover potentially relevant dimensions to compose subscales of the instrument. First, all aspects (i.e., attributes) relevant to AI-chatbot performance mentioned in the literature, assessed in existing instruments, suggested by the AI-agents, and proposed by team members, were listed in tables per information source. Subsequently, related attributes were grouped together under broader categories. For example, the attributes "contextually relevant", "relevance", and "understanding context" were grouped under the broader category "relevance". Subsequently, these broader categories were then further grouped into larger domains (e.g., categories such as "relevance", "understanding input", and "coherence" were grouped under the domain "dialog"). Finally, the domains of all information sources were combined, and similar domains were merged into dimensions.

Identification and Clustering into Dimensions

Our search yielded 12 research articles on AI-chatbot and AI-agent performance testing, and nine papers describing relevant existing instruments.

We analyzed these together with the generated responses of three AI-agents (ChatGPT, Gemini, and CoPilot), and descriptions of performance dimensions from research team members. This resulted in the extraction of 203 attributes (95 attributes drawn from the literature search, 38 from existing instruments, 61 from responses of AI-agents, and 9 from team members). These attributes were first aggregated per data source into categories (107 categories in total) and then aggregated across sources into 31 broader categories. To decrease the number of categories, these were further aggregated into eight domains. These domains were, in turn, merged into three dimensions (see Supplemental Materials Study 1 for an overview of the deductive thematic analysis) and defined based on the attributes of each dimension: -Technical Quality = Capacity of the chatbot to sustain a contextually relevant, understandable, and meaningful dialog emulating the natural flow of human conversation (i.e., correctly interpreting complex user input; providing correct, relevant, consistent and contextually appropriate responses).

This includes extracted categories, such as language, conversational quality, intelligence, and user interface.

-User Experience = Chatbot functionality (e.g., functional error handling, intuitive) and emotional engagement (e.g., pleasant, useful and engaging interaction). This includes extracted categories, such as an affective component (establishing an emotional/personal connection), user engagement, and technical functioning.

-Safety and Risk Management = Security of shared information, reliability of the system (e.g., cloud infrastructure, availability), and ethical behavior of the chatbot. This includes categories such as safety, risk management, and trust and transparency.

Generating Candidate Items

Candidate items were formulated based on each dimension's underlying attributes. Notably, our literature analysis revealed an affective component-a perceived personal/emotional bond with the agent-that is unique to AI-agents in behavioral and mental health context. In the healthcare domain, fostering a "therapeutic alliance" characterized by a collaborative and beneficial relationship between users and agents (Chen et al., 2025) has been suggested to be important. By simulating empathy and emotional reciprocity, these interactions may shift from being perceived as purely instrumental to relational (Albrecht et al., 2025). Consequently, our instrument explicitly incorporated items capturing this affective dimension.

Pilot of the First Set of Candidate Items

In total, 41 items were formulated. This first set of candidate items was piloted among a sample of the general population via Prolific (N = 49, M age = 22.35 years, SD = 2.07, n male = 25, 51%) who interacted with a chatbot and subsequently responded to the candidate items, among other questionnaires assessing psychological wellbeing. This chatbot ran on the Llama LLM (see for details the Technical Documentation on OSF) and was prompted to represent users' 10-year older self as this paradigm has shown to be an effective intervention mechanism in the behavioral and mental health domain to stimulate future orientation (e.g., Van Gelder et al., 2026).

Principal Axis Factoring with oblique rotation-as factors were expected to correlate-resulted in the deletion of 10 items based on communalities (< .50), factor loadings (< .40), and face validity (see for details the logbook of Study 1 on OSF). Most of these items represented aspects of a therapeutical setting, such as asking follow-up questions, depth of the conversation, and feeling comfortable to share feelings.

Final Changes to Candidate Items

After reviewing the identified attributes and categories again, nine items were added to the candidate items list. Seven of these items represented attributes listed under the dimension Safety and Risk Management. Although this dimension is stressed in the research literature as especially important for the healthcare domain given the potential of sensitive information being shared in conversations (e.g. Denecke et al., 2021), only few items of the first set addressed this domain. The other two added items focused on ease of use of the chatbot. One of these items ("The chatbot was easy to use.") was added again after initially being deleted; although its communality (.31) and factor loading (.44) were low, it was often mentioned in the literature and therefore deserved further investigation. The other item was new. The final list consisted of 40 candidate items.

Discussion of Study 1

After an extensive review of the results of four different searchapproaches, we aggregated 203 attributes into three dimensions that have potential to function as meaningful subscales of the instrument under development. Additionally, we generated an informed list of 40 candidate items for our chatbot-evaluation instrument. Given that our aim was to develop a brief instrument, these 40 items provided a solid base for selecting the most appropriate items.

Analyses

We calculated the Content Validity Index (CVI), to obtain an indication of the relevance and clarity of each subscale and item from the perspective of the two panels, and the Content Validity Ratio (CVR) for an indication of how essential each item is for the instrument according to both panels. The CVI was calculated by dividing the number of participants scoring the subscale/item a 4 or 5 on relevance/clarity by the total sample size. A CVI above .79 was interpreted as good (Madadizadeh & Bahariniya, 2023). CVR was determined based on the top 10 of items that each participant selected, where the 10 selected items were coded as "necessary" (= 1) and the other 30 items as "unnecessary" (= 0). CVR was calculated with the formula: CVR = (n e -(N/2))/(N/2), where n e represents the number of participants that selected that item as necessary (Madadizadeh & Bahariniya, 2023).

The results of the two panels were compared both quantitatively and qualitatively. For quantitative comparison, we assessed the interrater agreement on the CVI and CVR by calculating the Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC). For qualitative comparison, we compared the extent to which the selection of items based on the CVI and CVR would be similar across the panels.

Results

Quantitative data

The expert panel rated the three subscales as relevant (CVI range = .86-.93) but also as somewhat unclear (CVI range = .64-.71) and definitions as requiring improvement (CVI range = .50-.79). Eleven (28%) items were rated as both irrelevant (CVI range = .50-.79) and unclear (CVI range = .50-.79), 15 (38%) were rated as irrelevant (CVI range = .43-.79) though clear, and 4 (10%) were rated as unclear (CVI range = .71-.79) though relevant. Especially items from the subscale Technical Quality (n = 7; 41%) were rated as both irrelevant and unclear. Items from the subscale User Experience were generally rated as irrelevant though clear (n = 11; 69%), whereas items from the subscale Safety and Risk Management were rated as relevant though unclear (n = 3; 43%). Ranking items based on CVR showed that 13 items of the subscale Technical Quality, 4 items of User Experience, and 3 items of Safety and Risk Management composed the top 20 items.

The laypeople panel rated all three subscales as relevant (CVI range = .88-.96) and clear (CVI range = .84-.88) with good definitions (CVI range = .82-.86). Of the items, 22 (55%) were rated as both irrelevant (CVI range = .38-.75) and unclear (CVI range = .49-.79), 1 (3%) was rated as irrelevant (CVI = .74) though clear, and 2 (5%) were rated as relevant though unclear (CVI = .79 and .77). Items of the subscale User Experience in particular scored as irrelevant and unclear (n = 12; 75%). Item ranking based on CVR indicated that the top 20 items consisted of 11 items from the subscale Technical Quality, 6 items from User Experience, and 3 from Safety and Risk Management.

Interrater agreement between the panels was poor regarding both the rating of relevance (ICC = .23) and of clarity (ICC = .30). Disagreements were most profound on ratings of item clarity of the User Experience subscale and on both relevance and clarity of the Safety and Risk Management subscale items. Experts rated multiple items of the subscale User Experience as irrelevant though clear, where laypeople rated these as both irrelevant and unclear. Items of the subscale Safety and Risk Management were more often rated relevant though unclear by the experts, whereas laypeople rated these as irrelevant and unclear.

Qualitative data

Qualitative feedback overlapped substantially between the panels.

Regarding the subscale Technical Quality, the panels indicated a mismatch between the label of the scale, which implied a focus on the technological part of a chatbot, and the definition which focused on communication of the chatbot with the user. The subscale User Experience was believed to require a more elaborate definition and seemed to capture two separate constructs. Both panels suggested splitting this subscale into an affective component and a user satisfaction component. Additionally, the expert panel noticed an overlap between the definition of this subscale and that of the Technical Quality subscale. Concerning the subscale Safety and Risk Management, both panels indicated a need to further define "ethical behavior". Regarding items, panels suggested adding items, for example on personalization, transparency, usability, and usefulness.

Discussion of Study 2

Although the level of agreement between the experts and laypeople panel was low regarding the quantitative ratings of relevance and clarity, the substantial overlap in the qualitative feedback provided useful insights for improvement of the subscales, definitions, and items. More specifically, the subscale Technical Quality was relabeled as Conversational Quality to emphasize the focus on the relevance and understandability of the dialog with user and chatbot. Furthermore, User Experience was split into two separate dimensions: 1) Emotional Responsiveness, reflecting affective attitudes toward a chatbot -related to therapeutic alliance-and 2) Perceived Helpfulness, capturing the extent to which users perceived the interaction as able to help them and meet their needs.

Regarding the items, 19 were discarded (of which 8 were judged irrelevant and unclear, 10 as irrelevant, and 1 as unclear by the expert panel, and 15 were judged irrelevant and unclear by the laypeople panel) and 9 new items were added based on the panels' feedback. Both panels rated negatively phrased items consistently lower on relevance, clarity, and necessity than positively phrased items. Hence, negatively phrased items (11 items) were removed, except for two items: "I felt that some messages of the chatbot were harmful for me" and "I experienced technical errors during the conversation" -which was simplified into "I experienced technical errors". Additionally, one of the new items was negatively phrased, i.e., "The chatbot responded inappropriately". These items were retained/added as there were no positively phrased items that assessed similar aspects.

Study 3: Exploring the structure of the adjusted instrument

Method

Procedure

To explore the psychometric properties of CUE-Chat, participants first interacted with an AI-chatbot and then completed the iterated version of CUE-Chat and related questionnaires. Note that we deviated here from the preregistration; for feasibility reasons, all participants interacted with the same chatbot instead of different chatbots. The chatbot was an iteration of the one used to pilot the items in Study 1. It used the Llama LLM, was prompted to represent users' 10-year older self, and ran on protected serves (see for details the Technical Documentation on OSF). Participants were recruited from Prolific, directed via Qualtrics to the AI-chatbot, and instructed to interact with the chatbot for 10 minutes with the task to 1) find out more about their daily life 10 years from now, and 2) ask about steps they can take now in order to become that potential future self (see for details the codebook on OSF). After 10 minutes, the chatbot provided a standardized message terminating the interaction and requesting participants to go back to Qualtrics to complete the questionnaires.

Participants

Analyses

To determine the appropriate number of factors to extract, we used four different methods. First, we conducted Parallel analysis to compare eigenvalues obtained from the observed data with those of a randomly generated dataset. Factors with eigenvalues larger than those created with a random dataset were retained (Horn, 1965). Second, we generated a Scree plot to determine the number of factors to retain, also based on eigenvalues, by inspecting where the slope levels off (Cattell, 1966). Third, we applied the Hull method that balances model fit and number of parameters when fitting different models in order to retrieve only major factors (Lorenzo-Seva et al., 2011). Fourth, we used the Very Simple Structure that compares the original correlation matrix with a simplified factor matrix using solely the largest absolute loading of each variable, and then compares the fit of the simplified model to the original one (Revelle & Rocklin, 1979). Next, we performed an Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA) extracting the number of factors as suggested by the results of the previous four methods, using oblique factor analysis with promax rotation as we assumed the factors were correlated.

After determining the number of factors to extract, we conducted analyses on item-level for informed item culling. First, we inspected the factor loadings of items. Loadings below .30 were deemed inadequate (Carpenter, 2018). Second, we examined the variance (i.e., standard deviation) of each item, as variance predicts item's self-other agreement and retest reliability, with a high variance preferred (De Vries et al., 2016). Third, we tested convergent validity (i.e., empirical relation between construct and other theoretically related constructs) by inspecting items' correlations with corresponding constructs assessed with validated scales. Relatedly, we tested discriminant validity (i.e., extent to which a construct differentiates from other constructs) by inspecting item correlations with other dimensions assessed with validated scales. Ideally, items have high convergent correlations and low discriminant correlations (Yang & Yao, 2014). Based on these criteria, the top scoring items per subscale were qualitatively compared to the best scoring items based on panel feedback. Based on this overview, the number of items were systematically reduced to a maximum of 6 per subscale. The resulting factor structure was tested in a preliminary Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA). Sensitivity analyses were conducted for all these analyses, excluding participants who failed one or both attention checks in the questionnaire (e.g., "to check if you are paying attention, please select "strongly disagree").

Discussion of Study 3

The chosen factor structure roughly followed the same structure as the one proposed a priori. However, while the panels suggested splitting the original factor User Experience (Study 1) into a factor representing emotional responsiveness and a factor focused on perceived helpfulness, the data suggested splitting this factor into perceived efficacy (representing both emotional responsiveness and helpfulness) and usability of the chatbot. Given that the panels highlighted the importance of efficacy in the qualitative feedback and the term usability was often mentioned as an attribute, we decided to follow the suggestion of the data and created a subscale representing perceived efficacy and a subscale assessing usability. Furthermore, we relabeled the Conversational Quality subscale after item selection into Intelligence, as the remaining items mainly focused on the chatbot's ability to interpret input, remember previous information, and generate relevant responses.

The items measuring safety and risk management were clustered under the factor representing perceived efficacy and did not correlate with existing subscales assessing related aspects. However, the existing subscales assessing safety and risk management only contained items on privacy issues, confidentiality, misuse and data protection, which did correlate with CUE-Chat item on sharing personal information, but not with the other items that assessed other aspects such as honesty, transparency, and bias of the chatbot.

Nevertheless, we decided to retain a separate subscale representing safety and risk management based on theory and interpret the lack of correlations with existing subscales as due to the latter's narrow focus.

Taken together, the adapted version of the instrument consisted of four subscales and 21 items: 1) Perceived Efficacy (6 items), 2) Usability (6 items), 3) Intelligence (4 items), and 4) Risk and Safety Management (5 items) (for subscale definitions, see Table 1). Participants were recruited via Prolific, redirected to one of the three AIchatbots via Qualtrics and interacted for 10 minutes with it. Participants were given the goal "to walk 10,000 steps per day" and tasked to come up with a first step toward achieving this via the chatbot-interaction (for details see codebook on OSF). After 10 minutes, the chatbot sent a standardized message asking them to return to the Qualtrics questionnaire where they completed CUE-Chat and related questionnaires.

A power analysis based on the factor loadings of Study 3 indicated that 141 participants were needed for 90% power testing for global model fit based on an RMSEA of 0.05 or higher. Discussion of Study 4

The psychometric evaluation shows that CUE-Chat is a promising tool.

More specifically, it can reliably measure users' perceived efficacy, usability, intelligence, and safety and risk management of an AI-chatbot across different types of chatbots. The subscales showed good reliability, and validity by correlating with related constructs, showing weaker associations with unrelated constructs, and predicting users' overall rating of chatbot performance. Discriminant validity of the subscales distinguishing between constructs assessed by other subscales of the instrument was less strong, which is to be expected given the strong correlations between the four factors in the CFA model; when a chatbot understands users' inputs and provides relevant responses, this will be related to users perceiving the chatbot as easy to use and helpful. Taken together, CUE-Chat, consisting of four subscales with four items each, appears to be a valid instrument.

The current version of CUE-Chat can be considered as a solid starting point for further research on this standardized assessment instrument. It is important to replicate its structure, reliability and validity, in different settings, and among various AI-agents and diverse populations.

General Discussion

The exponential increase in the use of AI-driven conversational agents in behavioral and mental health interventions outpaces the development of instruments to assess these agents. To address this gap, we developed an instrument to measure user-subjective experiences with AI-agents that are integrated into healthcare interventions. We opted for a user-centered approach, as user perceptions seem to affect intervention effects over agents' objective features (Albrecht et al., 2025). A better understanding of users' experiences, preferences, and satisfaction can facilitate tailoring these AIagents to better serve those in need of behavioral and/or mental support (Chaudhry & Debi, 2024).

Strengths and Limitations

A strength of the current study is the thorough approach of the instrument's development through a multi-method and a multi-study approach. We based our instrument on a solid foundation of scientific literature, processed feedback from experts as well as laypeople, piloted our draft instrument and confirmed its structure among a new sample.

Nevertheless, some limitations need to be acknowledged. First, although we tested for measurement invariance of the instrument across three different AI-chatbots, replication with other AI-chatbots and agents is needed. There are large differences between AI-chatbots and agents in the healthcare domain, not only in their way of communicating and functioning but also in their training and assigned role. Although the terms "chatbot" and "conversational agent" are generally used interchangeably in the literature (Borsci et al., 2022; Radziwill & Benton, 2017), chatbots can be seen as a specific class of conversational agents. Therefore, it is important to continue assessing the psychometric qualities of CUE-Chat among a diverse range of AI-chatbots and test whether the findings can be extended to AI-agents in general.

Second, the instrument requires further evaluation among diverse samples. In the current study, it was evaluated among convenience samples 14. I felt comfortable sharing personal information with the chatbot. 15. The chatbot was transparent about its limitations.

Items were assessed on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = Strongly disagree to5 = Strongly agree) and systematically mixed, presenting one item of each scale in blocks of four. Although this increases the cognitive burden, it also reduces response bias and inflated reliability of subscales related to question order effects, which is especially relevant in the absence of negatively phrased items (Shimada & Katahira, 2023). The ChatBot Usability Scale (BUS-11; Borsci & Schmettow, 2024) assesses user's satisfaction with the experience of using chatbots and conversational agents for information retrieval. The User Engagement Scale (UES; O'Brien et al., 2018) measures four dimensions of user engagement (i.e., Focused attention, Perceived usability, Aesthetic appeal, and Reward) and consists of 30 items (e.g., "I felt in control while using this chatbot") assessed on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = Strongly disagree to5 = Strongly agree). The subscale Perceived privacy risk of the Questionnaire to Evaluate User Satisfaction in AI-Chatbots (Møller et al., 2025) was used to measure user concerns about information misuse. The subscale contains three items (e.g., "I trust the AI-chatbot protects my information") rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1 Strongly disagree to5 = Strongly agree).

How to Use CUE-Chat CUE-Chat can be used by researchers, programmers, designers and other stakeholders to evaluate how users perceive their AI-driven conversational agent implemented in a healthcare setting. With "users" we refer to the target population of an intervention in the healthcare domain, which can be people from general and (sub)clinical populations as well as healthcare professionals. While this instrument was evaluated among the general population, we believe that its use can be extended to a broad range of users. Moreover, CUE-Chat is primarily developed to evaluate AI-agents integrated in behavioral and mental health interventions but may also be applicable to the broader domain of healthcare. Scores on the instrument can provide useful insights during both the development of an AI-agent and its evaluation. In developmental phases, it can give information on opportunities for optimalization of the agent to improve users' perception of its performance. The scores on the subscales show, for example, whether all dimensions of an agent are evaluated positively by users or whether some (unintentionally) receive low scores and require further development. In evaluation phases, it can shed light on the agent's added value within the larger intervention program as experienced by users. It provides a deeper understanding of the extent to which users are satisfied with the interactions and perceive these as helpful. Furthermore, this standardized instrument provides the opportunity to compare user-subjective experience across AI-agents. The instrument can be applied in its entirety, or selected subscale(s) can be used. The calculation of a total score is facilitated by its balanced subscales (each consisting of four items) providing a score for an agent's overall performance as experienced by users. The good psychometric properties of the separate subscales allow also the use of separate subscales. The most relevant subscales for a certain study or agent can be selected to gain the needed from the Prolific platform. Although these samples are similar to the general population, and thus to a prevention population, they may differ from other populations in the healthcare domain such as (sub)clinical and healthcare professional samples. Although we have no reason to assume that the instrument cannot be applied among different populations, it is important to psychometrically evaluate CUE-Chat across diverse populations. Third, the literature review in Study 1 was not based on a systematic literature review, but through searching keywords, snowballing techniques, and forward and backward citation searches. Although these are widely applied search techniques, a systematic review would have simplified replication attempts and may have created a more comprehensive base. Nonetheless, we believe to have exhausted the search due to the multi-method approach, and the iterative process of knowledge acquiring. Conclusion CUE-Chat is a promising tool for assessing user-subjective experience with AI-driven conversational agents in the healthcare domain. The instrument can be used in multiple stages of the development and evaluation of AI-agents integrated in behavioral and mental health interventions. In early developmental stages, it helps to assess how the agent is received by users and identify opportunities for improvements. In later stages, it provides insight into the user-perceived added value of the agent in an intervention program. Consequently, CUE-Chat is especially relevant for researchers, designers, programmers, and other stakeholders aiming to integrate AI-agents into interventions. By capturing multiple domains of the user-subjective experience through a concise set of items, it can easily be combined with other measurements, rendering it both psychometrically valid and practically workable. Bridging rigorous psychometric evaluation with assessment needs, the CUE-Chat equips researchers and other stakeholders with a tool to guide the next generation of AI-driven conversational agents in healthcare.
