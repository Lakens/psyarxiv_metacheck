What we found

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Healthy participants were randomised to 50%-N2O (n=80) or medical-air (n=40) and completed the Psychotomimetic States Inventory (PSI) before and during gas inhalation.

Informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to any study procedure.

Inclusion criteria for participants (n=120) were: self-reported absence of contra-indications, age 18-40 years, fluency in English and (corrected-to-)normal vision and hearing.

Participants were ineligible if they self-reported a historical/current psychiatric disorder sufficiently severe to require treatment, any respiratory condition (e.g., asthma or recent pneumothorax), or B12/folate deficiency.

Participants were blinded to treatment through the use of identical demand valves, mouthpieces and hoses and concealment of gas cylinders using opaque sleeves.

Upon arrival, participants provided written informed consent and completed demographic and baseline (trait) measures, followed by the first set of state questionnaires (pre-inhalation; T1).

After a period of equilibration (~5 min), participants completed the state measures for the second time (peri-inhalation; T2), followed by the WNT.

One hour after inhalation ended, participants were required to successfully complete a series of field sobriety tests before being deemed ‘fit-to-leave’.

They were debriefed before departure.

As such, despite all true speech signals being affectively neutral, participants had four response options per trial: 1) no speech, 2) neutral speech, 3) positive speech or 4) negative speech via a keyboard response.

Prior to inhalation, participants were asked to complete two practice trials to ensure appropriate understanding of the task.

During the WTN, participants provided an additional response if they perceived speech (i.e., responded with options 2-4 above), the question ‘Did you hear your name being spoken?’ appeared on the screen along with response options (‘Y’ or ‘N’).

Speech illusions
The analytic strategy for speech illusions will depend upon the distributional properties of the data which is currently unknown:
• If the rate of speech illusions (i.e., false positive responses) is low, it may be most appropriate to treat this outcome as binary (i.e., presence versus absence of false positive responses). In this case, the probability of experiencing speech illusions will be analysed using generalised linear modelling with drug condition as the main predictor variable .
• If the experience of speech illusions is more frequent, we will use generalised linear modelling for count data, using either (zero-inflated) Poisson or (zero-inflated) negative binomial link functions, again using drug condition as the main predictor variable. The appropriateness of the distribution type to the data will be determined (e.g. using the countfit function in Stata).
• The role of additional predictor variables, such as trait schizotypy and state psychotomimesis, will be assessed in the binary/count models in order to assess their influence on the rate of speech illusions, or the probability of experiencing a speech illusion.


Affective and self-referential speech illusions
Given the likely low rates of affective and self-referential speech illusions (i.e., responding ‘I heard positive speech’ or ‘I heard negative speech’ and hearing own name), both outcomes will most likely be treated as binary (i.e., present versus absent). We will use generalised linear modelling with drug condition (N2O versus medical air) as the fixed component and participant as the random component. As above, the explanatory value of additional predictor variables such as state psychotomimesis and trait schizotypy will also be explored.

To analyse the effect of N2O on alpha and gamma power, we will use a repeated measures ANOVA with timepoint (T1-T3) as a within-subjects factor and drug condition (N2O versus medical air) as the between-subjects factor.

Using appropriate regression models, we will also explore the relationships between alpha power at T2 and the experience of false percepts in the speech illusion task, as well as between gamma power at T2 and PSI scores at T2.
research_questions Primary hypotheses

This study is part of a larger programme of research examining the effects of nitrous oxide (N2O) on cognitive functioning, memory formation and subjective experience. This pre-registration focuses on research questions relating to the psychotomimetic effects of N2O. Although all variables collected as part of the larger project will be listed below, only those of direct relevance to the current set of research questions will be described in detail. Separate preregistrations will be submitted relating to distinct research questions (e.g., the effects of N2O on intrusive memories - see osf.io/2hnfu).

Nitrous oxide is an NMDA receptor antagonist. In addition to overlapping clinical effects (e.g. dissociative anaesthesia; rapid antidepressant effects), it shares a number of molecular pharmacological characteristics with the prototypical NMDAR antagonist used in humans, ketamine (Izumi et al, 2022). Both drugs also have pro-opioidergic effects. The main aims of this study include determining the extent to which N2O performs as a ketamine-like psychotomimetic in healthy volunteers with no history of psychosis. This will be investigated using self-report measures and a behavioural index of psychosis-like effects (speech illusion performance). In addition, we will explore some of the relationships between (N2O-induced) dissociation and psychosis-like phenomena that have previously been reported in clinical groups with symptoms of psychosis.

RQ1: N2O administration has previously been shown to be associated with increased self-reports of state psychotomimesis and state dissociation, compared to medical air (Piazza et al., 2022). We hypothesise that, alongside this increase in psychotomimesis and dissociation (replicating Piazza et al, 2022), N2O administration will also be associated with a behavioural measure of psychotomimesis, namely an increased occurrence of anomalous perceptual experiences, as assessed by performance on the speech illusion task (i.e., reporting hearing a voice in noise-only trials), in comparison with medical air. We will test whether the occurrence of such perceptual anomalies is associated with the self-reported measures of state psychotomimesis and trait schizotypy.

RQ2: Ketamine, another dissociative anaesthetic with similar pharmacological actions, has been shown to produce alterations in resting-state neuronal oscillations at a range of frequency bands. For example, ketamine has been found to produce decreases in alpha-band oscillations (Vlisides et al., 2017; de la Salle et al., 2016), and increases in gamma-band oscillations (Rivolta et al., 2015; Shaw et al., 2015). Both of these alterations have also been found in individuals with psychosis (Haaf et al., 2018; Hamm et al. 2012; Tikka et al., 2014). We will examine whether the administration of N2O is associated with similar changes in resting-state oscillations, namely a decrease in alpha power and an increase in gamma power during the peri-inhalation period, in comparison to medical air. Tests of this hypothesis may be disseminated in a separate publication.


Exploratory aspects

a. In addition to our primary hypotheses, we will explore whether N2O administration is associated with a tendency to attribute affective salience to a falsely perceived signal (i.e., identifying voice illusions as affectively positive or negative versus neutral) in the speech illusion task. We will also explore whether N2O is associated with a tendency to report false self-referential percepts (i.e., hearing one’s own name in any trial) during the speech illusion task, and whether either of these biases (i.e., tendency to attribute affective or self-referential meaning to perceived signal) is associated with state psychotomimesis and/or trait schizotypy.

b. We will examine the relationship between childhood trauma and sensitivity to N2O’s dissociative effects (as well as other subjective effects, e.g. liking). Multiple separate lines of evidence suggest that N2O’s effects might vary according to the presence or extent of childhood adversity. Firstly, a history of childhood trauma is associated with a habitual tendency to have dissociative experiences in adulthood (e.g., as measured using the dissociative experiences scale; Pearce et al., 2017). Here, we will examine whether change in state dissociation is similarly a marker of childhood trauma. Secondly, sensitivity to the antidepressant effects of ketamine appears to be moderated by childhood adversity (O’Brien et al., 2019), although this has not yet been examined in studies of N2O’s antidepressant effects. Finally, adults with a history of childhood adversity show an increased sensitivity to opioids. Given that N2O’s (nociceptive) effects are thought to be partially mediated through opioid system activation (Fujinaga & Maze, 2002), moderation of sensitivity to N2O’s subjective effects by childhood adversity may similarly be observed here.

c. We will therefore explore whether childhood trauma is also a reliable predictor of sensitivity to N2O-induced (state) dissociation, using the self-report version of the Clinician Administered Dissociative Symptoms Scale (CADSS) and to reinforcing effects (liking and wanting) using the Drug Effects Questionnaire (Morean et al, 2013). We will also investigate whether both of the two factors of the CADSS (depersonalisation and derealisation) are associated with childhood trauma.

d. Alpha-band oscillations have previously been found to be associated with the experience of perceptual illusions (Lange et al., 2014). We will thus explore whether resting-state alpha power at T2 is associated with the frequency of false positives in the speech illusion task.

e. Previous research has found that resting-state gamma power in individuals with psychosis is positively associated with the severity of positive symptoms (Andreou et al., 2015; Uhlhaas & Singer, 2010). We will investigate whether gamma power at T2 shows a similar association with self-reported state psychotomimesis (i.e. PSI scores at T2). The EEG data may be fully or partially published in a separate report.

1. Screening
Potential participants responding to advertisements will be sent information about the study and invited to complete a preliminary online screening questionnaire. If eligible based on inclusion criteria above, a follow-up telephone screening interview will be conducted in which all additional eligibility criteria will be evaluated.

2. Main experimental session: Day 1
Upon arrival at the testing session, participants will be asked to provide written informed consent. Participants will complete the listed cognitive, physiological, and behavioural assessments in a fixed order. Experimenter interaction with participants is constrained to relatively limited set of ‘manualised’/scripted responses in order to limit between participant variability in expectancy and response to experimental material and drug. The following measures will be taken on Day 1:

2.1. Self-report measures
All participants will be asked to complete the following questionnaires about past and current mood, cognitive and emotional state, recorded directly onto a computer-based survey tool (Qualtrics Provo, UT).

2.1.1. ‘Trait’ (baseline) measures
• Depression, Anxiety and Stress Scale (DASS-21; Lovibond & Lovibond, 1995)
• Emotion Regulation Questionnaire (ERQ-9; Spaapen et al., 2014)
• Relationships Questionnaire (RQ; Bartholomew & Horowitz, 1991)
• Brief Experiential Avoidance Questionnaire (BEQ-15; Gámez et al., 2014)

• Detachment and Compartmentalization Inventory (DCI-22; Butler et al., 2019)
• Oxford-Liverpool Inventory of Feelings and Experiences, Short Form (sO-LIFE; Mason et al., 2005)
• Nevada Inner Experience Questionnaire (NIEQ; Heavey et al., 2019)
• Varieties of Inner Speech Questionnaire – Revised (VISQ-R; Alderson-Day et al., 2018)
• Timeline Follow-Back Questionnaire about alcohol use for the previous week
• Lifetime nitrous oxide use

2.1.2. Repeated ‘state’ measures (experimental session)
The purpose of repeated state measures is to assess changes in subjective state resulting from drug inhalation and ‘recovery’ following drug inhalation. As such, state measures will be taken at: T1 (baseline, i.e., pre-inhalation), T2 (peri-inhalation), T3 (post-inhalation) and T4 (final check of recovery, at least 40 minutes after stopping inhalation: DEQ, BAES and Similarity to Alcohol). The following questionnaires will be administered at the indicated timepoints:
• Positive and Negative Affect Scale, Short Form (PANAS-SF, T1-T3 only; Thompson, 2007)
• Clinician-Administered Dissociative Sates Scale (CADSS, T1-T3 only; Bremner et al., 1998)
• Psychotomimetic States Inventory (PSI, T1-T3 only; Mason et al., 2008)
• Drug Effects Questionnaire (DEQ, T1-T4; Fraser et al., 1961)
• Biphasic Alcohol Effects Scale (BAES, T1-T4; Martin et al., 1993)
• Similarity to alcohol (i.e., ‘How many alcoholic drinks does it feel like you have consumed?’ and ‘How similar to alcohol intoxication does your current state feel?’, T1-T4)

2.1.3. Memory measures
Acute involuntary memories
Note: this part of the procedure is described in greater detail in a separate pre-registration but is outlined here for transparency and completeness.

On Day 1, following the end of the film and a further 5-minute break, acute involuntary intrusions (both self-caught, and probed) will be recorded for 20 minutes, while participants complete a reading task intended to produce ‘task unrelated cognitive activity’ (i.e., mind-wandering). Participants will be instructed to read the text provided (which consists of five separate articles) and press the spacebar whenever they catch themselves thinking about the film (self-caught intrusion). Throughout the task, participants will also be presented with auditory probes (i.e., a 440Hz tone, 0.5s in duration) at random intervals, which will signal the presentation of the question ‘Were you thinking about the film just now? 0 – No, 1 – Yes’ until a response key is pressed (probed intrusion). Probes will be presented at random intervals ranging between 40-80 seconds. Spontaneous and probed responses will be dependent such that the inter-probe interval will reset after each spontaneous response.

A reading task was chosen for monitoring acute intrusions in order to standardise activity and cognitive load during the critical post-film period. The inclusion of probed and self-caught memory measures was chosen in order to assess participants’ ability to monitor own involuntary memories (i.e., meta-awareness; Baird et al., 2013).

2.2. Biological and physiological assessment

2.2.1. Blood pressure
Blood pressure readings (Automatic Blood Pressure Monitor, Boots Pharmaceuticals, UK) will be taken at T1, T2 and T3.

2.2.2. Heart rate variability
Heart rate will be monitored continuously via a Bodyguard 2 unit (Firstbeat, Jyväskylä, Finland) attached via two Ag/AgCl electrodes on either side of the midline across the chest. The Bodyguard 2 device acquires heart rate at a sampling rate of 1000Hz and internally calculates the duration of the interval between successive heartbeats (RR interval, ms units). This output forms the basis of the analysis of heart rate variability (HRV).

2.2.3. Electroencephalography (EEG)
EEG will be recorded using a 64-channel system (AntNeuro eego sports) arranged according to the 10-20 system. Participants will be fitted with the EEG cap at the beginning of the laboratory session, and it will remain in place until the end of this session. EEG data will only be collected for n = 60 participants (n = 40 for nitrous oxide and n = 20 for placebo).

Resting state measures will be recorded as a series of 6-minute periods at T1, T2 and T3. Each 6-minute period will consist of two 3-minute blocks, during which participants will be instructed to remain relaxed and either open (first 3-minute block) or close (second 3-minute block) their eyes. EEG will also be recorded continuously throughout the inhalation period, which includes the film, and the speech illusion task.

2.3. Speech illusion task

To assess the experience of auditory illusions, we will use a hearing task based on the white noise speech illusion task used by Galdos and colleagues (Galdos et al., 2011). The task will consist of two types of trials, namely ‘signal absent’ (50%), which are audio clips consisting of steady state speech shaped noise (SSN), and ‘signal present’ (50%), which are audio clips consisting of SSN plus human speech. All audio stimuli will be 2s in duration. SSN was chosen for this task instead of the commonly used white noise due to greater overlap between the spectra of SSN and human speech, thus potentially making the speech more difficult to discern to the listener (Faramarzi et al., 2021). ‘Signal present’ trial stimuli were constructed by embedding 1s-long snippets of speech in SSN. These snippets were taken from English audiobooks and consisted of speech from natural voices of four female and four male speakers. All audio stimuli were kindly provided by Faramarzi et al. (2021). The audio stimuli will be presented through noise-cancelling headphones.

In total, the task consists of 60 trials. Each trial begins with the presentation of the word ‘Listen’ on the screen. After a 200ms delay, the audio stimulus is played, followed by a 500ms presentation of a blank screen, after which the response options are shown. Participants are instructed to respond by pressing the corresponding number on the keyboard for one of the four options: 1) I heard no speech, 2) I heard neutral speech, 3) I heard positive speech and 4) I heard negative speech. If participants respond with option 1 (I heard no speech), the next trial begins after a 500ms presentation of a blank screen. If participants respond with any of the other three options, the question ‘Did you hear your name being spoken? is presented, and participants are again instructed to respond by pressing a key corresponding to Yes/No. The keyboard press is then followed by a 500ms presentation of a blank screen and the start of the next trial. Before the start of the main speech illusion task (and before start of inhalation, T2), participants will be asked to complete two practice trials: one signal-absent and one signal-present trial. The latter will consist of an audio clip of SSN with an audio clip of the participant’s first name (converted from text to speech using the Google Speech Client) embedded.


2.4. Drug administration
Entonox (nitrous oxide 50%, premixed with 50% oxygen) or medical air (BOC) will be self-administered by the participants via a demand valve. Participants will be instructed to begin inhalation after T1 measures and to stop inhalation immediately after the end of the film.

2.5. Trauma film
Participants will watch the stressful film (~14 minutes) which contains two scenes from the film ‘Irréversible’ (Studio Canal+, France, 2002). Participants will be warned in advance (verbally during telephone screening, and prior to the start of inhalation, as well as in the information sheet provided in advance of, and at the start of the lab session) that the film contains highly graphic scenes of interpersonal and sexual violence. Each scene will be preceded by a voiceover describing the context of the scenes. The voiceover preceding the second scene links the two depicted events, ensuring a single coherent narrative. Participants will view the film in a darkened lab, with audio presented through noise-cancelling headphones.

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Hallucinations are a core symptom of psychosis. Predictive processing accounts suggest that hallucinations arise from an imbalance between sensory evidence and prior expectations. Given the role of glutamatergic dysfunction in psychosis, the NMDA receptor antagonist nitrous oxide (N2O) may provide a model for investigating hallucinatory experiences. This pre-registered study (https://osf.io/4jhsx) examined the effects of N2O on subjective and behavioural indices of psychosis-like (hallucinatory) phenomena. Healthy participants were randomised to 50%-N2O (n=80) or medical-air (n=40) and completed the Psychotomimetic States Inventory (PSI) before and during gas inhalation. False alarms, hits, and exploratory signal detection indices from the white noise (speech illusions) task were compared between N2O and medical-air. Mediation of drug effects on canonical signal detection metrics -perceptual sensitivity and response bias -via change in psychotomimesis (∆PSI) and sedation was also examined. N2O administration was associated with fewer hits but no change in false alarms, reflected in reduced perceptual sensitivity and more conservative response bias. Larger ∆PSI was associated with more false alarms in the N2O group; ∆PSI (particularly positive psychotomimesis; ∆PSI-Positive) also mediated the effect of drug condition on perceptual sensitivity, while sedation and negative psychotomimesis did not have a reliable indirect role. Susceptibility to NMDA receptor antagonist-induced anomalous perceptual experiences and deficits may rely on or be preceded by subjective psychotomimetic effects. However, the unexpected pattern of reduced perceptual sensitivity and conservative response bias under N2O challenges the notion that reduced perceptual sensitivity following NMDA-receptor antagonism would necessarily lead to hallucination-like responding.

INTRODUCTION

Auditory verbal hallucinations (AVH) are a hallmark symptom of psychosis. Although highly prevalent in schizophrenia [1], hallucinations are widely viewed as a dimensional phenomenon which also occur in psychologically healthy individuals as auditory 'illusions' due to temporary aberrations in sensory processing and failures in top-down inferential processes, or as part of a trait which encapsulates a variety of aberrant psychosis-like experiences (e.g., 'schizotypy' [2, 3]). Extant theoretical accounts of AVH seek to explain a variety of experimental observations, incorporating dysregulations in cognitive, perceptual (e.g., [4]) and neurocomputational processes [5].

One prominent cognitive model suggests that AVH reflect impairments in source-monitoring, namely, difficulties in discriminating between the internal (self-generated) versus external origins of auditory stimuli [6, 7] and is instantiated in signal detection studies, which emphasise impairments in perceptual sensitivity and/or altered decision criteria (e.g., [8]). Relevant studies typically employ speech detection tasks (e.g., White Noise Task; WNT) that measure hits (veridical speech) and false alarms (illusory speech). Through the use of inherently ambiguous stimuli and the experimentally induced expectation of speech, the WNT induces illusory perceptions of speech even in psychologically healthy people (e.g., [9]). However, the WNT is also sensitive to variations in disease risk and severity. For example, the likelihood of experiencing AVHs during the WNT (i.e., hearing speech in noise-only trials) increases as a function of familial risk of psychosis [10]. In the absence of identified genetic risk, participants characterised by high levels of psychometrically defined schizotypy, or hallucination proneness may show an increased incidence of AVH in the WNT, although evidence for the latter is inconsistent. For example, the occurrence of speech illusions shows a positive relationship with hallucination-proneness [11] [12] [13] and positive schizotypy more generally [14], although some inconsistencies exist [15]. The WNT is also sensitive to acute changes in psychosis-like state, for example, after administration of a psychotomimetic drug. In particular, an increased incidence of speech illusions was observed in a study of healthy participants administered cannabis, although the effect did not appear to replicate [16].

Performance on the WNT is commonly analysed using a signal detection theory (SDT) framework. SDT allows the evaluation of participant behaviour in contexts requiring the detection of a signal in noise, and proposes that within each trial, participants respond affirmatively if the sensory stimulus exceeds a hidden threshold, or 'criterion'. SDT analysis methods allow for the derivation of this criterion value (a measure of response bias), as well as a general index of perceptual sensitivity (d-prime), which corresponds to the difference between 'hit' and 'false alarm' response distributions [17]. These SDT-derived indices provide a method for inferring the relative balance of sensory input and prior beliefs during perception.

Within a Bayesian framework, a relative increase in perceptual sensitivity may be consistent with an effective increase in the precision of sensory input, while a greater tendency to respond 'yes' (i.e., 'signal present') under uncertainty (i.e., a liberal response bias) may indicate greater reliance on prior perceptual expectations. Although these mappings between SDT metrics and Bayesian models of psychosis are indirect and do not uniquely identify the underlying computational mechanism, SDT nevertheless provides a valuable framework for investigating perceptual decision-making processes [18].

Hallucinations have been associated with a liberal response bias (lower c), and reduced sensitivity (lower d') in signal detection tasks [19], consistent with the 'strong priors' account of hallucinatory experiences -as sensory input becomes more uncertain, prior expectations come to dominate perception [20]. In other words, hallucinations may be a product of a bias towards top-down, versus bottom-up influences on perception [21].

Despite the lack of confirmatory sensory information that accompanies false (hallucinatory) percepts in psychosis, hallucinations are nonetheless experienced with high certainty regarding their origins (i.e., an external source rather than internal processes). At the less extreme end of psychosis-like experiences, perceptual illusions are non-veridical perceptual experiences which, while false, are not accompanied by as much certainty in their veracity as true hallucinations. These may occur when sensory experience is noisy and/or when priors are relatively precise, but not to the extent that they override (the lack of) sensory evidence. Although the occurrence of auditory (speech) illusions is dependent on prevailing sensory-environmental conditions (i.e., amount of auditory background noise) and may occasionally occur in the absence of risk factors for psychosis in psychologically healthy individuals, susceptible individuals (e.g., those with a family history, and/or adverse developmental experiences) may possess neurobiological and cognitive profiles that overlap with those with psychosis, and may, as a result be susceptible to speech illusions that have more in common with psychotic hallucinations than the false perceptions (e.g., perceptual illusions) occasionally experienced by healthy individuals.

Psychological health is maintained, in part, by adaptive predictive mechanisms that rely on a finely tuned balance between excitatory and inhibitory signalling in the brain [22], dependent upon intact glutamatergic and GABAergic transmission [23, 24]. Multiple avenues of research, including genetic, brain imaging and animal model studies, have provided evidence for a disruption to this excitatory/inhibitory (E/I) balance in schizophrenia (e.g., see [25] for review). Some findings have also identified a link between glutamatergic abnormalities in schizophrenia and hallucination severity [26]. In healthy participants, the Nmethyl-D-aspartate (NMDA) receptor antagonist, ketamine, can reproduce several markers of E/I imbalance seen in psychosis (e.g., [27] [28] [29] [30] [31]), errors of reality monitoring [32] and, less commonly, psychosis-like hallucinatory experiences [33].

Nitrous oxide (N2O) is an N-methyl-D-aspartate (NMDA) receptor antagonist with ketaminelike properties [34]. Given its actions on the glutamatergic system [35, 36], psychotomimetic effects [34], and considerable practical advantages over ketamine (e.g., ease of administration, tolerability, less severe side effects [37]), N2O may represent an attractive candidate for pharmacologically modelling psychosis in healthy volunteers. The purpose of the current study was to evaluate the effect of N2O on individual performance on the WNT. We hypothesised that administration of N2O would be associated with increased occurrence of speech illusions (i.e., perception of speech in noise-only trials, namely 'false alarms'), and that this increase would show a positive relationship with acute subjective psychotomimetic effects of the drug. We further expected that trait vulnerability to psychotic experiences (i.e., schizotypy) would predict greater rate of speech illusions irrespective of drug treatment. In addition to our preregistered analysis plan, we also evaluated the effects of N2O on individual performance on the WNT within an SDT framework. We hypothesised that its administration would lead to reduced sensitivity and a liberal bias in responding compared to placebo, mirroring changes observed in patients.

METHODS

This report is part of a larger pre-registered project (https://osf.io/4jhsx). Only measures directly relevant to the research questions/hypotheses outlined above are reported here. All study procedures were approved by the University College London Research Ethics Committee and performed in line with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to any study procedure.

Participants

Inclusion criteria for participants (n=120) were: self-reported absence of contra-indications, age 18-40 years, fluency in English and (corrected-to-)normal vision and hearing. Participants were ineligible if they self-reported a historical/current psychiatric disorder sufficiently severe to require treatment, any respiratory condition (e.g., asthma or recent pneumothorax), or B12/folate deficiency. Other exclusion criteria are outlined in Table S1 in the Supplement.

Self-report measures

All questionnaires were presented on a computer-based survey tool (Qualtrics, Provo, UT) and completed with limited researcher direction.

The short form Oxford-Liverpool Inventory of Feelings and Experiences was used to assess 'trait' schizotypy (sO-LIFE [38]). Baseline mental health was assessed using the Depression, Anxiety and Stress Scale (21-item version; DASS-21 [39]).

Subjective state changes in dissociation, psychotomimesis and sedation were assessed respectively using the Clinician-Administered Dissociative States Scale (CADSS [40]), Psychotomimetic States Inventory (PSI [41]) and Brief-Biphasic Alcohol Effects Sedation Subscale (B-BAES-sedation [42]; see supplement for other drug-state measures). Change scores for psychotomimesis (PSI) and sedation (B-BAES-sedation) between T1 and T2 -∆PSI and ∆Sedation, respectively -are predictors in the analyses. In exploratory analyses, we distinguished between positive and negative psychotomimesis, reflecting changes in positive (delusional thinking, perceptual distortion, paranoia: ∆PSI-Positive) and negative psychosis-like states (cognitive disorganisation and anhedonia: ∆PSI-Negative).

Speech illusion task

To assess the occurrence of auditory illusions, we used the White Noise (speech illusion) Task (WNT) adapted from Galdos et al. [10] (details in Supplement). Task instructions were designed to generate expectations that speech signals would be present in some trials and that these could be neutral, positive or negatively valenced. The task consisted of 60 trials, with an average of 30 'signal-absent' (no speech signal) and 30 'signal-present' (speech) trials presented via headphones. All stimuli were presented for 2s, which included 1s of neutral human speech in signal-present trials and only noise in signal-absent trials [43]. As such, no affective speech was presented. After each trial, participants responded: 'no speech', 'neutral speech', 'positive speech' or 'negative speech' via keyboard response. Four response categories were possible: Hits and Correct Rejections (correct responses), and Misses and False Alarms (incorrect responses), although hits and false alarms alone fully capture task performance. Typically, 'speech illusions' are inferred from an elevated false alarm rate. Note, we adapted the task to incorporate a self-referential component (see details in Supplement), which was found to be insensitive and is not discussed further.

In addition, signal detection metrics that disambiguate between different types of perceptual tendencies are reported. D-prime (commonly, d') is a measure of perceptual sensitivity, calculated as the difference between standardised hit and false alarm rates. We also report the criterion value (commonly, 'c'), a response bias measure, which is the average of standardised hit and false alarm rates [17] (multiplied by -1; see Supplement). Reductions in d-prime reflect reduced perceptual sensitivity (impaired ability to distinguish noise from signal + noise); an increase in the criterion value represents a more conservative response bias (a tendency to require more evidence to respond 'signal present'; see formulas in Supplement). Drugs Nitrous oxide 50%, premixed with 50% oxygen (Entonox, BOC, UK) or placebo medical air (BOC, UK), was (self-)administered according to each participant's randomisation code for up to 40 min. Participants were blinded to treatment through the use of identical demand valves, mouthpieces and hoses and concealment of gas cylinders using opaque sleeves. Procedure After a two-stage screening (Supplement), eligible participants attended the university research facility for a single session, which was conducted in a quiet lab. Upon arrival, participants provided written informed consent and completed demographic and baseline (trait) measures, followed by the first set of state questionnaires (pre-inhalation; T1). Participants then began inhalation of the assigned gas. After a period of equilibration (~5 min), participants completed the state measures for the second time (peri-inhalation; T2), followed by the WNT. Blood pressure was also measured at T1 and T2. Other tasks and measures were completed after the WNT (https://osf.io/4jhsx) and will be reported elsewhere. After the ~40 min inhalation, there was a recovery period after which additional measures were taken. One hour after inhalation ended, participants were required to successfully complete a series of field sobriety tests before being deemed 'fit-to-leave'. They were debriefed before departure. Analysis Analyses were performed using R (see Supplement). Linear mixed models were used to analyse acute drug effects with drug (N2O/placebo), timepoint (pre (T1), peri (T2)) and Drug x Timepoint as fixed effects, and per-participant intercepts as random effects. Associations between variables are expressed as Pearson's r (continuous variables) or Spearman's Rho (ρ; count variables). Based on a simulation study of false alarms (Supplement), a negative binomial data-generating process was assumed, and negative binomial regressions were used to analyse false alarms, in line with the pre-registered analysis plan. The moderating role of trait psychotomimesis on false alarms was tested by including a Drug x sO-LIFE interaction term alongside main effects. In addition, the moderating effect of ∆PSI on false alarms was similarly tested by including a multiplicative term (see Supplement for details on issues regarding correct interpretation of interaction effects in generalised linear models, as opposed to general linear models).

Signal detection metrics (d-prime and criterion values) were analysed using linear regression, although these were not pre-registered outcomes. Given the pattern of observed associations between d-prime, ∆PSI and ∆Sedation, the latter were included as mediators of drug effects on d-prime. Indirect effects were estimated using maximum likelihood estimation with bias corrected (BC) confidence intervals obtained from 5,000 bootstrapped samples. All reported hypothesis tests are two-tailed with α≤0.05. Effect sizes are reported as Cohen's d.

Hits and False Alarms Hit and false alarm rates from the WNT reported here were similar to those described in previous studies (e.g. [44]; see Supplement). Moreover, convergent validity of the task was implied by the positive association between false alarms and psychosis-like traits (sO-LIFE; ρ=0.235, p=0.010; no correlation was found between sO-LIFE and hits; ρ=0.058, p=0.529). However, state changes in psychosis-like experiences (∆PSI) were not correlated with false alarms (ρ=0.108, p=0.239), but were significantly negatively correlated with hits (ρ=-0.262, p=0.004). Moreover, ∆Sedation was also negatively correlated with hits (ρ=-0.325, p<0.001) but not false alarms (ρ=-0.020, p=0.832).

The between groups comparisons showed that the number of hits was significantly lower under N2O (M=17.38, SE=1.09) than medical air (M=23.38, SE=2.03; b=-0.297, SE=0.107, z=-2.77, p=0.006; Table S3). Moreover, the number of false alarms was virtually identical in the two drug groups (N2O: M=3.23, SE=0.58; medical air: M=3.38, SE=0.86; b=-0.045, SE=0.312, z=-0.15, p=0.884; Table S3). Relatedly, the proportion of participants in the two groups reporting ≥1 false alarm versus no false alarms (medical air: 68%; N2O: 58%) also did not differ (b=-0.43, SE=0.41, Wald(1)=1.11, p=0.292).

It is possible that the simple bivariate models described above predicting false alarms from drug condition conceal a conditional effect of drug condition. In particular, N2O's effects on false alarms might depend upon participants' sensitivity to its subjective psychotomimetic effects (alternatively, the ∆PSI-false alarms association might be drug condition-dependent).

If so, the null ∆PSI-false alarm association, which reflects averaging across drug conditions might conceal a drug-specific effect (alternatively, averaging across ∆PSI levels potentially obscure a ∆PSI level-dependent drug effect). We therefore tested the simultaneous effects of ∆PSI, drug condition and their interaction on false alarms (Table S4 in Supplement). This showed a modest interaction (b=0.043, SE=0.020, z=2.14, p=0.033; NB the coefficient is on a log scale), which, when explored further using simple slopes, revealed a significant positive relationship between ∆PSI and false alarms in the N2O condition (response scale marginal effect: 𝑑𝑦 𝑑𝑥 =0.097, SE=0.044, z=2.21, p=0.027), but not in the medical air group (response scale 𝑑𝑦 𝑑𝑥 =-0.027, SE=0.046, z=0.58, p=0.561). The pattern of hits and false alarms in the two drug conditions was reflected in substantially lower perceptual sensitivity (d-prime) in the N2O group (M=1.71, SE=0.11) relative to medical air (M=2.28, SE=0.15; b=-0.570, SE=0.188, t118=-3.04, p=0.003). This implies a greater overlap between underlying noise and signal distributions, consistent with a reduced signalto-noise ratio of sensory representations in the N2O group. Moreover, d-prime was negatively correlated with both ∆PSI (r118=-0.404, p<0.001) and sedation (r118=-0.317, p<0.001). When ∆PSI was added as a covariate alongside drug, a significant amount of additional variance in d-prime was explained (∆R² =10.4%, p<0.001) and the drug effect was reduced by >50% and was no longer significant (b=-0.270, SE=0.193, t117=-1.39, p=0.166, Table S5). ∆Sedation also reduced the drug effect (b=-0.361, SE=0.199, t117=-1.81, p=0.073) and the combination of ∆PSI and ∆Sedation attenuated the effect of drug by >70% (b=-0.167, SE=0.200, t116=-0.84, p=0.405; Table S6 in Supplement).

DISCUSSION

In this study we tested hypotheses based on the NMDA receptor dysfunction model of schizophrenia by evaluating the effects of the ketamine-like dissociative anaesthetic, nitrous oxide (N2O) on a speech detection paradigm (White Noise Task; WNT). We supplemented false alarm rate as an analogue of auditory verbal hallucinations with signal detection theorybased metrics which provide a more comprehensive framework for characterising task performance by distinguishing between perceptual sensitivity (d-prime) and response bias.

We showed that N2O produced an atypical pattern of effects on WNT outcomes, which did not conform to our basic prediction that N2O, acting primarily as an NMDA-receptor antagonist, would be associated with increased subjective psychotomimesis and, independently, an increase in false alarms on the WNT. Rather, a more nuanced and conditional pattern emerged such that an increase in false alarms was most evident in the N2O group in those with greatest sensitivity to N2O-induced subjective psychotomimesis. In addition, N2O administration was associated with an impaired ability to distinguish speech signals from noise (reduced perceptual sensitivity), which was driven by (mediated through) change in the subjective psychotomimetic state (∆PSI). Finally, we found that N2O caused a more conservative response bias, which was not associated with ∆PSI. Despite their differential pattern of associations with ∆PSI, the two signal detection metrics were significantly (negatively) correlated with each other such that difficulties distinguishing signal from noise (i.e., lower d-prime) were associated with a more cautious (conservative) response tendency in the N2O group.

Aspects of the findings from the signal detection analysis superficially resemble those reported in studies of people with schizophrenia or elevated risk of developing the disorder, and hallucination-prone individuals without schizophrenia. In particular, previous studies in these clinical/sub-clinical groups have shown reduced perceptual sensitivity in auditory signal detection tasks [9, [47] [48] [49], albeit inconsistently (see e.g., [6, 11, 50]). However, reduced perceptual sensitivity in these studies is commonly driven by a higher false alarm rate, while hit rate remains relatively unaffected [9]. Alternatively, hit rate and false alarms can be higher in people with schizophrenia, as observed with a more liberal response bias (assuming perceptual sensitivity does not change; e.g., see [13]). This pattern diverges from that reported here with N2O, particularly the conservative bias. Although we are not aware of previous studies of the WNT showing disorder-related impairments in perceptual sensitivity and a more conservative response bias, self-monitoring deficits reflecting this pattern have been observed in a source monitoring memory task in people with schizophrenia [51]. The trivial explanation that the impairing effect of N2O on perceptual sensitivity was primarily a consequence of N2O-induced sedation was undermined by the parallel mediation analysis which showed that a considerable component of the effect of N2O on d-prime was mediated by ∆PSI, even after accounting for the indirect effect of sedation. Indeed, this pattern of effects was not expected if the drug→perceptual sensitivity association was mostly explained by non-specific arousal, attentional or other cognitive impairment-related factors. Moreover, the finding that the indirect effect of positive psychotomimesis was reliable even after factoring negative psychotomimesis into a parallel mediation model, suggest that N2Oinduced reduction in perceptual sensitivity was also not likely primarily driven by an increase in anhedonia.

In predictive processing terms, the N2O-induced reduction in d-prime might be interpreted as a decrease in sensory precision [52]. However, the absence of an increase in false alarms, and the more conservative response bias might suggest that sensory degradation was not sufficient to produce a generalised shift to greater reliance upon prior expectations [20]. Considering the Drug x ∆PSI interaction on false alarms, it is possible that reduced sensory precision only translates into hallucination-like perception in those who experience a sufficiently strong psychotomimetic effect. We acknowledge, however, that such interpretations should be treated with caution because metrics derived from signal detection theory do not precisely map onto predictive processing approaches.

Contemporary neurobiological models conceptualise psychosis as a progressive disorder [53] in which early glutamatergic dysfunction and E/I imbalance contribute to the development of corticolimbic system abnormalities [54, 55], loss of inhibitory interneurons [56] [57] [58] and structural and functional hippocampal changes [59], ultimately contributing to altered striatal dopaminergic activity [60] [61] [62]. Within this framework, acute NMDA receptor antagonism may model an early stage of this pathological cascade [63, 64]. It is possible that a liberal bias in tasks such as the WNT may depend on later-emerging adaptations rather than arising directly from acute glutamatergic dysfunction itself. Although speculative, this pattern is compatible with the notion that impaired sensory processing represents an earlier consequence of glutamatergic perturbation, whereas alterations in perceptual decision making that produce a liberal bias emerge only as downstream pathological changes become established [65].

It is unclear whether the atypical pattern of psychosis-like responding observed here with N2O is seen with other NMDA receptor antagonists -or indeed, with other psychotomimetic drugs. In fact, while the WNT is a commonly used tool in experimental psychopathology, its use in psychopharmacological studies is limited. We identified a single relevant publication on the effects of the psychotomimetic, tetrahydrocannabinol, on WNT performance [16]. This showed an inconsistent pattern of results (one study showing a larger number of false alarms following the drug, and another showing no difference relative to placebo) and did not report any signal detection-based analyses. Moreover, the effects of the most commonly used pharmacological model of psychosis -ketamine -on these behavioural measures of experimentally-modelled auditory hallucinations remain unexplored. For a more comprehensive picture of the changes in perceptual inference associated with putative NMDA receptor dysfunction in psychosis, further research is required to systematically assess changes in indices such as sensitivity and response bias in response to psychotomimetic manipulations, as well as across the developmental span of psychosis from increased risk to prodrome, first-episode and chronic psychosis.

A number of limitations of the current study should be acknowledged. Firstly, the rate of affectively salient speech illusions was low in the current sample and did not allow for meaningful analysis of effects of N2O on positive versus negative illusions. Since psychotic hallucinations are often laden with affective content [66], individual differences in the tendency experience affective speech illusions in WNT may represent particularly informative markers of vulnerability. Increasing the expectation of affectively salient speech in future studies using the WNT, for example, by including clearly positive/negative stimuli in signal-present trials, may be helpful in this respect.

To minimise participant burden during N2O-intoxication, the WNT used here was selected to be a low demand task, imposing a limited load on (working) memory or other higher order cognitive operations. We deliberately avoided additional response requirements (e.g., confidence judgements for each trial). However, we acknowledge that including such measures would be particularly helpful for interpreting results within a predictive processing framework [67]. Relatedly, we did not include any measure of general cognitive functioning. It is possible that the perceptual sensitivity impairment observed here might have reflected impairments in more basic cognitive functioning. Although we controlled for the direct and indirect effects of subjective sedation statistically, sedation may not be an effective proxy for inattention and distractibility (which are likely increased under N2O). As such, future studies might consider assessing cognitive functioning objectively. Despite the potential contribution of N2O-induced impairment on perceptual sensitivity, the robust finding that sensitivity was associated with state changes in positive (as opposed to negative) psychotomimesis is less readily explained by cognitive impairment.

We also did not include a measure of trait suggestibility, which predicts anomalous experiences in experimental settings [68, 69], is associated with schizotypal tendencies in healthy volunteers [50] and is enhanced by N2O [70]. The combination of high schizotypy and high trait suggestibility may be particularly powerful in eliciting hallucinatory experiences in healthy participants in the WNT [68]. Given that suggestion is implicit in the design of the WNT, future work in this area should include a measure of this important variable.

Finally, we acknowledge that our mediation analyses must be interpreted with caution and should not be taken to imply causation, particularly in relation to the temporal sequences implied by the path models outlined here. Relatedly, although we demonstrated a role for N2O-induced psychotomimesis -particularly positive psychotomimesis -in perceptual sensitivity, we cannot discount a role for other variables, including sedation and negative psychotomimesis (the variables were potentially important mediators, despite non-significant indirect effects).

In summary, our findings show that sensitivity to the psychotomimetic effects of N2O were associated with greater incidence of speech illusions during the WNT, and signal detection metrics suggested degraded perceptual sensitivity and tendency to respond more conservatively to ambiguous stimuli.

White Noise Task There were 60 trials consisting of non-valenced auditory stimuli (signal-present) or noise only (signal-absent) trials presented in random order via noise-cancelling headphones. The instructions for the task were designed to generate high expectations of speech signals, which could be emotionally neutral, positively valenced, or negatively valenced. As such, despite all true speech signals being affectively neutral, participants had four response options per trial: 1) no speech, 2) neutral speech, 3) positive speech or 4) negative speech via a keyboard response. In contrast to most previous studies employing the WNT that used white or pink noise for stimulus construction [1] [2] [3] [4], we employed steady-state speech shaped noise (SSN [5]). SSN was chosen as it shows greater overlap with the spectra of human speech and was therefore expected to engender greater ambiguity. Notably, the WNT version using SSN appears to show a higher base rate of FAs [6] than is typically seen with white/pink noise WNT protocols.

In an adaptation to the task, we also aimed to generate expectations that some trials might include their name. Prior to inhalation, participants were asked to complete two practice trials to ensure appropriate understanding of the task. Of the two practice trials, one was a signalabsent, and the other a signal-present trial, whereby the snippet of speech present in the noise was the participant's first name (converted from text to speech using the Google Speech Client). During the WTN, participants provided an additional response if they perceived speech (i.e., responded with options 2-4 above), the question 'Did you hear your name being spoken?' appeared on the screen along with response options ('Y' or 'N'). This additional question was included to assess whether any auditory illusions had self-referential features. However, only a single participant (medical air group) responded 'yes'. As such, this metric was deemed insensitive and was not reported further.

Based on the structure of the WNT, participants' responses could be accurate, namely correctly identifying signal-present and absent trials (Hits and correct rejections respectively), or, inaccurate, namely failing to identify signal-present stimuli (Misses) or incorrectly responding to signal-absent trials (False Alarms).

Statistical analysis

Data were analysed using R statistical software (R Core Development Team, 2024, version 4.5.1).

The acute effects of nitrous oxide (i.e., DEQ, BAES, CADSS and PSI scores) across timepoints were analysed using linear mixed models, employing the lme4 and lmerTest packages in R [9, 10] with drug condition (N2O or medical air), timepoint and Drug x Timepoint as fixed effects, and per-participant intercepts as random effects. Note, because the current study is primarily focused on sensitivity to (rather than recovery from) acute drug effects, in a departure from the preregistered analyses, the relevant timepoints (T1 and T2) were isolated in the analyses described here (rather than T1-T3).

Outcomes assessed only at the peri-inhalation (T2) timepoint (signal detection indices derived from WNT responses) employed general linear or generalised linear regression models using the glmmTMB package in R [11]. Simulations using the DHARMa package [12] tested for over-dispersion and zero-inflation and suggested a negative binomial regression model of drug effects on false alarms performed more effectively than Poisson, Generalised Poisson or zero-inflated Poisson (based on model fit metrics AIC and BIC). The use of appropriate simulation-derived analytic strategy for count variables (number of false alarms; generalised linear model) rather reducing occurrences to a binary variable (≥1 illusion versus none) and using logistic regression (e.g., [2, [13] [14] [15] [16]) is an important consideration. The use of binary logistic regression for count variables inevitably entails a loss of statistical power, and can introduce additional research degrees of freedom (varying definitions of 'occurrence'), increasing the potential for Type II and I errors respectively.
