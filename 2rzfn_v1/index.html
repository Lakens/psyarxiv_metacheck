How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

When a statement is expected

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Background. Suicidal ideation changes within hours and varies across individuals. However, the accuracy of the short-term personalized (i.e. idiographic) prediction remains modest and how we can improve the model has not been systematically appraised.

1. Introduction

>Introduction

1.1. Short-Term Prediction in Suicide Ideation

Suicide is one of the leading causes of death worldwide. 1 However, decades of research to prevent suicide have not been able to meaningfully reduce suicide rates. 2 One of the challenges in suicide research is to understand the time-sensitive characteristic in suicide risk, shifting even within a day rather than remaining stable over weeks or months. [3] [4] [5] It reframes the central question on understanding suicide risk from who is at risk to when and how much risk a person has in the near-term. 6, 7 Individuals with major depressive disorder (MDD) are at substantially increased risk of suicide, making these individuals especially critical to study. The Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9), a widely used depressive symptom instrument, directly captures thoughts of suicidal ideation and self-harm through item 9. 8 This item forecasts suicide risk over weeks to months. 9, 10 However, a single retrospective rating makes it hard to capture acute short-term suicide risk that fluctuates even within a single day. Ecological momentary assessment (EMA), an active selfreport method, repeatedly samples symptom changes over time. Meanwhile, passive sensing enables the continuous monitoring of behavioral and physiological signals through smartphones and wearable devices.

1.2. Open Questions in Idiographic Prediction

Heterogeneity of suicide risk has underscored limitations of group-level prediction models in capturing individual differences in risk factors. 11 An idiographic approach, which builds a separate model for each person, has shown promise in addressing this heterogeneity. However, idiographic model accuracy has remained modest, explaining 4% to 17% of the variance in suicidal urges and intent. 12 Three questions remain on how to improve idiographic prediction models.

Multimodality in idiographic prediction.

Combining EMA and passive sensor data has shown promise for suicide risk monitoring by capturing self-reported symptom changes with continuous behavioral and physiological context. However, passive sensing, particularly sleep and activity features, has shown little to no incremental value over self-report alone in several prediction models. [13] [14] [15] One potential reason is that group-level modeling approaches have treated the heterogeneity of individual differences in behavioral and physiological risk factors as noise, rather than as informative signal. Passive data streams (e.g., smartphone screen content) have shown strong within-person predictive accuracy that fails to generalize across individuals. This suggests that predictive signals may be highly individual-specific rather than shared at the group level. 16 We therefore used smartphone and wearable signals from domains linked to ideation in earlier work, namely sleep, 17, 18 mobility, 19, 20 social contact, 21 heart rate and battery level.

Temporal feature engineering. Suicidal ideation risk factors fluctuate over time, and basic variability metrics like within-person standard deviation already have shown improvement in prediction beyond mean-level data alone. 4, 22 However, standard deviation only quantifies overall dispersion and cannot isolate rapid, abrupt shifts from gradual change, even when overall variability is identical. 23 Of the dynamic indices used here, sample entropy is order-sensitive, whereas the median absolute deviation (MAD) and the position of the current value within the person's 10th-to-90th percentile range (P90-P10 position) describe spread but not the order of observations. Their contribution to predictive accuracy beyond mean levels remains largely untested. In passive sensing, dynamic indices have seen limited application. 13 To date, only one study has examined location entropy derived from GPS data as a predictor, but it showed limited predictive value. 19 Indeed, dynamic features of frequently assessed suicidal thinking have improved the prediction of suicide attempts after discharge, 24 suggesting that dynamic indices may be especially useful for detecting rapid fluctuations that sparser sampling would miss or dilute.

Compounding this gap, the time window used to capture these risk factors has been fixed and applied inconsistently across studies, despite evidence that predictive performance varies by timescale and prediction horizon. 25 This inconsistency runs counter to Bryan and Rudd's argument that the transition from suicidal thought to behavior unfolds at individually variable speeds, 26 suggesting a single fixed window may not capture how risk unfolds for every individual.

Two-step hurdle model structure.

Suicidal ideation can be absent on many days but can increase rapidly and substantially when it occurs. Its occurrence and its level when present may therefore be driven by different processes, so estimating them separately in a two-part model may predict better. 27 Whether idiographic hurdle models, which explicitly separate the probability of ideation occurrence from its conditional rating, improve out-of-sample prediction of suicide ideation has not been tested. Multilevel hurdle models have recently been applied to characterize how affect predicts ideation presence versus what those authors call severity as distinct processes, 28 but this work evaluated within-sample associations rather than held-out prediction accuracy.

1.3. The Present Study

To address the research gap, this study first tested whether an idiographic modeling approach could predict momentary suicidal ideation and tested whether multimodal integration, feature construction and hurdle versus continuous structure further improve the baseline models. Following the preregistration (https://osf.io/4ztq7/), two hypotheses were tested. First, we hypothesized that each model (EMA-only, passive sensing-only and multimodal) would outperform the baseline model, which predicts each person's training-mean ideation (H1). Second, we hypothesized that the multimodal model (EMA + passive sensing) would outperform both single-modality models (H2). Whether dynamic, within-person variability features, the hurdle structure and a person-specific timeframe improve prediction were examined as exploratory questions. The models are a research tool for adults with depression and are not intended for clinical use. Suicide mortality differs by sex and age, 2 but performance was not compared across these groups (Section 4.5).

>Method

2.2. Sample

Participants were recruited online through Meta and Google Ads across the United States and interviewed to confirm eligibility. A total of 300 adults were enrolled who were 18 or older, lived in the United States, owned an Android device and met past-month MDD criteria on the Structured Clinical Interview for DSM-5 (SCID-5). 30 People with a psychotic episode, bipolar disorder or high acute suicide risk per the Columbia-Suicide Severity Rating Scale (C-SSRS) 31 were excluded. Recruitment ran from February 17, 2021, to December 1, 2023, and data were collected remotely from June 15, 2021, to March 19, 2024. Information on treatment received during the study (for example, medication or psychotherapy) was stored with personally identifiable information and was not available for this analysis, so treatment could not be described or accounted for.

2.3. Procedures

Participants received a Garmin Vivoactive 4S by mail and installed the MLife smartphone app. Over 90 days they wore the watch and completed EMA surveys, while passive data were collected continuously. Reminders and technical support were provided when compliance was low, and participants could earn up to $487. People who endorsed thoughts of how to attempt suicide, intent or a plan (C-SSRS items 3 to 5) at screening were not enrolled. 32 During the study, a high item 9 score triggered a safety question offering support and a link to emergency services, and expressed intent alerted the study team so that a clinician could reach out. At least two clinical psychologists or psychiatrists were available. 33 >Measures

2.4.1. Ecological momentary assessment

Participants completed EMA surveys three times a day. The items were nine depressive items adapted from the PHQ-9 8 and two anxiety items from the Generalized Anxiety Disorder-2 (GAD-2), 34 rated on a 101-point scale. The depressive items showed good internal consistency (α = .91) and convergent validity with the PHQ-9 (r = .71), 35 but these estimates are for the full scale, not for item 9 as a single-item, within-person measure. The outcome was suicidal ideation (SI), the EMA version of PHQ-9 item 9 ("In the past 4 hours, I have had thoughts of hurting myself or that I would be better off dead", rated from 0, not at all, to 100, constantly), is referred as the level of suicide ideation (SI). A rating above zero was defined as item 9 endorsement. Endorsement was the binary outcome for AUC and the occurrence component of the hurdle models, and the rating of endorsed prompts is termed the conditional rating.

2.4.2. Passive sensors

Smartphone data captured battery level, social contact (SMS and call length, conversation duration) and GPS-derived mobility (time at home, distance traveled). Garmin data captured steps, accelerometry, heart rate and sleep (total and REM duration). These streams cover domains linked to suicidal ideation in prior work (Section 1.2, Supplementary Table S1). Heart rate variability and electrodermal activity were not available. Note. N = participants with at least one assessment (or logged event for SMS, calls and conversation).

2.4.3. Feature engineering

EMA predictors summarized earlier responses to all 11 items, including item 9, over the preceding 1 and 7 days, and passive predictors the preceding 1 hour, 4 hours, 1 day and 7 days. Every window ended at least 4 hours before the prompt (t -4 h, Section 2.4.4), so no predictor overlapped the outcome's recall window. Sleep, summarized per night, was exempt as registered.

Sensor binning, zero-filling of event streams, home-location detection and sleep timing are detailed in Supplementary Methods M1. Each window yielded mean-level features (for passive streams, the window means and sum) and, in the dynamic configurations, the MAD, the P90-P10 position and sample entropy (TSEntropies version 0.9, embedding dimension 2, tolerance 0.2 times the window's standard deviation). For EMA the only dynamic index was the 7-day MAD. For heart rate and battery level, the window sum mostly reflects the number of recorded samples, so it functions as a proxy for recording density (wear time or logging) rather than as a measure of arousal or charge level. Indices were computed only with enough data points (Supplementary Methods M2). After cleaning there were 33 EMA-derived and 140 passive features (Table 1), and the candidate pool for the selected window contained 11 to 76 features (Supplementary Methods M3). All available features in the registered domains were used. The pipeline is available at https://github.com/AhhyunMLee/phq_item9_idiographic.

2.4.4. Prediction timeline

Every prompt is a separate prediction target. Let t be the time at which a prompt was completed.

The outcome is the item 9 rating given at t, which covers the preceding 4 hours, and predictors use only data completed at or before t -4 h (Figure 1A), so the last predictor observation ends exactly where the outcome window begins. The task is thus a 4-hour-ahead forecast made at t -4 h. For example, a prediction made at 8 a.m. from data up to 8 a.m. is scored against the item 9 rating completed at noon, which covers 8 a.m. to noon. Because t is the actual completion time, which a live system would not know in advance, this is a retrospective evaluation of a forecasting task. It is not next-day prediction. Earlier same-day responses can be predictors if completed by t -4 h. Consecutive prompts were a median of 4.0 hours apart (interquartile range [IQR] 3.6 to 4.4) and 49.6% were less than 4 hours apart, so the preceding prompt fell inside the gap about half of the time and was not used then. The three prompts of a test day are predicted by one model fit on the preceding 55 days only.

2.5. Prediction models

>Prediction models

2.5.1. Analytic inclusion criteria

Participants were included if they had (1) at least 75% compliance in EMA and in passive sensing (passive coverage was the mean of the participant's per-sensor compliance values), (2) non-zero variance in the outcome, (3) at least 10 SI-positive (item 9 above 0) and 20 SI-negative (item 9 equal to 0) observations, and (4) at least 195 completed assessments, the number of prompts in 65 days at three a day (45 training, 10 validation and 10 test days). Criterion 3 was applied to the whole study period, including test days, and excluded 82 of the 225 participants who met the compliance criteria (36%), so the results apply to people with recurrent but not constant ideation. No sample size calculation was performed, because this is a secondary analysis.

2.5.2. Handling of missing data

Missing values were imputed by k-nearest neighbors (k = 5) 36 using the caret package (version 7.0.1) 37 and RANN (version 2.6.3) in R version 4.6.1, 38 fit within each training fold to prevent leakage and then applied to the validation and test days. Predictors with fewer than 50% nonmissing training values or zero variance were excluded first, so a participant with no data from a stream simply had no predictors from it. Predictors were centered and scaled within the training split, which does not change the tree-based models and serves only the imputation. All analyses were run on macOS in R (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria); packages were obtained from CRAN (https://cran.r-project.org).

2.5.3. Prediction models

Random forest (R package randomForest version 4.7-1.2, 39 500 trees) was used for the continuous models, which predict item 9 directly, and gradient boosting (caret version 7.0.1, 37 and gbm version 2.3.1, 40 100 trees, interaction depth 3, shrinkage 0.1) for the hurdle models, which combine a classifier for the occurrence of ideation (item 9 above 0) and a regressor for its level, fit only on training prompts with ideation, whose product is the prediction. Seven configurations were built per participant, namely EMA-only, passive-only and combined models, each with mean-level features only or with dynamic indices, plus a baseline (Table 2). The random forest tried 2, 5 or 10 variables per split, chosen jointly with the window on the same validation days. In every fold the aggregation window (1 or 7 days for EMA, 1 hour to 7 days for passive features, applied to all passive streams together, with both EMA windows used in the combined models) was the candidate with the lowest mean absolute error on the validation days, and the chosen model was refit on the 55 training and validation days (Supplementary Methods M4). The baseline predicted each person's training-mean item 9 over the same 55 days. No resampling or class weighting was used. Five reference predictors need no model. These were persistence (the last response at least 4 hours before the prompt), the previous prompt, the previous 1-day and 7-day means, and always predicting zero.

2.5.4. Model validation

Validation used sliding-window time-series cross-validation within each participant (Figure 1). After a 6-day run-in, each model was trained on 45 days, the window was selected on the next 10 days, the selected model was refit on all 55 days, and it predicted the following day. The window then advanced one day at a time. The first test day was study day 62 and the last day 90, giving up to 29 test days per participant. A fold was skipped if its test day had no answered prompt or fewer than 60 training or 10 validation observations were available, so participants contributed 11 to 29 test days (median 29, 24 to 29 for the 142 compared, Supplementary Methods M5). 2.5.5. Performance evaluation

Metrics were computed within each participant from pooled test-day predictions and summarized as the median across participants, with a 95% bootstrap confidence interval or the interquartile range (IQR), as stated in the note to each table or figure. Discrimination was the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC, computed with pROC version 1.19.1) for the binary outcome (item 9 above 0), scored by the predicted value (continuous models) or the occurrence probability (hurdle models), with higher scores indicating more ideation (direction fixed in advance). Error metrics were the mean absolute error (MAE), overall and on prompts without and with ideation, root mean squared error (RMSE), Pearson's r and R² relative to each participant's test-period mean, using the point prediction of the continuous models and the combined prediction of the hurdle models (Supplementary Methods M6). The registered metrics were R², r, MAE and RMSE. The others are exploratory. All comparisons use only the participant-days predicted by every model, which left 142 participants because no fold of the EMA-only models could be fitted for one participant at either the 1-day or the 7-day EMA window. Calibration and clinical utility were not evaluated (Section 4.5).

2.5.6. Statistical comparisons

Comparisons were paired within participants. We report the median difference with a 95% bootstrap confidence interval (5,000 resamples) and Wilcoxon signed-rank p values corrected by the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure within each family of comparisons and metric (q). Because the registered hurdle (gradient boosting) and continuous (random forest) models differ in learner, each configuration was also fitted with same-learner partners as exploratory comparisons. 3.4. Reliability within individuals

Per-person tests (Section 2.5.7) showed that few participants were above chance for any model at p < .05, and none after correction. Compared with each person's own baseline, the combined dynamic random forest had a higher AUC for 59.9% of 137 people (median gain +.040) and lower absolute error for 43.0% of 142 (median gain -0.03 points), and 6.6% (AUC) and 7.0% (error) were significant at p < .05. Across all 12 models, 3.6% to 9.5% of people had a significantly higher AUC and 7.0% to 21.8% a significantly lower error than their baseline (uncorrected), and none after correction (Supplementary Table S3).

3.5. Window selection

A participant's most frequent window accounted for a median of 79% of folds in the EMA-only random forest models and 41% to 72% in the others, and the selected window changed between consecutive days in 11% to 57% of cases (Supplementary Results R1). Choosing the window per person did not improve discrimination over a single fixed window. Median AUC was .512 to .552 with adaptive windows and .506 to .550 with fixed windows (Supplementary Table S4), no adaptive-versus-fixed AUC comparison was significant (40 comparisons, all q > .14), and median MAE differences were 0.00 to 0.08 points (Supplementary Results R1).

3.6. Hurdle and continuous frameworks

Hurdle models had lower absolute error overall than continuous models (2.17 to 2.44 against 2.49 to 2.81, Supplementary Table S5), which came from prompts without ideation. On prompts with ideation their error was similar or higher. When both structures were fitted with the same learner (Supplementary Table S6), the hurdle version had lower MAE (by 0.04 to 0.08 points with random forest and 0.17 to 0.35 with gradient boosting) and higher R² (+0.03 to +0.05 and +0.07 to +0.12), with no difference in AUC (all q = .943). Always predicting zero had lower absolute error still (1.37).

3.7. Multimodality and dynamic features

Combining EMA with passive sensing improved R² over the single-source random forest models (+0.07 to +0.14, all q < .001) and over the passive-only hurdle models, but AUC gains were small (at most .02) and significant only against the mean-only EMA models (q = .024, Supplementary Table S7). Dynamic features improved the R² of the random forest models (+0.02 to +0.04) but not their MAE, and improved AUC only for the EMA-only hurdle model (+.018, q = .026). These R² gains, and those of the combined models, probably reflect flatter predictions rather than added signal.

3.8. Sensitivity to the SI-positive cutoff

We re-scored the saved predictions, without retraining the models, against stricter definitions of SI-positive (item 9 above 2, 5 and 10). The main findings were unchanged (Supplementary Table S8). The number of participants with a defined AUC fell from 137 to 94 at the strictest cutoff, so differences across cutoffs reflect changes in both the outcome definition and the sample. At every stricter cutoff the combined dynamic random forest stayed close to its original AUC, the previous-day mean was at least as high, and the training-mean baseline stayed below chance.

4. Discussion

>Discussion

4.1. Summary of findings

This study examined whether multimodality, dynamic features, person-specific windows and a hurdle structure improve idiographic prediction of suicidal ideation beyond simple baselines, using both EMA and passive sensing data. Across 142 adults with MDD, no model predicted reliably better than simple benchmarks. Discrimination was weak (median AUC .51 to .55), in the range reported for personalized models of related outcomes. 12, 41 Contrary to our first hypothesis, none of the models outperformed the training-mean baseline on R². Our second hypothesis was partly supported, as the combined model outperformed the EMA-only model on R² and RMSE and the passive-only model on all four metrics.

4.2. Multimodal integration

Combining EMA with passive sensing gave the most consistent gains in R² over single-source models, but AUC improved by at most .02. This agrees with a systematic review in which only one of four studies found added value of passive data over self-report. 13 Self-report of recent ideation may already carry most of the information that sensors capture indirectly, although we did not test this directly.

4.3. Dynamic features and time windows

Dynamic features improved AUC in only one of six comparisons and did not improve absolute error. Variability in ideation has been proposed as a risk marker 5 and linked to attempt history 42 and to post-discharge attempts, 24 but once mean levels are included such indices added little over a horizon of a few hours, perhaps because of the short windows and sparse data per person.

Choosing the window per person did not improve discrimination over a single fixed window, and selected windows were only moderately stable, so our data do not show that accommodating individual timescales 26, 43 improves prediction.

4.4. Hurdle versus continuous models

When both models were trained with the same algorithm, hurdle models had lower absolute error and higher R² than continuous models but did not differ in AUC. This is consistent with previous literature suggesting that the presence and level of ideation can behave as distinct processes, 28, 44 and with the suitability of a two-part structure for zero-inflated outcomes. This indicates that the hurdle structure improved predictions for the many prompts without ideation but did not improve the identification of ideation.

4.5. Strength, Limitations and future research

A main strength of this study is the systematic evaluation of each strategy for improving idiographic prediction against a common set of simple benchmarks. This showed that beating the training-mean baseline did not mean useful prediction. Moreover, to limit information leakage, models were refit before every test day and tested only on days later than those used for training, windows were selected on separate validation days, and imputation was fitted within training folds. Finally, the time window was selected from candidate windows for each person, which reduced reliance on arbitrary window choices.

Several limitations should be noted for the study. First, individuals at high acute suicide risk were excluded at screening (C-SSRS items 3 to 5), making it hard to generalize the result for those at highest risk. The sample was also predominantly White (81%) and female (85%) and used Android phones. We treated item 9 as largely reflecting suicidal ideation, although it also captures thoughts of self-harm and passive death wishes and does not ask about suicide directly. The outcome therefore cannot separate these constructs, and our findings may not apply to active suicidal ideation alone. Future studies should include higher-risk and more diverse samples, with appropriate safety procedures.

Second, our modeling choices may have limited discrimination. Windows and the number of variables per split were chosen by validation MAE. Because most prompts had no ideation, MAE favors predictions near zero rather than the ability to separate prompts with and without ideation. The validation set was also small (10 days, at most 30 prompts, few with ideation), so these choices were probably noisy. In addition, each model was fitted on at most 165 prompts with up to 76 candidate predictors, and one window was used for all passive streams. Future studies should select settings with criteria that reward discrimination, such as log loss, and test hierarchical or pooled models that share information across people. Because we did not compare personalized with pooled models, our results do not show whether personalized models outperform group-level models.

Supplementary Methods

Technical details of feature construction, modeling and evaluation. Section numbers refer to the main text.

M1 Predictor windows and binned sensors (Section 2.4.3)

EMA predictors summarized the preceding 1 and 7 days of the participant's earlier EMA responses to all 11 items, including item 9 and including earlier prompts on the same day. Passive predictors summarized the preceding 1 hour, 4 hours, 1 day, and 7 days. Every window ended at least 4 hours before the participant completed the prompt (t -4 h).

Sleep predictors were the previous night (the Garmin record whose wake date matched the date of the prompt) and the mean of the 7 nights up to and including that night. As registered, sleep was exempt from the 4-hour gap, so for early prompts the night's record may end less than 4 hours before the prompt.

For sensors recorded in bins (30 minutes for SMS, calls, GPS distance, and time at home, 5 minutes for conversation), the cutoff was moved back to the last complete bin, and bins without a logged event were set to zero for SMS, calls, and conversation between a participant's first and last event, with the first and last event defined over the whole recording, including events after the prediction time. Windows after the last event were therefore missing rather than zero, and logging outages were coded as zero. Time at home was defined as the proportion of GPS fixes within 40 m of a participant's home location. Home locations were identified once per participant using density-based clustering (DBSCAN; 100 m radius, at least 800 points) of nighttime (10 p.m. to 7 a.m.) GPS fixes from the whole recording, including the test period, and each cluster center was taken as a home (more than one if the participant moved or had several homes). For nine participants without a dense cluster, the home location was set manually from maps of their night-time fixes.

M2 Definitions of the dynamic indices (Section 2.4.3)

Each window yielded mean-level features (for passive streams, the window means and the window sum) and, in the dynamic configurations, three indices of within-person variability and pattern. For heart rate and battery level, the window sum mostly tracks the number of recorded samples and therefore functions as a proxy for recording density (wear time or logging) rather than as a measure of arousal or charge level.

The median absolute deviation (MAD) was scaled by 1.48 to be comparable to a standard deviation.

The P90-P10 position was the position of the most recent value within the window's distribution, (x -P10)/(P90 -P10), where x was the mean of the last 30 minutes before the cutoff and P10 and P90 were the 10th and 90th percentiles of the window. It required at least 20 points, was not clipped, and was missing when P90 equaled P10.

Sample entropy used an embedding dimension of 2 and a tolerance of 0.2 times the window's standard deviation. It required at least 250 non-missing points and was missing when the standard deviation was 0 or no template matched.

For EMA, the only dynamic index was the 7-day MAD, and for sleep it was the MAD over the 7 nights. Indices were computed only with enough data points in the window (mean N ≥ 1, MAD N ≥ 2, P90-P10 N ≥ 20 and sample entropy N ≥ 250, above the N > 200 that Yentes et al. 1 recommend), and features missing for more than half of the assessments in the analytic sample were removed. This filter and the removal of features without variability were applied once to all rows of the analytic sample (all participants, including test days) using predictor values only. Within each fold, a predictor additionally required at least 50% non-missing values in the training rows, and imputation, centering and scaling were fitted on the training rows only.

M3 Size of the candidate predictor pool (Section 2.4.3)

Each model drew on one candidate set per fold, determined by the selected window. Depending on the window, the number of candidate predictors was 11 for the EMA-only mean-only models, 11 to 22 for the EMA-only dynamic models, 18 to 20 for the passive-only mean-only models, 29 to 43 for the passive-only dynamic models, 40 to 42 for the combined mean-only models and 62 to 76 for the combined dynamic models.

M4 Model settings and fallback rules (Section 2.5.3)

The random forest (continuous models) used the R package randomForest with 500 trees, and the number of variables tried at each split (mtry) was chosen from 2, 5, and 10 on the validation days.

Gradient boosting (hurdle models) used the R packages caret and gbm with 100 trees, interaction depth 3 and shrinkage 0.1, with fixed settings that were not tuned. The minimum number of observations per terminal node (n.minobsinnode) was 10 for the occurrence classifier and 5 for the conditional-rating regressor; caret applied a Bernoulli loss to the classifier and a Gaussian loss to the regressor by default. The continuous gradient boosting partner (Section 2.5.6) used the settings of the conditional-rating regressor: 100 trees, interaction depth 3, shrinkage 0.1, a minimum of 5 observations per terminal node and a Gaussian loss.

Same-learner partners and elastic net (Section 2.5.6). The hurdle random forest partner comprised 500 trees for each part (a classification forest for occurrence and a regression forest for the conditional rating), with mtry selected from 2, 5 and 10 on the validation days. Elastic net models (glmnet version 5.1, alpha = .5) used standardized predictors, and the penalty (lambda) was selected within each fit by five-fold cross-validation in contiguous time blocks of the rows used for that fit (the training days when comparing windows and the training and validation days for the final fit). Cross-validated squared error (continuous models and conditional rating) or binomial deviance (occurrence) served as the criterion, and the largest lambda within one standard error of the minimum was retained. Continuous models used a Gaussian family; in hurdle models, occurrence was modelled with a binomial family and the conditional rating with a Gaussian family fitted to prompts with ideation.

At least 10 such prompts were required. Otherwise, the conditional rating was predicted by the mean of the training prompts with ideation, and when the training days contained only one outcome class the training occurrence rate was used and therefore, no fold was skipped for lack of prompts with ideation. In the 55-day training window, participants had a median of 41 SIpositive prompts (IQR 15 to 86). For 18 participants the median number of such prompts was below 10 and their prediction of the conditional rating was calculated as the mean of the available positive prompts. All predictions were limited to the 0 to 100 scale.

Windows also differ in the number of predictors they offer. Sleep features exist only at daily windows, and dynamic indices are more often missing in short windows. Across all fits, 77 participant-days (about 0.1%) were skipped because no candidate predictor set could be fitted (44) or preprocessing failed (33).

M5 Fold arithmetic (Section 2.5.4)

After a 6-day run-in, days 7 to 90 remained. Each fold required 55 earlier days (45 for training and 10 for validation). Test days therefore ran from day 62 to day 90 (29 folds). The first fold was trained on days 7 to 51 and validated on days 52 to 61. A fold was skipped if its test day had no answered prompt or if fewer than 60 training or 10 validation observations were available. Participants contributed 11 to 29 test days (median 29; 24 to 29 among the 142 participants in the comparisons).

M6 Performance metrics (Section 2.5.5)

AUC treats a higher score as more ideation, counts ties as half, and is defined only for participants with and without ideation in their test period. The baseline score was the training mean (continuous version) or the training occurrence rate (hurdle version), so the two baselines have different AUCs.

Pearson's r is undefined for a constant prediction. R² = 1 -SSE/SST, with each participant's own mean over their test days as the reference. A negative value therefore means the prediction was worse than a mean that cannot be known in advance.

Because a single test day has at most three observations, all metrics were computed from each participant's pooled test-day predictions.

M7 Circular-shift test of individual reliability (Section 2.5.7)

To test whether a participant's discrimination exceeded chance, we compared their AUC with the AUCs obtained by circularly shifting their outcome series against their predictions (every non-zero shift, in time order), which keeps the autocorrelation of both series. The one-sided p value was (1 + the number of shifts with an AUC at least as large as the observed one) divided by the number of observations, corrected within model.

R1 Window selection (Section 3.5)

A participant's most frequent window accounted for a median of 79% of folds in the EMA-only random forest models and 41% to 72% in the other models, and the selected window changed between consecutive days in 11% (EMA-only random forest) to 57% (combined hurdle) of cases. This stability check is lenient, because neighboring folds share most of their training days. Supplementary Table S4 gives median AUC by window. For the passive-only models the shortest windows gave the lowest AUCs (.51 for 1 hour in all four models and .51 to .52 for 4 hours), against .52 to .54 for the 1-day and 7-day windows. For the combined models, AUC varied little by window (.52 to .55).

Choosing the window per person did not improve discrimination over a single fixed window. AUC differed in none of 40 adaptive-versus-fixed comparisons (all q > .14). For error metrics, adaptive selection was better in 9 of 40 comparisons on MAE (3 worse), 8 on RMSE (2 worse), and 7 on R² (2 worse), but the differences were small (median MAE differences of 0.00 to 0.08 points on the 0 to 100 scale). They were concentrated in the passive-only hurdle models compared with 1-hour and 4-hour windows and in the combined random forest models compared with the 1-day and 7-day windows.
