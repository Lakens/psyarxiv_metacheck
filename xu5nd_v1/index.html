Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

If you want to extend the package to be able to download files from additional data repositories reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. Abstract

The Theory of Dyadic Morality (TDM) argues that moral condemnation revolves around intuitive perceptions of victimization. We provide a curated collection of items for measuring moral judgment and perceived victimization, organized around one question: "From someone's perspective, who is victimizing whom, how, and to what extent?" Each part of the question connects to a core element of our moral psychology, including the dyad itself (an intentional agent causing damage to a vulnerable victim), the continuum of perceived victimization (mild to severe), different kinds of victimization (e.g., physical violence versus spiritual suffering), and the important worldviews that shape perceived victimization (e.g., cultural and politics). We show how classic paradigms, from moral dilemmas to moral dumbfounding, can be measured with TDM, and we outline future directions, including how TDM can align artificial intelligence with human moral judgment.

Theory of Dyadic Morality and its Debates

Before we unpack this key question and its measures, we summarize TDM and its main debate with Moral Foundations Theory (MFT; Graham et al., 2013). MFT explains differences in moral judgment via four (Haidt & Joseph, 2004), six (Atari et al., 2023), or potentially "74, or perhaps 122, or 27" (Graham et al., 2013, p. 58) distinct moral foundations or "modules" (Fodor, 1983). Some modules are argued to be possessed by everyone (e.g., fairness), while others possessed primarily by conservatives (e.g., purity)-giving them a moral "advantage" (Graham et al., 2009).

In contrast to the "modular" MFT, TDM is a constructionist theory of morality (Cameron et al., 2015). Constructionist theories are best known from emotion research (Lindquist, 2013), and challenge the idea that the mind is divided into a fixed number of distinct psychological categories (Feldman Barrett, 2011). Instead, they suggest psychological experiences are constructed by combining basic cognitive processes (Lindquist et al., 2012). TDM roots moral judgments in theory of mind (intentionality; Young et al., 2007), causal reasoning (how harm is caused; Cushman, 2008), and empathy (vulnerability; Batson et al., 1981; Decety & Cowell, 2014).

Together, the dyadic elements form a cognitive prototype of immorality. Drawing on general principles of categorization (Rosch, 1978), TDM predicts that acts matching this prototype elicit stronger moral condemnation, a prediction supported by work suggesting condemnation of acts that violate moral foundations hinges upon people's perception of those acts as causing victimization (Ochoa, 2022).

However, people seem to condemn "harmless wrong" like Haidt's famous examples of consensual incest. But, as with moral judgment (Haidt, 2001), understandings of harm are intuitive (Gendler, 2008) and people condemn these acts because of gut feelings that sexting your sibling might cause harm (Gray et al., 2014; Royzman et al., 2015)-an intuition that seems both evolutionary adaptive (Gray, 2025) and societally sensible (Shor & Simchai, 2009).

Critics argue TDM is reductionist, overlooking moral pluralism. Although TDM seeks a parsimonious account of moral cognition (Schein & Gray, 2018), it accommodates political and cultural diversity through differing perceptions of victimization-who can be an agent, what causes harm, and who is vulnerable (Womick et al., 2026). Explaining moral diversity through differing conceptions of harm has a long tradition, including Shweder's account of culturally distinct "ontologies of suffering" (Shweder et al., 1997).

A Measure of Perceived Victimization

Despite the power of perceived victimization in our minds and in society, there is no onestop guide for measuring moral judgment and people's perceptions of interpersonal victimization. There are questionnaires for measuring values (e.g., (Atari et al., 2023; Janoff-Bulman & Carnes, 2013; Schwartz, 1992), measures of moral conviction (Skitka et al., 2005) and measures of moral expansiveness (Crimston et al., 2016). But there is no standard measure for harm-based morality.

One reason for this gap is that these questions have long been hiding in plain sight.

Psychologists, anthropologists, philosophers, and legal scholars have long studied intentional agency, harmful acts, and victim vulnerability (Hart & Honoré, 1985; Shweder et al., 1997; Weiner, 1995). A second reason is the dominant paradigm in moral psychology has treated harm and victimization as an objective fact rather than a subjective perception, where researchers simply declare acts harmful or harmless, rather than asking people whether, and to what extent, they perceive victimization. This guide outlines how to measure moral judgment and perceived victimization. Given the hundreds of papers measuring harm and its elements, this guide is less a new questionnaire than a curated collection of items, drawn on years of work on TDM and from growing interest in dyadic morality across fields (Gray & Pratt, 2025; Ochoa, 2022). We break harm into its parts with a single guiding question-From someone's perspective: Who is victimizing whom, how, and to what extent? We provide measures for each part, both for specific acts and general character. We then explain how the parts of the dyad shape one another and how dyadic measures shed new light on classic paradigms in moral psychology. We end with directions for future research.

Initial Caveats

Before turning to measurement, four clarifications are worth noting. First, other than briefly above, there is not enough space in this piece to revisit debates about the structure of moral judgment-which are discussed in more detail elsewhere (e.g., Graham et al., 2013; Gray & Pratt, 2025; Schein & Gray, 2018). Second, although TDM has touched on both evil and goodness (e.g., Gray & Wegner, 2011a), here we focus on moral condemnation. Being good is important, and people often see themselves as heroes, as we explore below (i.e., MIPS, Goranson et al., 2022) but-empirically speaking-bad is stronger than good, both in the mind (Baumeister et al., 2001) and in society (Brady et al., 2017).

Third, as phenomena-focused psychologists, we aim to capture laypeople's perceptions of victimhood rather than philosophers' definitions. Some measurement imprecision is inevitable given the fuzziness of concepts like victimhood. Fourth, there is no single "true" measure of harm. As scientists, our north star of measurement should be construct validity, not allegiance to a particular questionnaire.

Measuring Moral Judgment

Understanding perceived victimization's role in moral judgment requires carefully measuring moral judgment itself. To do so, simply ask someone how "immoral" or how "morally wrong" an act is. Avoid asking about only "wrong" or "bad"-people can use the "wrong" fork at dinner and see a "bad" movie, but neither is immoral.

If you want to avoid the term "immoral"-which can be difficult to translate across cultures-you can ask about the consequences of moral condemnation, like blame or punishment (Cushman, 2008). For example, past work suggests Chinese participants perceive reading someone's diary without permission as equally "immoral" as murder. But they judge murderers as more deserving of jail than illicit diary-readers (Buchtel et al., 2015).

If you want to evaluate the person rather than the act, ask how immoral the person seems (Schein & Gray, 2018) or whether they have bad moral character (Hartman et al., 2022). Or, for a more concrete assessment, ask whether you would trust them to babysit your children.

Whatever the measure, measuring moral judgment separately from perceived victimization allows researchers to test how much victimization explains condemnation.

A Framework for Measuring Perceived Victimization

Victimization may seem obvious-like pornography, people "know it when they see it."

Yet psychology must formalize these perceptions, especially because researchers often overlook others' genuine perceptions of victimization. Our framework for measuring perceived victimization is captured by a comprehensive yet parsimonious question:

From someone's perspective: Who is victimizing whom-how, and to what extent?

There are 4 levels in this question, if you move from the middle outwards: At Level 0: is victimizing-the essential core of TDM. Next at Level 1 are the entities seen as victimizer (who; the agent) and victim (whom; the patient). Then at Level 2 is how, the kind of victimization as dictated by the act (e.g., financial, spiritual), and also to what extent-the perceived severity of this harm from mild to extreme. The outermost layer, Level 3, is Someone's perspective, the cultural beliefs, political leanings, and individual history of the perceiver, which shapes all these judgments. We describe each part below, from the core outward, see Figure 1. The outer bands (Level 3, in green) captures perceptions and how differences between perceivers influence their perceptions. Figure 3 and Table 1 provide measures for each element.

Before unpacking the levels, we make one more distinction: between acts and character.

Most moral judgments concern acts, such as dog-kicking or bribe-taking, but acts also reveal character (Goodwin et al., 2014; Uhlmann & Zhu, 2014). Judgments of character can be harsher than judgments of acts (Tannenbaum et al., 2011), and often reflect concerns about the person's capacity to cause future victimization (Anderson et al., 2024). Most components of our guiding question can be measured for both acts (whether an act was intended, how much a victim suffered in a specific event) and character (someone's general capacity to harm or suffer).

Level 0: "Is Victimizing"

The core of dyadic morality is the perception harm/victimhood is occurring. The simplest measure to assess perceived harm is "How harmful is this act?" and to assess perceived victimization "How victimizing is this act?". Despite their simplicity, these items are strongly associated with moral condemnation (e.g., r =.63; Gray et al., 2022).

Although perceived harm predicts moral judgment, the two remain conceptually distinct: it is logically possible to condemn an act without perceiving it as harmful (Haidt et al., 2000; Kant, 2002). But this philosophical possibility seems empirically unsupported: even when acts are explicitly described as harmless, people intuitively perceive harm and condemn accordingly (Gray et al., 2014; Royzman et al., 2015). The key word is intuitively: people's perceptions of harm can persist even when explicitly argued against. Therefore, consider assessing harm/victimhood with intuitive measures, including the semantic Affective Misattribution Paradigm (AMP; Payne et al., 2010) and judgments under time pressure (Gray et al., 2014).

Experiments using these measures reveal perceptions of harm are often more intuitive than reasoned.

Level 1: "Who" and "Whom"

Typical victimization involves a victimizer (Who) harming a victim (Whom), forming the agent and patient of the moral dyad (Schein & Gray, 2018).

Who (The Agent)

The agent is the person victimizing and can be assessed by considering their role in the act itself or their character.

Act. Questions of mental agency are central to moral judgments of agents' acts, especially intentionality and foresight (Cushman, 2008; Young et al., 2007)-which are linked to attitude moralization (e.g., D'Amore et al., 2022). Researchers can ask, "How much did [agent] intend [outcome]?" or "Did [agent] know [outcome] would occur?" Intentions can be distinguished from motives (why someone acted) and desires (what they wanted), which also shape judgments (Carlson et al., 2022; Malle & Knobe, 1997). To establish responsibility for harm, researchers can ask how much an agent's actions-and their mental states-caused the outcome (Cushman, 2008; Lagnado & Channon, 2008) and whether they could have prevented an outcome (Malle et al., 2014; Spellman & Gilbert, 2014).

Character. People differ in their overall perceived agency, their general capacity to intend, plan, remember, and exert self-control (Gray et al., 2007)-which can be assessed via mind perception measures (Gray et al., 2007), which reliably predict judgments of blame, responsibility, and punishment (Gray et al., 2012; Gray & Wegner, 2009). TDM, with its roots in the idea of victims as "prey" (Gray, 2025), also suggests measuring how much a person seems like a predator, should be a strong predictor of moral judgment (Scrivner et al., 2020). These moral character judgments revolve around someone's general capacity for or tendency of causing harm.

Whom (The Victim)

The victim is the person victimized and can be assessed by considering their role in the act itself or their character.

Act.

To assess how much an act harmed a victim, researchers can ask "How much was [victim] harmed" or "mistreated" or "victimized." (adapted from; Womick et al., 2026). To capture suffering more directly, they can ask "How much did [the victim] suffer?" (Gray et al., 2014; Schein et al., 2016).

Victimization varies in scale, from individual victims to groups facing institutional discrimination or entire populations experiencing genocide (Kubin & Gray, 2026). Related research on collective victimhood examines how histories of group suffering can shape who is seen as the victim in intergroup conflicts (Noor, 2012; Vollhardt, 2009). Researchers should assess both the perceived scale of victimization and collective victimhood to understand their roles in moral condemnation.

Character. How generally vulnerable to victimization is an entity? Researchers might use mind-perception items that tap the dimensions of experience, the capacity to feel emotions and sensations, including pain and fear (Gray et al., 2007). But having the general ability to suffer is not necessarily the same as being vulnerable to victimization. A child and a boxer can both feel pain, but kids seem more vulnerable. This is why the Assumptions of Vulnerability (AoV; Womick et al., 2026) scale measures how much a target seems "especially vulnerable" to victimization.

Different assumptions of vulnerability (AoVs) explain moral disagreement across many contentious issues, from abortion to immigration. Womick et al. (2026) identify four clusters of potential victims: the Othered (e.g., immigrants and marginalized groups), the Environment (e.g., animals and ecosystems), the Powerful (e.g., police officers and business leaders), and the Divine (e.g., God and sacred texts). Liberals tend to see the Othered and Environment as especially vulnerable to victimization, whereas conservatives see all clusters as similarly vulnerable (Figure 2). These perceptions predict moral judgments beyond moral-foundations, and experimentally changing perceived vulnerability shifts moral evaluations.

Beyond perceptions of these specific groups, liberals and conservatives differ in how they generally understand vulnerability. Liberals focus on group-based differences in vulnerability, dividing the moral into invulnerable oppressors (e.g., the Powerful) and the vulnerable oppressed (e.g., the Othered). Conservatives focus more on individual vulnerability, emphasizing anyone can be victimized regardless of group membership. Thus, TDM suggests moral disagreement stems not from disagreement about the importance of harm but stems instead from different assumptions about who is most vulnerable to it (Womick et al., 2026).

Level 2: "How" and "To What Extent"

Moral controversy seems to revolve around questions victimhood, but not all acts of victimization are the same, and not all acts are seen as equally harmful.

How. How exactly is victimhood occurring? Harms can include physical, financial, spiritual, and symbolic victimization, which can range from tangible to abstract (Kubin & Gray, 2026). A punch to the face is concrete, whereas systemic discrimination feels more abstractrequiring greater inference, and thus more room for disagreement between perceivers.

Researchers can measure the type of victimhood perceived (e.g., bodily, financial, symbolic) and its tangibility (Kubin & Gray, 2026). Related distinctions include realistic versus symbolic threats (Kachanoff et al., 2021; Stephan et al., 2016), action versus omission (Spranca et al., 1991), and direct versus indirect victimization (Paharia et al., 2009).

To what extent. How severe is the victimhood? Gossip may hurt your feelings, while theft may hurt your feelings and your finances. TDM defines victimhood as a perceived continuum rather than a binary, with greater perceived harm predicting stronger condemnation (Schein & Gray, 2018). Researchers should therefore use continuous rating scales, such as "How victimizing is this act?" or "How harmful is this act?" from 1 (not at all) to 5 (extremely). In their studies, they should also sample many different acts-from missing the bus to murderspanning the severity continuum (Gray et al., 2022; Schein & Gray, 2015). Judgments of character should similarly use continuous ratings.

Level 3: "Someone's Perspective"

TDM is about perceived harm, and the outermost layer captures the perceiver, whose beliefs, experiences, and identity shape what counts as victimization. Two people may both witness two social media posts-one misgendering someone, another blaspheming God-and see vastly different amounts of harm in each.

Perception.

Researchers should ask what participants perceive rather than assume victimhood is present. Questions can use language such as "How much does it seem [victim] was hurt?" or "I believe [target] is vulnerable to victimization" (Womick et al., 2026). Items focusing on feelings, beliefs and intuitions (Gray et al., 2014) help to capture subjectivity.

The perceiver. People see victimization (and therefore morality) differently because of their worldviews (e.g., Biedron et al., 2024). A Brahmin widow eating fish, a teenager swearing at his mother, or someone burning a flag may seem harmless to some but deeply harmful to others. Cross-cultural research shows people differ in their "ontologies of suffering"understandings of what acts cause harm and how. For example, Indians (more than Americans) link food impurity to harming a dead parent's soul (Shweder et al., 1987 (Shweder et al., , 1997)). As discussed above, people with different political leanings make different assumptions of vulnerability (Womick et al., 2026), likely because they differ in the extent they attribute entities' circumstances to their own decisions versus structural forces beyond their control (Skitka et al., 2002; Zucker & Weiner, 1993).

In addition to measuring various cultural and political variables, researchers can assess individual differences in processes connected to TDM, including empathy, pain sensitivity, and victimhood mindsets, all of which shape harm perceptions (Davis, 1983; Gabay et al., 2020; Gollwitzer & Rothmund, 2011; Lee & Ma, 2023). someone's perspective, who is victimizing whom, how, and to what extent?" Perceptions of Agents and Victims Impact Each Other

The moral dyad consists of agents and victims, but both entities are psychologically interconnected through dyadic completion and moral typecasting.

Dyadic completion.

Cognitive templates are dynamic, and are subject to coherence processes (Thagard & Verbeurgt, 1998), whereby the mind fills in missing information to form a complete Gestalt. This is why, when looking at the Kanizsa triangle, ones' mind automatically perceives a triangle despite its "objective" absence. Likewise, our mind strives to perceive a complete "moral dyad" in ambiguous circumstances.

Victim dyadic completion.

When people learn about an evil agent, they "complete" the presence of a suffering victim (Gray et al., 2014), explaining why people tend to not see "wrongs" as "harmless" (Schein & Gray, 2018). Even watching someone get harmed makes people attribute more mind to the victim, whether a vegetative patient, a robot, or a corpse (Ward et al., 2013). Victim completion is why researchers must measure intuitions of victimization, since post-hoc rationalization often dampens intuitive perceptions of victimization (Gray et al., 2014).

Agent dyadic completion.

When someone appears to suffer unjustly (i.e., victimization) people thirst for an agent to blame. Agentic dyadic completion explains why people see powerful agents (e.g., God, or the government) behind events that cause mass suffering (Gray & Wegner, 2010), and they readily find other culprits for victimizing events ranging from pandemics to recessions. To measure agent completion, researchers can present suffering without a stated cause and ask who is responsible, how much they are to blame, and whether the harm seems intentional.

Moral typecasting.

Courtrooms revolve around competing moral roles (Gray et al., 2026)-who is really the victim and who is really the perpetrator-and the same is true of everyday life. This tension between being either a perpetrator or victim is called "moral typecasting" (Gray & Wegner, 2009).

Victims are not villains. People portrayed as victims receive less blame for subsequent wrongdoing (Effron & Miller, 2012). In one study, participants blamed a person less for stealing $10 when he had previously been described as a victim rather than heroic or neutral (Gray & Wegner, 2011). Researchers can test this effect by manipulating prior victimhood and measuring blame for later offenses.

Villains are not victims. Once someone is cast as a perpetrator, this label can be remarkably difficult to shake. For example, in the criminal justice system, people can continue to be perceived as perpetrators even after exoneration, contributing to the continued condemnation of those cleared of wrongdoing (Gray et al., 2026). Importantly, the persistence of this label also makes it difficult for perpetrators to subsequently be seen as victims: wrongdoers are perceived as less sensitive to pain and less capable of suffering harm themselves (Gray & Wegner, 2009).

This tendency extends beyond villains to people who are seen as more generally "agentic". For example, heroes are also perceived as less vulnerable to harm, whether they are doctors or veterans (Goranson et al., 2022; Shepherd et al., 2019). Researchers can examine moral typecasting by manipulating whether someone is portrayed as a victim or agent, and measuring both blame for subsequent wrongdoing and perceived suffering from subsequent harmful acts.

How TDM Explains Moral Paradigms

Moral psychology has a handful of classic paradigms, which often involve researchers making assumptions about harm. Researchers tell participants an act is harmless (Haidt et al., 2000), and assume killing five people involves more harm than killing one (Greene et al., 2001), and then interpret judgments in light of those assumptions. TDM suggests we should measure perceived victimization whenever moral judgment is measured-and that this perceived harm might help explain some of these classic effects.

Pluralism: Taxonomies, Cultures, and Politics

Moral judgment is pluralistic, involving discussions of many different values and frequent disagreement across politics and culture. Many moral taxonomies explain pluralism by arguing liberals and conservative have distinct moral foundations (Graham et al., 2009), but moral diversity does not require distinct cognitive mechanisms. Not only are moral foundations not distinct (Graham et al., 2011), but perceived victimization predicts condemnation across foundations and political groups (Schein & Gray, 2015), especially concerning hot-button issues (Womick et al., 2026).

TDM offers an alternative taxonomy of morality that can account for moral pluralism without assuming distinct psychological mechanisms for different moral domains. Rather than categorizing moral violations into separate foundations, TDM organizes them around a common set of components: perceptions of who is victimizing whom, how, and to what extent. This framework captures moral diversity by allowing people across cultures and political groups to disagree about who is vulnerable, what constitutes harm, and how severe that harm is.

Affect and Disgust

Moral psychologists have long pointed to examples of immoral acts like sex with a chicken carcass or eating a dead pet provoking disgust, as evidence that disgust-rather than harm/victimization-drives moral condemnation (Rozin, 1999). Yet, after correcting for publication bias, disgust has no effect on moral judgment (Landy & Goodwin, 2015). In fact, perceived victimization helps explain condemnation even for purity violations, often predicting moral judgment better than disgust (Schein et al., 2016).

While emotions do not map neatly onto moral violations (Cameron et al., 2015), affect, of course still matters. The affective harm account (AHA) proposes perceived victimization involves an intuitive feeling someone is being hurt, distinct from general arousal or disgust (Gray et al., 2022). Researchers should therefore measure perceived victimization separately from reactions such as disgust, weirdness, and unpleasantness, then test which best predicts condemnation.

Moral Dilemmas

Moral dilemmas have long helped psychologists understand moral reasoning and cognition. For example, in the classic trolley dilemma, most people would pull a switch to sacrifice one person and save five, but refuse to push someone off a bridge to achieve the same outcome (Greene et al., 2001)-highlighting people's greater reluctance to cause harm through direct, personal actions. These judgments are often explained through competing ethical principles or the tension between reasoning and emotion (Conway & Gawronski, 2013; Greene et al., 2001).

From a TDM perspective, trolley dilemmas involve competing moral dyads: allowing the trolley to continue victimizes five people, while intervening victimizes one (Gray & Schein, 2012). TDM suggests people's decisions depend on how they perceive these competing harms.

When pulling a switch, the death of one person is a side effect of saving five others. In contrast, in the footbridge version of the dilemma, a person must physically push a stranger off a bridge and onto the tracks to stop the trolley from killing five people. Here, saving the five requires deliberately harming one person, making their victimization more intentional and salient, and increasing reluctance to intervene (Greene & Haidt, 2002). Consistent with this account, research finds people are more reluctant to sacrifice one person when they vividly imagine the harm that person would suffer (Amit & Greene, 2012).

To better understand how people make moral decisions in dilemmas, researchers can measure how much harm participants perceive in each possible outcome, including the suffering of those who would be harmed and those who would be saved. These measures complement approaches such as process dissociation and the Consequences, Norms and Inaction (CNI) model (Conway & Gawronski, 2013; Gawronski et al., 2017), helping explain why people favor some decisions over others. This approach also applies to real-world dilemmas, such as weighing the harms of pandemic restrictions against the risks of infection, or medical triage decisions in which doctors must decide which patients receive life-saving treatment.

Moral Dumbfounding

In a classic scenario, adult siblings Julie and Mark have consensual, protected sex, yet participants condemn the act even when they cannot explain why (Haidt, 2001; Haidt et al., 2000). This moral dumbfounding is often interpreted as evidence that condemnation can occur without perceived harm. But describing an act as harmless does not mean participants perceive it that way. Many doubt the scenario's assumptions or perceive victimization, and genuine dumbfounding becomes rare when these participants are excluded (Royzman et al., 2015). Many see harm in these acts intuitively-especially when under time pressure (Gray et al., 2014).

Researchers should therefore assess whether participants believe the act is harmless, whether they perceive a victim, and how severe the victimization seems, using both intuitive and deliberative measures.

Perceiving Your Own Morality

Moral dyads not only shape how we judge others, but also how we see ourselves. Just as we perceive others as perpetrators or victims, we can also see ourselves as people who cause harm or suffer it. But moral interactions are not limited to harm: people can be heroes who help others or beneficiaries who receive help. Together, these four moral roles offer distinct ways for people to understand their own moral identities. Traditional moral-identity measures assess how central being a good person is to one's identity (Aquino & Reed, 2002), but identifying as a hero differs from identifying as a victim, even if both feel morally good. The Moral Identity Picture Scale (MIPS) measures identification with four moral roles through illustrated interactions (Goranson et al., 2022; Figure 3). These identities are distinct and associated with different psychological profiles: heroes report greater empathy and self-efficacy, villains greater narcissism and deception, and victims greater depression and lower self-efficacy, offering insight into how individuals perceive their own morality.

Future Research

Social Media and Text Analyses. Social media amplifies moral outrage (Brady et al., 2017 (Brady et al., , 2021)), making it an important context for studying perceived victimization. Research increasingly highlights the role of perceived victimhood in online moral conflicts. For example, virality can make victimhood seem more widespread (Puryear et al., 2024), while debates over social media content moderation involve competing perceptions of who is being victimized (Kubin et al., 2025). Researchers can measure these perceptions directly or use text analysis to identify perceived victims, perpetrators, and harms in online discussions. Language models offer scalable tools for this task, provided their judgments are validated against human ratings (Ochoa, 2022; Rathje et al., 2024).

Large Language Models.

People are often reluctant to trust machines with moral decisions (Bigman & Gray, 2018). However, recent research suggests AI language models make better moral judgments when first prompted to assess harm, help, intentionality, and vulnerability. This approach, known as a cognitive "bottleneck," guides models through the dyadic template before making a moral judgment, improving both their predictions and perceived trustworthiness (Dillion et al., 2026; Koh et al., 2020). The same template can be written as an algebraic equation (Varshney, 2026), which makes TDM unusually easy to build into machines. Future research can examine where human and machine perceptions of victimization diverge and whether dyadic reasoning makes AI moral judgments more transparent and reliable.

Conclusion

Although moral judgments vary considerably across individuals, cultures, and political groups, TDM suggests they share a common psychological foundation in perceived victimization. Understanding moral judgment requires researchers to examine not only whether people condemn an act, but also who they perceive as causing harm, who they believe is vulnerable, and how severely they believe someone has suffered. This paper provides a framework for measuring these perceptions, organized around one guiding question: From someone's perspective, who is victimizing whom, how, and to what extent? By systematically measuring these components, researchers can test how perceptions of victimization shape moral condemnation and help explain why people reach different moral judgments about the same acts.
