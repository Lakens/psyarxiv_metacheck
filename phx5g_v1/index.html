To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 2 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

The present study optimized, validated, and refined the Chinese version of the Barcelona Music Reward Questionnaire (C-BMRQ) to address key limitations of the existing culturally adapted iteration. We aimed to deliver a psychometrically robust, culturally tailored tool for assessing music reward sensitivity-a core component of musical aesthetic experience and creative engagementamong Chinese populations. Two sequential validation studies were conducted: Study 1 completed preliminary optimization and validation with two Beijing samples (Ns = 387, 391); Study 2 comprehensively verified the scale's psychometric properties using three independent nationwide datasets plus Study 1's second-round data (N = 1,650). Rather than developing a novel scale, we employed a rigorous culture-adaptive strategy to optimize the existing version, preserving its original 20-item, five-factor structure (Music Seeking, Sensorimotor, Emotion Regulation, Emotional Evocation, Social Reward) without item deletion. Validation results confirmed the optimized C-BMRQ exhibited a stable five-factor model, strong internal consistency and test-retest reliability, configural and metric measurement invariance across gender and musical-training groups, and satisfactory convergent validity. Notably, the 10th percentile corresponded to a C-BMRQ total score of 69 in the pooled sample, providing a sample-derived reference value for low music reward sensitivity in Chinese adults. This value is intended for research use and requires independent clinical validation before being interpreted diagnostically. The optimized C-BMRQ is a reliable, culturally resonant instrument for Chinese populations, suitable for investigating musical aesthetic experiences, creativity in musical engagement, and cross-cultural differences in music reward processing, with meaningful implications for aesthetic neuroscience, creative arts research, and clinical practice.

[div-01]

Music elicits profound physiological and emotional responses in listeners (Blood & Zatorre, 2001; Salimpoor et al., 2009), yielding pleasure and aesthetic experiences that are collectively recognized as "musical reward" (Brattico & Pearce, 2013; Zatorre & Salimpoor, 2013). However, not everyone perceives music as rewarding. While some individuals are deeply immersed in extremely beautiful music, they often experience "chills", manifested as scalp tingling and goosebumps (Panksepp, 1995) and subsequently activating the brain's reward system and releasing dopamine (Salimpoor et al., 2011). Others remain indifferent-they retain the ability to enjoy physical rewards such as food and money (Mas-Herrero et al., 2014), but exhibit markedly reduced musical reward responsiveness, which is known as "music anhedonia" (Martinez-Molina et al., 2016 , 2019). This specific individual difference necessitates specialized measurement tools. Conventional general reward scales (e.g., monetary or food reward measures) are ill-suited for quantifying musical reward sensitivity, as they fail to capture domain-specific dimensions of musical experience, making the Barcelona Musical Reward Questionnaire (BMRQ) a critical tool for this specific construct.

The Barcelona Music Reward Questionnaire (BMRQ) comprises five dimensions (4 items each)-Music Seeking, Sensorimotor, Emotion Regulation, Emotional Evocation, and Social Reward -and 20 items scored on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = "not at all",5 = "extremely" ). It has demonstrated robust reliability and validity across diverse Western adult and adolescent populations, including North America (Mas-Herrero et al., 2013), South America (e.g., Brazil : Pereira & Bortoloti, 2025), European countries (e.g., Spain: Mas-Herrero et al., 2013; France: Saliba et al., 2016; Denmark: Lippolis et al., 2025; Italy: Mannino et al., 2024) and also East Asia (e.g., China: Wang et al., 2023 and Japan: Honda et al., 2026).

Accumulating evidence suggests that musical reward sensitivity is shaped by a range of demographic and individual cognitive factors, with notable cross-cultural variability (Belfi et al., 2022; Mas-Herrero et al., 2013; Kreutz & Cui, 2022). Among these moderators, gender and musical training are the most robust and consistently replicated predictors of individual differences in musical reward experience across cultural contexts. Higher musical reward sensitivity was found in females (Fuentes-Sánchez et al., 2025; Mas-Herrero et al., 2013; Wang et al., 2023) and musicians (Fuentes-Sánchez et al., 2025; Mas-Herrero et al., 2013; Matthews et al., 2024).

Given profound cross-cultural divergence in musical aesthetic templates and perceptual frameworks between Eastern and Western populations, simple literal translation is insufficient for scale adaptation; rigorous relocalization is therefore crucial when adapting the BMRQ for Chinese contexts. Wang et al. (2023) undertook an initial Chinese adaptation of the BMRQ, laying a foundational groundwork for research on musical reward within Chinese-speaking contexts.

Nevertheless, this initial adaptation carries certain practical and psychometric limitations that motivate further refinement: in terms of reliability, its highly homogeneous sample of students from only four universities in Changsha undermines generalizability, and the absence of a retesting procedure leaves test-retest reliability unexamined; regarding validity, translations with limited cultural contextual consideration yielded low factor loadings, prompting unjustified item deletions that reduced construct validity-some dimensions were left with only two items-while the lack of norm development further restricts the scale's applicability and score interpretation.

In the present investigation, we aimed to address critical limitations in the existing Chinese adaptation of the BMRQ through a rigorous re-localization process. In Study 1, we re-validated the BMRQ using a nationwide sample and compared four versions: two versions developed in the present study (a 20-item literal translation and a 20-item culturally adapted translation), and two versions from Wang et al. (2023), including the 20-item original translation and their 16-item revised version, with the goal of selecting the optimal Chinese version. We hypothesized that, while retaining the full 20-item structure of the original scale, the culturally adapted version would yield improved model-fit statistics, higher factor loadings, and higher internal consistency relative to the literal translation.

Study 2 further validated the optimal candidate version by evaluating its test-retest reliability and convergent validity against two theoretically relevant constructs: general reward sensitivity measured by the Chinese Physical Anhedonia Scale (CPAS), and music-related sophistication measured by the Goldsmiths Musical Sophistication Index (Gold-MSI).

Method

Participants

Two rounds of data collection were conducted online through Wenjuanxing (https://www.wjx.cn/) in the Beijing area. Data from the first round served for the preliminary validation in Study 1, whereas data from the second round were reserved for the pooled dataset reported in Study 2. In the first-round collection, 453 participants aged 18-35 years finished the literally-translated version of BMRQ, and in the second-round collection, 430 participants aged 18-35 years finished the adjusted-translated version of BMRQ (C-BMRQ). We excluded participants who failed the attention check (66 in the first round and 39 in the second round). The final sample for Study 1 (N = 387) came from the first-round collection (156 males, 231 females, 23.42 ± 3.54 years old). The second-round valid sample (N= 391; 123 males, 268 females, 22.46 ± 2.90 years old) is incorporated into Study 2's pooled dataset (see Study 2 Participants section). The prespecified five-factor, 20-item CFA model was fitted separately in each sample. Both samples exceeded the COSMIN recommendation of at least seven participants per item and N ≥ 100 for factor analysis, corresponding to a minimum N of 140 for the present 20-item scale (Mokkink et al., 2010; Terwee et al., 2012). This criterion was treated as a practical sample-adequacy benchmark rather than evidence of model-specific statistical power. In the first-round collection, 74 participants (26 males, 48 females) reported having professional music training before (mean duration: 6-9 years) and 30 participants (9 males, 21 females) reported having professional music training (mean duration: 3-5 years) in the second round. There was no overlap between the two rounds. All participants were provided with digital informed consent and paid 5 RMB for their participation. Measures

Barcelona Music Reward Questionnaire (BMRQ).

The English version of BMRQ (Mas-Herrero et al., 2013) was a 20-item self-report measure structured into five theoretically derived facets-Musical Seeking, Emotion Evocation, Mood Regulation, Sensory-Motor, and Social Reward -each comprising four items designed to assess distinct dimensions of musical reward experiences.

Items 2 and Item 5 are reverse coded to mitigate acquiescence bias, requiring re-coding during data analysis. Participants respond to items on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = Fully disagree to5 = Fully agree), yielding total and subscale scores for each facet. Higher total scores indicate stronger overall musical reward sensitivity, while subscale scores reflect specific dimensions (e.g., a high "Emotion Evocation" score suggests greater emotional responsiveness to music).

Procedure

Guided by the International Test Commission (ITC) Guidelines for Translating and Adapting

Tests ( International Test Commission, 2005), we adopted a forward-backward translation procedure.

Two independent translators-an English major and a music psychology Ph.D. candidate-conducted separate forward translations from English to Chinese. Both possessed deep knowledge of Chinese culture and held either test translation experience or domain expertise in the scale's subject matter, ensuring accurate technical term translation. An expert then reviewed and reconciled the two Chinese versions. A backward translation was subsequently performed, with discrepancies resolved through comparison between the original and back-translated English versions. Finally, a bilingual psychology translator provided feedback on equivalence and finalized the "literally-translated version" through comprehensive revisions. The first-round online survey was administered to assess the version's reliability and validity.

As the literally-translated version exhibited insufficient construct validity, we revised it for Chinese cultural alignment and collected a second-round sample. For instance, "16. At a concert I feel connected to the performers and the audience." was initially translated as "在音乐会上， 我会觉得我与演奏者及其他听众的心是相通的 "; however, concerts are a highly specialized and infrequently experienced form within Chinese music performances, which limits the generalizability of statements related to them. Therefore, "concert" was replaced with the more inclusive term "music performance", transforming the English item into "在音乐表演中，我会 觉得我与表演者及其他观众的心是相通的" ("At a music performance, I feel a connection with the performers and other audience members"). Similarly, "10. Music often makes me dance." (initially translated as "我经常随音乐起舞"). Given that Chinese respondents tend to express their musical engagement in a more subtle and reserved manner rather than overt dancing, this item was revised to "听到节奏感强的音乐时，我有时会随之律动或摇摆" ("When exposed to strongly rhythmical music, I occasionally move or sway in time with the music."). Furthermore, certain phrases from the original English questionnaire, such as "7. I inform myself about music I like." (initially translated as "我很明确地知道我喜欢哪些音乐。") , proved ambiguous and hard to comprehend within the Chinese cultural context. Thus, it was revised to "I will actively seek out the music I like to listen to" ("我会主动寻找我喜欢的那些音乐") to better fit the music-seeking dimension. Moreover, overly intense frequency adverbs were toned down. For instance, "17. I spend quite a bit of money on music and related items." ("我在付费音 乐和周边产品上的花费颇多") was changed to "I am willing to spend money on musicrelated products and activities." ("我愿意在音乐相关的产品及活动上消费"). The comparison between the literally-translated and adjusted-translated versions is shown in Table S1 in the Supplemental materials.

Data analysis

All analyses were conducted in R (version 4.3.1) using the lavaan package (Rosseel, 2012) for structural equation modeling. Consistent with the structure reported in Mas-Herrero et al. (2013), Confirmatory Factor Analyses (CFAs) were carried out on each sample separately using maximum-likelihood estimation with 2000-iteration bootstrap standard errors. Model fit was evaluated using both absolute and relative fit indices: χ² test, Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA), Comparative Fit Index (CFI), and Tucker-Lewis Index (TLI). All standardized factor loadings and latent covariances were estimated and reported.

Study 2: Comprehensive Validation and Empirical Application of the C-BMRQ

Building on Study 1, which identified the optimal candidate Chinese version of the BMRQ (C-BMRQ), Study 2 focused on validating aspects left unaddressed. Although Study 1 established the C-BMRQ's basic psychometric properties, it did not evaluate its test-retest stability, subgroup equivalence, or convergent validity. In Study 2, we employed three datasets for further psychometric evaluation: one subsample drawn from Study 1 for retesting, and two datasets from separate independent behavioral studies that administered this candidate scale. This allowed us to: (1) evaluate three-month test-retest reliability; (2) examine score differences across gender and music-training groups; (3) establish normative score distributions for Chinese-adult participants; and (4) explore convergent validity via with theoretically relevant self-report instruments: correlations with the PAS and Gold-MSI.

Method

Participants Dataset 1 (N = 1,019): To further evaluate the validity and reliability of the optimal Chinese version of BMRQ (C-BMRQ), we recruited a new sample of 1,087 participants (aged 18-35 years) across China to complete the online survey. After excluding 68 participants who failed the attention check, the final sample consisted of 1,019 individuals (278 males, 741 females, 22.31 ± 2.80 years old). Of the final sample, 215 participants (68 males, 147 females) reported prior professional music training (mean duration: 3-5 years).

The five-factor CFA model specified in Study 1 was refitted to Dataset 1 (N = 1,019). This sample exceeded the COSMIN benchmark and fell within the range reported in published factor-analytic validations of other BMRQ versions (N = 209-1,550), supporting its adequacy for structural-validity analysis without implying model-specific statistical power.

Additionally, 472 participants (89 males, 383 females) from the final sample completed the retest of the C-BMRQ after 3 months, and 92 (12 males, 80 females) of them reported prior professional music training (mean duration: 3-5 years). Given the relatively large sample and well-identified measurement structure, the sample size was considered adequate for longitudinal CFA and measurement invariance testing (Wolf et al., 2013; Kline, 2023).

Datasets 2 and 3 (from other behavioral studies, N = 136 for Dataset 2 and N = 110 for Dataset 3): To enhance the generalizability of our findings, we also incorporated data from two unpublished independent behavioral studies that applied the C-BMRQ. In Dataset 2, after excluding 10 participants who failed the attention check, 136 participants (81 males, 55 females; 22.18±2.41 years old) were included, and their music training experience was not assessed. In Dataset 3, after excluding 3 participants who failed the attention check, 110 music responders (defined as individuals who experience "chill" responses to music; 48 males, 62 females; 23.53±2.93 years old) were included, and of these 84 participants (36 males, 48 females) had professional music training experience (mean duration: 2 years). These two subsets, drawn from separate unpublished behavioral studies, were used solely for convergent-validity correlation analyses. Given the size of these extracted subsets, correlational results should be interpreted with appropriate caution.

Pooled data (N = 1,650, Study 1-round 2 and Study 2): Subsequently, all samples that completed the C-BMRQ were pooled, including the second-round sample from Study 1 and the three datasets from the Study 2. Duplicate responses (5 from Dataset 2 and 1 from Dataset 3) were excluded by retaining only each participant's first submission, yielding a final combined sample of 1,650 participants (527 males, 1,123 females; 22.41±2.82 years old). This pooling strategy aimed to increase statistical power and account for potential variability across different subgroups.

Participant distributions across Study 1 and Study 2 datasets are presented in Table S2. 1 for more details).

Chinese versions of the Revised Physical Anhedonia Scale (CPAS).

We also employed the CPAS as a calibration scale for the reward-related pleasure sensitivity. (Chan et al., 2012). CPAS was translated from the Physical Anhedonia Scale (PAS; Chapman et al., 1976), which assesses self-reported deficits in experiencing pleasure from physical stimuli. Comprising 61 dichotomous (True/False) items, the scale evaluates an individual's inability to derive enjoyment from prototypical physical experiences, including gustatory (e.g., food), tactile, sexual, or environmental domains (e.g., relaxing settings). Total scores range from 0 to 61, with higher scores indicating greater severity of physical anhedonia. The CPAS has demonstrated good internal consistency (Cronbach's α = .86) in a non-clinical Chinese population in prior research (Chan et al., 2012).

Dataset 3.

Derived from an offline behavioral experiment, this dataset obtained demographic information, music training experience, and full-scale data for the C-BMRQ and Gold-MSI online via an online questionnaire on Tencent Questionnaire (https://wj.qq.com).

Data analysis

Reliability and Construct Validity. Reliability and construct validity were evaluated using Dataset 1 (N = 1019). Consistent with Study 1, we conducted all the validation analyses of the C-BMRQ in R (version 4.3.1) using the lavaan package (Rosseel, 2012), with the same model fit indices. To examine the test-retest stability of the construct, longitudinal measurement invariance (LMI) was evaluated using a series of nested single-group CFA models, testing configural, metric, scalar, and strict invariance (Brown, 2015). Model comparisons employed χ² difference tests, complemented by changes in the comparative fit index (ΔCFI ≤ 0.01) (Cheung & Rensvold, 2002) and the root mean square error of approximation (ΔRMSEA ≤ 0.015) (Chen, 2007). The most constrained model without significant deterioration in model fit was selected to confirm the stability of the measurement structure over time. Test-retest reliability was assessed using Spearman's rank-order correlation, and significance levels were adjusted using the Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons.

Construct equivalence.

We used pooled data excluding Dataset 2 (due to missing music training data; N = 1,519), to test the construct equivalence between groups defined by gender and music training using multigroup confirmatory factor analysis (MGCFA) (Brown, 2015).

Configural, metric, scalar, and strict invariance models were fitted sequentially, with model comparisons based on χ² difference tests, ΔCFI ≤ 0.01, and ΔRMSEA ≤ 0.015 to determine the highest level of invariance supported. Because the gender and training groups were unbalanced (male/female = 448/1071, trained/untrained = 329/1190), all estimates are presented as 95% confidence intervals derived from 1,000 bootstrap samples. For each grouping factor, we controlled for the other factor and used equal-sized subsamples based on the smaller group size to reduce potential bias from unequal group sizes in the invariance tests.

Musical Reward Sensitivity and Group Differences.

The overall level of musical reward sensitivity as measured by the C-BMRQ in the Chinese sample was examined using the pooled data (N = 1,650). Following Mas-Herrero et al. (2014), low music reward sensitivity was operationalized for descriptive research purposes as a C-BMRQ total score below the 10th percentile of the pooled sample. This sample-derived threshold was not treated as a clinically validated diagnostic cutoff for music anhedonia. To test the potential influencing factor of musical reward sensitivity, we employed a two-stage resampling approach to compare gender (male vs. female) and music training (with vs. without): for each of 1,000-iteration bootstrap subsamples with replacement, matching the size of the smaller group, we performed 1,000 permutation tests on the t-statistic for mean-difference. This yielded an observed t-value and its percentile position within the permuted t-distribution for each subsample. Statistical significance was determined by examining whether the 95% confidence interval of this percentile excluded α = 0.05. Because music training data were not collected in Dataset 2, this dataset was excluded from analyses comparing music training groups.

Convergent Validity. Spearman's rank-order correlations were carried out to examine the associations between the C-BMRQ and CPAS, Gold-MSI in separately in Datasets 2 and 3.

Significance levels were adjusted using the Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons.

Results and Discussion

Model Fit and Factor Structure of the C-BMRQ

With a larger sample (N = 1019), the model fit of the original five-factor structure improved and reached an acceptable level (χ²(160) = 866.69, p < .001, CFI = 0.921, TLI = 0.906, RMSEA (90% CI) = 0.066 (0.062-0.070)). The absolute value of factor loadings ranged from 0.55 to 0.80 and all were statistically significant (ps < .001, Figure 1).

Longitudinal Stability and Test-Retest Reliability of the C-BMRQ

Table S4 shows fit indices for a series of nested longitudinal CFA models examined using the first test and retest datasets from the sample in Study 1 (N = 472). Longitudinal measurement invariance analyses supported configural invariance (CFI = 0.921, TLI = 0.909, RMSEA = 0.051, 90% CI [0.048, 0.055]) and metric invariance (Δχ²(Δdf) = 15.00 (15), p = .451). The scalar model showed acceptable absolute fit (CFI = 0.920, TLI = 0.912, RMSEA = 0.050, 90% CI [0.047, 0.054]), but fit deteriorated significantly relative to the metric model (Δχ²(Δdf) = 35.26 (20), p = .019), suggesting partial scalar non-invariance across test and retest. Crucially, partial scalar noninvariance should not be taken as evidence of poor reliability or structural instability. Given the supported configural and metric invariance, the C-BMRQ showed stable factor structure and factor loadings across test and retest, although some item intercepts varied over time. This may reflect shifts in baseline response levels or response thresholds, consistent with the view that music reward sensitivity can vary with time, context, and experience rather than being completely fixed (Mas-Herrero et al., 2013; Mas-Herrero et al., 2026). Taken together, these results indicated that the factor structure and factor loadings of C-BMRQ were equivalent between the first test and retest (see Figure S1 for details).

Complementing the longitudinal invariance results, the C-BMRQ total score and all five subscale scores showed strong positive test-retest correlations (ρs = 0.64-0.75, ps < .001, Bonferroni-corrected) (Figure 2), further supporting the temporal stability and reliability of the scale.

Measurement Invariance Across Gender and Music Training Groups

After 1,000-iteration bootstrap, the configural invariance models across genders showed acceptable to good fit (Table S5), with CFI ranging from 0.906 to 0.927, TLI ranging from 0.889 to 0.913, and RMSEA ranging from 0.062 to 0.071. Across 1,000 bootstrap iterations, 675 metricversus-configural comparisons were non-significant, whereas only 76 of these also showed nonsignificant scalar-versus-metric comparisons; none supported strict invariance. These findings support configural invariance across genders and provide preliminary evidence for metric invariance of the scale.

Across 1,000 bootstrap iterations, the music-training-based configural invariance models also showed acceptable to good fit (Table S5), with CFI ranging from 0.895 to 0.928, TLI ranging from 0.875 to 0.915, and RMSEA ranging from 0.059 to 0.071. Among these iterations, 989 metricversus-configural comparisons were non-significant, whereas all 989 showed significant deterioration in the scalar-versus-metric and strict-versus-scalar comparisons. These findings support configural invariance across music-training groups and provide strong resampling-based evidence for metric invariance of the scale. Note that metric invariance supports equivalence of factor loadings and permits comparisons of relationships among latent factors, but not latent factor means, which generally require scalar invariance. Because scalar invariance was not established, subsequent observed-score group comparisons were treated as exploratory and descriptive rather than as unbiased estimates of latent mean differences. Bootstrap resampling quantified sampling uncertainty in these contrasts but did not correct for potential bias arising from measurement non-invariance.

Group Differences in Musical Reward Sensitivity of Chinese population

Following Mas-Herrero et al. (2014), the 10th percentile of the pooled C-BMRQ total-score distribution was used as a sample-derived reference threshold for low music reward sensitivity; item-level descriptive statistics for the pooled dataset are provided in Table S3. This cutoff was 69 in the present Chinese sample (Figure 3), slightly higher than the cutoff of 65 reported by Mas-Herrero et al. (2014), indicating a potentially higher empirical threshold for identifying low music reward sensitivity in the present sample. Group differences by gender and music training status were examined in subsequent analyses.

Gender group comparisons revealed no consistent differences in the C-BMRQ total score or most subscales, except for Emotion Evocation and Sensory Motor (Figure 4). For all other indices, the 95% bootstrap intervals of the t statistics included zero, indicating unstable and non-significant differences (Figure S2). Emotion Evocation yielded a 95% bootstrap interval [-3.85, -0.43] (males < females); across 1,000 bootstrap iterations there were 590, 308, and 106 tests reaching p < .05, p < .01, and p < .001, respectively. Sensory Motor yielded a 95% bootstrap interval [-4.65, -1.21] (males < females); across 1,000 bootstrap iterations there were 855, 633, and 355 bootstrap tests reaching p < .05, p < .01, and p < .001, respectively. The observed gender differences in Emotion Evocation and Sensory Motor are broadly consistent with previous findings using the Chinese version of the BMRQ (Wang et al., 2023) and international adaptations (e.g., Mas-Herrero et al., 2013; Mannino et al., 2024), suggesting that gender-related differences in these dimensions may be reproducible within this measurement framework.

Music-trained individuals scored significantly higher than non-musicians on the C-BMRQ total score and all subscales (Figure 5), with all 95% bootstrap intervals of the t statistics above zero (Figure S3). The total score yielded a 95% bootstrap interval [4.24, 7.97], with 998 of 1,000 bootstrap tests reaching p < .001. Across subscales, Social Reward showed the largest difference, 95% bootstrap interval [5.06, 8.82], with all 1,000 tests reaching p < .001. Followed by Music Seeking, [3.46, 7.25], with 986 tests reaching p < .001. Emotion Evocation showed a 95% bootstrap interval of [2.61, 6.53], with 972 and 892 tests reaching p < .01 and p < .001, respectively. Mood Regulation showed a 95% bootstrap interval of [2.40, 6.10], with 960 and 858 tests reaching p < .01 and p < .001, respectively. Sensory Motor showed a 95% bootstrap interval of [1.79, 5.55], with 965, 876, and 858 tests reaching p < .05, p < .01, and p <0.001, respectively. These results indicate a stable association between music training and higher music reward sensitivity across all C-BMRQ dimensions.

C-BMRQ correlates with physical anhedonia and musical sophistication

As shown in Table 3, the C-BMRQ total score and subscales were negatively correlated with PAS (|ρs| > 0.33, Bonferroni-corrected), replicating findings from Mas-Herrero et al. (2013). After distinguishing music-related from other PAS items, C-BMRQ correlated negatively with both subscales. Among the C-BMRQ subscales, Sensory Motor showed the strongest association with music-related PAS items, whereas Social Reward showed the strongest association with non-music PAS items.

Gold-MSI's Active Engagement was positively correlated with C-BMRQ with ρs ranging from 0.25 to 0.54. Significant correlations were limited to the C-BMRQ total score, Social Reward, and Music Seeking (ps < .001, Bonferroni-corrected), with Music Seeking displaying the highest correlation (ρ = 0.54). Perceptual Abilities was also positively correlated with the C-BMRQ with ρ ranging from 0.29 to 0.41; significant associations for the total score, Social Reward, Music Seeking, and Emotion Evocation (ps < .01, Bonferroni-corrected). Singing Abilities showed significant positive correlations only with the C-BMRQ total score and Social Reward (ps < .05, Bonferronicorrected). The Emotions subscale showed significant positive correlations with the C-BMRQ total score and all subscales (ρs = 0.40-0.57, ps < .001, Bonferroni-corrected), with the strongest association observed for Emotion Evocation.

No consistent association was found between Music Training and the C-BMRQ. Although Dataset 2 showed a weak positive correlation between Music Training and Social Reward (ρ = 0.29, p = .021, Bonferroni-corrected), a similar effect in Dataset 3 did not survive correction (ρ = 0.28, p = .113, Bonferroni-corrected). General Musical Sophistication was significantly correlated with the C-BMRQ total score, Social Reward, and Music Seeking in both datasets, with ρs ranging from 0.33 to 0.45 in Dataset 2 and from 0.36 to 0.42 in Dataset 3 (ps < .01, Bonferroni-corrected). Social Reward showed the strongest association with General Musical Sophistication in both datasets (Dataset 2: ρ = 0.45, Dataset 3: ρ = 0.42).

General Discussion

The present research completed the development and comprehensive validity validation of the (Boateng et al., 2018). Both literal translations exhibited consistently poor model fit, confirming the inherent limitation of pure literal translation in capturing cultural connotations of the construct; the clear advantage of the culturally adapted version further demonstrates that "linguistic revision integrated with cultural adaptation" constitutes a more methodologically rigorous strategy for cross-cultural scale optimization than item deletion (Maneesriwongul & Dixon, 2004), offering actionable methodological insights for the localization of comparable measurement tools in behavioral research.

Beyond the optimized adaptation strategy, the C-BMRQ also demonstrates good psychometric robustness and cross-group applicability through multi-dimensional validation, with criterion validity specifically confirming its efficacy in assessing music anhedonia. Integrated validation results from the two studies showed that the scale's test-retest reliability meets psychometric standards (ten Berge, 1995), and it passed measurement invariance tests across gender groups and individuals with and without music training, ensuring comparability among different subgroups. More importantly, these results confirm from both longitudinal and group difference perspectives that music reward sensitivity possesses a stable structural foundation while exhibiting malleability across time and experience. Specifically, the partial scalar non-invariance observed across test and retest does not necessarily indicate compromised scale reliability. Instead, this pattern may plausibly reflect the inherent malleability of music reward sensitivity-a dynamic affective-motivational trait shaped by time, context, and experience (Mas-Herrero et al., 2013; Mas-Herrero et al., 2026)-while the C-BMRQ retains a stable underlying factor structure, underscoring its structural integrity. Regarding cross-group differences, the observed gender disparities in Emotion Evocation and Sensory-Motor (females > males) and higher scores among music-trained individuals align with prior cross-cultural findings (Mannino et al., 2024; Mas-Herrero et al., 2013; Mas-Herrero et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2023). These group-level differences reflect stable baseline variations in affective and sensory processing, especially as shaped by musical experience, consistent with the malleable nature of music reward sensitivity and further supporting the construct validity of the C-BMRQ.

Critically, to validate the scale's ability to capture music reward sensitivity, particularly its utility for identifying music anhedonia, we verified concurrent and discriminant validity using an independent sample, employing two theoretically complementary criterion measures. From a musical-specific perspective, the Goldsmiths Musical Sophistication Index (Gold-MSI; Müllensiefen et al., 2014) assessed objective musical skills and knowledge, with the C-BMRQ showing significant positive correlations with multiple dimensions of musical sophistication, confirming that higher music reward sensitivity aligns with greater musical competence, a key theoretical expectation. From an affective-deficit perspective, the Physical and Cognitive Anhedonia Scale (CPAS; Chan et al., 2012) measured generalized loss of pleasure capacity, and the C-BMRQ exhibited significant negative correlations with physical anhedonia, supporting that reduced music reward sensitivity co-occurs with broader anhedonia traits, a hallmark of music anhedonia (Belfi & Loui, 2020; Kathios et al., 2024; Martinez-Molina et al., 2016 , 2019). Together, these findings from dual complementary perspectives confirm the cross-cultural generalizability of the music reward construct, the critical role of localized adaptation, and the C-BMRQ's specificity and relevance for assessing music anhedonia.

The most prominent theoretical innovation of the present study is the improvement of the scale's construct validity and stability without deleting any items, including all reverse-coded items.

The current study fully preserved the original five-factor structure (4 items per dimension) through cultural adaptation and linguistic revision, adhering to the core principle of scale development that theoretical integrity should take precedence over statistical fit (DeVellis, 1991). Psychometric research clearly indicates that subscales with fewer than 3 items face fatal flaws such as poor internal consistency and incomplete construct capture (Worthington & Whittaker, 2006), and the original BMRQ's design of only 4 items per dimension means any item deletion, let alone the targeted removal of items from core dimensions, would inevitably compromise the theoretical and psychometric integrity of the scale. Specifically, Wang et al. 's (2023) item-deletion strategy reduced the Music-seeking and Sensory-Motor dimensions, both of which represent fundamental components of music reward motivation, from 4 items to only 2 items. For the Music-seeking dimension, the deleted items included the reverse-coded item "In my free time I hardly listen to music." and the item "I spend quite a bit of money on music and related items.". For the Sensory-Motor dimension, the deleted items were the reverse-coded item "I don't like to dance, not even with music I like" and the item "Music often makes me dance". Music-seeking and sensory-motor responding are central, driving components of music reward motivation (Mas-Herrero et al., 2013), reflecting the "wanting" component of reward processing (Berridge & Kringelbach, 2008 , 2015). Meanwhile, monetary expenditure and willingness to pay for music represent a robust, ecologically valid behavioral indicator of the subjective reward value that individuals attribute to music, a widely adopted and validated measure in music reward research (Mas-Herrero et al., 2018 , 2021; Salimpoor et al., 2013). As a result, this truncation eliminated the motivational component of music reward and undermined the scale's ability to assess the multifaceted nature of music reward. Moreover, reversecoded items are a critical component of psychological scales, as they mitigate response biases such as acquiescence bias; their elimination further undermines the scale's ability to accurately capture individual differences in music reward sensitivity.

Additionally, from a practical standpoint, the 10th percentile of the pooled C-BMRQ distribution corresponded to a total score of 69. This sample-derived value was higher than the cutoff of 65 reported in previous Western-sample research (Mas-Herrero et al., 2013). This difference highlights potential cross-cultural variation in score distributions and the need to evaluate normative reference values within specific populations (Carraturo et al., 2025; Lippolis et al., 2025; Pereira & Bortoloti, 2025; Saliba et al., 2016). This issue may be particularly relevant given recent evidence of atypical music-reward processing in Chinese clinical and neurodevelopmental samples (Jin et al., 2025).

The value of 69 may serve as a preliminary research reference for low music reward sensitivity in comparable Chinese adult samples. However, the present study did not include a clinical reference standard or evaluate diagnostic accuracy. Independent studies using clinical interviews, behavioral and physiological measures of music-evoked pleasure, assessments of non-musical reward processing, and appropriate diagnostic-accuracy analyses are required before the threshold can be used for clinical screening or decision-making.

While the present study has achieved progress, it has limitations and future directions. First, the lack of rigorous cross-cultural comparisons (e.g., with Western samples) and the exclusion of low-education, elderly, and clinical populations limit the generalizability of the conclusions and the validation of diagnostic scenarios. Second, despite using an independent behavioral sample for criterion validation, the core scale development still relied on self-report data; future research could integrate more objective behavioral tasks (e.g., music preference choice paradigms) or physiological indicators (e.g., skin conductance responses) for multi-method cross-validation. Nevertheless, the C-BMRQ remains a reliable, valid, and culturally appropriate tool for assessing music reward sensitivity in the Chinese population, with broad application prospects in scenarios such as research on cultural differences in music perception and evaluation of music therapy outcomes. improved items originally with low factor loadings. *Reverse Item. Building on their foundational work, the current study further improves the scale's generalizability and utility in the Chinese context by addressing several unresolved research gaps: (1) Their item-deletion procedure altered the original theoretical structure of the BMRQ, and tests of measurement invariance and population-specific reference values for low music reward sensitivity were not included in their validation framework; (2) Their sample was geographically restricted to Changsha, which limits nationwide representativeness given China's diverse demographic and musical engagement patterns. The core methodological breakthrough of the present study lies in its systematic comparison of four questionnaire versions, which clearly reveals the progression of cross-cultural scale adaptation from formal linguistic equivalence to functional and cultural equivalence. The comparison explicitly included Wang et al. 's (2023) literal translation and item-deletion revised version, as well as the current study's initial literal translation and final culturally adapted version, each representing distinct localization logics: literal translations pursue mere consistency in linguistic form, the itemdeletion version improves model fit at the cost of the original scale's structural and psychometric integrity, and the culturally adapted version achieves functional equivalence through targeted linguistic revision and contextual adaptation.
