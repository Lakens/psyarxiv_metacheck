Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. 1. Introduction

The pattern theory of self (PTS), first articulated by Gallagher (2013) and subsequently developed across several publications (Gallagher & Daly, 2018; Gallagher et al., 2024a), proposes that "self" is not a single entity or substance but a complex pattern of dynamically related constituent processes. These processes form a self-organizing gestalt without any central controller or homunculus. This self-pattern is not reducible to any one of its components; rather, it emerges from the ongoing dynamical relations among its constituent processes (Gallagher & Daly, 2018). This framework departs from both reductionist accounts that identify the self with a single feature -such as psychological continuity or narrative identity -and eliminativist positions that deny the self altogether. Building on this foundation, Berkovich-Ohana et al. (2024) introduced the pattern theory of selflessness (PTSL), a heuristic model proposing that Buddhist meditative practices can induce a reorganization of the self-pattern toward a "selfless" condition. The PTSL describes six transformations that unfold in a nonlinear and iterative manner: consolidating and integrating the self-pattern (T1), cultivating concentration and present-moment awareness (T2), cultivating mindful awareness (T3), self-deconstruction states (T4), self-pattern flexibility (T5), and self-liberation as a trait (T6).

The PTSL is a rich interdisciplinary achievement, drawing together insights from phenomenology, Buddhist psychology, cognitive neuroscience, and contemplative studies.

[2] The authors developed seven processes pertaining to the internal dynamics of the self-pattern, while noting that three further processes -behavioral, ecological, and normative -reflect world orientation (Berkovich-Ohana et al., 2024 , p. 2121). The present paper takes up the ecological thread among these three, and its guiding question is: what shape does the PTSL take when transformation of the self-pattern is reconceived as the enacting of an organism situated in a world? The answer, I will argue, is a more textured account in which the selfpattern transforms through the specific material, social, and institutional configurations in which the practitioner is embedded. Whatever environment the practitioner inhabits -the village temple, the urban apartment, the commuter train, the hospital waiting room, the corporate office -is already participating in how the self-pattern reorganizes. Different environments do not merely provide different "settings" for the same internal transformation; they constitute different affordance landscapes that shape what kind of transformation is possible and how it unfolds.

To develop this argument, I draw on two interrelated theoretical traditions. First, ecological psychology, particularly the Gibsonian tradition as variously developed by Chemero (2003), Stoffregen (2003), Rietveld and Kiverstein (2014), and Heft (1989 Heft ( , 2020)), provides a rigorous account of how animal-environment systems constitute -rather than merely contain -the possibilities for action and experience. Second, enactivism, as articulated by Thompson (2005 Thompson ( , 2007)), Colombetti (2017), and Varela, Thompson, and Rosch (1991), and as developed by Di Paolo (2009) through the concept of adaptivity, offers a complementary framework in which cognition is understood as embodied action in a world that the organism actively brings forth. The relationship between these traditions remains contested: while some identify productive convergences and complementarities (McGann et al., 2020; McGann, 2020), others argue that foundational divergences preclude synthesis (Heft, 2020). Both the convergences and the divergences are instructive for the present [3] argument. Where the traditions converge -in their shared insistence that organism and environment are coupled rather than independent -they together support the central claim that self-pattern transformation cannot be adequately located inside the organism alone.

Where they diverge -ecological psychology foregrounding the structure of action possibilities, enactivism foregrounding the constitution of experience and affect -they illuminate distinct dimensions of how ecological processes participate in each transformation, and it is across both dimensions that the present argument moves.

The paper proceeds as follows. Section 2 provides an overview of the PTS and PTSL frameworks, attending to how ecological processes are defined and positioned within them.

Section 3 develops a theoretical account of ecological processes drawing on ecological psychology and enactivism and argues for their constitutive rather than merely causal role in self-pattern transformation. Section 4 traces how ecological processes participate in each of the six PTSL transformations. Section 5 draws out the implications of this analysis: for understanding the self-pattern as an organism-environment system (5.1), for cross-cultural variation and the secularization of mindfulness (5.2), for Buddhist psychology (5.3), and for directions in empirical research (5.4). Section 6 concludes.

2. The Pattern Theory of Self and Selflessness

>The Pattern Theory of Self and Selflessness

2.1 The Pattern Theory of Self

The PTS begins with the premise that the self is neither a fixed entity nor an illusion but a pattern -a complex of interrelated processes that are dynamically organized without any central coordinating agent (Gallagher, 2013). This approach is informed by phenomenological, enactivist, and dynamical systems perspectives, and it implicitly distinguishes itself from both Cartesian substantial accounts of the self and deflationary or bundle-type views that treat the self as nothing more than a collection of discrete mental [4] states (Gallagher, 2013; Gallagher & Daly, 2018). The self-pattern is a "recursively selforganizing" gestalt (Gallagher et al., 2024a, p.797) characterized by operational closure (Varela, 1997): each process constrains and is constrained by the others, generating a coherent pattern without requiring a separate entity to hold it together (Gallagher & Daly, 2018). Gallagher's (2013) original formulation identified eight aspects of the self-pattern: minimal embodied, minimal experiential, affective, intersubjective, psychological/cognitive, narrative, extended, and situated aspects. Subsequent work expanded and refined this list. Gallagher et al. (2024a), drawing on Gallagher (2021), articulate ten constituent processes: (1) bodily, (2) prereflective experiential, (3) affective, (4) behavioral/action-related, (5) social/intersubjective, (6) cognitive and psychological, (7) reflective, (8) narrative, (9) ecological, and (10) normative.

This evolution is significant: the later formulations differentiate what was previously grouped under "situated" aspects into distinct ecological, behavioral, and normative processes, making explicit the world-oriented dimensions of the self-pattern that are central to the present argument. Among the first seven processes -bodily, prereflective experiential, affective, behavioral, social/intersubjective, cognitive/psychological, and reflective -and the eighth (narrative), the dynamics are primarily (though not exclusively) internal to the organism. The final three -ecological, normative, and behavioral -orient the self-pattern more explicitly toward the world. As Gallagher and Daly (2018) emphasize, these processes are dynamically interrelated: narrative processes reflect embodied action; affective processes are shaped by social interaction; and cognitive dispositions are dynamically integrated with ecological and extended structures.

The ecological process, as defined by Gallagher et al. (2024a, p. 797), refers to how "our embodied-situated actions engage with (and sometimes incorporate) artifacts, [5] instruments, bits and structures of the environment in ways that define us and scaffold our identities." The extended mind thesis (Clark & Chalmers, 1998) elaborates this point, demonstrating how specific artifacts and instruments can become constitutively incorporated into cognitive processes and scaffold identity. This understanding of the ecological process carries directly into the PTSL: as Berkovich-Ohana et al. (2024 , p. 2117) state, selfhood extends beyond material belongings to encompass the action possibilities afforded by the technologies we use, our professions, the groups we belong to, and the institutions we work in. This reflects a commitment shared across the frameworks to understanding selfhood as fundamentally world-involving. It is this fundamentally world-involving self-pattern that PTSL takes as its object of transformation.

2.2 The Pattern Theory of Selflessness

PTSL proposes that Buddhist meditative practices reorganize the self-pattern through six transformations that unfold nonlinearly (Berkovich-Ohana et al., 2024). Transformation 1 (T1), consolidating and integrating the self-pattern, corresponds primarily to the ethical dimension of Buddhist training (sīla). The practitioner stabilizes the self-pattern by restructuring maladaptive self-schema (Dahl et al., 2015), creating conditions for further transformation through practices that cultivate emotional qualities. These include lovingkindness and compassion -understood as patterns of dynamically interrelated affective, cognitive, and intersubjective processes distinct from empathy or sympathy (Berkovich-Ohana et al., 2024; Gallagher et al., 2024b) -and potentially extend to the classical brahmavihāras. Transformation 2 (T2), cultivating concentration, corresponds to samādhi training through focused attention (FA) practices, which stabilize attention and reduce mindwandering (Lutz et al., 2008). Transformation 3 (T3), cultivating mindful awareness, corresponds to paññā training through open monitoring (OM) practices. While contemporary [6] psychology characterizes T3 as developing non-judgmental, present-centered awareness (Kabat-Zinn, 2003; Bishop et al., 2004), classical Buddhist sources emphasize a more situated capacity that includes retentive and evaluative dimensions -sampajañña -that resists reduction to decentered observation (Dreyfus, 2011; Bodhi, 2011); the PTSL draws on both registers, and the present paper follows that dual orientation.

These first three transformations work on specific processes of the self-pattern: T1 primarily reshapes affective, reflective, and social/intersubjective processes; T2 reshapes experiential/prereflective and cognitive processes through attentional training; and T3 cultivates a form of reflexive awareness that loosens the grip of narrative and cognitive elaboration (Berkovich-Ohana et al., 2024). Together, they contribute to Transformation 4 (T4), self-deconstruction states -deep meditative experiences in which the boundaries of the self-pattern are temporarily dissolved, including states of absorption (samādhi) and self-less awareness whose neural correlates have been investigated by Lutz et al. (2007) and whose phenomenological features Dambrun and Ricard (2011) characterize in terms of witness consciousness and cessation experiences.

Transformation 5 (T5), self-pattern flexibility, describes the developing capacity to move fluidly between different modes of self-experience without becoming rigidly attached to any one configuration. This fluidity resembles Gallagher's (2021) double attunement in theatrical performance: "not one, not two; not identity, but not complete distinction" (Lecture 3, p. 22). Just as the skilled actor maintains a unitary experience encompassing both the character portrayed and the self-awareness of performing, the practitioner developing T5 maintains a "meshed architecture" (p. 22) integrating vertical (embodied-affective) and horizontal (environmental-contextual) axes. This double attunement operates as a transformed mode of engagement within the ecological landscape: the practitioner perceives the full field of affordances without being captured by any particular solicitation, achieving [7] the capacity to hold "the possibility of grabbing this thread" of awareness while remaining fully immersed in the performance of action (p. 20). Transformation 6 (T6), self-liberation, marks the transition from flexible self-pattern to an irreversible reorientation of the organism- Harvey (1995) describes as the Arahant's boundaryless engagement and Olendzki (2010) characterizes in terms of awakening to the constructed nature of self (Berkovich-Ohana et al., 2024). In this condition, the modality of craving and aversion ceases to orient experiences of world.

In focusing on seven internal processes, this heuristic model necessarily foregrounds a picture of transformation that is primarily intrapsychic -the practitioner transforms through the cultivation of internal capacities such as attention, awareness, equanimity, and insightwith the environment receding to background. The ecological, behavioral, and normative processes that the PTS recognizes as constitutive of the self-pattern are treated, in the PTSL, as changing in response to the accumulating transformations (Berkovich-Ohana et al., 2024 , p. 2121) rather than as active contributors to each stage of transformation as it unfolds. The present paper builds on PTS's recognition of ecological processes as constitutive and expands this commitment into PTSL by arguing that ecological dynamics are not merely responsive but are actively at work in shaping each stage of transformation.

3. Ecological Processes: A Theoretical Framework

>Ecological Processes: A Theoretical Framework

3.1 Ecological Psychology and the Organism-Environment System

To understand what it means for ecological processes to be constitutive of self-pattern transformation, we need a precise account of how environments participate in cognition and experience. Ecological psychology, originating in the work of James Gibson (1979), provides such an account through the concept of affordances -the action possibilities that environments offer to organisms. Crucially, affordances are not properties of the environment [8] alone, nor of the organism alone. Beyond this shared ground, theorists diverge. Chemero (2003), drawing on Heft (1989), argues that affordances are best understood as relations between features of the environment and abilities of the organism. Stoffregen (2003) disagrees on the precise formulation: affordances are not relations but emergent properties of the animal-environment system as a whole, belonging to neither component in isolation. Turvey (1992) develops this further, characterizing affordances as environmental dispositions complemented by animal effectivities -neither defined independently of the other, with the manifest properties of the system emerging only when these complementary dispositions are actualized (see Heras-Escribano, 2019, on the ontological characterization of affordances as properties of the organism-environment system).

This relational ontology has important consequences for understanding how environments shape self-experience. If affordances are relational properties that emerge in the coupling between organism and environment, then changes in the environment directly alter the field of possibilities available to the organism -and thereby alter the organism's selfpattern. This structural claim aligns with Chemero's (2009) concept of 'ecological ontology,' which holds that affordances are relations constitutive of cognition. The environment participates in constituting the self-pattern by co-specifying the affordances through which the organism exists. Rietveld and Kiverstein (2014) extend this insight through their concept of a "rich landscape of affordances," arguing that affordances are not limited to simple motor possibilities but encompass the full range of action possibilities relevant to the organism's form of life. For human beings, this landscape includes social, cultural, institutional, and normative affordances -the possibilities for meaningful action that are structured by the sociomaterial practices in which we are embedded (Rietveld & Kiverstein, 2014; see Bruineberg & Rietveld, 2014, on the neurodynamical grounding of this landscape in the Free Energy Principle). The landscape of affordances is, then, a dynamic field that shifts as the [9] organism's skills, sensitivities, and concerns change. A musician hears affordances in a soundscape that a non-musician does not; a meditator may come to perceive affordances for stillness or responsiveness in environments that previously solicited only habitual reactivity (see Dreyfus, 2002, on skillful coping and expertise).

3.2 Enactivism and the Constitution of Experience

Where ecological psychology provides a rigorous account of how organism-environment systems structure possibilities for action, enactivism extends this analysis to experience and affect. Affordance theory tells us what the environment makes possible for an organism; enactivism tells us how the organism's sensorimotor engagement with that environment constitutively shapes what it experiences, and how affective and emotional life is itself organized through material and social arrangements. For a paper concerned with how the self-pattern transforms through meditative practice -a practice that is fundamentally about the reorganization of experience and affect, not only of action -this extension is necessary. Thompson (2005), extending the framework developed by Varela (1997), articulates the enactive view that experience is not a passive registration of pre-given information but an active bringing-forth of a world through sensorimotor engagement. From this view, what the organism experiences is constitutively shaped by how it acts -and how it acts is shaped by the environment in which it finds itself. Sensorimotor subjectivity, as Thompson calls it, is not an interior process that happens to unfold in an environment; it emerges from the structural coupling of organism and environment (Thompson, 2005 (Thompson, , 2007;; Varela et al., 1991; Merleau-Ponty, 1962).

Di Paolo (2005 Paolo ( , 2009) ) extends the enactive framework through the concept of adaptivity, arguing that living systems are not merely self-maintaining (autopoietic) but actively regulate their interactions with the environment in relation to viability conditions that [10] arise from their own self-organization. This emphasis on the organism's active engagement with environmental conditions is particularly relevant for understanding meditation, which involves the deliberate restructuring of one's relationship to the sensory and social environment -wherever that restructuring occurs. When a practitioner sits down to meditate, whether on a cushion in a monastery or on a chair in their kitchen, they are not merely changing their internal cognitive processes; they are reorganizing the organism-environment system in ways that alter the conditions under which experience arises.

The affective dimensions of this organism-environment relationship deserve particular attention. Colombetti and Krueger (2015) extend Sterelny's (2010) scaffolded-mind framework to the affective domain, articulating how material and social structures function as affective scaffoldings that support and regulate emotional experience -how, for instance, particular environments, objects, or social arrangements facilitate certain affective states while inhibiting others. Colombetti (2017) pushes this analysis further, arguing that the material vehicles of affectivity -and specifically moods -can, under certain conditions, literally extend into the environment when non-organic items are integrated into a composite adaptive autonomous system. On this stronger constitutive view, the distinction between scaffolding (causal support from outside) and extension (constitutive participation from within) matters: the claim is not merely that environments help regulate emotions, but that certain material and social arrangements can become part of the affective system itself. For the purposes of the present argument, both scaffolding and extension are relevant. A religious altar, a contemplative text, a meditation bell, a familiar corner of a bedroom -such material arrangements may function as affective scaffolds that support particular emotional orientations, and in some cases may become constitutive elements of the ecological conditions under which the self-pattern transforms.

3.3 Toward an Ecological Account of Self-Pattern Transformation

Ecological psychology and enactivism approach organism-environment relations from different directions, but their convergence for the purposes of this paper is consequential.

Ecological psychology establishes that the possibilities for action -the affordance landscapeare not properties of the environment alone, nor of the organism alone, but belong to the coupled system as a whole (Chemero, 2003; Stoffregen, 2003; Turvey, 1992). Enactivism establishes that experience and affect are equally constituted through organism-environment coupling: what an organism experiences, and how it feels, arises from the active, adaptive engagement of organism and world rather than from internal processes that subsequently interface with an external environment (Thompson, 2005 (Thompson, , 2007;; Di Paolo, 2009; Colombetti, 2017). Together, these claims converge on a single conclusion that is foundational to the present argument: the self-pattern -which spans action, experience, and affect -is an organism-environment enactment. It cannot be adequately located inside the organism without losing something essential to what it is (McGann et al., 2020; Heft, 2020).

This understanding requires more than adding "environment" as a new variable to an existing account of self-pattern transformation. It requires a shift in the unit of analysis -from the organism as the site of transformation to the organism-environment system as the field within which transformation unfolds. This shift is precisely what Dewey (1922) had in mind when he wrote that human nature "is not 'in' that environment as coins are in a box, but as a plant is in the sunlight and soil. It is of them, continuous with their energies, dependent upon their support" (p. 296). The plant does not transform in spite of its ecology or alongside it; it transforms as an ecological process. The same principle, transposed into the framework of ecological psychology and enactivism, entails that the self-pattern transforms as an organismenvironment system -not as an internal process that ecological conditions happen to influence from outside.

[12]

The theoretical stakes here turn on the distinction between causal and constitutive contributions. A causal account holds that ecological conditions influence self-pattern transformation from outside: the environment produces effects on the organism, but the transformation itself remains an organismic event that can, in principle, be fully described without reference to the specific ecology in which it occurs. A constitutive account holds something stronger: that ecological processes are part of what the transformation is, such that specifying the transformation without specifying its ecological dimension yields not a less detailed description but an incomplete one. The distinction matters because it entails a shift in the unit of analysis. If ecological processes are merely causal, then the organism remains the proper site of investigation, and the environment figures as an external variable -important, but separable. If ecological processes are constitutive, then the organism-environment system is the unit within which transformation occurs, and attending to the organism alone is attending to an abstraction. This is not a taxonomy of which conditions count as constitutive and which as merely causal -as if the practitioner's social ecology were inherently constitutive while the temperature of the room was inherently causal. Whether a condition is constitutively entangled in transformation depends on the nature of the organism-environment coupling: a practitioner who works with the discomfort of heat as the object of practice is reorganizing the organism-environment system in a way that makes that discomfort part of what the transformation is. As Turvey (1992) establishes, affordances are ontologically nonexistent without their complementing organism properties -"an affordance is not defined (i.e., is nonexistent) without a complementing animal property" (p. 179). Disposition and suitable circumstance together equal actuality. Transformation, on this view, is an actualization of the organism-environment system.

[13] PTS's own theoretical commitments point in precisely this direction. If the selfpattern includes ecological processes among its ten constitutive dimensions (Gallagher, 2021; Gallagher et al., 2024a), and if affordances are properties of the organism-environment system rather than of either component in isolation (Chemero, 2003; Stoffregen, 2003), then the ecological account developed here follows through on what the framework already recognizes. Section 4 undertakes this work for each of the six PTSL transformations, attending to how different ecological configurations -different affordance landscapes, different affective scaffoldings, different social ecologies -constitute different pathways through T1-T6 (Rietveld & Kiverstein, 2014; Colombetti & Krueger, 2015).

4. The Ecology of Meditative Self-Transformation

The six transformations of the PTSL are qualitatively distinct reorganizations of the selfpattern, each with its own structure, demands, and developmental logic. What the ecological perspective reveals is that this distinctiveness extends to the organism-environment system itself: each transformation involves a specific mode of ecological participation -a particular way in which affordance landscapes, affective scaffoldings, and social ecologies enter into what the transformation consists of. T1 involves the restructuring of the affordance landscape through ethical commitment; T2 involves the material engineering of an ecology of attention; T3 involves the progressive generalization of open awareness across diverse and demanding environments; T4 involves the systematic withdrawal of the ecological scaffolding that sustains ordinary self-boundaries; T5 involves the cultivation of fluid responsiveness across the full range of contexts the practitioner inhabits; and T6 involves a fundamental reorientation of the organism-environment relation itself. The analysis that follows traces these ecological structures in detail.

4.1 T1: Consolidating and Integrating the Self-Pattern

The first transformation involves stabilizing and integrating the self-pattern through ethical living (sīla) and the cultivation of emotional qualities. The PTSL focuses on how practices such as loving-kindness meditation reshape affective, cognitive, and intersubjective processes (Berkovich-Ohana et al., 2024; for a pattern-theoretic treatment of compassion as a related but distinct affective-intersubjective configuration, see Gallagher et al., 2024b). From an ecological psychology perspective, T1 involves a reorganization of the practitioner's landscape of affordances. Adopting the five precepts (pañcasīla) restructures what the environment affords. When one undertakes the precept against intoxicants, the bar ceases to afford drinking in the way it previously did -not because its physical properties change, but because the organism-environment relation has been restructured by a normative commitment enacted in and through the material world (Rietveld & Kiverstein, 2014). The same principle operates in secular contexts: the practitioner who commits to non-harm begins to perceive different affordances in interpersonal situations -opportunities for patience rather than reaction, for generosity rather than competition. The ecological process is constitutive: the ethical consolidation of the self-pattern cannot proceed without a corresponding transformation of the affordance landscape, wherever the practitioner lives and works.

But T1 involves not only the restructuring of affordances through ethical commitment but also the reshaping of affective and intersubjective processes -and this affective dimension is equally ecologically constituted. The cultivation of brahmavihāras does not occur in an affective vacuum; it is scaffolded by the material and social arrangements within which the practitioner is embedded. Colombetti and Krueger's (2015) framework of affective scaffolding is directly relevant here: specific environments, objects, and social configurations support and regulate the emotional restructuring that T1 requires. 4.2 T2: Cultivating Concentration and Present-Moment Awareness

The second transformation involves the development of attentional stability through focused attention (FA) practices, corresponding to samādhi training. The PTSL describes this primarily as a shift in reflective processes (Berkovich-Ohana et al., 2024; for related concepts [16] of meta-cognitive monitoring and reflexive awareness, see Lutz et al., 2008). But concentration does not arise in an environmental vacuum -it arises in a specific organismenvironment coupling, and the character of that coupling is constitutive of the attentional transformation.

The early discourses already specify this. The Satipaṭṭhāna Sutta (MN 10) opens by locating the practitioner ecologically -"gone to the forest, or to the root of a tree, or to an empty hut" -before specifying posture: "he sits down; having folded his legs crosswise, set his body erect, and established mindfulness in front of him" (Anālayo, 2003, p. 4). Together, the environmental and postural specifications restructure the organism-environment coupling: the secluded location withdraws the affordances of social life, while the posture restricts the range of available actions -and thereby reshapes what experience is possible (Thompson, 2007). The Visuddhimagga details how this restructuring unfolds: as sensory input is restricted through postural stillness and environmental quietude, the practitioner encounters the uggaha-nimitta (learning sign) -a perceptual anchor that emerges from the coupling of attention with the meditation object. This develops into the paṭibhāga-nimitta (counterpart sign), a purely cognitive percept that no longer depends on external sensory support (Ñāṇamoli, 2011, Chapter IV). The progression from uggaha to paṭibhāga emerges ecologically: the organism-environment coupling is progressively restructured until attentional stability no longer requires the environmental conditions that initially scaffolded it.

Traditional intensive retreat environments systematically engineer this ecology of attention. The structured schedule -alternating sitting and walking meditation, fixed mealtimes, noble silence -creates a temporal affordance landscape that supports rhythmic attentional stabilization. Noble silence withdraws the social affordances for verbal interaction, removing a powerful source of cognitive elaboration and self-narrative. When the [17] affordances for social communication are withdrawn, the self-pattern's narrative and social/intersubjective processes lose their primary environmental scaffolding, creating conditions under which attentional processes can reorganize. The retreat environment, then, constitutes a specific pathway through T2, one in which the progressive reduction of the affordance landscape is itself the medium of transformation.

But retreat is not the only ecology of T2, and the contrast is theoretically instructive.

A practitioner doing ānāpānasati on a morning commuter train faces a radically different affordance landscape: ambient noise, physical proximity to strangers, the rhythmic vibration of the vehicle, the visual flow of passing environment. Here the practitioner learns to sustain attention amid environmental solicitations rather than in their absence -the stimuli become part of the practice rather than obstacles to it (Kabat-Zinn, 2003). Cook's (2016) study of mindfulness in the British Parliament makes the same point in an institutional register: the affordances of a Westminster committee room -political debate, adversarial exchange, time pressure -constitute a qualitatively different attentional training from that of a meditation hall. What these contrasting cases reveal is that different affordance landscapes generate different forms of attentional development -and that specifying the transformation without specifying the ecological conditions leaves the description incomplete.

4.3 T3: Cultivating Mindful Awareness

The third transformation involves the development of open monitoring (OM) awareness -a mode of attending to experience as it arises that is characterized by receptivity and decentering. The PTSL describes this primarily as a shift in reflective processes (Berkovich-Ohana et al., 2024; Lutz et al., 2008), aligning with psychological operationalizations of mindfulness as nonjudgmental, decentered awareness (Bishop et al., 2004). This transformation has constitutive ecological dimensions that are particularly illuminating.

[18] Open monitoring involves a distinctive relationship to the affordance landscape: rather than narrowing attention to a single focus (as in T2), the practitioner cultivates a mode of engagement in which affordances are perceived without being automatically acted upon.

From an ecological psychology perspective, this represents a shift in the organismenvironment coupling -the practitioner learns to perceive affordances without being solicited by them, to be aware of action possibilities without enacting them (Rietveld & Kiverstein, 2014). This capacity is sometimes described in the contemplative literature as "bare attention" or "choiceless awareness," though such language risks understating the active complexity of what classical Buddhist accounts describe as sati. Dreyfus (2011) emphasizes its retentive and evaluative dimensions; Bodhi (2011) emphasizes its discriminative character. What T3 cultivates is more precisely a mode of awareness that is simultaneously receptive and discerning, open and retentive -ultimately, a capacity to hold experience in view without the habitual reactivity that normally drives the perception-action cycle. This shift is ecologically mediated in at least two ways. First, any practice environment -whether a reduced-stimulus retreat or a busy household -provides its own specific texture of affordances that the practitioner learns to relate to differently. In a retreat, the practitioner attends non-reactively to minimal stimuli; in daily life, the same capacity is cultivated amid the affordance-rich environments of domestic, professional, and social life.

The Satipaṭṭhāna Sutta itself recognizes this gradient: its instructions to be mindful "when going forward and returning he acts clearly knowing; when looking ahead and looking away he acts clearly knowing; when flexing and extending his limbs he acts clearly knowing" (MN 10; Anālayo, 2003) describe mindful awareness in the midst of ordinary bodily engagement with the environment, not in withdrawal from it.

Second, the development of T3 requires a widening of practice across ecologies. This would be the progressive extension of open monitoring capacity into increasingly complex [19] affordance landscapes. Rather than a transfer of an internal skill to new contexts, this is an ongoing restructuring of the organism-environment relation itself: each new ecological context requires the practitioner to reconstitute the same quality of awareness within a different affordance landscape (Thompson, 2005; Di Paolo, 2009). Contemplative traditions have long recognized this structure: certain practices are introduced in ecologically simplified conditions before being extended into the complex affordance landscapes of social and worldly life, with each extension requiring the practitioner to reconstitute the same quality of awareness within a qualitatively different ecological context.

Rietveld and Kiverstein's (2014) concept of a "rich landscape of affordances" is revealing here. They argue that skilled individuals are responsive to affordances across multiple domains -not just motor but social, cultural, and normative. The mindful practitioner, on this reading, has developed a distinctive relational skill: the capacity to be responsive to the full richness of the affordance landscape without being captured by any particular affordance. T3, understood ecologically, is the progressive cultivation of precisely this capacity -and its development cannot be specified without reference to the affordance landscapes through which it is constituted.

4.4 T4: Self-Deconstruction States

The fourth transformation involves deep meditative states -including jhāna (absorption) and witness consciousness, potentially extending to "full-absorption episodes" or "consciousnessas-such" (Berkovich-Ohana et al., 2024) -in which the boundaries of the self-pattern are temporarily dissolved. From an ecological perspective, these states involve withdrawals of sensorimotor engagement rather than purely internal cognitive processes. Buddhaghosa's analysis traces how progressive jhāna attainment involves the temporary suspension of the [20] five-sense-field and bodily awareness -effectively dismantling the ecological scaffolding that sustains ordinary self-boundaries (Ñāṇamoli, 2011, Chapter X).

The early discourses specify ecological prerequisites for these states. The Cūḷasuññata Sutta (MN 121) offers a particularly relevant account: the Buddha describes a progressive practice of attending to increasingly "empty" perceptions -first the perception of "forest," then "earth," then progressively subtler objects -until the practitioner attains a perception void of all ordinary content (Ñāṇamoli & Bodhi, 2001). This discourse models T4 as the progressive simplification of the perceptual field which corresponds to a progressive transformation of the organism-environment relation. The practitioner does not merely reduce internal "noise" -they actively restructure the conditions under which experience arises. This restructuring has theoretical implications. The withdrawal of ecological scaffolding in intensive practice does not merely limit action possibilities -it dissolves the affordance structure itself. When the environment ceases to offer the action possibilities that normally sustain the self-pattern -social interaction, purposive activity, narrative-generating events -the pattern loses the ecological conditions of its coherence, and the possibility of dissolution arises. These conditions correspond to classical accounts of the temporary stopping (nirodha) of the personality-factors (khandhas) and the emergence of unsupported discernment (appatiṭṭhita-viññāṇa) (Harvey, 1995, pp. 185-189, 203-205), neuroscientifically paralleled by decreased default mode network activity and reduced medial prefrontal cortex activation associated with self-referential processing (Hölzel et al., 2011).

What this implies is that T4 involves a fundamental shift in how the organism relates to its environment. The practitioner in deep absorption has not moved to a different world; they have restructured the terms of their coupling with the one they are in. The affordance structure does not disappear because stimuli are removed; it dissolves because the organism's relation to those stimuli is transformed. A practitioner sitting in a noisy household may enter [21] absorption not by blocking out the sounds but by ceasing to be the kind of organism for whom those sounds solicit narrative, reaction, or self-referential elaboration -the sounds remain, but their affordance structure relative to this organism has fundamentally shifted.

Similarly, the classical accounts of jhāna attainment do not describe a practitioner who has found a perfectly silent room; they describe one who has reorganized their sensorimotor engagement with whatever environment they inhabit to the point where ordinary affordances no longer grip. The question T5 takes up is what kind of organism emerges from that restructuring -and how it learns to move fluidly across the full range of affordance landscapes it continues to inhabit.

4.5 T5: Self-Pattern Flexibility

Where T4 involves the temporary dissolution of self-pattern boundaries, T5 describes the developing capacity to move fluidly between different modes of self-experience. From an ecological perspective, such flexibility is not an internal capacity, but a constitutive enactment of the organism-environment system characterized by the capacity to reconstitute that coupling across qualitatively different ecological conditions (Rietveld & Kiverstein, 2014). Gallagher's (2021) concept of double attunement, introduced in Section 2, provides a theoretical anchor for this ecological reading. Where that concept describes a "meshed architecture" integrating embodied-affective and environmental-contextual axes, its significance for T5 lies specifically in the horizontal axis -the environmental-contextual dimension. The practitioner developing flexibility does not toggle between immersion and detachment -they maintain a mode that is "not one, not two", fully engaged with the demands of a given ecological context while retaining the capacity to disengage from habitual patterns of self-referential elaboration that the context solicits. The practitioner's double attunement is always an attunement to a specific affordance landscape, and its [22] character necessarily shifts as that landscape changes -the double attunement demanded by a silent retreat is structurally different from the double attunement demanded by a charged interpersonal encounter.

Canonical literature offers a striking articulation of this ecological understanding of flexibility. The Indriyabhāvanā Sutta (MN 152) presents the Buddha's account of the "development of the faculties" (anuttarā indriyabhāvanā) in explicit contrast to the brahmin Pārāsariya's teaching that developing faculties consists in simply not seeing forms or hearing sounds. The Buddha's response is pointed: on that account, a blind person and a deaf person would have developed faculties (Ñāṇamoli & Bodhi, 2001). The rejection is significant for the present argument: to develop one's faculty is not to avoid sense contact, but the capacity to encounter the full range of sensory experience and establish equanimity. The sutta describes this capacity across all six sense domains (eye, ear, nose, tongue, body, intellect), specifying that the practitioner recognizes agreeable, disagreeable, and mixed reactions as "conditioned, gross, dependently arisen" while establishing equanimity with the ease and rapidity of opening and closing one's eyes (MN 152). MN 152 further distinguishes between three developmental levels that illuminate the ecological structure of T5. The person in training (sekha) encounters agreeable and disagreeable reactions and responds with aversionfeeling "horrified, humiliated, and disgusted" by their own reactivity. The noble one with developed faculties (bhāvitindriyo) has a qualitatively different capacity: "If he should wish: 'May I abide perceiving the unrepulsive in the repulsive,' he abides perceiving the unrepulsive in the repulsive. If he should wish: 'May I abide perceiving the repulsive in the unrepulsive,' he abides perceiving the repulsive in the unrepulsive" and, critically, "If he should wish, avoiding both the repulsive and unrepulsive, he abides in equanimity, mindful and fully aware" (MN 152; Ñāṇamoli & Bodhi, 2001). This "if he should wish" (ākaṅkhamāno) formulation describes a mode of flexibility in which the practitioner can [23] adopt different perceptual orientations at will across the full range of sensory encounters.

Read through the lens of ecological psychology, this is the capacity to reconstitute one's relation to the affordance landscape in multiple ways without being captured by any single solicitation (Rietveld & Kiverstein, 2014). The affordances remain -the repulsive and the unrepulsive are both present -but the practitioner's relation to them is no longer rigid.

The ecological constitution of T5 becomes vivid in contrast between practice contexts. Consider a practitioner who has developed substantial flexibility during intensive retreat -the capacity to observe arising and passing phenomena without identification or reactivity. When this practitioner returns to the demands of professional life -a meeting, say, where social hierarchy, performative competence, and time pressure all solicit habitual patterns of self-assertion and anxiety -the retreat-cultivated flexibility must be reconstituted within a radically different affordance landscape. The meeting room affords performances of competence, deference, and assertion; it solicits self-narrative ("I need to impress," "I must not appear uncertain"). The flexibility required here is not the same capacity exercised in retreat, now merely applied in a new location; it is a different organism-environment enactment -one that involves perceiving these social affordances without being captured by their solicitations (Rietveld & Kiverstein, 2014). The practitioner who can sit equanimously with pain on the cushion but cannot navigate a tense interpersonal exchange without rigidity has achieved flexibility in one ecology but not another. The traditional Buddhist path reflects this: the practitioner does not remain indefinitely in retreat but returns to engagement with the world's full complexity, and the movement between seclusion and engagement is itself an ecological practice that cultivates flexibility across different affordance landscapes. This progression parallels T3's ecological generalization but operates at a deeper structural levelwhere T3 extends a mode of attentional awareness across diverse environments, T5 involves the capacity to flexibly reconfigure the entire organism-environment coupling -perceptual, [24] affective, and behavioral -in response to whatever ecological conditions the practitioner inhabits. Cassaniti's (2019) anthropological research on Thai Buddhist mindfulness illustrates this ecological character of flexibility. Her account reveals sati operating as a capacity whose absence -mindlessness -is experienced as distress across a range of everyday activities: choking on a fishbone while eating, stepping on something dangerous while walking, failing to manage emotional difficulty. These can be understood as failures of organism-environment coupling across the affordance-rich textures of ordinary life. Sati succeeds or fails within the context of ecologies -the kitchen, the path, the interpersonal encounter -and its success in one does not guarantee its success in another. T5, then, is not a destination but a developmental condition -the practitioner who can reconstitute organism-environment coupling across the kitchen, the path, and the interpersonal encounter is approaching the threshold of a more fundamental reorientation. T6 asks what it means for that reorientation to become a stable feature of how they inhabit any ecology at all.

4.6 T6: Self-Liberation

Where T5 established ecological flexibility as a capacity -the practitioner's developing ability to reconstitute the organism-environment coupling across qualitatively different conditions -T6 marks the transition from this capacity to an irreversible reorientation. The ecological question T6 poses is therefore not "how does the practitioner navigate different affordance landscapes?" but "what happens when the organism-environment coupling itself is structurally transformed?" This is a different question, and it demands a different kind of analysis. Dambrun and Ricard (2011) describe a stable, functional mode of selflessness; the PTSL identifies this with self-liberation (Berkovich-Ohana et al., 2024; Olendzki, 2010).

[25] What the ecological perspective adds is an account of what that reorientation consists of at the level of the organism-environment system.

The constitutive logic developed in Section 3 is directly relevant. If the self-pattern is constitutively ecological -if selfhood is partly constituted by the mode in which the organism engages with its affordance landscape, not merely influenced by it -then T6 cannot be a purely internal achievement. When Turvey (1992) establishes that affordances are ontologically nonexistent without their complementing organism properties, the implication runs in both directions: if the organism changes in the fundamental way T6 describes -if craving (taṇhā) and aversion (dosa), which structured the organism's relation to its affordance landscape throughout the unawakened life, cease to function as organizers of that relation -then the affordance structure available to this organism has itself changed. Not the world, but the coupled system. This is what makes T6 an ecological event and not merely an internal one. The liberated self-pattern is not a pattern that has transcended its ecology; it is a pattern that relates to its ecology differently, and that different relating is part of what T6 consists of.

The Migajāla Sutta (SN 35:63) specifies what this reorientation consists of at the level of sensory engagement. The Buddha distinguishes between two practitioners: one who retreats to isolated forest dwellings and yet is "living with a partner," and another who lives near a village, associating with monks and nuns, with lay followers, with kings and royal ministers, and is nonetheless described as "lone dweller" -because the craving that was previously his companion has been abandoned (Bodhi, 2000). The principle is radical: what determines ecological solitude is not the presence or absence of social affordances but the mode of engagement with them. The liberated practitioner inhabits the affordance landscape of ordinary social life -forms, sounds, tastes, tactile sensations, ideas -without relishing them, welcoming them, or remaining fastened to them. The affordances remain; the craving- [26] driven structure of engagement has dissolved. The Arahanta Sutta (SN 1:25) makes this visible at the level of language: the arahant continues to use conventional expressions -"I speak," "they speak to me" -engaging with the social affordances of communication and community, but without the conceit "I am" (asmimāna) structuring that engagement, using "expressions just as expressions" (Bodhi, 2000). In ecological terms, the organismenvironment coupling continues uninterrupted; what has changed is the structure of the organism's side of the coupling.

What distinguishes this ecological perspective on T6 from a merely dispositional account of selflessness is that the transformation is not a property of the organism alone but a reorganization of the coupled system. The Devadaha Sutta (SN 35:134) illuminates this from the practitioner's side: the Buddha declares that arahants have completed the work of heedfulness regarding the six contact-media, such that agreeable and disagreeable forms cognizable through all six sense domains no longer persist in consuming the mind even when contacted repeatedly (Bodhi, 2000). What was previously an effortful capacity -the T5 flexibility that had to be actively reconstituted in each new ecological context -has become the structural mode of the organism-environment coupling itself. The practitioner has not escaped the affordance landscape; craving is no longer its companion within it. The equanimity that characterizes T6 is not achieved against the environment as an obstacle to be overcome; it is enacted through ongoing engagement with whatever material and social world the practitioner inhabits (cf. Varela et al., 1991; Thompson, 2007). Engagement continues in the compassion pattern with a configuration of equanimous intersubjective orientation and altruistic motivation that operates without the self-centered constraints of the unawakened self-pattern (Gallagher et al., 2024b).

The early discourses illustrate this transformation with ecological metaphors. The Loṇakapalla Sutta (AN 3:100) addresses how the fruition (vipāka) of past action (kamma) [27] varies depending on the practitioner's developmental state: the same "salt" of past action renders a small bowl of water undrinkable, but has negligible effect when thrown into the River Ganges (Bodhi, 2012). The sutta distinguishes between the abhāvitakāya (undeveloped in body, virtue, mind, and wisdom) and the bhāvitakāya (developed in these qualitiesbhāvitakāyo, bhāvitasīlo, bhāvitacitto, bhāvitapañño): it is the developed practitioner's expanded capacity, not a larger external system, that absorbs kammic results with less intensity (Bodhi, 2012). Read through ecological psychology, this metaphor suggests that the capacity to absorb and transform experience depends on the scale and texture of the organism-environment system. T6 involves becoming a river rather than a bowl: fundamentally reconfiguring the ecological relation through which experience arises, such that the same conditions no longer organize the self-pattern around craving and aversion. The liberated practitioner does not transcend their ecology; they inhabit it as a transformed organism-environment system.

>Discussion

5.1 The Self-Pattern as Organism-Environment System

The analysis of T1-T6 in the preceding sections articulates an overarching claim: self-pattern transformation through meditation is not primarily an intrapsychic process that happens to occur in an environment but a reorganization of the organism-environment system as a whole. This is consistent with the PTS's own theoretical commitments -the inclusion of ecological processes among the ten constitutive dimensions of the self-pattern (Gallagher et al., 2024a) and the recognition that the self-pattern involves dynamical relations extending beyond the individual organism (Gallagher, 2013; Gallagher & Daly, 2018). What the T1-T6 analysis does is make these commitments operative: it shows, for each transformation in turn, what they mean concretely.

[28]

The stronger theoretical claim that follows from this analysis can now be stated plainly. A description of self-pattern transformation that omits ecological specification fails to accurately represent the complexities of the phenomenon that self is. To describe T2 without specifying the affordance landscape in which concentration is cultivated, or T5 without specifying the ecological conditions across which flexibility is constituted, is to describe something that does not occur anywhere. The organism-environment system, not the organism alone, is the proper unit of analysis; the organism alone is a theoretical artifact of the frameworks the PTS explicitly departs from.

This invites an objection worth addressing directly. One might argue that the ecological perspective offered here is merely an elaborate way of saying that context matters -a claim so broad as to be trivially true. The response is that the constitutive claim is stronger and more specific than the contextual one. The contextual claim says: different environments causally produce different meditation outcomes. The constitutive claim says: the transformation just is, in part, an ecological restructuring -such that specifying the transformation without specifying the ecological dimension yields not a less detailed description but an incomplete one. The T1-T6 analysis above demonstrates this specificity: each transformation involves a distinct mode of ecological participation -restructuring of the affordance landscape, engineering of the ecology of attention, ecological generalization of open monitoring, dissolution of ecological scaffolding, reconstitution of organismenvironment coupling across diverse conditions, irreversible reorientation of that couplingthat is not captured by a generic appeal to context. This reorientation carries methodological implications. If self-pattern transformation is ecologically constituted, then the study of meditation cannot be confined to the measurement of internal psychological and neural variables. Researchers must attend to the material, spatial, social, and institutional conditions under which meditation occurs -not as [29] confounding variables to be controlled but as constitutive conditions to be investigated in their own right. The social environment, for instance, is not merely a support structure for individual practitioners; it is the social ecology through which ethical consolidation (T1) and self-pattern flexibility (T5) become possible, functioning as interpersonal affective scaffolding (Colombetti & Krueger, 2015) and analyzed as the participatory sense-making through which social cognition is itself constituted (De Jaegher & Di Paolo, 2007).

5.2 Cross-Cultural Variation and the Secularization Question

If the self-pattern transforms through engagement with specific affordance landscapes, then differences in the material, social, and institutional conditions of practice will produce differences in the kind of transformation that occurs -qualitative differences in pattern, not merely differences in degree. Three ecological configurations illustrate this. The Thai understanding of sati, as Cassaniti (2019) documents, operates within an ecology of village temple life, communal ritual, and embedded lay-monastic relations, producing a relational and culturally distributed form of mindfulness whose failure is experienced as interpersonal and embodied distress. The Burmese vipassanā tradition that gave rise to Western mindfulness emerged, as Braun (2013) documents in detail, from specific material and institutional conditions -print-mediated dissemination of monastic learning, the production of meditation manuals for lay practitioners, colonial-era institutional restructuring -that constituted a systematized, individually-oriented, progress-marked form of practice unavailable in that form before the twentieth century. Secular mindfulness in clinical and corporate settings, as Cook (2016) analyzes, operates within yet another affordance landscape, one shaped by institutional time constraints, therapeutic frameworks, and the absence of the ethical and relational scaffolding that structures the other two configurations.

These are not the same practice in different costumes; they are different ecological [30] configurations that constitute different transformation pathways. Cross-cultural variation in meditation outcomes is therefore not noise to be controlled in comparative research but signal about ecological constitution -and attending to it as such is one of the most important methodological implications of the present framework (McMahan & Braun, 2017).

The ecological framework provides a more precise account of what is at stake in secular transposition than is typically offered in critical mindfulness literature. What changes when contemplative practice is transposed between ecological configurations is not simply "context" but the entire affordance landscape -the sangha, the teacher-student relationship, the ritual calendar, the material culture of practice -through which specific transformations are constituted. The ethical scaffolding of T1, the temporally structured ecology of T2, the graduated ecological generalization of T3: each depends on affordance conditions that secular transposition may restructure fundamentally. This is why the architects of secular mindfulness programs themselves have been attentive to the risks of transposition -the concern that translating meditative challenges into vernacular idioms may obscure the practice's deepest features -while nonetheless seeking to navigate productively between preservation and adaptation (Williams & Kabat-Zinn, 2011). The present framework does not adjudicate this navigation, but it specifies what is at stake in it with greater precision than a generic appeal to decontextualization allows. Practitioners in secular settings may achieve ecologically constituted transformations of a genuinely different kind -not lesser, but structurally distinct -and different ecological conditions may produce transformations that are valuable in different ways (Berry et al., 2020). What is essential is precision about what different ecological configurations can and cannot constitute, since such precision is the condition for understanding what different forms of mindfulness practice can actually achieve (Furnell et al., 2024).

5.3 Implications for Buddhist Psychology

The special collection in which this paper is situated seeks to explore Buddhist psychology as providing overarching models for understanding the human mind and meditation practice.

The ecological extension of the PTSL presented here contributes to this project by demonstrating that Buddhist psychology's understanding of practice has always been implicitly ecological -and that the present paper makes this understanding explicit with support from the framework of ecological psychology and enactivism.

The threefold training of sīla, samādhi, and paññā describes a progressive restructuring of the practitioner's relationship to the material and social world (Olendzki, 2010). The Vinaya, the monastic code, is among other things a detailed specification of the ecological conditions under which particular transformations are possible: from the design of monastic dwellings to the regulation of communal meals to the structuring of daily and seasonal time. It functions, in the terms of the present framework, as an affordance engineering document, as a systematic attempt to constitute the organism-environment conditions under which the self-pattern can reorganize. Buddhist psychology has always understood that the mind does not transform independently of its environmental conditions.

More strikingly, the canonical sutta evidence examined in Section 4 demonstrates that this ecological understanding is not merely implicit in the institutional architecture of the tradition but is articulated at the level of doctrine. The Satipaṭṭhāna Sutta (MN 10) locates mindfulness practice within a specific environmental configuration from its opening lines, treating ecological situation as constitutive of the practice rather than incidental to it (Anālayo, 2003). The Indriyabhāvanā Sutta (MN 152) defines the development of the faculties not as withdrawal from sense contact but as the capacity to engage the full range of sensory affordances without being captured by their solicitations -an explicitly ecological account of T5 (Ñāṇamoli & Bodhi, 2001). The Migajāla Sutta (SN 35:63) redefines solitude [32] in terms of the structure of the organism's engagement with its affordance landscape: a practitioner living near kings and ministers is "living alone" if craving no longer structures their engagement; a practitioner in a forest hermitage is "living with a companion" if it does (Bodhi, 2000). These are not incidentally ecological formulations -they are structurally so.

The present paper makes what Buddhist psychology has long understood explicit within the framework of contemporary ecological and enactivist theory, demonstrating that the extension proposed here is a development the tradition's own sources already support.

5.4 Directions for Empirical Research

The PTSL was put forward explicitly as a heuristic model intended to ground empirical research, and Berkovich-Ohana et al. (2024) outline several directions for that research: neurophenomenological investigation of the transformation sequence, cognitivepsychological assessment of the markers associated with each transformation, and computational modeling within the free energy principle and active inference framework (see Lutz et al., 2025, for a review of neurophenomenological approaches to meditation and their integration with computational modeling). These are substantial and well-motivated directions. The present analysis puts forward the ecological specification for each to be realized adequately. The PTSL's call for cognitive-psychological assessment of transformation markers -concentration, self-centeredness, self-bias, ethical aspects, emotional aspects -assumes, at least implicitly, that these markers can be assessed in a given environment and the results will generalize. The ecological account challenges this assumption directly. If T5, self-pattern flexibility, is an organism-environment enactment rather than an internal capacity, then assessing it in a single ecological setting -a laboratory, a clinical intake, a single retreat environment -will not measure what the PTSL's own model describes. A practitioner who [33] demonstrates equanimous responsiveness in one affordance landscape but rigidity in another has not achieved T5 in the sense the model intends; single-context assessment will systematically overestimate the depth of T5 development. The ecological complement to the PTSL's cognitive-psychological research program is therefore cross-contextual assessment: the same transformation markers measured across multiple, qualitatively distinct ecological conditions -for instance, retreat environments, domestic settings, and high-demand professional or interpersonal contexts -with the expectation that it is the pattern across contexts, not performance in any single one, that indexes self-pattern transformation.

Ecological momentary assessment (EMA) methods, which sample experience and behavior across the naturally varying affordance landscapes of daily life, are particularly well-suited to this purpose (see Shiffman et al., 2008, for a methodological review of ecological momentary assessment as real-time sampling of behavior and experience in naturalistic contexts).

PTSL's neurophenomenological direction calls for combining neural investigation with phenomenological inquiry to compare stages of meditative attainment and test the proposed transformation sequence (Berkovich-Ohana et al., 2020). What the ecological account specifies is the site where the sequence is most consequentially tested: not within a retreat setting, but in the retreat-to-daily-life transition. If self-pattern transformation is ecologically constituted, then re-entering a structurally different affordance landscape is not a post-hoc test of a previously completed transformation but a moment in the transformation process itself. A purely causal account -one in which ecological conditions influence but do not constitute the transformation -would predict that retreat-cultivated capacities transfer to new contexts with varying degrees of efficiency, declining gradually as contextual support is withdrawn. The ecological account generates a more specific and distinguishable prediction: that the character of the transformation itself differs qualitatively depending on the affordance landscape in which it is enacted. T5 flexibility reconstituted amid the social affordances of a [34] charged interpersonal encounter is a structurally different organism-environment enactment from T5 flexibility enacted on the meditation cushion -different not only in difficulty but in what the flexibility consists of at the level of the coupled system. This qualitative difference should be detectable in phenomenological reports (practitioners describing not just reduced stability but a different kind of awareness), in behavioral signatures (distinct patterns of affordance engagement rather than uniform performance decline), and in neural dynamics (reorganization patterns rather than simple attenuation of meditation-related markers).

Conversely, if retreat-to-daily-life transitions produce only the gradual decay predicted by transfer-of-training models -performance declining uniformly as a function of contextual dissimilarity -this would challenge the constitutive claim and favor a causal account. The presence of qualitative reorganization, rather than quantitative decline, at ecological transition points is the specific empirical signature that distinguishes the constitutive hypothesis from its causal alternative.

Finally, PTSL's proposed direction of computational modeling within the free energy principle and active inference framework opens a precise technical question that the ecological account can specify. In contemplative neuroscience, free energy minimization modeling has been applied primarily at the scale of the individual organism's predictive processes, even though the broader FEP literature has begun to address organismenvironment dynamics (Bruineberg & Rietveld, 2014; Clark, 2013). A computational model that brackets ecological dynamics and models only internal predictive processes will, by construction, be unable to represent what PTSL's own ecological dimension describes.

Extending active inference models to encompass organism-environment dynamics -the way affordance landscapes shape the precision-weighting of sensory signals, or the way socialecological scaffolding constrains and enables attentional deployment -is a technically tractable direction that would give the PTSL's empirical program ecological teeth [35] (Bruineberg & Rietveld, 2014; Clark, 2013). These three directions are complementary and amenable to a coordinated mixed-methods research program. The combination of ecological momentary assessment with neurophenomenological methods has been demonstrated in contemplative research (Berkovich-Ohana et al., 2020; Lutz et al., 2025), and computational modeling within the FEP already calls for integrating brain-body-environment dynamics (Bruineberg & Rietveld, 2014). What has not yet been attempted is the systematic coordination of these methods around the ecological question: how does the organismenvironment system reorganize across the PTSL's transformation sequence?

6. Conclusion

Before synthesizing the argument, several limitations should be acknowledged. The claims advanced here are primarily theoretical. While I draw on anthropological evidence (Cassaniti, 2019), historical case studies (Braun, 2013), and contemporary ethnographic research (Cook, 2016; Sharf, 2015), the constitutive role of ecological processes in each transformation remains a conceptual argument that requires the kind of empirical investigation outlined in Section 5.4. The present paper focuses specifically on ecological processes; the PTSL also set aside behavioural and normative processes, which are closely intertwined with ecological ones. A complete account of the world-orientation dimension of self-pattern transformation would need to integrate all three, attending to how behavioural habits are ecologically scaffolded (Colombetti & Krueger, 2015; Verplanken & Sui, 2019) and how normative commitments restructure the affordance landscape (Sevinc & Lazar, 2019). Finally, while all ecologies are equally real agents in transformation, what can vary is the fit between the selfpattern's characteristic mode of engagement and the ecological configuration it inhabits: a relational quality that belongs to the organism-environment coupling itself, not to either relatum in isolation. This fit or misfit is part of what each transformation concretely consists [36] of -and empirical research attending to it would need to investigate the coupling as its unit of analysis.

With these qualifications in view, the analysis can be drawn together. The pattern theory of selflessness represents an important advance in our understanding of how Buddhist meditation transforms the sense of self. By grounding this transformation in the pattern theory of self's framework, the PTSL provides a nuanced, multidimensional account that avoids both the reification of self and the simplistic claims of self-elimination. This paper has sought to contribute to the ongoing development of this framework by articulating what the PTSL yields when the ecological dimension is brought back into view.

The analysis has shown that ecological processes are constitutive contributors to each stage of the PTSL's six-transformation model. Ethical consolidation (T1) unfolds within social ecologies that structure the affordances for ethical action -whether a sangha, a family, or a workplace. Attentional training (T2) depends on the specific material ecology in which concentration is cultivated -whether the reduced affordance landscape of a retreat or the rich textures of everyday life. Mindful awareness (T3) involves a distinctive mode of engaging with the affordance landscape that must be generalized across diverse environments. Selfdeconstruction (T4) is enabled by ecological conditions that withdraw the scaffolding on which the ordinary self-pattern depends. Self-pattern flexibility (T5) demands the capacity to navigate diverse environments without rigidity. Self-liberation (T6) involves not the transcendence of ecology but its transformation -a reconfigured mode of engagement with whatever world the practitioner inhabits.

By integrating ecological processes as constitutive elements of the PTSL, we arrive at a more authentic account of meditative self-transformation -one that is faithful to both the philosophical commitments of the PTS and the ecological wisdom of the Buddhist tradition.
