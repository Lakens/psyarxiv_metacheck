To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

[div-01]

Social norms are pervasive in human societies, yet groups vary in norm strength and tolerance of deviance-a dimension known as cultural tightness-looseness (Gelfand et al., 2006 (Gelfand et al., , 2011)). Lin et al. (2026) extended this framework to social class, proposing that social class is not merely a marker of socioeconomic standing but a form of within-society cultural ecology. In their account, lower-class contexts afford less margin for error and therefore foster stronger norms, lower tolerance of deviance, and greater support for punishment. These class-based differences in norm strength were further expected to extend to a broader orderopenness trade-off. The class-tightness link was theorized to operate through a threat-based pathway in which lower-class contexts expose individuals to greater socioeconomic threat, heightening vigilance toward danger and thereby promoting the experience, endorsement, and socialization of tight norms. Lin et al. (2026) presented findings from five main studies and two supplemental studies as evidence for these claims.

This account is consequential. It could help explain class-linked differences in norm adherence, tolerance of deviance, and support for punishment, as well as how such differences may contribute to the persistence of social inequality (Lin et al., 2026). It could also inform more responsive institutional policies and practices for lower-class individuals. These implications depend, however, on how consistently the predicted class-tightness pattern is observed and how well the proposed threat pathway is supported. The direction, generality, and mechanism of the class-tightness relation in Lin et al.'s (2026) article therefore warrant closer examination.

We reanalyzed Lin et al.'s (2026) publicly available data to assess the evidence for their proposed class-tightness account. Across Studies 1, 3, and 4, as well as the supplemental word-embedding analysis, the evidence did not consistently support the predicted lower-class-tightness pattern. Study 2 yielded average associations consistent with the hypothesized lower-class preference for order over openness, but substantial heterogeneity and prediction intervals spanning zero left the generality of this pattern unresolved. In Studies 3 and 4, significant threat-related indirect effects coexisted with total associations that were mostly nonsignificant or opposite to the proposed lower-classtightness pattern. These indirect effects therefore did not, on their own, establish a threatbased explanation of a general class difference in tightness. In Study 5, assignment to the lower-class Bimboola condition produced higher scores on both tightness measures, but neither indirect effect through the preregistered mediator, perceived socioeconomic threat, was significant. Together, our reanalyses indicate that the evidence is insufficient to establish the proposed general lower-class-tightness pattern or its threat-based mechanism.

Are Lower-Class Norms Tighter? Lin et al.'s (2026) first hypothesis was that lower-class contexts are characterized by tighter norms than higher-class contexts. Here, we examine evidence from Study 1, Supplemental Study 1, total associations in Studies 3 and 4-which were designed primarily to test the proposed threat pathway-and the supplemental word-embedding analysis.

Our reanalyses of Supplemental Study 1, a diary study, supported the reported associations of lower social class with greater punishment motivation and a higher likelihood of self-reported punishment. However, our reanalyses also identified ambiguity in the interpretation of sanction support in Study 1, sensitivity of its focal results to respondentlevel adjustment, mostly nonsignificant or opposite-direction total associations between social-class indicators and tightness outcomes in Studies 3 and 4, and limited support for the full predicted pattern in the word-embedding analyses.

Study 1 Does Not Provide Consistent Evidence for Tighter Lower-Class Norms

In Study 1, Lin et al. (2026) reanalyzed data from Eriksson et al. (2021), who asked participants in 57 countries to rate the appropriateness of norm-violating behaviors and several possible responses to those violations. Social class was operationalized using parental education. Participants were classified as lower class (coded 1) if neither parent had a college degree and as higher class (coded 0) if at least one parent did. Lin et al. (2026) reported that lower-class participants rated norm violations as less appropriate and showed greater support for punishment. Their inference of greater punishment support, however, rested on reversecoded appropriateness ratings of "doing nothing." The article and supplemental materials did not explain why the Study 1 analyses used inaction ratings rather than the available ratings of specific sanctioning responses. Eriksson et al. (2021) distinguished among metanorms governing verbal confrontation, gossip, social ostracism, and inaction. A participant may view doing nothing as inappropriate without viewing gossip, verbal confrontation, or social ostracism as appropriate. Higher reverse-coded inaction ratings therefore indicate stronger disapproval of doing nothing, but they do not by themselves establish greater endorsement of the specific sanctions.

To assess this issue, we examined the original appropriateness ratings of the specific sanctions in Lin et al.'s (2026) released version of Eriksson et al.'s (2021) data. We used Lin et al.'s (2026) three-level random-intercept specification, with ratings nested within participants and participants nested within countries. Lower social class predicted lower appropriateness ratings for gossip, B = -0.084, SE = 0.012, p < .001, and verbal confrontation, B = -0.021, SE = 0.010, p = .027; the association with social ostracism was also negative but not significant, B = -0.016, SE = 0.012, p = .160. Thus, the original ratings did not support greater lower-class endorsement of the measured sanctions; lower-class participants instead rated two of the three sanctions as significantly less appropriate.

A second issue concerns the treatment of respondents' overall rating levels. Response tendencies can bias observed scores and correlations between measures (Baumgartner & Steenkamp, 2001; Cronbach, 1946; Jackson & Messick, 1958). To control for response sets in the appropriateness ratings, Eriksson et al. (2021) used a respondent-level adjustment that they termed standardization. We therefore examined whether Lin et al.'s (2026) reported class differences in norm-violation and reverse-coded inaction ratings persisted when this procedure was applied. Each participant's 50 appropriateness ratings were adjusted by subtracting that respondent's mean supplied in Lin et al.'s (2026) released Study 1 data and adding the average of all the respondents' means. This adjustment expresses ratings relative to each participant's overall rating level while preserving differences among their ratings (Eriksson et al., 2021).

We refitted Lin et al.'s (2026) focal models using the adjusted ratings. Social class no longer significantly predicted appropriateness ratings of norm violations, B = 0.004, SE = 0.007, p = .554, or reverse-coded inaction ratings, B = 0.007, SE = 0.008, p = .404. The adjusted analyses showed a mixed pattern across specific sanctions: lower-class participants rated verbal confrontation and social ostracism as significantly more appropriate but gossip as significantly less appropriate. The full results of our reanalyses using the original and adjusted ratings are reported in Supplemental Table S1. Lin et al.'s (2026) inference of greater lower-class punishment support from disapproval of inaction is undermined by the original ratings of specific sanctions. Their reported class differences in norm-violation and reverse-coded inaction ratings also did not remain significant after Eriksson et al.'s (2021) respondent-level adjustment. Although respondents' overall rating levels may reflect substantive evaluations as well as response tendencies, neither the original nor the adjusted analyses provide consistent evidence that lower-class individuals are both less tolerant of norm violations and more supportive of the measured sanctions.

Total Associations in Studies 3 and 4 Do Not Support a General Lower-Class-Tightness Pattern

Although Studies 3 and 4 were designed primarily to test the proposed threat-based pathway, they also provide data bearing on Lin et al.'s (2026) first hypothesis that lower social class is associated with greater tightness. We examined total associations using zeroorder Pearson correlations between the social-class indicators and tightness outcomes.

Because higher values on all class indicators represented higher social class, the proposed lower-class-tightness pattern implies negative associations with tightness outcomes.

In our reanalysis of Study 3, the composite measure of current social class was formed by averaging standardized household income, education, and subjective socioeconomic status, following Lin et al.'s (2026) procedure. The composite was not significantly associated with experienced tightness in life, r = .02, p = .541, or perceived situational constraint, r = -.03, p = .263. Its only significant association was in the opposite direction, indicating that higher current social class was associated with greater desire for societal tightness, r = .12, p < .001.

When household income, personal income, education, and subjective socioeconomic status were examined separately, most associations with the three tightness outcomes were nonsignificant. Of the three significant associations, two were positive and therefore opposite to the predicted direction. Education was positively associated with desire for societal tightness, r = .06, p = .041, as was subjective socioeconomic status, r = .20, p < .001. The only significant association in the predicted direction was between subjective socioeconomic status and perceived situational constraint, r = -.07, p = .018. The full correlation matrix for our reanalysis of Study 3 is reported in Supplemental Table S2.

Study 4 showed an even clearer departure from the predicted total-association pattern.

The current-class composite, constructed following Lin et al.'s (2026) procedure, was positively associated with all three parental tightness outcomes: tight parental practices, r = .12, p < .001, rules-as-protection beliefs, r = .08, p = .012, and punitive responses to child deviance, r = .07, p = .028. Thus, higher rather than lower current social class was linked to greater parental tightness.

When the current-class indicators were examined separately, none showed a significant negative association with any parental tightness outcome. Household income and personal income were not significantly associated with any of the three outcomes, |r|s ≤ .06, ps ≥ .102. Education was positively associated with all three outcomes, with rs ranging from .09 to .12 and ps ≤ .011. Subjective socioeconomic status was positively associated with tight parental practices, r = .12, p < .001, and rules-as-protection beliefs, r = .09, p = .007, but was not significantly associated with punitive responses to child deviance, r = .04, p = .205.

Childhood financial condition, the measure of class origin in Study 4, was not significantly associated with any of the three parental tightness outcomes, with rs ranging from -.01 to .06 and ps ≥ .069. The full correlation matrix for our reanalysis of Study 4 is reported in Supplemental Table S3.

Across the outcomes measured in Studies 3 and 4, total associations were mostly nonsignificant or opposite to the predicted direction. These results do not support the proposed general association between lower social class and greater tightness.

Word-Embedding Results Support a Relative Contrast but Not the Full Predicted

Pattern Lin et al. (2026) also conducted a supplemental word-embedding analysis to examine whether collective semantic representations link lower social class with tightness and higher social class with looseness. Using GloVe Common Crawl embeddings, they computed pairwise cosine similarities between higher-and lower-class words and tightness-looseness words and tested whether these similarities varied by class, tightness-looseness, and their interaction. Their analysis yielded a significant Class × Tightness-Looseness interaction and two significant simple effects in the predicted directions, with tightness words more similar to lower-class than to higher-class words and looseness words showing the reverse pattern.

They interpreted these findings as evidence that collective semantic representations link lower social class to tighter norms and higher social class to looser norms.

We re-estimated the interaction and simple-effect models after addressing two issues in Lin et al.'s (2026) original GloVe analysis. First, the released dataset contained 4,120 rows, whereas Lin et al.'s reported word lists imply 4,080 word pairs [(51 higher-class words + 51 lower-class words) × (20 tightness words + 20 looseness words)]. The additional 40 rows resulted from duplicate pairings involving the lower-class word "impoverished." Second, the original simple-effect models did not fully account for the crossed structure of the data, in which each social-class word was paired with multiple tightness-looseness words, and each tightness-looseness word was paired with multiple social-class words. Lin et al.'s simpleeffect models included a random intercept only for tightness-looseness words, whereas their interaction model accounted for this crossed structure by including random intercepts for both word sets. We therefore removed the 40 additional rows and fitted each simple-effect model with crossed random intercepts for both social-class and tightness-looseness words.

After these corrections, the Class × Tightness-Looseness interaction remained significant, B = 0.039, SE = 0.005, p < .001. The interaction indicates that the class difference in cosine similarity varied between tightness and looseness words. However, tightness words were not significantly more similar to lower-class than higher-class words, B = 0.023, SE = 0.013, p = .092. Looseness words were not significantly more similar to higher-class than lower-class words, B = -0.016, SE = 0.014, p = .256. Both simple-effect coefficients represent lower-class minus higher-class cosine similarity. Although both simple-effect estimates were in the predicted directions, neither was significant. Thus, the corrected analysis supports a relative contrast but does not establish the two directional class differences that constitute the full predicted pattern.

The full predicted pattern was also not reproduced across alternative embedding models. To assess robustness to variation in training corpora and embedding methods, we fitted the same interaction and simple-effect models to pairwise cosine similarities computed using four embedding models previously applied in psychological research (Prystawski et al., 2022; Wulff & Mata, 2025). These included two static word-embedding models-fastText Common Crawl + Wikipedia English (300d) and Google News word2vec-and two textembedding models-OpenAI text-embedding-3-large and sentence-transformers/all-mpnetbase-v2. None of these models simultaneously reproduced two significant simple effects in the predicted directions. The Class × Tightness-Looseness interaction, however, was significant in three of the four alternative models. Full model-specific estimates are reported in Supplemental Table S4.

Taken together, these word-embedding results generally support a relative semantic contrast, but they do not establish the full pattern of two directional class differences reported by Lin et al. (2026).

A "Fundamental Class Divergence" in Order Versus Openness? Lin et al.'s (2026) second hypothesis extended their tightness-looseness account to a broader divergence in preferences, with lower-class individuals favoring order more and openness less than higher-class individuals. Study 2 examined this hypothesis through a meta-analysis of correlations derived from national and cross-national survey databases spanning 52 years and 140 countries. The analyses covered 175 survey items grouped into 10 topics. The authors reported that both current social class (respondents' socioeconomic position at the time of the survey) and class origin (their socioeconomic background during upbringing) were negatively associated, on average, with preferences for order over openness. They interpreted these findings as identifying a "fundamental class divergence in order versus openness" (p. 10). Our reanalyses revealed substantial heterogeneity and prediction intervals spanning zero, both overall and within most topics, raising questions about the generality of the proposed class difference.

The 10 topics were conventionalism, need for structure, tight values, morality, support for punishment, law and governance, experiences of freedom versus constraint, gender attitudes, ethnocentrism, and prejudice (Lin et al., 2026). Extending the account across these diverse topics required judgments about which survey items represented order versus openness and how they should be grouped. For example, similarly worded questions about attitudes toward having immigrants or foreign workers as neighbors were assigned to the ethnocentrism topic in Afrobarometer and the prejudice topic in the World Values Survey/European Values Study (their Supplemental Table S13). Lin et al. (2026) drew on prior tightness-looseness research and provided topic descriptions and search terms, but did not explain this distinction or report independent checks of item selection or classification.

Interpreting the results as a broad class divergence also requires examining how well the pooled means characterize associations across these varied measures and survey settings.

We therefore reanalyzed Lin et al.'s (2026) publicly released Pearson correlations, retaining their item selection and topic assignments. We transformed the correlations to Fisher's z and fitted separate multilevel meta-analytic models for current social class and class origin. The models incorporated sampling variances and random intercepts for database-specific items, countries, and survey waves. This cross-classified structure modeled clustering by shared items and survey contexts (Fernández-Castilla et al., 2019).

In our models, the pooled associations were negative for both current social class and class origin (Fisher's zs = -0.074 and -0.132, respectively), but the associations contributing to these averages varied substantially. We quantified this variation using I², which estimates the proportion of variation in effect estimates attributable to heterogeneity rather than sampling error (Higgins et al., 2003). For the multilevel models, total I² was calculated using the sum of the estimated item-, country-, and survey-wave-level heterogeneity variances and the typical sampling variance (Cheung, 2014). The resulting estimates were 90.6% for current social class and 88.2% for class origin, both indicating high heterogeneity. Under the fitted models, approximately nine tenths of the total variation in effect estimates was attributable to differences in the underlying associations rather than sampling error. Assessing the generality of the proposed class difference therefore requires examining the range of associations underlying these negative averages.

The pooled means and their confidence intervals describe the average associations and uncertainty about those averages, rather than the spread of the underlying associations (Deeks et al., 2024). We therefore calculated 95% prediction intervals to characterize the range of underlying associations. These intervals incorporate heterogeneity as well as uncertainty about the pooled mean, providing a model-based range for the true association in a comparable combination of survey item, country, and wave (Riley et al., 2011). Both overall prediction intervals crossed zero: [-0.239, 0.091] for current social class and [-0.271, 0.006] for class origin, on Fisher's z scale. Despite the negative pooled means, these ranges encompassed an absence of the hypothesized class difference for comparable item-setting combinations. The uncertainty therefore concerned whether the class difference would be present for particular measures and settings, as well as how strong it would be. Full estimates are reported in Supplemental Table S5. Lin et al. (2026) also reported negative average associations across individual topics.

To assess how well the topic-specific averages characterized the underlying associations, we repeated the meta-analyses separately for each topic and examined both heterogeneity and prediction intervals. Heterogeneity remained substantial within topics, with total I² ranging from 87.0% to 98.3% for current social class and from 80.2% to 97.4% for class origin. All 10 current-class prediction intervals crossed zero, as did six of the eight class-origin prediction intervals we estimated (Supplemental Table S6). Moreover, adding topic as a moderator to the overall models accounted for little of the estimated heterogeneity (Supplemental Materials, Section S2.2). The heterogeneity was therefore not confined to differences between topics.

We also examined whether country-level cultural tightness accounted for heterogeneity in the overall class-preference associations, controlling for survey year. We reproduced Lin et al.'s (2026) finding of a weaker negative association for current social class in tighter countries. Extending this analysis to class origin, we found no significant moderation. Adding tightness accounted for little of the total heterogeneity, and prediction intervals at the 25th, 50th, and 75th percentiles of country-level tightness included zero for both class indicators (Supplemental Table S7).

Our reanalyses identified substantial heterogeneity, with predictive ranges encompassing an absence of the hypothesized class difference both overall and within most topics. The negative pooled averages alone therefore do not establish the generality implied by the claim of a "fundamental class divergence in order versus openness" (Lin et al., 2026, p. 10).

Is the Threat-Based Mechanism Established? Lin et al.'s (2026) third claim was that class-based differences in tightness-looseness arise because lower-class contexts expose individuals to greater socioeconomic threat, which heightens perceptions of danger and, in turn, promotes the experience, endorsement, and socialization of tighter norms. Studies 3 and 4 reported indirect associations consistent with this account, with socioeconomic threat followed by perceived danger (Study 3) or danger vigilance (Study 4) as sequential mediators. These cross-sectional mediation analyses, however, do not establish the proposed causal sequence (MacKinnon et al., 2007), a limitation that Lin et al. (2026) acknowledged when discussing Study 3 in their supplemental materials (p. 30).

The pattern of total and direct associations further qualifies the interpretation of these indirect effects. As shown above, total associations in Studies 3 and 4 did not consistently follow the proposed lower-class-tightness pattern. We also examined Lin et al.'s (2026) publicly released mediation-model outputs for current social class and class origin, summarized in our Supplemental Table S8. In seven of the nine models, both the serial and total indirect effects were significantly negative, whereas the direct effects were significantly positive. This pattern is commonly described as inconsistent or competitive mediation (MacKinnon et al., 2000; Zhao et al., 2010). In those seven models, the negative total indirect effects were offset by positive direct effects, leaving total associations that were either nonsignificant or significantly positive. These indirect effects therefore do not, on their own, establish that socioeconomic threat explains a general lower-class-tightness pattern.

In Study 5, Lin et al. (2026) tested the class-tightness link experimentally using an adapted Bimboola paradigm that jointly varied class position and threat-related socioecological conditions. The conditions differed in assigned group income, job eligibility, housing affordability, neighborhood crime, and grocery constraints. Participants in the lowerclass condition rated tightness-related behaviors as more important (importance of tightness) and perceived stronger norms in their hypothetical Bimboola environment (tightness in life) than those in the higher-class condition. Lin et al. (2026) interpreted these differences as a coping response to socioeconomic threat. The condition effects, however, do not establish that perceived socioeconomic threat accounted for the differences in tightness.

Their Study 5 preregistration (Question 8) explicitly proposed testing whether perceived socioeconomic threat, assessed using the manipulation check, mediated the condition effects. This mediation analysis was not reported in the article or supplemental materials and was not included in the released Study 5 analysis code. We therefore tested this hypothesis using the publicly available data. The two manipulation-check items asked participants to rate their assigned group on response scales ranging from financially insecure to financially abundant and from poor to rich. We used the two-item composite provided in the released data and reverse-scored it so that higher scores represented greater perceived socioeconomic threat.

Experimental condition was coded 1 for the lower-class condition and 0 for the higher-class condition. The mediator composite and both outcome scores were standardized before model estimation. We fitted separate single-mediator models for importance of tightness and tightness in life, using 5,000 bootstrap resamples to estimate 95% percentile confidence intervals for the indirect effects (MacKinnon et al., 2007; Shrout & Bolger, 2002).

Before standardization, participants in the lower-class condition had higher perceivedthreat scores (M = 5.89) than those in the higher-class condition (M = 2.64), p < .001.

However, after accounting for condition, perceived socioeconomic threat did not significantly predict importance of tightness, b = -0.094, p = .278, or tightness in life, b = -0.019, p = .822. Neither indirect effect was significant: ab = -0.175, 95% CI [-0.517, 0.167], for importance of tightness, and ab = -0.035, 95% CI [-0.392, 0.320], for tightness in life. Full mediation estimates are reported in Supplemental Table S9.

Taken together, Studies 3 and 4 leave the proposed causal sequence unresolved, and their indirect effects do not, on their own, substantiate a threat-based explanation of a general lower-class-tightness pattern. Our mediation analyses of Study 5 did not support perceived socioeconomic threat as the process accounting for the experimental effects. These accounts suggest that stricter responses may be expected among lower-or higher-class individuals, depending on the conduct at issue and how it is interpreted. Lin et al.'s (2026) cultural claim requires evidence that class associations across distinct normative responses support a general lower-class-tightness pattern. The inconsistent directions in our reanalyses undermine the empirical basis for that generalization.

Interpreting Study 2's overall and topic-specific averages as evidence of a broad class divide in order versus openness requires considering the variation among the associations they summarize. Our reanalyses revealed substantial heterogeneity and prediction intervals that included zero, both overall and within most topics. The uncertainty about generality therefore arose within the evidence selected to support the cultural divide, rather than only at unexamined boundaries of the account. The proposed cultural account needs to address variation within topics and specify which class differences in preferences it explains and under what conditions.

The threat-based explanation requires evidence that socioeconomic threat accounts for the proposed class differences in tightness. The cross-sectional mediation analyses in Studies 3 and 4 did not establish causal ordering. Moreover, their negative indirect effects often coexisted with positive direct effects, so the threat-related paths did not, by themselves, characterize the overall direction of the class-tightness associations. An adaptive account should connect specific features of socioeconomic disadvantage to the psychological processes through which they shape behavior (Sheehy-Skeffington, 2020). Study 5 showed that the combined Bimboola manipulation affected tightness-related responses, but our mediation reanalysis did not support perceived socioeconomic threat as the process accounting for those effects. The present evidence therefore leaves open which psychological processes link class contexts to normative responses.

Further theoretical development requires accounting for the variation in class-related normative responses and specifying when and why class differences in norm strength emerge.

A general contrast between tighter lower-class and looser higher-class cultures must be empirically established before it can serve as a premise for explaining the persistence of social inequality.
