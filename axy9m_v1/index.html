This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Several features outside of the conventional assumptions in traditional item response theory (IRT) models have been revealed to be related, yet no unifying framework explains the features simultaneously. They can be unified by treating each cause as a distinct reason, and then regarding every reason as a choice in a latent multiple-choice item. Here, we propose the Microstate Response Model (MiRM), which decomposes each response category (macrostate) into multiple latent reasons (microstates) using Boltzmann weights. The consequences of this simple framework are substantial; for example, it reads the lower and upper asymptotes of item characteristic curves (ICCs) as additional states, and ICC asymmetry and item complexity as multiple states. The model unifies many classical models from the dichotomous logistic family to mixture models. Moreover, the algebra of the model provides the intuition that items and tests are the same object. Two consequences naturally come out: partial credits and test scores are unified, and a new test-level monotonicity condition is proposed. We examine item characteristics including nonmonotonicity on PISA 2015, and show that MiRM provides a deeper understanding of items while achieving the strongest overall performance across fit, parsimony, and prediction.

1. Introduction

Item response theory (IRT) aims to estimate a person's latent trait or ability from item response data, and further understand each item's characteristics that enable the measurement. Since the true latent ability cannot be observed, the theory typically assumes certain properties of the items (Rasch, 1960) and the trait (Holland & Rosenbaum, 1986; Sijtsma, 1998), thereby making persons and items identifiable and interpretable on a common scale. For example, the characteristics of an item should not depend on which group of respondents takes it, the same ability should be obtained regardless of which items are used, no variable other than the ability should explain the responses, and a higher ability should lead to a higher probability of a correct response. In fact, not every assumption holds perfectly in real-world data. For example, respondents sometimes answer correctly by chance or wrongly by mistake, and items may work differently for different groups of respondents. We call these exceptions departures from the conventional IRT assumptions, and many of them appear in the shapes of item characteristic curves (ICCs), which show how the probability of a correct response changes with ability.

One approach to resolving the tension between theory and practice is to model the mechanism that is believed to generate each departure, by adding a parameter for it. The 3-parameter logistic (3PL) model adds a guessing parameter 𝑝 𝑐 , as low-ability respondents sometimes 'guess' the answer, thereby keeping the lower asymptote of its ICC -the probability of a correct response at the lowest abilitiesabove zero (Birnbaum, 1968). The 4-parameter logistic (4PL) model (Barton & Lord, 1981) likewise adds a parameter for high-ability respondents who 'slip' through careless mistakes, thereby keeping the upper asymptote below one. Furthermore, some empirical ICCs are asymmetric; they rise steeply on one side and slowly on the other. The Logistic Positive Exponent (LPE; Samejima, 2000) model and the Residual Heteroscedasticity (RH; Molenaar, 2015) model tried to capture this asymmetry by adding interpretable parameters: an exponent parameter in the LPE model, and a population-level heteroscedasticity parameter in the RH model, which lets the randomness of the responses differ across ability levels. Mixture IRT models (Mislevy & Verhelst, 1990; Rost, 1990) assume population-level latent classes that make people respond differently.

As can be seen, each departure has usually been studied individually, and the shapes of ICCs are categorized differently across fields. However, several recent studies have revealed that such departures are closely related to each other, and also to the complexity of an item, such as the number of steps needed to solve it (Bolt & Liao, 2022; Bolt et al., 2018; Lee & Bolt, 2018b; Liao & Bolt, 2021). For example, Lee and Bolt (2018a) reported that the guessing parameter 𝑝 𝑐 of the 3PL model can help model the asymmetry of an ICC, and vice versa. In addition, Culpepper (2016) and Meng et al. (2020) showed that the 4PL model can be understood as a mixture model with careless and guessing groups. These relationships also mean that a parameter does not always describe what its name suggests; the guessing parameter, for example, may in fact be describing asymmetry, and what we learn about an item then depends on which model we choose. Although these relationships have been formalized through systematic definitions (Feuerstahler et al., 2026; Shim & Bonifay, 2026), a unified model conceptually combining the departures remains absent.

If we take a step back, the various mechanisms above can be reduced to a single cause. A correct answer from a lucky guess is recorded simply as correct, and a wrong answer from a careless mistake simply as wrong. A difficult and complex item makes people fail for different reasons, while an item open to many shortcuts lets people succeed for different reasons. Seen within a single item, the fact that people respond differently according to their ability levels or dispositions also means that the same response can be chosen for different reasons. Therefore, every response suffers an information loss, as it does not record the 'reason' for the choice.

Item formats that record such reasons already exist. Interviews and essay questions are typical examples, and polytomous items, which record which option was chosen rather than only whether it was correct, are a more standardized form. Many models have brought such detailed information into IRT; let us consider the most basic one, the Nominal Response Model (NRM; Bock, 1972). Noting that not all distractors, the wrong options of an item, are chosen in the same pattern, Bock gave each distractor its own exponential term so that they could be analyzed individually. Applying this idea to any dichotomous item, one could suspect that several latent distractors lie behind its single wrong response. If so, what prevents us from asking whether information about the correct response has likewise been lost? Any response, correct or wrong, can have more than one reason, as the lucky guess above already shows. Both can therefore be decomposed into latent 'reasons for the response' from the perspective of polytomous IRT, even though the reasons are not observed.

This approach is not new: Maris (1995) introduced latent responses and their condensation into an observed response as general modeling principles, and related ideas appear in the Nested Logit Model (NLM; Suh & Bolt, 2010) and in IRTree models, which describe a response as the end of a sequence of latent decisions (Böckenholt, 2012; De Boeck & Partchev, 2012). Even within ratios-of-sums-ofexponentials, there have been 'don't know' models (Samejima, 1979; Thissen & Steinberg, 1984) and their subsequent extensions (Jin et al., 2022; Shigemasu & Ueno, 1993; Thissen et al., 1989) that assume a latent 'guessed response' category. However, our model differs from these models because we assume that the number of reasons and their exact cognitive counterparts are also unknown. Hence, we allow an arbitrary number of terms, which comes at a price: each term can be identified only partially from the responses, and the number of terms has to be chosen for each item. Yet being unable to identify a reason exactly does not mean that we should leave it unused. We therefore make room for latent reasons, and let data decide the configuration.

Under this simple assumption, applying the NRM to the latent 'reasons' yields a model that we call the Microstate Response Model (MiRM). The terminology comes from statistical mechanics (Landau & Lifshitz, 2013), where microstates are the distinct states of a system, some of which cannot be distinguished by macroscopic observation, and a macrostate is a coarse-grained state, that is, a group of microstates that show the same observation. Thus, a macrostate corresponds to an observed response as an aggregation of its latent reasons, and microstates correspond to the reasons. This correspondence is more than an analogy, as it brings mathematical tools and interpretations. Smith and Bendjilali (2022) captured the similarity between the NRM and the canonical ensemble, the Boltzmann distribution over the states of a system, by relating the energy of each state to the discrimination of each response option. They further examined whether the concept of coarse-graining can be applied. However, they did not define IRT counterparts to 'microstates'; the absence is where our approach begins.

The microstates put the 'reasons' that have been modeled separately on a single layer. If we regard guessing as a microstate, it keeps a share of correct responses even among the least able respondents, and MiRM reduces to the 3PL model. More generally, MiRM reads departures such as the asymptotes, asymmetry, item complexity, and nonmonotonicity directly from its item parameters, instead of adding a new parameter for each of them. The item discrimination 𝑎 and difficulty 𝑏 of the 2-parameter logistic (2PL; Birnbaum, 1968) model, which tell how sharply and at which ability an item distinguishes respondents, have been diluted by such additional parameters. MiRM restores them as functions of ability, 𝑎(𝜃) and 𝑏(𝜃), that can be identified from the ICC. Furthermore, the framework covers nonparametric IRT models through the Universal Approximation Theorem (Theorem 4.1), providing them with an interpretable algebraic lens.

This approach does not stop at combining existing item models; it naturally covers an object beyond the item level as well. Just as an item discards the reasons behind a response and compresses them into a single observed response, psychometrics has one more response system that discards the hidden information behind several responses and compresses them into a single result: a test. Although a test has been regarded as a set of items, it is an item itself. Following this intuition, we prove algebraically that an item and a test are the same object under MiRM, and from this we unify partial credits and total scores, which have been regarded as different objects. Therefore, the monotonicity condition that has been imposed on each item can be moved to the test, the unit that actually measures ability. We can thereby model items that show nonmonotonic ICCs in real data while keeping the ability scale meaningful.

The core contributions of this paper are itemwise model unification, item-test equivalence and a new test-level condition, and validation through large-scale datasets. Overall, by proposing MiRM, we aim to observe how the theoretical microstates represent practical structures in empirical data, without introducing departure-specific item parameters to resolve the tension between theory and practice. In doing so, we recover part of the information lost in the responses, thereby identifying individual ability precisely and providing a better understanding of items.

3.2. Perfect-Response Monotonicity

One reason for developing a single framework for items and tests is to allow individual items to have locally nonmonotonic ICCs without losing the meaning of ability. If 𝜃 served only as a coordinate that improves model fit, it would not constitute a measurement scale. IRT therefore requires a condition under which greater ability provides stronger evidence of successful performance. Itemwise monotonicity offers a direct way to impose this requirement and has consequently been treated as a central assumption of unidimensional IRT. It may be sufficient, however, rather than necessary: previous studies have suggested that respondents may remain ordered even when some individual ICCs are nonmonotonic (Feuerstahler et al., 2024; Junker, 1993; Stout, 1990). We likewise seek to identify individual ability by imposing a monotonicity constraint at the test level, as we measure ability using tests, but usually not a single item. However, the major challenge of defining monotonicity at the test level is that there are a lot of aggregate statistics for multiple items, but none of them are fully credible. We do not know which items weigh more, how to average them, and even whether their importance varies over ability, before we assess the data.

Hence, we rely on two criteria: the all-correct pattern C and the all-distractor pattern D, which were used above to define the generalized credit. When neither the ordering of items, the number of similar or duplicated items, nor any arbitrary scores or weights assigned to them are taken as trustworthy, these are the only two states that remain invariant under any monotone rescoring. 4.4. The Universal Approximation Theorem

Theoretically, MiRM with sufficiently many finite microstates can approximate any continuous item response model, including nonparametric models. The theorem has three consequences. First, the linear exponent of each microstate does not restrict the shape of an ICC; the flexibility comes from the number of microstates. Second, under local independence, a unidimensional test model is determined by its ICCs, so MiRM can approximate not only a single item but the whole test model. Third, a many-state MiRM fitted to a nonparametric model can serve as an atomization filter: the local characteristics 𝑎(𝜃) and 𝑏(𝜃) and the generalized credit can be computed for it, which gives the nonparametric model the same algebraic reading. The theorem only shows existence, and does not tell how many microstates are needed; we examine this empirically in Section 7.2.

5. Estimation

For response data given as D = {(𝑖, 𝑗, 𝑘)}, the true microstate indices 𝑚 are generally not observed, as the true abilities 𝜃 𝑖 are not. The distribution 𝜃 ∼ N (0, 1) for any data D is the working prior adopted in this paper, but not a structural assumption. It makes the model's results comparable to traditional models; any other unidimensional distribution can be used with the model.

All models under the data D are estimated by marginal maximum likelihood (Bock & Aitkin, 1981), the standard method for item parameters in IRT. Since ability is unidimensional and follows a normal prior, it is integrated out by Gauss-Hermite quadrature with 𝑄 = 41 nodes. The marginal likelihood is maximized directly by a quasi-Newton method (L-BFGS with a strong Wolfe line search; Liu & Nocedal, 1989) rather than by the EM algorithm, since the PRM constraint can simply be added to its loss (Section 5.1). Every model is fitted with the same implementation, quadrature, and optimizer, so that differences in fit come from the models and not from the estimation. MiRM-specific estimation procedures are described below, with full details in Appendix D.

5.1. A Normalized PRM Loss

By default, we impose PRM during estimation as a constraint on the 𝐶-and 𝐷-side margins over a grid Θ P (𝑄 P = 2𝑄 -1). Because 𝑎 J𝐶 and 𝑎 J𝐷 are proportional to the scale of the slopes and the number of items, the sum of itemwise contributions is normalized by their absolute sum. When the denominator is zero at a certain 𝜃, the ability point is excluded from the loss estimation to prevent 0/0.

For the constraints 𝑔 𝐶 𝑞 =ã J𝐶 (𝜃 𝑞 ) ≤ 0 and 𝑔 𝐷 𝑞 =ã J𝐷 (𝜃 𝑞 ) ≤ 0, we use the Powell-Hestenes-Rockafellar augmented Lagrangian (M. R. Hestenes, 1969; Powell, 1969; Rockafellar, 1974) penalty, which is added to the negative log-likelihood to form the total loss. For more details, see Appendix D.1.

5.2. Configuration Selection

The number of microstates can differ across items. The model having a different microstate configuration (𝑐, 𝑑) per item is called the per-item MiRM, or MiRM(𝑐 𝑗 , 𝑑 𝑗 ). To search for the best configuration for each item, four adjacent models MiRM(𝑐 𝑗 , 𝑑 𝑗 ), MiRM(𝑐 𝑗 , 𝑑 𝑗 + 1), MiRM(𝑐 𝑗 + 1, 𝑑 𝑗 ), and MiRM(𝑐 𝑗 + 1, 𝑑 𝑗 + 1) are trained simultaneously for every item 𝑗 ∈ {1, . . . , 𝐽}, with all four models sharing 𝑐 𝑗 correct microstates and 𝑑 𝑗 distractor microstates. Without sharing these base microstates, fitting the four models separately always favors the largest configuration, whereas sharing them forces the base states to remain meaningful across all four models and prevents unconstrained growth of the state counts.

After the training is done, (𝑐 𝑗 , 𝑑 𝑗 ) is shifted to a preferred direction under a shared ability posterior θ, and then the training resumes. The preferred direction is determined by four conditions: for example, to shift toward a larger 𝑑 𝑗 for each item 𝑗, (𝑐 𝑗 , 𝑑 𝑗 + 1) must improve on (𝑐 𝑗 , 𝑑 𝑗 ), (𝑐 𝑗 + 1, 𝑑 𝑗 + 1) must improve on (𝑐 𝑗 + 1, 𝑑 𝑗 ), and both improvements must still hold after subtracting their mean across all items. The loss of each model is calculated under the geometric mean of the four models' ability posteriors. The cycle terminates when no (𝑐 𝑗 , 𝑑 𝑗 ) is shifted after training. To select the smallest number of microstates, we start from (𝑐 𝑗 , 𝑑 𝑗 ) = (1, 1) for every item at the first cycle. A full illustration of the estimation procedure is provided in Appendix D.2.

However, since estimating per-item MiRM is computationally heavy, we also use MiRM(𝑐, 𝑑) with constant 𝑐, 𝑑. In empirical use, MiRM(2, 2) is usually sufficient for both obtaining enough performance (see Figure 6) and analyzing macrostate-level characteristics. We additionally use MiRM(5, 5) as an analog to flexible nonparametric models.

6. Simulation

Some important properties of MiRM cannot be checked on empirical data, because the true ICCs, abilities, microstate configurations, and local dips are not known. In this section, we therefore generate data with known truth and evaluate the accuracy of ICC and ability estimation when the generating model is not known (Section 6.2), the recovery of itemwise microstate configurations and parameters (Section 6.3), and the detection of local dips with and without PRM (Section 6.4).

6.1. Design

The main experiments used 4,096 students with abilities drawn from a standard normal distribution and 128 items over 30 replications, with item parameters taken from models fitted to the PISA data (Section 7). For Section 6.2, responses were generated from the 2PL, 3PL, LPE, I-spline (Ramsay & Abrahamowicz, 1989), and MiRM models. The MiRM generator used monotone items of the per-item MiRM, so that (𝑐,𝑑) configurations differ across items. We fitted the 2PL, 3PL, LPE, and MiRM(2, 2) to each dataset. Under the MiRM generator, we also varied the number of students from 128 to 2,048 and the number of items from 8 to 64. For Section 6.3, each bank had all monotone (1,1) and (2,1) items of the per-item MiRM fits (13 and 44 items) and monotone (1,2) and ( 2,2) items in proportion to their numbers (32 and 39 items), which were resampled in each replication; the per-item MiRM was fitted in addition. The monotonicity study differs in its items, respondents, and the number of replications; its design is given in Section 6.4.

Thus, when true dips exist, capturing them accurately may help to recover the order of abilities, whereas this gain is lost in conditions where false dips are frequent.

7. Empirical Study

We examined item characteristics, model performance, nonmonotonicity, and ability paths in PISA 2015 Reading, Science, and Mathematics (OECD, 2016). PISA is a large public dataset: our data contain 2.5 to 9.1 million responses per domain. The three domains provide three separate analyses, and since many students took multiple domains, they can also be fitted as a single bank (Section 7.4). We chose the 2015 cycle because it was mainly computer-based and was the last cycle without adaptive testing. We used valid responses to every computer-scored item from the computer-based assessment, followed the official scoring rules, and dichotomized full-credit versus other valid responses. Missing responses (not reached, omitted, or invalid) were excluded. Details are given in Appendix E.

Alongside the 2PL and 3PL, the comparisons included RH and 3PLQ, which had performed well in an earlier PISA 2009 analysis (Robitzsch, 2022). For the prediction comparison, we chose the number of spline basis functions of the monotone spline model (Ramsay, 1988; Ramsay & Abrahamowicz, 1989) so that the spline had one more free parameter per item than MiRM(5, 5), avoiding a disadvantage in parameter count for the spline. The LPE, which was included in the simulations, was omitted here because it showed no distinct performance advantage over the 3PL and RH.

In Section 6.4, the fitted ICCs alone flagged many false dips. Hence, a fitted dip alone does not establish that an item is nonmonotone. We therefore examined three items together with evidence that does not depend on the fitted model.

Figure 8 shows one item from each domain for which the per-item MiRM fitted a local dip. For all three, a decline in the fitted ICC was accompanied by a decline in the observed proportion correct across rest-score bins. The 𝑧 value in each panel is the difference in observed proportions correct between the rest-score bins corresponding to the fitted ICC's pre-dip shoulder and minimum, divided by its standard error. The values were 6.29 for Reading 22, 6.71 for Science 81, and 4.02 for Mathematics 42. These items were chosen as cases in which the local decline can be examined alongside the response structure, rather than as a representative sample of all items with dips. Additional checks using alternative ability bins, complete 15-item blocks, and within-country stratified analyses are reported in Appendix F.

Reading 22 is scored correct only when all three yes-or-no judgments are correct. The probability of getting all three right by random guessing is 0.125, almost identical to the overall proportion correct of 0.126. Yet the fitted ICC is near chance at low ability, falls below chance at intermediate ability, and rises again at high ability. An overall proportion near chance does not mean that everyone guessed. Above-chance performance at high ability and below-chance performance at intermediate ability can offset each other in the overall average; the ICC separates the two. In every data split, the per-item MiRM selected at least two correct microstates and more than two distractor microstates for Reading 22 (Figure 5). This is consistent with an item that can be answered correctly through knowledge or chance, but can be answered incorrectly in several ways. The observed subitem responses show this pathway directly: while the correct pattern (Yes, No, No) accounts for 12.6%, the most common pattern is (Yes, No, Yes) at 56.4%, missing only the final judgment. Across rest-score deciles (proportion correct on the remaining items in the domain), the first two judgments increase monotonically, whereas the final judgment drops from 33% in the first decile to 17% in the fifth before rising to 38% in the tenth. Intermediate students who resolve the first two judgments but favor the wrong answer on the last one therefore fall below the all-three guessing rate.

Science 81, Weather Balloon, is a four-option multiple-choice item. The correct option (Option 2) states that the balloon's material cannot be stretched any farther, whereas 79% of incorrect respondents selected the option that the pressure inside increases (Option 4). Across rest-score deciles, the selection rates of Options 1 and 3 decrease monotonically, whereas Option 4 peaks at 68% in the sixth decile as Option 2 dips to 16% in the third decile, showing that the local decline is driven by a single competing response rather than errors dispersed across the options.

Mathematics 42 requires both of two yes-or-no judgments to be correct, giving a chance probability of 0.25. The left portion of its fitted ICC is higher than that of Reading 22, which requires three correct judgments; here too, the observed proportion correct and the ICC both decline at intermediate ability. In these three examples, nonmonotonicity is a decline over a limited ability range, not a reversal over the entire scale. Its location and height can be read alongside the item's scoring and option structure.

7.4. Full-Bank Estimation With and Without PRM

We showed that PRM guarantees orderability and directionality of ability in Section 3.2, and mentioned the correspondence between MiRM and multidimensional NRM in Table 1. The guarantees and the correspondence are closely related (Appendix A.3), and these together provide clear intuition about how PRM aligns the global ability ordering, as shown in Section 6.4. As unidimensional IRT models place all respondents on a single scale, they seek the ability path that the test supports the most, even if the true ability space is multidimensional. Here, PRM prevents the path from being reversed in the ability space (Theorem A.2(ii)). However, investigating this property empirically is challenging; the true ability space is not given in real data. The PISA dataset provides such an opportunity: since many students take forms across domains, we can regard the full-bank data combining the three domains as a single dataset and then fit the model. Furthermore, since the full bank contains the items of each domain, the ability scale of the full bank can be compared with the abilities obtained from each domain in the previous analyses. We therefore draw the ability scale of the full bank as a path in the three-dimensional space made of the three domain abilities, and examine how the path is drawn.

We fitted the per-item MiRM to the three PISA domains together as a single 241-item bank, once with PRM and once without PRM (Figure 9). >Discussion

8.1. On Microstates

Traditional IRT models have worked well because they provide economical descriptions of corresponding psychometric item characteristics. Parameters for guessing-like behavior, slipping-like behavior, item complexity, or residual heteroscedasticity efficiently represent lower and upper asymptotes, and ICC asymmetry. However, item characteristics vary even among items within the same testlet, and introducing a separate parameterization for each departure does not explain why these characteristics are related. MiRM offers a different level of explanation through a single principle: an observed response is a coarse-grained outcome of competition among latent response states.

From this perspective, the results of this paper are not independent features added to a flexible item model. The model relations in Table 1 reinterpret conventional item parameters through the number, arrangement, or spectrum of microstates. Local nonmonotonicity arises when the mean slopes of the correct and distractor macrostates reverse their ordering (Theorem 2.1). The giant-MiRM result (Theorem 3.1) follows by combining item-level microstates across a test. PRM and the generalized total score then determine the direction and credit of the resulting giant item. Item characteristics, ICC geometry, test composition, and scoring are therefore different consequences of the same latent-state construction. In this sense, the microstate representation is mathematically coherent and structurally informative rather than merely a device for adding parameters.

This structural claim should nevertheless be distinguished from a cognitive one. The response distribution, its local discrimination and difficulty, and a reduced microstate representation are statistical objects. Calling a particular state a strategy, misconception, reason, or response pathway is an interpretation placed on those objects. However, this distinction is not peculiar to MiRM: a lower-asymptote parameter can be identified under the 3PL without establishing that the corresponding responses were actually produced by guessing, just as an upper asymptote does not by itself establish slipping. Likewise, the present results do not show that every fitted microstate corresponds one-to-one with a distinct cognitive process. Even if such a literal correspondence fails, the geometric, compositional, and information-theoretic results of MiRM remain intact. Our narrower claim is that microstates provide a common explanatory vocabulary in which previously separate item characteristics can be understood and empirically investigated.

Coarse-graining also places a fundamental limit on that investigation. The information decomposition in Eq. 212 shows that observing only a macrostate loses information, in the amount of the expected within-macrostate variance of the microstate slopes. When several reasons for responding are reduced to the same category, some ability-relevant variation becomes unavailable. MiRM cannot recover information absent from the observations; it instead uses the structure that remains visible in the aggregate response curves. Not every microstate, however, must remain entirely latent. Option choices, response times, eye movements, intermediate work, or verbal reports may anchor particular states and restrict the representations compatible with the data. Open-ended assessment can be understood similarly as reducing coarse-graining by retaining part of the reasoning that selected-response scoring discards. With such external anchors, the information lost through coarse-graining may eventually become useful for assessing how clearly an item separates its underlying reasons for correct and incorrect responses.

A stronger cognitive interpretation requires validation against response processes established independently of binary outcomes. Concept inventories and diagnostic datasets such as the FCI (D. Hestenes et al., 1992), FMCE (Thornton & Sokoloff, 1998), and Eedi (King et al., 2024) offer promising settings because their options are informed by prior accounts of misconceptions or solution processes. Microstates estimated from dichotomized responses could be compared with withheld option choices, process measurements, or expert classifications. Such analyses would directly address whether statistically inferred states predict independently observed response-generating processes. The information decomposition specifies how much ability-relevant information has been destroyed by dichotomization, although translating that quantity into a bound on the recovery of individual state labels would require additional assumptions.

The behavior of the 3PLQ provides a more indirect illustration of the explanatory value of microstates. Its quadratic logit corresponds to a Gaussian spectrum of slopes in one macrostate, with the magnitude of the quadratic coefficient representing the variance of that spectrum (see Appendix C.2). The model can consequently summarize diffuse pathway heterogeneity with a single parameter, helping to explain its strong BIC performance (Table 3). The same account explains its limitation: a Gaussian spectrum is unbounded, whereas a finite MiRM has extremal slopes. This appears as the extremal reversal of the 3PLQ's ICCs in Figure 7. Thus, the central flexibility and problematic tail behavior of the 3PLQ arise from the same structural assumption. Explaining both the strength and the weakness of a competing model in a common language supports the explanatory usefulness of the microstate representation without requiring every inferred state to be cognitively literal.

8.2. Relations Between MiRM and PRM

When introducing PRM in Section 3.2, we noted that PRM provides several theoretical guarantees for modeling ability, whereas the fit of a flexible model without PRM is unreliable. Figures 4 and 9 show this clearly; in particular, the upper-left panel of Figure 9 is a typical example of the ICCs that MiRM produces when fitted without PRM. PRM is therefore essential for fitting MiRM. Conversely, although PRM was proposed as a property of a test independent of MiRM, it loses its practical meaning in most existing models because they always satisfy PRM. This shows why MiRM and PRM need to be introduced together. Moreover, the PRM conditions are available in closed form in MiRM, making their joint use practical. However, this does not mean that PRM is the single invariant answer for ability measurement. While PRM has the conceptual strength of scoring invariance, other test-level monotonicity conditions with different properties can also be proposed.

Moving the monotonicity condition from the item to the test has a broader implication. PRM as a test-level condition implies that item characteristics may change slightly depending on how the test is composed. Remark 3.2 shows this well: a nonmonotonic item within a test is refitted as monotonic if the item itself is used as a test. This is not a defect of the condition, but its intended meaning. What we measure through a test is not the true ability itself. We only define from the response patterns the tendency that the test, as a measuring device, explains best, and expect that this tendency follows the true ability. Therefore, whether a test measures the true ability is a question of validity, and it cannot be answered inside the model. The model only shows the direction on which the items of a test agree. Whether this direction is the trait that the test was designed for must be checked outside the model. Without such an investigation, it is difficult to claim that an item's characteristics are strictly intrinsic to the item. One of the best practical approaches may be to regard the microstates or item characteristics that survive across different test sets and respondent groups as the item's own.

Meanwhile, since an ideal test will automatically generate the best unidimensional explanation, it may pass PRM as a diagnostic criterion even when PRM is not imposed as a constraint. Furthermore, if the ability path folds back or some ICCs reverse direction when fitted without PRM, as in Figure 9, this may be read as a sign that the test is intrinsically multidimensional.

8.3. Implications for Practice

MiRM's unification framework helps not only theory, but also practical item analysis by placing several departures on a single layer. Before MiRM, practitioners had to choose a specific model in advance to reflect an item's expected behavior-for instance, the 3PL for guessing or the RH model for asymmetry. However, items within the same test often exhibit different departures, leaving mismatches under any single traditional model, while fitting multiple competing models and comparing their parameters is cumbersome. In MiRM, the data make this choice instead of the practitioner. The model captures these departures simultaneously within one model family and summarizes them on a common scale. Although slight instability in configuration selection and heavier computation remain practical costs, we address these in Section 8.5.

Putting item characteristics on a single layer also changes how we handle nonmonotonicity. We do not claim that nonmonotonic ICCs are practically healthy, nor do we suggest using nonmonotonic items or negative item credits. Our claim is narrower: itemwise nonmonotonicity should be detected rather than assumed away, and in rare cases a correct response can indeed serve as negative evidence for ability. By the results in Section 2.1.2, MiRM can serve as both a diagnostic tool and an operational model in a single step. If a practitioner prefers all items to be monotonic, MiRM can directly flag nonmonotonic items for revision or removal without a separate misfit analysis, and the stability of 𝜔 𝑗 (𝜃) across ability can guide practical item weights for sum scores. On the other hand, an educator or researcher who wishes to retain dip items for substantive analysis can use the fitted MiRM directly as the operational scoring model under PRM.

A local dip can also be informative in its own right. In educational assessment, a dip may capture U-shaped behavioral growth (Strauss, 1991), where intermediate learners temporarily perform worse than novices as they transition from superficial rules to deeper concepts, as suggested by Science 81 in Section 7.3. Our analysis was restricted to computer-scored items, many of which are selected-response items, where the chance to guess naturally provides a second correct pathway that satisfies Corollary 2.3 (𝑐 ≥ 2). Detecting a dip in an open-ended or constructed-response item would therefore be an even stronger substantive finding.

Beyond educational testing, nonmonotonic curves over an ordered trait are also natural in psychological measurement. In Kohlberg's Heinz dilemma (Kohlberg, 1981), for example, the binary decision of whether Heinz should steal the drug can oscillate across stages of moral development even as the underlying reasoning matures monotonically. Many psychological items may share this structure when distinct reasons are compressed into a binary choice.

Finally, from the perspective of preventing unintended nonmonotonicity in item writing, Corollary 2.3 offers a counterintuitive lesson. Traditional item development has focused heavily on crafting plausible distractors, yet a local dip requires at least two correct pathways (𝑐 ≥ 2, such as intended mastery alongside guessing or a shortcut). To prevent a dip, ensuring that the correct answer is reachable through a single intended pathway (𝑐 = 1) is therefore important, rather than focusing only on distractors. Conversely, Corollary 2.2 and Section 4 show that diversifying attractive distractors (𝑑 ≥ 2) -long treated as an unquestioned virtue in item design -may enable ICC peaks and flatten discrimination at high ability.

8.4. Outside of MiRM

MiRM is primarily a unidimensional parametric IRT model, yet it bridges parametric and nonparametric approaches through the Universal Approximation Theorem (Theorem 4.1). Wei (2026) pursued a similar goal with the Beta-Bernstein IRT model, establishing an expressive space comparable to that of MiRM, though its direct readings into traditional parametric models were limited. By contrast, MiRM recovers the structural interpretations of classical models (Section 4) and restores the 2PL parameters locally by microstate statistics (Eq. 2.6). Consequently, fitting a MiRM to any nonparametric ICC can serve as an algebraic parameterization filter that equips the curve with interpretable derivatives and state-level readings.

Moreover, because a finite MiRM with sufficiently many states can approximate any continuous ICC on a compact domain, it represents the theoretical ceiling of what a single ordered ability coordinate can explain. From this perspective, the small gains in performance across models in Table 3 do not mean that MiRM contributes little; rather, they may indicate that the standard 2PL already captures the bulk of the unidimensional signal and that spline and MiRM approach the ceiling, with the per-item MiRM reaching this level parsimoniously. In fact, since the 3PLQ and spline are used mainly for fit rather than interpretation, it is fairer to compare MiRM with interpretable models such as the 2PL, 3PL, and RH, where the performance gain is much clearer.

What truly stands outside MiRM are the alternative explanations for unidimensional IRT itself: latent dimensions (Multidimensional IRT; Reckase, 2006), latent populations (Mixture IRT; Mislevy & Verhelst, 1990), group-item relations (DIF; Holland & Wainer, 1993), or item-item relations (Network Psychometrics; Epskamp et al., 2018). They account for features that lie outside a single universal, comparable ability 𝜃. However, just as with the departures within unidimensional IRT models, their representation spaces can still overlap (Kruis & Maris, 2016). They can even overlap with the unidimensional departures: Liao et al. (2026) showed that ICC asymmetry can arise when the data are generated from multidimensional latent traits, while MiRM is algebraically related to mixture models (Table 1). The fundamental question is therefore which features can be truly reduced to a single ability and which cannot. Selecting among these external accounts before characterizing the full expressive capacity of an individual item may be conceptually premature. As we measure people on a single scale rather than teaching or treating them, what the test directly controls is the item, not the person; attributing extra causes of item-person or item-item interactions to each item should therefore precede introducing external traits. Establishing such a within-item reference family provides a basis against which additional structures can be added. The RH model (Molenaar, 2015) exemplifies this approach: population-level heteroscedasticity can become an itemwise asymmetry parameter once the pattern arises systematically from the item. In the same sense, MiRM is important because it does as much of this work as possible inside the item.

8.5. Limitations

Although attributing unidimensional explanations to the item as much as possible is a useful starting point, the alternative accounts above are neither meaningless nor absent in real data. Because MiRM is a flexible model, it can unintentionally absorb local dependence (Bradlow et al., 1999), population-level features, or extra dimensions into its curve, as occasionally seen in MiRM(5, 5). In addition, where data are sparse, extreme-tail reversals similar to those of the 3PLQ can occasionally appear, such as in MiRM(5, 5) on Item 16 in Figure 7, while the sharp boundaries in the local characteristics of Item 29 may come from insufficiently dense Gauss-Hermite quadrature nodes. Empirically, these issues could be mitigated by bounding the slopes 𝛼 𝑠 , applying PRM to smaller item subsets, using early stopping when the loss plateaus, or increasing the number of quadrature nodes. However, these remedies are all heuristic, and a more fundamental approach is needed.

Because the algorithm that selects (𝑐, 𝑑) affects all of these issues, improving configuration selection is an important task for future work. The consistency results in Figure 5, together with the performance and item characteristics in Section 7, show that configurations are selected fairly consistently and that item-specific configurations exist in data. One may improve the selection consistency by selecting the most frequent configuration of each item across multiple splits. Yet we do not claim that our current procedure always finds the optimal configuration for every item. Specifically, in a preliminary simulation, items generated from (3,1) were recovered poorly and were mostly assigned (2,1). The algorithm is thus conservative for configurations beyond the (2,2) grid, and the state counts in Figure 5 may be underestimated. Part of this limit is fundamental, since ratios of exponential sums are not fully identified (Appendix A.1).

Connecting the theoretical quantities of MiRM to real-world features is equally important. Although 𝑎(𝜃) and 𝑏(𝜃) are theoretically clear and offer intuitive empirical readings, further validation is needed to establish what cognitive features they actually reflect. A natural candidate for 𝑎(𝜃) is how deterministically ability governs the response at that level, as seen in the RH derivation (Section 4.3). If these local characteristics are understood more concretely, they could be directly useful in computerized adaptive testing (CAT) and adaptive learning.

Finally, an important direction for future work lies in the choice of prior. Fundamentally, MiRM makes no structural assumption on the prior distribution of ability. As noted earlier, the Gaussian prior 𝜃 ∼ N (0, 1) is a safe and convenient baseline for comparing models, but there is no guarantee that true ability follows a normal distribution on that scale. In statistical mechanics, choosing the ability scale corresponds to setting the scale of inverse temperature. Just as temperature has a physical lower bound, setting upper or lower bounds on the ability domain may be substantively meaningful in psychometrics, suggesting that a normal distribution over all real numbers may not always be the most natural choice. However, if the prior is changed or learned from data in future work, some MiRM characteristics, such as 𝑎(𝜃) and 𝑏(𝜃), will also change with the prior. This implies that even the prior distribution of ability may be an exchangeable feature in psychometrics, just as itemwise departures, multidimensionality, population characteristics, and interactions are.

F.2. Addressing Alternative Explanations for Nonmonotonicity

Here we examine alternative explanations for the dips in Figure 8. Note that our aim is not to reject every alternative account. We view nonmonotonicity not as always strictly intrinsic to an item, but as interchangeable with representations of multidimensionality, asymmetry, or group characteristics. However, saying that nonmonotonicity can be re-expressed through such accounts is different

Lee & Jo from saying that it lacks reality and must be absorbed into them. Our claim is the former, and we examine below whether the dips survive under several alternative explanations.

Several factors can be pointed out as sources of nonmonotonicity, especially data sparsity and group differences. First, because PISA administers items through a rotated-booklet design, respondents answer different subsets of items, and simulation results in Section 6.4 showed that data sparsity can increase false-positive dip detections. To examine whether the empirical dips in Figure 8 are artifacts of missingness or incomparable rest-score compositions, we extracted for each showcase item the largest complete respondent-by-item submatrix of 15 items in which every respondent answered all 15 items (9,988 respondents for Reading 22, 11,166 for Science 81, and 9,290 for Mathematics 42). Within each complete block, the rest score 𝑅 -𝑗 ∈ {0, . . . , 14} is computed over the exact same 14 items for every respondent; consecutive rest-score values were merged from the bottom until each group contained at least 10% of the block sample and placed on the ability axis via the expected rest score E[𝑅 -𝑗 | 𝜃 ]. To test whether the observed dips are an artifact of cross-national aggregation, we evaluated the peak-minus-dip contrast separately within each participating country or economy with at least 300 respondents on the item (56, 58, and 55 countries for Reading 22, Science 81, and Mathematics 42, respectively) on the common ability scale.
