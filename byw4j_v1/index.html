Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. Some differences come from the ground truth: it lacks p-values with a correction name (“p Bonf = .061”), and counts partial eta squared written with its subscript after it (“η 2 p = .05”), “p = .05”, and some table notes and figure legends (“† p < .10, *p < .05”), which this module leaves out, as non-significant p-values. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

While reduced fractional anisotropy (FA) on diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) is the most consistently reported finding in psychosis, whether these alterations precede the first episode and progress with illness stage, and whether the review evidence can answer this, is unresolved. We carried out an umbrella review that covered systematic reviews and meta-analyses, appraising the quality of every included review with the AMSTAR-2 tool. We also built an evidence map that charts what the published reviews conclude about white matter microstructure by tract and illness stage, measured how far the reviews share primary studies, and tested how stable their agreement is. In total, 17 systematic reviews and meta-analyses of standard-tensor DTI were included, and all were of critically low quality. Their 460 citations covered only 235 unique studies, so apparent agreement among reviews reflects shared data, not independent replication. Although 24 of 27 cells covered by three or more reviews were majority-reported as reduced FA, only 12 held across all thresholds, and 54 of 75 votes came from narrative reviews, including every clinical-high-risk cell. Taken together, the reviews report reduced FA in the corpus callosum, cingulum bundle and long association tracts in clinical-high-risk, first-episode and mixed schizophrenia-spectrum samples. Stage-specific alterations in early psychosis and in chronic schizophrenia could not be confirmed. Whether white matter alterations progress across illness stages could neither be supported nor refuted. Whether medication, sex or age moderate these alterations is largely unexamined in the reviews to date.

1. Introduction

White matter alterations are among the most consistently reported findings in psychosis, but whether they precede the first episode and progress with illness stage remains unresolved, which matters for staging and early-intervention models. Psychotic experiences lie along a continuum (van Os and Reininghaus, 2016), and clinical staging frameworks describe psychiatric disorders as passing through stages (McGorry et al., 2006): the clinical-high-risk (CHR) state, characterised by attenuated psychotic symptoms; first-episode psychosis (FEP); the early years after onset; and chronic schizophrenia. Staging and prognostic models depend on knowing at which stage a given biological alteration arises (Sanfelici et al., 2020; Koutsouleris et al., 2021), and mental disorders remain a leading cause of disease burden (GBD 2019 Mental Disorders Collaborators, 2022).

Diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) is widely used to quantify white matter microstructure in vivo. Fractional anisotropy (FA), the most frequently reported index, is sensitive to several tissue properties and is not specific to myelin or axonal integrity (Jones and Cercignani, 2010). The largest multi-site study in schizophrenia (1,963 patients and 2,359 controls) found lower FA in 20 of 25 white matter regions, most prominently in the anterior corona radiata and the corpus callosum (Kelly et al., 2018). The primary literature is heterogeneous across populations, tracts and metrics and may be moderated by antipsychotic exposure, age, sex and acquisition; the evidence on medication is mixed (Patel et al., 2011; Kelly et al., 2018).

Many systematic reviews and meta-analyses have summarised these findings for particular populations or structures (Kanaan et al., 2005; Patel et al., 2011; Tamnes and Agartz, 2016; Waszczuk et al., 2021; Merola et al., 2026). They range from narrative syntheses to coordinate-based and effect-size meta-analyses, so their conclusions are difficult to compare.

Agreement among reviews is easily read as corroboration, but it is informative only if the reviews are independent; because they may draw on the same primary studies, they can converge through data overlap rather than independent replication, and we know of no test of this.

What is missing is a test rather than another summary: which tract-by-category claims reviews support, how many independent primary studies stand behind each, how stable that support is to analytic choices, and where no review exists. Umbrella reviews that appraise review quality and quantify overlap can supply it, as in psychosis in this journal (Groening et al., 2023) and, with an overlap analysis, in another field (Zwickl et al., 2025). Earlier overviews were not built for this in DTI: a structural MRI meta-review (Shepherd et al., 2012) predates 12 of the 17 standard-tensor reviews we include, an umbrella review of 1,478 biomarkers searched only to 2018 (Fuentes-Claramonte et al., 2024), and an early-onset psychosis umbrella review spanned several domains (Salazar de Pablo et al., 2024). The closest synthesis covers whole-brain voxelwise meta-analyses across several modalities (Magioncalda et al., 2025), whereas ours is DTI-specific and includes narrative and region-of-interest reviews, nine of our 17 being narrative; the nearest quantitative synthesis pooled 96 primary studies (Merola et al., 2026). Our searches identified no overview that combined DTI-specific scope, stratification by population category, appraisal of every included review, quantified overlap and tests of threshold stability.

We examined reviews of standard-tensor DTI in schizophrenia-spectrum and CHR samples and compared six population or illness-course categories. Advanced diffusion models (free-water imaging, neurite orientation dispersion and density imaging [NODDI], and DTI along the perivascular space [DTI-ALPS]) are summarised separately and were never combined with tensor results. The protocol specified a primary hypothesis (reduced FA in the corpus callosum and cingulum bundle, to be rejected if fewer than 60% of reviews rated high or moderate quality on AMSTAR-2 reported it) and secondary hypotheses on population category, medication status, sex and review quality. We aimed to map which tract-by-category combinations reviews address, appraise their quality, quantify the overlap of their primary studies, test the stability of their agreement and compare it with consortium data, because tractlevel alterations are already proposed as biomarkers (Merola et al., 2026) and claims may rest on fewer independent studies than the number of reviews implies.

2. Methods

We conducted an umbrella review of systematic reviews and meta-analyses and summarised it as a systematic evidence map. The review was registered in PROSPERO (CRD420261575113); its primary hypothesis is given in Section 1, and eligibility decisions fixed before extraction and post hoc analyses are reported in Supplementary Table S8. As nine of the 17 standard-tensor reviews were narrative, effect measures differed and reviews shared primary studies, we did not combine effect sizes but summarised the direction of tract-level findings by vote counting. Reporting follows PRISMA 2020 (Page et al., 2021), PRIOR (Gates et al., 2022) and SWiM (Campbell et al., 2020) (Supplementary Files S1 to S3), and the design follows Fusar- Poli and Radua (2018). Full methods are in the Supplementary Methods.

2.1. Search method

We searched PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, PsycINFO, Web of Science, the Cochrane Library and Scopus from inception, without language restrictions, using terms for psychosis, diffusion tensor imaging and systematic review or meta-analysis (strings and record counts in Supplementary Table S10). The searches were repeated to capture newly published records, including from 2025 and 2026 (Merola et al., 2026; Chen et al., 2026), and both runs were screened with the same criteria. We also searched reference lists and carried out forward citation searching.

2.2. Selection criteria

Systematic reviews or meta-analyses of white matter microstructure with standard-tensor DTI (FA, mean diffusivity [MD], axial diffusivity [AD] or radial diffusivity [RD]) in people with a schizophrenia-spectrum psychotic disorder or at clinical or ultra-high risk of psychosis, compared with healthy controls, were eligible if they documented eligibility criteria and a reproducible search; a formal risk-of-bias assessment was not required. Reviews combining DTI with other modalities were eligible if DTI findings could be extracted separately, and reviews of mixed schizophrenia and bipolar samples or of advanced diffusion models were allocated to separate streams. Conference abstracts, dissertations, unpublished preprints, umbrella reviews, primary consortium analyses and reviews of unaffected relatives alone were excluded; one antipsychotic review, one umbrella review and three consortium analyses served as background or triangulation records. Merola et al. (2026) was appraised from its preprint version (Supplementary Table S5).

2.3. Data extraction and quality assessment

Two reviewers independently screened titles and abstracts and then full texts against the criteria in Section 2.2; all 34 full-text exclusions are accounted for by reason in Fig. 1 and Supplementary Table S11.

From each review we extracted design, population category, medication status, diffusion metrics, tract-level direction of findings, pooled effects with heterogeneity, longitudinal study counts and acquisition reporting. Two reviewers extracted data in duplicate and verified them against the full texts; the workbook holds 937 distinct findings, each with a verbatim quote and locator (Supplementary Table S12).

The 16-item AMSTAR-2 tool (Shea et al., 2017) was applied independently in duplicate by two authors to the 23 included reviews and two background reviews (25 records; Supplementary Table S5). Disagreements were resolved by taking the more conservative rating, with the most favourable resolution as a sensitivity check; agreement is reported as percentage agreement, Cohen's kappa (Landis and Koch, 1977) and Gwet's AC1 (Gwet, 2008). Overall ratings followed Shea et al. (2017), with items 2, 4, 7, 9, 11, 13 and 15 as critical and the rating (high, moderate, low or critically low) set by the number of critical flaws and non-critical weaknesses; partial yes was not a weakness, and items inapplicable to narrative reviews were scored not applicable (Supplementary Tables S5 and S23). As a sensitivity analysis we repeated the classification with the five critical items of a previous umbrella review in this journal (items 4, 9, 11, 13 and 15; Groening et al., 2023) and with item 9 also removed.

2.4. Evidence map and summary

We created evidence maps of population category by white matter tract. The six categories followed the definitions given by the review authors: CHR or ultra-high risk, FEP, early psychosis (within five years of onset), chronic schizophrenia, mixed or unspecified schizophrenia-spectrum samples, and early-onset (child and adolescent) samples. They are not equivalent diagnoses or sequential states and were not harmonised (Supplementary Table S24), so contrasts between categories compare different samples, not change within individuals. We used only data published in the reviews and their supplements.

The unit of analysis was the review. For each combination of tract and population category, each review contributed one vote on the direction of the patient-control FA difference, a conclusion stated by the review authors taking precedence over single-study rows. Conclusions were mapped to 30 tracts and six categories by stated rules (Supplementary Table S21), and a strict anatomical-match analysis dropped rows needing a non-identical mapping. A cell was classified as reduced, increased, no difference or inconsistent when at least 60% of the reviews addressing it agreed (50%, 70% and 80% as sensitivity thresholds; with two reviews, both had to agree); this is a coding convention, not a test. Vote counting ignores effect size, sample size, heterogeneity and quality and treats dependent reviews as units, so counts are read as agreement among reviews, not replicated effects, and as descriptive; percentages are given beside counts where the denominator is ten or more. Votes were also split by method family and repeated for meta-analytic reviews only (post hoc). Agreement was to be stratified by review quality in three tiers. Exact (Clopper-Pearson) 95% intervals are given only for the primary hypothesis.

To assess independence, we listed the primary DTI studies of each review and matched them across reviews by first author and publication year (tolerant matching, with strict matching as a sensitivity analysis). The corrected covered area (CCA; Pieper et al., 2014) was read as slight (up to 5%), moderate (to 10%), high (to 15%) or very high (above 15%). For cells in which at least 60% of three or more reviews reported reduced FA we also calculated unique primary studies, the cell-level CCA and an overlap-weighted vote, over full reference lists and over studies labelled with the cell's category (Supplementary Table S20). Because the reviews did not link studies to tract-level conclusions, claim-specific independence is unknown and the two lists bracket it.

An exploratory review-level certainty appraisal adapted from GRADE principles (Guyatt et al., 2008) summarised how much confidence the reviews' conclusions allow; it is not validated and rates review-level findings, not primary-study bias. Consortium data served only as a contextual comparison, not independent validation, because overlap between consortium samples and the reviewed primary studies was not established. We used the secondary, exploratory patient-control contrast of Barendse et al. (2026) and matched each of its 19 regions to a review tract family (Supplementary Fig. S3); a region was reduced in both sources when FA was significantly lower after false-discovery-rate correction and at least 60% of the reviews examining the family reported reduced FA and none an increase. The count was repeated under alternative mappings, thresholds, category restrictions, single-review omission and a sign-only comparison (Supplementary Table S23). Warren et al. (2026) had no patient-control contrast and changed no classification. Analyses not specified in the protocol are labelled post hoc (Supplementary Table S8).

3. Results

The searches identified 342 records in the databases and 18 through reference lists and citation searching; after removal of 112 duplicates, 248 were screened and 186 (75.0%) excluded. Of 62 full texts, 34 (54.8%) were excluded (Fig. 1; Supplementary Table S11), leaving 28 records: 23 reviews (82.1%) and five background or triangulation records, including an antipsychotic review (Sagarwala and Nasrallah, 2021).

The 23 reviews comprised 17 of standard-tensor DTI in the schizophrenia spectrum (published 2005 to 2021; 5 to 95 DTI studies each, median 22; Table 1 and Supplementary Table S17), three of mixed schizophrenia and bipolar samples (Dong et al., 2017; Zhao et al., 2022; Merola et al., 2026) and three of advanced diffusion models. Nine of the 17 (52.9%) were narrative and eight (47.1%) quantitative (Supplementary Results 3).

Review quality (Table 1; Supplementary Fig. S1), overlap and stability are reported first, because they determine how tract-level findings should be read, followed by findings by category and tract (Table 2; Figs. 2 and 3).

3.1. Quality, overlap, and stability of the review evidence

Quality. All 17 standard-tensor reviews (100%) were critically low, with three to seven of the seven critical items scored no (median five). The raters agreed on 89.7% of 272 item ratings (Cohen's kappa 0.81; Gwet's AC1 0.87) and on every overall rating, however the 28 differing ratings were resolved. Sixteen reviews (94.1%) had no protocol (item 2) and none listed excluded studies with reasons (item 7); 15 (88.2%) lacked a satisfactory risk-of-bias technique (item 9) and 15 (88.2%) a comprehensive search (item 4). Ten of 16 reviews (62.5%) did not combine results appropriately (item 11), six of 17 (35.3%) did not account for risk of bias in interpretation (item 13) and nine of 14 (64.3%) did not investigate publication bias (item 15).

With the five critical items of Groening et al. (2023), 15 reviews (88.2%) remained critically low and two (11.8%) were low; with item 9 also removed, 12 (70.6%) were critically low, four (23.5%) low and one (5.9%) moderate.

Overlap. The 17 reviews cited 460 primary-study records, of which 235 (51.1%) were unique (CCA 6.0%, moderate, with tolerant matching; 5.7%, also moderate, with strict matching; Supplementary Table S14 and Fig. S2). In all, 106 studies (45.1% of the 235) appeared in two or more reviews, and 24 of 136 review pairs (17.6%) shared at least half of the smaller review's studies. Nearly half of the citations (48.9%) are therefore duplicates, so agreement among reviews cannot be read as independent replication.

Stability.

The 15 reviews with directional findings covered 30 tracts in six categories (180 cells): 64 (35.6%) were addressed by no review, 116 (64.4%) by at least one and 27 (15.0%) by three or more. Of the 116, 89 (76.7%) were classified as reduced FA, 23 (19.8%) inconsistent, three (2.6%) no difference and one (0.9%) increased (Fig. 3; Supplementary Table S13). In 24 of the 27 cells covered by three or more reviews (88.9%), at least 60% of reviews reported reduced FA (Table 2), but only 12 of these 24 (50.0%) stayed reduced at every threshold from 50% to 80%, and 26 of the 116 cells (22.4%) changed class across thresholds.

Counting all source rows equally changed eight cells, and the strict anatomical-match analysis, which dropped 42 of 331 source rows (12.7%), left 102 cells with all 24 majority-reported cells keeping their majority (Supplementary Table S23). After overlap weighting, 23 of the 24 cells (95.8%) stayed at 60% or more; the median cell-level CCA was 8.0% with full reference lists and 20.5% with category-restricted lists, exceeding 15% in all 15 CHR and FEP cells (Supplementary Table S20). The 12 stable cells were no better supported than the 12 unstable ones: each rested on three reviews that all reported reduction, so stability reflected unanimity, not independence.

Method family.

The 15 reviews cast 208 votes: 33 (15.9%) from seven meta-analytic reviews and 175 (84.1%) from eight narrative reviews (132 reduced, 75.4%). In the 24 majorityreported cells, 54 of 75 votes (72.0%) were narrative (45 reduced), 13 coordinate-based (all reduced) and eight effect-size (five reduced). Nine cells, including all six CHR cells, rested on narrative reviews alone, and meta-analytic and narrative majorities agreed in 16 of 23 shared cells (69.6%; Supplementary Tables S21 and S23). The apparent agreement is therefore driven predominantly by narrative reviews.

3.2. Findings by population category

Table 2 and Figs. 2 and 3 show the tract-level pattern; every contributing review was critically low (Table 1). Pooled effect sizes are in Supplementary Table S22.

3.2.1. Clinical high risk

Three narrative reviews (Vijayakumar et al., 2016; Parnanzone et al., 2017; Waszczuk et al., 2021) covered 20 tracts. All three reported reduced FA in the corpus callosum, the superior and inferior longitudinal fasciculi and the corticospinal tract. This agreement rests on three critically low narrative reviews with pairwise overlap of 32% to 75% of the smaller review's studies (Section 3.1), so no tract-level alteration in CHR can yet be offered as a robust marker of risk or stage.

3.2.2. First-episode psychosis

FEP was the most densely covered category (six reviews, including Yao et al., 2013; 26 tracts, 10 addressed by three or more; Supplementary Table S2). All three reviews reported reduced FA in the inferior fronto-occipital and inferior longitudinal fasciculi, fornix, internal capsule and frontal and temporal white matter, and three of four in the corpus callosum, but findings for the genu and splenium conflicted.

3.2.3. Early psychosis

Two narrative reviews covered early psychosis (Kuswanto et al., 2012; Cookey et al., 2014), and only the superior longitudinal fasciculus was addressed by both, which allows no tract-level conclusion.

3.2.4. Chronic schizophrenia

Three reviews covered chronic schizophrenia (Kanaan et al., 2005; Zhuo et al., 2016; Parnanzone et al., 2017). Only the corpus callosum was addressed by all three (two in support), and 17 of 27 tracts (63.0%) were classified as reduced, the lowest share of any category. The evidence does not show more consistent alterations than in earlier categories (Section 3.3).

3.2.5. Mixed or unspecified schizophrenia-spectrum samples

Seven reviews did not separate categories (Kyriakopoulos et al., 2008; Ellison-Wright and Bullmore, 2009; Patel et al., 2011; Geoffroy et al., 2014; Zhuo et al., 2016; Vitolo et al., 2017; Shahab et al., 2018). They reported reduced FA in the cingulum bundle and fornix (three of three) and in the corpus callosum and splenium (three of four), whereas the genu was conflicting.

3.2.6. Early-onset samples

Five reviews covered early-onset samples. Eleven of 15 tracts (73.3%) were classified as reduced, but only the cingulum bundle and frontal white matter were addressed by three reviews (two in support each; Supplementary Table S2), so conclusions remain tentative.

3.3. Moderators of white matter alterations

None of the three secondary hypotheses was supported: more consistent alterations in chronic than in earlier categories were not shown, medication status and sex were unclear (too few reviews or no stratified data; Table 2, part B), and the review-quality hypothesis was untestable because all reviews were critically low. Among 19 tracts examined in both chronic schizophrenia and CHR, agreement was higher in chronic schizophrenia for four (21.1%), higher in CHR for five (26.3%) and equal for 10 (52.6%); this comparison is descriptive and between different samples, not change within individuals. Nine of 17 reviews (52.9%) drew conclusions about staging or progression, but seven of them did not report how many primary studies were longitudinal (Table 3). Medicated and drug-naive samples (Patel et al., 2011) and the sexes (Shahab et al., 2018) could be compared only in single reviews at one tract each (Supplementary Results 3.3).

3.4. Other diffusion metrics, advanced models and consortium data

FA was reported by two to eight reviews per category, whereas MD, AD and RD were reported by at most three and by none for chronic schizophrenia or early psychosis. Of the three advanced-model reviews, one meta-analysis found higher free-water in patients in 15 of 19 findings (78.9%; pooled Hedges' g = 0.38, 95% CI 0.07 to 0.69, six studies; Carreira Figueiredo et al., 2022), DTI-ALPS was reduced in mixed and chronic but not first-episode samples (Chen et al., 2026), and NODDI findings were mixed (Kraguljac et al., 2023; Supplementary Table S19).

Against the schizophrenia patient-control contrasts of Barendse et al. (2026), 11 of 19 regions (57.9%) were reduced in both the consortium data and the review majority and two (10.5%) in neither. In the remaining six (31.6%) the consortium contrast was not significant after correction although at least 60% of reviews reported reduction; its estimate was lower in four, and 15 of 19 regions (78.9%) agreed on sign alone (Supplementary Fig. S3). The number reduced in both was 8 to 11 under 13 alternative analyses but fell to 9 of 14 regions with one-toone label matches and 6 of 13 using meta-analytic reviews only (Supplementary Table S23).

3.5. Primary hypothesis and certainty of evidence

Because no review was of high or moderate quality, the proportion in the protocol is undefined and the primary hypothesis could not be tested at its specified standard. Descriptively, 7 of the 12 reviews that examined the corpus callosum (58.3%; descriptive exact interval 27.7 to 84.8) and 7 of the 10 that examined the cingulum bundle (70.0%; 34.8 to 93.3) reported reduced FA in at least one category and never an increase. The corpus callosum figure ranged from 54.5% to 63.6% with single reviews omitted and was 50.0% when all source rows counted equally (Supplementary Tables S15 and S23). The direction is descriptively supported, but the planned quality-stratified synthesis could not be formed. The 24 majority-reported cells and two further findings were rated very low in the exploratory certainty appraisal (Supplementary Table S4). Motion or quality control was reported by two of 17 reviews (11.8%), b-value by two (11.8%), field strength by eight (47.1%) and harmonisation by none (Table 3); no review analysed findings by motion control, so whether motion explains the 27 inconsistent votes could not be examined.

4. Discussion

This review maps which claims reviews address and where none do, shows that the agreement is thin and partly redundant, and sets the review majority beside consortium data as context. The nearest comparators differ in design: a multimodal umbrella review of voxelwise meta-analyses (Magioncalda et al., 2025), a meta-analysis of 96 primary studies (Merola et al., 2026), a multisite benchmark in established schizophrenia (Kelly et al., 2018) and an early-onset psychosis umbrella review across domains (Salazar de Pablo et al., 2024). What is new is an audit of how agreement changes when review quality, reference overlap and thresholds are considered together.

Although the reviews were uniformly critically low in quality, they suggest a recurrent direction of reduced FA in the corpus callosum, cingulum bundle and long association tracts in CHR, FEP and mixed samples. The comparison with primary data is contextual, not independent validation: in 11 of 19 regions of the consortium contrasts (Barendse et al., 2026) both sources showed lower FA and in two neither did, while in the other six the consortium contrast was not significant, although its estimate was lower in four. The consortium patient sample was modest (464 patients and 841 controls, against 1,963 and 2,359 in Kelly et al., 2018), so limited power may explain some nulls. Because the reviews were of low quality, predominantly narrative and overlapping, their agreement is not independent replication but a signal of direction from a thin evidence base.

The corpus callosum illustrates why the findings need cautious wording. Reviews agreed on reduced FA in CHR samples, and the direction is also reported in schizophrenia and psychosis-spectrum samples by a pooled analysis of 22 studies (Zhuo et al., 2016), a metaanalysis of 96 studies (Merola et al., 2026) and the consortium analysis of Kelly et al. (2018; d = 0.39). Because only 12 of the 24 majority-reported cells (50.0%) held at every threshold from 50% to 80%, we describe these reductions as reported by a majority of reviews, not as consistently observed.

The apparent agreement is driven predominantly by narrative reviews: in the 24 majorityreported cells, coordinate-based meta-analyses reported reduced FA in all 13 votes, effect-size meta-analyses in five of eight and narrative reviews in 45 of 54 (83.3%). Across all cells, metaanalytic reviews voted reduced in 28 of 33 votes (84.8%) and narrative reviews in 132 of 175 (75.4%), so narrative reviews do not appear to inflate the overall direction; yet 54 of 75 votes (72.0%) in the 24 cells and every CHR cell rest on narrative reviews, so the comparison remains descriptive.

Whether white matter is altered in specific tracts in early psychosis and chronic schizophrenia remains unclear: early psychosis rests on two narrative reviews and chronic schizophrenia on a sparse, conflicting set. Our categories compare different people, and change within patients can be inferred only from longitudinal primary studies, which most reviews did not count, so our data neither support nor contradict progressive white matter deterioration. A multimodal umbrella review placed white matter disruption in fronto-temporal regions at firstepisode and early phases and described widespread alterations in the chronic phase (Magioncalda et al., 2025), and a meta-analysis of 96 studies concluded that callosal reductions contribute to pathophysiology rather than reflecting progression (Merola et al., 2026), an inference from crosssectional data.

Higher free-water estimates in some advanced-model studies were not measured alongside the FA findings in the same samples and tracts, so they do not establish which tissue properties account for lower FA. Free-water imaging can separate extracellular water from tissue (Pasternak et al., 2009), and multi-compartment models such as NODDI (Zhang et al., 2012) and SANDI (Palombo et al., 2020) could test category-specific hypotheses within the same samples.

Future syntheses should record harmonisation (Fortin et al., 2017) and pipelines such as tractbased spatial statistics (Smith et al., 2006).

Crossing fibres and partial-volume effects limit what lower FA reflects, and head motion biases diffusion imaging and can differ between patients and controls (Jones and Cercignani, 2010; Yendiki et al., 2014); registration-based correction (Andersson and Sotiropoulos, 2016) does not remove all motion effects, yet only two of 17 reviews (11.8%) reported motion control, and acquisition parameters and harmonisation were seldom reported. The vote count therefore maps review conclusions rather than tissue pathology. For early intervention, no tract-level alteration reported in CHR can yet be offered as a robust marker of risk or clinical course; the corpus callosum has been proposed as a potential biomarker (Merola et al., 2026), and our conclusion differs not in direction but in the certainty the review evidence allows. Table 3 proposes a minimum reporting set.

All 17 reviews were of critically low methodological quality, as in AMSTAR-2 appraisals in other areas of psychiatric research (Leclercq et al., 2020), and how the overall rating is derived influences the result (Pieper et al., 2021). AMSTAR-2 and PRIOR were developed for intervention reviews, and several included reviews predate registries and current reporting guidelines. AMSTAR-2 appraises review methods, not bias in the primary imaging studies, so the adapted output is called an exploratory review-level certainty appraisal. A critically low rating describes what a reader can verify from the published report, not whether the reported direction is wrong; two classifications with fewer critical items left 12 to 15 of the 17 reviews critically low and none high. The absence of a list of excluded studies, of advance registration and of a satisfactory risk-of-bias assessment (Table 3) were the most consistent gaps.

Strengths are that this review combines, visualises and evaluates the available review evidence across tracts and population categories, quantifies the overlap between reviews and reports which pre-specified analyses could not be carried out. Each of the 937 extracted findings carries a verbatim quote and a checked locator, so any cell in Table 2 can be audited (Supplementary Data 1). AMSTAR-2 was rated independently in duplicate, and every review was critically low whichever rater's ratings were used. Overlap was quantified for the whole pool and for each majority-reported cell, separating agreement from independence; the overlap-weighted vote stayed at 60% or more in 23 of 24 cells, but overlap was not reconstructed for each tract, metric and population contrast, and category-restricted lists suggest greater overlap in the CHR and FEP cells (Supplementary Table S20).

The limitations are as follows. First, the synthesis relies on the direction of findings: vote counting ignores effect size and sample size and treats partly dependent reviews as units; classification depends on the threshold (26 of 116 cells changed class) and on coding judgement (eight changed when the precedence rule was ignored); and votes combine voxel-cluster, regionof-interest and narrative statements (Supplementary Table S21). Second, we report no secondcoder agreement statistic for vote coding, the certainty appraisal is unvalidated, both AMSTAR-2 raters are authors, and the applicability of items 11, 12 and 15 to narrative reviews was settled by a severity rule that leaves overall ratings unaffected (Supplementary Table S23). Third, errors and selective reporting in the published reviews propagate into our synthesis. Fourth, we did not search grey literature or conference abstracts, and the most recent standard-tensor review dates from 2021, so recent primary studies are not captured. Fifth, overlap was estimated from reference lists matched by author and year, not from the studies supporting each tract, so repeated cohorts count as separate studies, and full and category-restricted lists only bracket claim-level overlap. Sixth, the consortium comparison rests on a secondary, exploratory contrast in a single analysis with a modest patient sample and unknown cohort overlap, compares classifications and not effect sizes, and matches regions to review tract labels. Seventh, category definitions were not harmonised, and medication and sex were examined in single reviews at one tract each. Eighth, acquisition, processing and region definitions differed or went unreported, so technical comparability cannot be established.

5. Conclusion

Reviews of standard-tensor DTI most often report reduced fractional anisotropy in the corpus callosum, cingulum bundle and long association tracts in clinical-high-risk, first-episode and mixed schizophrenia-spectrum samples: at least 60% of reviews reported reduction in 24 of the 27 cells covered by three or more reviews (88.9%), and the majority direction agrees with a contextual consortium comparison in 11 of 19 regions (57.9%). That agreement rests on a thin and partly redundant evidence base: all 17 reviews (100%) were critically low in quality, 460 citations covered 235 unique studies (51.1%), only 12 of the 24 majority-reported cells (50.0%) stayed reduced at every threshold, and 54 of the 75 votes (72.0%) came from narrative reviews, 15 of them (27.8%) resting on a single primary-study statement. No tract-level alteration in clinical high risk can therefore yet be offered as a robust marker of risk or stage. Because every category contrast compares different samples, the reviews cannot speak to progression, and whether white matter changes advance with illness remains open.
