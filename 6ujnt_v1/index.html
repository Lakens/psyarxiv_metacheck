Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). Statcheck might still miss one-sided tests that are described in another sentence, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. Some differences come from the ground truth: it lacks p-values with a correction name (“p Bonf = .061”), and counts partial eta squared written with its subscript after it (“η 2 p = .05”), “p = .05”, and some table notes and figure legends (“† p < .10, *p < .05”), which this module leaves out, as non-significant p-values. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

If you want to extend the package to be able to download files from additional data repositories reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Abstract

Low-intensity focused ultrasound (LIFU) is a noninvasive neuromodulation technique that uses acoustic energy to modulate neural activity. LIFU reaches both cortical and deep brain structures with millimeter precision, making it a candidate treatment approach for various neuropsychiatric conditions. The neuromodulatory eMects of LIFU depend on the acoustic parameters of the protocol in conjunction with the underlying neurobiology of the target, though the mechanisms underlying these eMects are still being characterized. Early clinical work spans depression, anxiety, substance use disorder, epilepsy, Parkinson's disease, and Alzheimer's disease, with trials that remain small and heterogeneous in target and protocol. Here we review the state of the field, covering how LIFU is delivered, the mechanisms proposed to underlie its eMects, the evidence for target engagement in humans, and the clinical literature to date. In addition to investigating LIFU's clinical eMicacy, considerable foundational work remains before LIFU can be eMectively deployed as a therapy. Overall, LIFU has considerable potential, but rigorous mechanistic and clinical research will be essential to define its therapeutic role.

Introduction

Low-intensity focused ultrasound (LIFU) is a noninvasive neuromodulation technique under active investigation as a therapeutic approach for a range of psychiatric and neurologic disorders. Also known as transcranial ultrasound stimulation (TUS) or transcranial focused ultrasound stimulation (FUS; tFUS), LIFU is a noninvasive neuromodulation technique that combines a strong safety profile with the ability to precisely target deep brain structures [1] [2] [3] . Early results across a range of conditions are promising, but much remains to be learned and refined, from elucidating its therapeutic mechanism(s) of action to establishing clinically eMective parameters and dosing.

Low-intensity focused ultrasound for neuromodulation is distinct from several other ultrasound-based neural therapeutics (Figure 1). Focused ultrasound, shaped narrowly toward a specific target, is distinct from unfocused ultrasound, which spreads acoustic energy across large brain regions, and is commonly used in diagnostic imaging 4 . Highintensity focused ultrasound (HIFU) delivers suMicient intensity at the focus to cause heatbased lesioning and is used clinically to noninvasively ablate parts of the thalamus and pallidum in essential tremor and Parkinson's disease. LIFU delivers intensities orders of magnitude lower and does not cause lasting tissue damage 5 . Focused ultrasound at intermediate intensities can be used to temporarily open the blood-brain barrier for liquid biopsy 6 or targeted drug delivery: gas-filled microbubbles are injected into the bloodstream, and sonication drives them to oscillate in size, transiently disrupting the blood-brain barrier so that drugs can reach the brain 7 . Like HIFU, this application generally requires intensities higher than the low-intensity focused ultrasound regime 8 (though not always see 9 ). Because these biological eMects depend on the ultrasound intensity, a protocol intended to be low-intensity can enter damaging regimes if its parameters are not controlled [10] [11] [12] (Figure 1).

Clinical interest in LIFU for neuropsychiatric conditions has surged in recent years, with 150 new trials registered in the past five years alone 13 . This interest builds on a broader expansion of interest in neuromodulation 14 . The most widely deployed neuromodulation tool available to patients is deep brain stimulation (DBS), which delivers electrical stimulation to a targeted brain region through implanted electrodes. DBS is invasive, requiring surgical implantation, and any subsequent repositioning of the electrode requires additional surgery. These drawbacks drive interest in scalable, noninvasive tools for targeted neuromodulation, such as transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) and transcranial electrical stimulation (tES), which are FDA-approved treatments for some neuropsychiatric disorders like depression and OCD [14] [15] [16] . However, these modalities' direct eMects are confined to the cortical surface and can only modulate deep brain structures indirectly 14 . LIFU is positioned to address these limitations, reaching both cortical and subcortical structures with millimeter precision. LIFU operates using high-frequency sound waves (typically in the 200-700 kHz range) to deliver mechanical pressure to brain structures along the sound path. The pioneering ultrasound studies were conducted by Fry and colleagues in the 1950s, demonstrating the feasibility of using ultrasound to both modulate (low-intensity) and ablate (high-intensity) human neural tissue 17 (though also see the preclinical work by Harvey in 1929 18 ). However, these early studies required removing a section of the skull to avoid both surface tissue damage and distortions of the ultrasound beam caused by the skull, which impeded their practicality 19 . Interest in ultrasonic neuromodulation resurfaced in the late 2000s, when Tyler and colleagues demonstrated in ex-vivo preparations then in rodent models that lowintensity ultrasound could evoke neuronal activity, a line of work that extended to the first transcranial human studies by 2014 [20] [21] [22] . As computational modeling and neuroimaging have advanced in recent years, researchers can now simulate how the acoustic beam propagates and attenuates through intact, heterogeneous cranial bone to reach a specific target, enabling precise delivery of ultrasound to the brain without surgical intervention 19 . This review covers the state of the field for low-intensity focused ultrasound: current best practices for its use, leading hypotheses concerning its mechanisms of action, and the evidence for its clinical utility across a range of neurologic and psychiatric conditions. We also point the reader to additional reviews targeting specific subtopics in the field in Table 1.

Basics of focused ultrasound neuromodulation

Focused ultrasound modulates neural activity by delivering ultrasonic pulses from an external transducer to an intracranial target. The transducer converts electrical energy into mechanical energy through piezoelectric crystals mounted on its face. An oscillating voltage makes these crystals expand and contract in thickness, generating pressure waves that radiate outward. These waves are then focused with an acoustic lens. A given LIFU protocol is defined by the properties of these waves: the fundamental frequency, the pulse regime, the intensity, and the duration (Figure 2). The fundamental frequency is the number of wave oscillations per unit time. Higher fundamental frequencies produce tighter focal spots but attenuate more as they cross the skull 4 . The pulse regime comprises the pulse repetition frequency, the pulse duration, and the duty cycle; diMerent combinations of these parameters are thought to determine the magnitude and direction of the eMect, together with the underlying biology of the target 3, 4, 8, 23 . Ultrasound dose is characterized by two intensity measures that separately emphasize the mechanical and thermal eMects of the acoustic wave (Figure 2). The spatial-peak pulse average (ISPPA) is the average intensity over a single pulse and indexes the mechanical stress delivered during that pulse. The spatial-peak temporal average (ISPTA) is the intensity averaged across the sonication duration, including the time between pulses, and indexes cumulative heating. The corresponding safety limits (see below) follow this division: the mechanical index (MI), calculated from peak rarefactional pressure and fundamental frequency, approximates the likelihood of cavitation, while maximum thermal rise bounds tissue heating 4.

Because the skull attenuates, reflects, and distorts the acoustic beam, the intensity delivered to the target cannot be measured directly and is instead estimated by simulation 4 . These simulations use the patient's own CT and MRI images to model how bone thickness and density bend and weaken the beam, so that the protocol can be adjusted to deliver the intended dose. While individual simulations are critical, establishing ground truth for dosing remains a challenge 24 . Transducers with many independently driven elements (phased arrays) can partly correct for this distortion by adjusting the timing across elements to reshape the beam through the skull and steer the focal target of the beam. Additionally, there are transducers that use a single element or a small number of elements, in which case skull-induced distortion is accounted for during target planning rather than corrected in real time 4 . Targeting is aided by neuronavigational approaches 4.

Biological Mechanisms of Action

The neuromodulatory eMects of LIFU are thought to arise from a complex interplay among the sonication parameters, the target's neurobiology, and the current physiological state. The proximate physical cause is acoustic radiation force (the transfer of momentum from the pressure wave to the tissue it passes through), which strains elastic tissue and deforms the structures within it 4 . This force produces strain in proportion to the tissue's elasticity, which means the stiMness of the tissue determines how much mechanical deformation a given pressure delivers 23 . As the field has progressed, an increasing number of cell types, subcellular constituents, channels, and even components of the membrane glycoproteins and extracellular matrix have been shown to be mechanosensitive, and the response at any given target depends on which of these are present at the beam's focus (Figure 3). DiMerent mechanisms may also dominate at diMerent times: an important distinction is whether an eMect is 'online' , present during active sonication, or 'oMline' , persisting after the sonication terminates. A parallel line of research asks whether a given protocol is inhibitory or excitatory of the overall neuronal activity within a brain region 29 , and here identical protocols can produce divergent eMects depending on the neurobiology of the target 22, 30 , including its balance of excitatory and inhibitory neurons, its complement of astrocytes, and its prevalence of Piezo and transient receptor potential (TRP) channels (Figure 3) 8, 23, 31 . Mapping the ultrasound parameter space in conjunction with specific targets remains an important project for the field.

Acute neuronal mechanisms:

The most widely accepted mechanism of LIFU neuromodulation is that acoustic radiation force opens mechanosensitive ion channels 3, 8, 23 . The best-characterized of these are non-selective cation channels that conduct calcium, sodium, and potassium upon membrane deformation by ultrasound vibrations, causing conformational changes and activation of the channels. As calcium itself is a secondary messenger, the ultrasound-evoked calcium influx, in addition to depolarizing the membrane potential, triggers secondary intracellular signaling, such as recruiting other ion channels and activating intracellular enzymes and signaling cascades 32 . TRPM4 has been implicated as one such amplifier, depolarizing the membrane in response to rising intracellular calcium 23, 32 . Supporting this account, replacing the bath with calcium-free artificial cerebrospinal fluid abolishes the LIFU-induced depolarization 32 . Beyond this acute depolarization, the same calcium entry triggers intracellular signaling cascades that can lead to transcriptional activation in the nucleus, including the upregulation of genes such as c-Fos and brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF), which could mediate reported oMline eMects of sonication 8, 23.

Many mechanosensitive ion channels have been implicated in the LIFU response, including Piezo1/2 channels, TRP channels, TREK and TRAAK channels, and NMDA receptors (Figure 3). Piezo1 has been the most extensively studied: conditional knockout in mouse motor cortex reduces ultrasound-evoked calcium signaling, c-Fos expression, and behavioral readouts 33 , while Piezo1 expression in ultrasound-insensitive HEK cells confers ultrasound sensitivity 34 . Piezo1 is expressed widely across cortical and subcortical regions, in the central and peripheral nervous systems, in neurons and glia, and in the vascular endothelium 3, 8, 25 . More recent work, however, suggests Piezo1 may be only moderately responsive relative to other candidates, with TRPC1 and TRPP2 showing stronger responses and TRPC6 reported as essential for ultrasound-induced excitability 32 . Ultrasound may also modulate potassium flux by activating TREK1 and TRAAK channels 35, 36 , which are enriched at the nodes of Ranvier 37 . Because TRAAK conducts potassium outward, their opening hyperpolarizes the membrane and suppresses firing, raising the possibility that ultrasound may also modulate conduction in white matter tracts. Finally, NMDA receptors have been shown to be moderately responsive to ultrasound 23, 32.

The distribution of mechanosensitive channels across cell types, therefore, shapes how a given region responds to sonication. A single protocol can produce short bursts of activity in mouse primary motor cortex but longer, rhythmic firing in the hippocampus 38 . Cell-type composition oMers one account of such diMerences. The neuronal intramembrane cavitation excitation (NICE) model predicts that particular protocols preferentially engage excitatory or inhibitory neurons, so that the net eMect at a target would depend on its ratio of the two 39 . This ratio varies substantially across the brain: roughly 80% of cortical neurons are excitatory, 70% of hippocampal neurons are excitatory, while 95% of striatal neurons are inhibitory 25, 40, 41 . Empirical work supports the premise, with one parameter set increasing the activity of parvalbumin-expressing interneurons while suppressing excitatory neurons within the hippocampus 30 . Additionally, the same cells have been shown to respond diMerently to varying sonication parameters. In one example using anesthetized rodents, increasing the PRF corresponded to increases in firing rates in excitatory, but not inhibitory, neurons 42 . Other reports have found that the response is even varied within cell types, where neurons of the same subclass at a single target can react diMerently to the same sonication 3 . Together, these findings indicate that a single protocol can produce varying eMects in diMerent regions, and that predicting the direction of an eMect may require knowing the cellular and channel composition of the sonication target.

Downstream and non-neuronal mechanisms: The brain's mechanosensitivity extends beyond the acute channel-level eMects described above. Some of the durable, 'oMline' eMects could also arise from a broader set of responses, including those originating outside the neuron 3, 8, 23 . This section covers five that have received the most attention: astrocytic signaling, microglial and inflammatory responses, vascular eMects, remodeling of the extracellular matrix, and the synaptic plasticity on which several of these converge (Figure 3). Reviews covering additional proposed mechanisms, including flexoelectric eMects, sonoporation, microtubule resonance, lipid rafts, and a possible thermal contribution, are highlighted in Table 1, but are beyond the scope of this review.

As noted above, mechanosensitive channels are not confined to neurons. Both Piezo1 and TRP channels are strongly expressed in astrocytes, where they mediate mechanically evoked calcium responses and glutamate release that, in turn, drives firing in neighboring neurons 2, 25, [43] [44] [45] . The best-characterized route runs through TRPA1: ultrasound-evoked TRPA1 activation drives astrocytic glutamate release via Best1 channels, which activates synaptic NMDA receptors on neighboring neurons and produces a motor response in mice 23, 45 . This account diMers from the cell-autonomous neuronal mechanism described above in assigning astrocytes and NMDA receptors a central role, and research to reconcile these accounts is ongoing 25, 32, 45 . Other groups have found that astrocyte-specific Piezo1 knockout mice show impaired hippocampal long-term potentiation (LTP) and corresponding oMline deficits in learning and memory behaviors 44 . Astrocytes also release and synthesize neurotrophic factors in response to sonication, including BDNF, VEGF, and GDNF, oMering a route by which astrocytic activation could additionally contribute to oMline eMects 46, 47 . Astrocytes also express other mechanosensitive proteins, connexin 43, pannexins, and integrins, which mediate responses to mechanical force in both physiological and pathological contexts [48] [49] [50] [51] [52] [53] [54] [55] [56].

Additional work shows that sonication can modulate microglial activity, attenuate neuroinflammatory pathways, and promote neurotrophic pathways. Microglia express many TRP-family channels along with Piezo1, which sense the stiMness of extracellular material 23, 57, 58 . This detection triggers calcium influx, microglial clustering, phagocytosis, and compaction of plaques 25, 57, 59 . Consistent with an anti-inflammatory shift, LIFU reduces pro-inflammatory cytokines such as tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α, interleukin (IL)-1β, and IL-6 while increasing expression of cyclic adenosine monophosphate response elementbinding protein (CREB) and BDNF 60, 61 . A separate study found that focused ultrasound cleared pathogenic material from the cerebrospinal fluid, improving behavioral outcomes and increasing survival rates in a rodent model of intracerebral hemorrhage 62 . Sonication has also been shown to increase neurogenesis in aged mice, an eMect potentially mediated by upregulation of CREB and BDNF 60, 63.

Sonication additionally acts on the structures surrounding neurons and glia. Emerging work shows that ultrasound alters neurovascular activity: in rodents, focused ultrasound induces microvascular vasodilation that persists for minutes after sonication ends 64, 65 , and in humans, an initial study found that ultrasound applied to the brachial artery produced arterial dilation lasting from 2 to 20 minutes post-sonication 66 . Ultrasound also acts on the extracellular matrix, the macromolecular network that fills the space between cells and forms perineuronal nets around many neuronal cell bodies and dendrites 63, 67 . Because components of this matrix associate with ion channels and constrain their lateral mobility, its disruption oMers an indirect route to altered neuronal excitability 63, 67, 68 . Sonication has been shown to reduce perineuronal nets, and this reduction correlates with improved cognitive performance in aged mice, an eMect attributed to a more permissive environment for synaptic remodeling 9 . As stated above, the stiMness of the extracellular matrix also alters how the ultrasound is transduced, where properties such as membrane cholesterol composition and extracellular matrix density contribute to the degree of tissue elasticity 23.

Several of the mechanisms above converge on synaptic plasticity: calcium-driven c-FOS and BDNF expression, astrocytic neurotrophin release, and remodeling of the extracellular matrix all favor synaptic change, and each operates on a timescale longer than the sonication itself. Consistent with this, sonication of mouse prefrontal cortex promoted dendritic spine formation, slowed spine loss, and upregulated BDNF-TrkB signaling 69 , while repeated sonication of barrel cortex increased dendritic spine turnover alongside improved whisker-based object discrimination 70 . Several reports describe NMDA-receptor-mediated LTP and long-term depression (LTD) following sonication 8, 9, 71, 72 , though the parameters governing which occurs are not established.

In summary, there is likely no single mechanism that drives the neuromodulatory changes after LIFU sonication. While opening mechanosensitive ion channels is suMicient to modulate neuronal firing, a cascade of additional responses may contribute to or drive the therapeutic eMects. Furthermore, the neurobiology of a given target, in conjunction with the sonication parameter set, likely determines whether the immediate or lasting eMects of sonication are excitatory or inhibitory, and this complexity may partially explain the heterogeneity in responses.

LIFU in healthy humans and nonhuman primates

Basic research using LIFU in humans has focused on establishing target engagement via neuroimaging, electrophysiology, and behavioral readouts, exploring how target engagement changes under diMerent protocol parameters, and establishing best practices for handling confounds.

Target engagement-physiological readouts: Common methods for assessing target engagement leverage TMS-driven motor-evoked potentials (MEPs), sensory evoked potentials (SEPs), functional MRI, and novel MR sequences such as acoustic radiation force imaging (MR-ARFI). When using MEPs, focused ultrasound is applied to a target such as the sensory thalamus or M1 while concurrent TMS pulses drive suprathreshold motor firing; the resulting MEP amplitude is then compared against sham or alternative ultrasound protocols. SEP studies compare tactile, auditory, or visual evoked potentials before, during, or after sonication of sensory or thalamic regions to test for amplification or suppression of the evoked response. Both task-based and resting-state fMRI have been used to probe local and connectivity changes around sonication. Many fMRI studies have reported long-lasting oMline eMects 73, 74, 74, 75 , which have often been interpreted as evidence of LTP-or LTD-like plasticity, but ultrasound's eMects on the vasculature oMer an alternative or additional contribution, since the BOLD and perfusion signals that index connectivity are themselves vascular. Disentangling the two is an active area of work, though the interpretations are not mutually exclusive: plasticity changes and vascular eMects could coexist, and methods such as arterial spin labeling are increasingly used to characterize the vascular component directly. These diMiculties have also motivated MR sequences that specifically measure tissue displacement. MR-ARFI localizes the acoustic focus by imaging the displacement produced by radiation force, confirming where energy was delivered rather than whether tissue responded, and work is ongoing to translate it to humans 76, 77.

Target engagement-behavioral readouts: LIFU applied to humans and nonhuman primates produces behavioral changes across a wide range of targets and tasks, spanning multiple cortical and subcortical areas 3, 78 . As suggested by the proposed mechanisms of action, whether the reported eMects are facilitatory or inhibitory appears to depend on both the target and the protocol. Across multiple subregions in the prefrontal cortex, anterior cingulate cortex, insula, and sensory cortices, sonication has produced facilitatory eMects on learning rate, choice, control, and discrimination tasks under some protocols 21, [79] [80] [81] [82] [83] [84] [85] [86] [87] and inhibitory eMects under others 74, 75, [88] [89] [90] [91] [92] [93] . When applied to subcortical regions such as the basal ganglia and thalamus, both facilitatory 73, [94] [95] [96] and inhibitory [97] [98] [99] eMects on learning, motivation, sensory discrimination, and motor inhibition have been reported across a range of parameters. Furthermore, while facilitatory and inhibitory behavioral eMects have been reported across diMerent targets and protocols, how these behavioral changes emerge from shifts in neuronal suppression or excitation is often unclear. The eMect sizes and durations of these changes also remain under investigation.

Parameter space: Predicting how changes to the protocol will translate into physiological or behavioral eMects remains poorly understood. Some studies have directly compared the eMect of sonication when changing pulse repetition frequency (PRF), duty cycle, pulse duration, or intensity 29,100-103 . For example, several studies showed that applying lower PRFs, such as 5 or 10 Hz resulted in neuromodulation that outlasted the sonication, with eMects enduring for 30 to 60 minutes 73, 74, 90, 100, 104 . Few studies have compared long and short PRFs using the same design, however, and at least one of them found enduring eMects at higher PRFs as well 102 . In terms of PRF's eMect on direction of eMect (e.g., inhibitory vs. excitatory), the pattern is even less settled. At M1, much of the oMline work using lower PRFs reports excitatory eMects, using TMS-evoked MEP as the readout 100,105-107 . However, across non-motor cortical targets, there have been reports of higher PRFs resulting in excitation 82 and lower PRFs resulting in inhibition 108 , with no studies directly comparing them. Furthermore, even within M1, a recent paper found that changing the pulse duration while maintaining the PRF at 5Hz resulted in long-lasting depression rather than the usual excitation 105.

Auditory confounds:

Adding to these diMiculties, recent work has shown that eMects in many target-engagement studies may be driven in part by an auditory confound 103 . Sonication produces an audible sound related to the PRF. For example, in the standard MEP setup, the ultrasound pulse slightly precedes the TMS pulse, so participants can learn to anticipate the TMS pulse from the click; that anticipation alone alters the motor response. The confound is not limited to human work. Ultrasound activates the auditory system through a cochlear pathway in rodents, and deafening the animals or transecting the cochlear nerve abolishes much of the cortical response previously attributed to direct neuromodulation 109, 110 . Masking the auditory cue and including sham conditions matched for acoustic properties have accordingly become standard practice 64, 103.

Clinical Applications

Clinical applications of low-intensity focused ultrasound remain in their infancy. Most trials to date are small, single-arm studies of safety and feasibility, and the randomized controlled trials that exist are modest in size and heterogeneous in target, protocol, and indication. The evidence is correspondingly preliminary. Still, early signals across several conditions are encouraging enough to motivate the larger and better-controlled trials the field now needs.

Parkinson's:

The preliminary research in Parkinson's disease has concentrated on two targets: the globus pallidus internus (GPi) and the primary motor cortex (M1). Lin et al. (2026) 111 compared bilateral M1, GPi, and simultaneous M1 and GPi theta-burst sonication in 13 patients on medication, using a randomized, blinded design. GPi sonication reduced motor cortex excitability and improved clinical scores relative to baseline and to the other targets, whereas M1 sonication raised MEP amplitudes relative to GPi without changing clinical scores. Samuel et al. (2023) 112 delivered three 30-minute bilateral M1 sonications using a theta-burst protocol in 10 patients oM medication and likewise found increased MEP amplitudes after active but not sham sonication, again without accompanying clinical change. Grippe et al. (2024) 113 , also targeting M1 with theta-burst sonication, reported both increased MEP amplitudes and improved bradykinesia scores in an open-label study, though only in patients on dopaminergic medication, in contrast to Samuel et al. Across these studies no serious adverse events were reported, although several participants experienced transient fatigue.

Recent studies have begun to record the eMects of LIFU directly through implanted DBS electrodes. Eraifej et al. (2026) 114 conducted a randomized, blinded crossover study in 4 patients, sonicating the left GPi against a control site, with parameters chosen to approximate DBS-like modulation. GPi sonication reduced ipsilateral STN beta power by roughly 10% relative to sham and shortened online reaction times during a motor task, with no change in clinical scores assessed immediately after sonication. Sarica et al. ( 2026) 115 recorded STN activity in 17 patients on medication during randomized sham and active sessions of theta-burst sonication to M1, GPi, and an occipital control site. M1 sonication suppressed STN beta oscillations and improved clinical scores, whereas GPi sonication increased beta activity without improving motor symptoms, although recent work suggests this beta increase may be an artifact related to the PRF. No adverse events were reported apart from fatigue in 9 participants.

Alzheimer's: Two randomized controlled trials have evaluated pulsed ultrasound protocols in Alzheimer's disease, and none met their primary endpoints (Fuh et al. 2026 & Matt et al. 2025) 116, 117 . Transcranial pulse stimulation (TPS) delivers ultrashort, broadband pulses rather than the longer sinusoidal pulse trains used in conventional LIFU, producing very low time-averaged intensities alongside high peak pressures. Matt et al. (2025) 117 ran a crossover trial in 60 patients, each receiving both active and sham treatment, with active sonication targeting prefrontal cortex, parietal cortex, and precuneus simultaneously. Active treatment was no better than sham overall, though a planned contrast found an interaction between active sonication and age, with patients under 70 showing less decline. Fuh et al. (2026) 116 conducted a pilot in 9 patients (6 active, 3 sham) sonicated 30 times over 6 weeks. Decline on the primary cognitive measure was not significantly attenuated at 52 weeks, while a secondary measure favored active treatment at 24 weeks and again at 52. Matt et al. (2025) 117 also reported mild to moderate adverse events, most commonly transient depressive symptoms, and one transient ischemic attack during a sham washout.

Open-label pilot studies provide preliminary evidence of target engagement. In 8 participants with AD, a single session of focused ultrasound to the right hippocampus increased regional cerebral glucose utilization, and the magnitude of that increase correlated with improved recognition memory (Jeong et al. 2022) 118 . Jeong et al. (2025) 119 sonicated the left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (dlPFC) in 10 patients with early-stage AD across 6 sessions over two to three weeks, and found improved memory performance.

Epilepsy: Small pilot studies and one randomized trial have found LIFU targeted to the seizure onset zone to be feasible and tolerable, with mixed evidence for a therapeutic eMect 120 . In a single-blind, randomized, sham-controlled crossover trial, a single session of LIFU to the seizure onset zone produced no diMerence in seizure frequency between active and sham conditions over the following four weeks, though a significant reduction emerged during the open-label five-month follow-up 121 . Open-label studies have been similarly mixed. Lee et al. (2021) 122 treated six patients with a single session and found reduced seizure frequency in two, no change in three, and an increase in one. Bubrick et al. (2023) 123 delivered six sessions over three weeks and reported reduced seizure frequency in five of six patients at six months. 124 report the only randomized, double-blind, shamcontrolled trial in patients with schizophrenia. Twenty-six patients were randomized to 15 sessions of active or sham LIFU over three weeks, targeting the left dlPFC using a protocol with a 100Hz PRF. Active LIFU reduced negative symptoms and overall illness severity relative to sham, with mixed eMects across cognitive measures. Treatment was well tolerated, and no serious adverse events were reported. These results appeared as a brief communication, however, and a full report has not been published. Subramaniam et al. (2026) 125 report blinded, sham-controlled sonication of striatal circuitry in three patients with hallucinations, who showed reduced hallucination severity after active stimulation. 127 sonicated the bilateral nucleus accumbens in participants with substance use disorder, but those sonications were not strictly in the low-intensity regime 11, 1211, 12 . As such, the eMicacy and feasibility of low-intensity focused ultrasound for SUD have yet to be evaluated.

Chronic Pain:

LIFU research in chronic pain remains at an early stage, with most work targeting the anterior cingulate cortex, thalamus, and insula. Riis et al. (2024) 128 conducted an RCT with two 40-minute sessions of active or sham sonication in 20 patients and reported that 60% of participants achieved a clinically meaningful reduction in pain immediately after active sonication and again at one week, compared with a substantially smaller proportion in the sham condition. Shin et al. (2023) 129 targeted the ACC in 11 individuals with chronic neuropathic pain and likewise found reduced pain scores and improved daily functioning at four weeks relative to baseline. 132 each sonicated the dlPFC in patients with depressive symptoms and each found greater improvement in depression scores with active sonication than with sham. Session counts ranged from 6 to 12 across multiple weeks, with clinical benefit persisting for up to four weeks after the final session. Reznik et al. (2020) 133 targeted the right frontotemporal cortex five times over one week in university students with mild-to-moderate depression. Depression scores did not improve relative to sham, though trait worry fell in the active group and rose in sham. Riis et al. (2025) 134 used a crossover design in patients with treatment-resistant depression or bipolar depression to target the subcallosal cingulate. Sonication produced target-specific deactivation at the site, and active sessions outperformed sham on sadness and depression ratings within 24 hours, though the diMerence was no longer detectable at one week. Two serious adverse events occurred following active sonication: worsening depression with suicidal ideation, one involving an intentional overdose that did not require medical intervention, with both resolving over the subsequent two weeks.

Two open-label studies have targeted the amygdala. Barksdale et al. (2025) 135 treated 21 individuals spanning depressive, bipolar, anxiety, and post-traumatic stress diagnoses in a single-arm phase, and found that amygdala sonication reduced amygdala activity and general distress. No serious adverse events were reported, though headache, irritability, reduced concentration, limb tingling, and dizziness occurred across both sham and active conditions. Mahdavi et al. (2023) 136 sonicated the amygdala once weekly for eight weeks in patients with treatment-resistant generalized anxiety disorder, and observed reduced anxiety across multiple clinical measures.

Conclusion

Research on low-intensity focused ultrasound remains in its early days. Foundational work is needed on several fronts, including standardizing how protocols are reported, establishing ground-truth estimates of the dose delivered to the target, and developing readouts that confirm target engagement. Progress in these areas will allow the field to distinguish a protocol that failed to engage its target from one that engaged it but provided no therapeutic benefit. Mechanistic work faces a parallel problem, since the direction and duration of an eMect depend on the target and the full parameter set in ways that are not currently predictable, and the systematic comparisons that would resolve this have largely not been done. Most importantly, clinical eMicacy has yet to be established for any condition, target, or protocol. However, these are tractable problems, and the field has already begun to address them.
