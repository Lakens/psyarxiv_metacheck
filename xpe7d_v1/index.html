Power Analysis Check

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Abstract

Despite academic concern over mass publics' commitments to democracy, contemporary synthesis on the subject of "support for democracy" is conspicuously absent. This review fills that gap by using large language models to code and analyze the Support for Democracy (SFD) corpus, a collection of 442 peer-reviewed journal articles spanning 1967 to 2026 that use survey data to understand how citizens think about democracy. Three findings emerge. First, the field is largely organized around Easton's distinction between specific and diffuse support. Second, this divide subsequently structures distinct reading communities where papers devoted to one of these evaluative dimensions are limited in their citation practices of the other. Finally, the relationship between these dimensionsi.e. whether dissatisfaction with performance ultimately erodes commitment to democracy itself -is rarely tested directly. I close by describing how future research would benefit from sharper measures, clearer research designs, and a broader focus on democracies globally.

Introduction

The study of citizens' support for self-rule long predates the modern survey infrastructure that measures it today. Classical and early modern political theorists, for example, viewed popular attachments to regimes as central features of governmental stability. Aristotle's Politics (Books V-VI) warned that political conflict may emerge when citizens become disillusioned with governing authorities' promises about equality and fairness. Polybius' Histories (Book VI) builds on that reasoning, arguing that democracy is particularly susceptible to degenerating into mob rule when citizens take freedom and equality for granted.

Montesquieu's The Spirit of the Laws puts an even finer point on this warning: democracies become corrupted not from outside threats, but by an erosion of the democratic spirit within. Formal institutions set the conditions under which democracy is procedurally experienced, but citizens' commitments to the principles those institutions are meant to embody are an essential ingredient of democratic peace.

These early philosophical works anticipate a central question of modern democratic theory: when faced with (inevitable) disappointment, what binds citizens to the democratic project? Because government cannot escape turmoil, Lipset (1959) names legitimacy as the foundation of democratic stability, arguing that a regime's acceptance by its citizens helps it withstand crises of effectiveness. Easton's (1965 Easton's ( , 1975) ) subsequent work on systems theory helps unpack the logic of this delicate relationship. Citizens may hold a deep attachment to the political system (diffuse support) that lets them withstand episodic dissatisfaction with what government delivers (specific support). Linz and Stepan (1996) place those commitments at the center of democratic consolidation, which occurs when citizens accept democratic procedures as "the only game in town," even when government disappoints them. 1Today, attention to these attitudes largely manifests in debates over whether mass publics will resist or enable democratic backsliding. Around the world, scholars have documented weaker satisfaction with democracy (e.g., Armingeon and Guthmann 2013; Turner and D'Art 2025), public sympathy for authoritarian alternatives to democracy (e.g., Foa and Mounk 2016, 2017; Lavrič and Bieber 2021; Claassen and Magalhães 2023), and tolerance, if not outright acceptance, of politicians who violate democratic norms and principles (Graham and Svolik 2020; Gidengil, Stolle and Bergeron-Boutin 2021; Dahlum, Langsæther and Wig 2025). Yet, despite this academic interest, there are few integrative or systematic reviews that investigate the study of support for democracy as a body of public opinion literature, although narrower reviews of outcomes and measures exist (Valgarðsson and Devine 2022; Claassen et al. 2025; Welzel 2021).

The practical result is that while this domain of research has matured, it has developed without a clear agenda for connecting findings across outcomes, settings, and research traditions into a cumulative account of why citizens support democracy. Because different measures, explanations, and findings paint a portrait of support for democracy that can be difficult to harmonize, this review takes stock of this growing body literature. Drawing on the Support for Democracy (SFD) corpus, which contains more than 440 peerreviewed journal articles, I first describe how the corpus was built and coded, then map where the research takes place and which survey programs it relies on. I next trace how support for democracy is measured, review what predicts specific and diffuse support, and summarize what the field can say about spillover between them. I then conclude with a brief discussion regarding the field's limitations and the questions it has yet to answer.

Building the Support for Democracy Corpus

The Support for Democracy (SFD) corpus was built in two stages. 2 First, using Harzing's (2007) Publish or Perish web scraper, I scraped Google Scholar between October 1 and 8, 2026, for papers that included one of thirteen phrases in the title: (1) attitudes toward, (2) commitment to, (3) preference for, (4) satisfaction with, (5) support for, (6) trust in, and (7) the meanings of democracy, along with (8) support for democratic norms and (9) democratic support, (10) values, (11) norms, (12) norm violations, and (13) backsliding. Collectively, the searches returned 974 records and 884 unique works. An initial procedural cull was then performed using large language model (LLM) agents where the corpus was screened for English-language journal articles that were indexed via Scopus-or Web of Science, resulting in 498 peerreviewed articles (books, book chapters, working papers, and other unpublished manuscripts were omitted). 3 The full text of six of these articles could not be obtained, leaving 492 to screen. Next, a substantive screening scheme was implemented, which resulted in removing papers whose outcome is not a mass attitude toward democracy (n = 72), papers that use democracy only as a predictor (n = 21), papers without survey data (n = 3), and papers that report neither an inferential test nor a systematic comparison of the outcome across groups (n = 3). Coding research works in the SFD corpus

After screening, each paper was coded for the type of analysis (e.g., observational, experimental), the source of its public opinion data (e.g., the World Values Survey), and the countries and regions it studies.

Categorizing outcomes was straightforward because a narrow list of outcomes quickly emerged; coding predictors, however, was considerably more complex. Paper-level coding followed a written codebook, developed and revised over several rounds of human hand-coding before turning loose the LLM coders. Using Anthropic's Claude.ai (Opus 5.5), agent coders then read each paper's full text and recorded (1) inclusion eligibility, namely whether a mass attitude toward democracy is modeled as a dependent variable, whether democratic attitudes serve as more than predictors, whether the paper analyzes standardized survey responses, and whether it reports at least one inferential test of the democracy outcome, (2) the country and region associated with data collection, (3) data source, (4) sample type, (5) the democratic outcome, (6) each predictor the paper frames as central and tests against that outcome using a list of 29 predictor labels, (7) moderation, when an effect varies with country-or context-level conditions, (8) research design (e.g., observational, experimental), and (9) measurement, when a paper explicitly states that its aim is to study the structure of democratic beliefs. For analysis, I later grouped the 29 predictor labels into 13 predictor families; those families are an analytical grouping generated after the predictor labels were applied to facilitate discussion of trends in this research.

The codebook was developed iteratively. Across three rounds of testing, I independently coded 90 papers and compared my coding with coding conducted by LLM agents. I adjudicated each disagreement, and where a disagreement exposed an ambiguity in the rules, I amended the rules before further coding continued. Once the rules were final, they were frozen, and a fresh batch of LLM coders, working blind to all earlier coding, recoded every paper with an available full text in a single final pass under those frozen rules. The codebook and logbook of those decisions is available in the appendix.

Agreement between human coding and the final coding conducted by LLM agents was high. As mentioned above, the screening process began by determining whether a paper ought to be included in the main corpus; agreement on inclusion was 94% (Cohen's κ = .84). Next, outcomes were coded according to a set of criteria, and agreement on the focal outcome family was 90% (98% when either of the two outcome families I coded matched either of the two in the final coding). For predictors, my predictor families matched those of the final coding in 87% of papers. Agreement on which predictor a paper treats as "primary" was much lower (61%), largely because coders ordered the same predictors differently or chose neighboring concepts (e.g., an election coded as representation by one coder and as an external shock by another). As such, I abandoned the focus on a single primary predictor. Instead, each paper is represented by the full set of predictors it frames as substantively important: those named in its title, stated aims (per abstract), or hypotheses and tested against a democracy outcome. All subsequent analyses of predictors use this set rather than a single focal choice, which keeps what each paper reports as central without forcing an arbitrary ranking among them. 5

Approaches to measuring support for democracy

In early work, Almond and Verba (1963) asked whether citizens held the orientations a stable democracy needs. Surveying publics in five countries, they argued that democracy is most stable where citizens' expectations of government are tempered by a measure of trust and deference, a balance they call the "civic culture." Despite its canonical place in the literature, this early research largely treated orientations toward democracy as a single cultural syndrome. In other words, it did not separate citizens' attachment to the political system from their judgments of how that system and its leaders perform, which meant that its findings could not say whether disappointment over government outputs would erode commitment to democracy itself. Easton's (1965 Easton's ( , 1975) ) field-setting work on systems theory provided a template for filling that gap by distinguishing diffuse from specific support -a distinction that has largely structured how the subsequent research on support for democracy has evolved over the last sixty years. The earliest articles in the SFD corpus tend to include to both specific (e.g., Kornberg and Clarke 1994; Mishler and Rose 1996; Anderson and Guillory 1997) and diffuse support for democracy (e.g., Crotty 1967; Shin, Chey and Kim 1989; LoGerfo 1996). A series of developments, however, then pulled the field toward the performance dimension of Easton's systems framework. Satisfaction with democracy, asked routinely in Eurobarometer surveys and later in the Comparative Study of Electoral Systems, made performance evaluations cheap to study across many countries; in no small part, the significant scholarly investment in studying satisfaction cannot be divorced from the dramatic growth in cross-national public opinion research more generally.

In the aftermath of this attention, a measurement debate emerged, with researchers questioning what, precisely, satisfaction with democracy meant in practice. 6 Here, criticism divided over the implications of the conceptual ambiguity inherent in "satisfaction." Some concluded that because satisfaction blends support for the regime, the system, and incumbents, researchers needed to clarify the implications of such support (Canache, Mondak and Seligson 2001). Others, instead, narrowed the interpretation of the instrument, treating it as a gauge of how democracy performs in practice rather than of commitment to democratic principles, although even this reading depends on institutional context (Linde and Ekman 2003). Thus, it is perhaps not surprising that satisfaction varies so closely with both the underlying institutional rules (e.g., Anderson and Guillory 1997; Aarts and Thomassen 2008; Bernauer and Vatter 2012) and the status of citizens as "winners" or "losers" (e.g.

, Blais and Gélineau 2007; Curini, Jou and Memoli 2012; Singh 2014) -features that have implications for democratic experiences. Today, although satisfaction with democracy still predominates as an outcome in the SFD corpus (appearing in 57% of papers), a new phase has developed in the aftermath of scholarly concerns about democratic deconsolidation. In the mid-2010s, the volume of work on support for democracy rose sharply -272 of the 442 included papers date from 2017 or later -at which point the literature's motivating question shifted to backsliding. Two changes mark this late development. First, attention moved from abstract regime preference toward commitment to liberal-democratic norms and the rejection of autocratic alternatives (i.e. 9 papers from 2010-2016 compared to 43 papers from 2017 to present). Second, research designs became more experimental: 50 of the papers since 2017 rest wholly or partly on a survey, natural or quasiexperiment, against only 4 of the 170 earlier papers.

The theoretical distinction between diffuse and specific support is only as good as the empirical evidence that sustains it, yet even substantive papers largely concede measurement challenges before setting them aside. Nevertheless, 75 corpus papers carry measurement tags: 35 papers whose main contribution involves measurement of support for democracy and 40 that make a measurement argument alongside a substantive one. The literature is mostly recent: 32 of the 75 date from 2020 or later and largely concentrates on a few questions (Table 3). To assess the prevalence of different measures of support for democracy within the corpus on a more granular level, I pair Norris' (1999 Norris' ( , 2011) ) work with Easton's (1965) distinctions between specific and diffuse support. If the latter provides a blunt dichotomy between evaluation types, the former provides a second layer that helps nest different outcomes within these broad buckets, separating out support for the political community, regime principles, regime performance, regime institutions, and incumbent actors. 7 Specifically, four broad families of measures manifest in the corpus. These attitudes involve abstract support for democracy as an admirable, if not ideal form of government. These commitments are embodied by preferences for democratic government (democracy preferred to its alternatives), autocratic alternatives (rejection of strongman, military, or expert rule) and liberal-democratic values (commitment to rights, tolerance and limits on power).

 Diffuse support, understanding of the regime (2%). Across the world, different people hear different things when they consider what democracy means. The "meanings" of democracy questions ask respondents to consider whether certain features of democracy are more or less important or essential. These attitudes are classed as diffuse in nature because they concern what citizens take democracy to be, though they are a precondition of support rather than support itself.

 Specific support, regime performance and a few institutions (51%). These attitudes are classic performance evaluations. The standard example is satisfaction with democracy, along with other evaluations of how well democracy is performing at a given moment. 7 One could also make the argument that beliefs about democracy may fit within an alternative framework that distinguishes thin (endorsement of democracy in the abstract) and thick support (commitments to what democracy requires). Thin support is often instrumental; that is, people say that they prefer democracy because they associate it with positive outcomes. Even though the evaluative object is the regime, that kind of support can behave like specific support, collapsing when performance falls. Whether mass support for democracy rests on intrinsic or instrumental grounds is a question within this literature (e.g. Bratton and Mattes 2001; Kilavuz and Sumaktoyo 2020; Spierings 2024); I do not adopt that framework here, however, because it cuts across outcome families rather than replacing or offering an alternative to them. 8 Each paper is assigned a level from its focal outcome. The outcome family alone decides three levels: regime preference, rejection of autocratic alternatives and liberal-democratic values are principles; meanings of democracy is understanding; and support for direct democracy or referendums is a procedural preference. Evaluation outcomes default to regime performance, except that items rating particular institutions are classed as institutions and indices that combine principle and performance items are classed as mixed. Of the 442 included papers, 208 fall on the principles side (principles or understanding), 223 on regime performance, 2 on institutions, 6 on direct democracy, and 3 are mixed. Comparisons of principles and performance set the last 11 aside or, where noted, keep them as a third group.

 Procedural preferences (1%). These attitudes constitute a small share of the attitudes measured in the corpus and most directly measure support for direct democracy. Preferring a way of making decisions is neither an evaluation of how democracy is performing nor a commitment to its principles, so I treat these papers as a separate category, outside the diffuse-specific divide; the comparisons of principles and performance below set them aside. In addition to these shares is a second feature worth acknowledging that involves how composite measures are built. Often, where papers measure principles or other items included under the banner of diffuse support, researchers combine items into composite indices. Of the 53 papers (12%) whose focal outcome combines items from two or more outcome families, 51 sit on the principles side, most commonly in the form of the democracy-autocracy index built from World Values Survey items (c.f. Inglehart and Welzel 2005), which adds endorsements of democracy and subtracts support for rule by a strong leader or the army. More broadly, roughly a quarter of papers describe their focal outcome as an index, scale, or latent score. This distribution includes 90 papers measuring principles, whereas 23 of the 228 papers focused on evaluations of performance do so. That result produces an interesting asymmetry. Performance is generally measured with one standard question, while principles are measured with a shifting set of studyspecific composites that often merge embracing democracy with rejecting its alternatives, two attitudes that need not move together.

Findings on the principles side are therefore harder to compare across studies, and the two literatures are, in a sense, not even measuring support for democracy in the same format.

Recent research on support for political institutions like the Supreme Court offers a cautionary parallel to this development. There, diffuse support (legitimacy) has typically been measured with multiitem batteries and specific support with a single approval item, a mismatch Davis and Hitt (2026a, 2026b) address by validating a three-item measure of specific support. The SFD corpus shows the same asymmetry: performance is usually measured with one item, while more durable commitments to democracy (values, principles, and regime support) are measured with composites.

The mismatch matters for several reasons.

First, single items carry more random measurement error than multi-item scales. When satisfaction is the outcome, that error makes estimates noisier and weakens standardized associations; when it is a predictor, as in tests of spillover, it biases estimated effects toward zero. Apparent differences in how strongly predictors relate to performance and to principles may therefore partly reflect differences in reliability rather than substance. Second, composites that combine endorsing democracy with rejecting its alternatives fold distinct quantities into one score, so their correlates are averages across components that need not move together. Third, the relationship between the two kinds of support is then estimated between a noisy single item and a heterogeneous composite, which leaves it underspecified and likely understated.

What predicts support for democracy?

Given the motivational distinctions between specific and diffuse support, it seems prudent to expect that the correlates of these attitudes will differ, as well. To unpack these relationships, I begin by giving a broad overview of what the SFD corpus can tell us about the wellspring of support for democracy. I then devote separate space to reviewing what past scholarship says about the common correlates of specific and diffuse support. The studies I cite as examples are drawn largely from the most-cited papers in each predictor family and on each side of that divide (by Google Scholar citation counts), supplemented by recent work that marks new directions; they illustrate the literature rather than sample it. However, as Figure 4 illustrates, these explanations vary dramatically based on whether researchers model specific or diffuse support. Elections -and their consequences -are reliably associated with satisfaction, while other forms of system-level performance (government, economy) are connected to both specific and diffuse support, alike. A variety of additional explanations ranging from partisanship and group identities to social and political trust are more often tied to commitments to democratic governance and principles.

Specific support tracks performance and context

The largest body of work within the SFD corpus investigates satisfaction with democracy, which is rendered as a verdict on how the system works. This research is anchored by Anderson and Guillory (1997), the most-cited paper in the entire corpus, which found that electoral losers are more satisfied, and winners less so, in countries with consensual institutions. This focus on representation, and whether citizens backed the winner or loser, forms a throughline through this body of work (Blais and Gélineau 2007; Rohrschneider 2002; Curini, Jou and Memoli 2012; Singh, Karakoç and Blais 2012), although institutional explanations for specific support extend beyond this domain. Indeed, other work explores how electoral and constitutional design (Aarts and Thomassen 2008; Bernauer and Vatter 2012) and, increasingly, the quality rather than the form of institutions -for example, institutional quality (Wagner, Schneider and Halla 2009), procedural fairness (Magalhães 2016) and the salience of corruption (Christmann and Torcal 2017) -are all associated with satisfaction.

Economic explanations also follow a similar logic. Satisfaction is higher among citizens who expect the national economy to improve in post-communist Europe (Mishler and Rose 1996) and where macroeconomic performance is stronger across the European Union (Quaranta and Martini 2016), and it fell where austerity was imposed from outside during the euro crisis (Armingeon, Guthmann and Weisstanner 2016). All but one of the 40 tested economic relationships on the performance side are significant. Smaller literatures extend the performance logic to trust and social capital (Zmerli and Newton 2008), to the information environment, where social-media news use and perceived misinformation go with lower satisfaction (Ceron and Memoli 2016; Nisbet, Mortenson and Li 2021), and to events, where a COVID-19 lockdown raised satisfaction (Bol et al. 2021) and scandal elections were followed by lower satisfaction (Kumlin and Esaiasson 2012).

Diffuse support involves socialization, values and political experience

The socialization tradition predicts that support for democracy develops through learning, and education is among the correlates of diffuse support that is most routinely significant, and in the widest range of settings, from post-communist Europe (Waldron-Moore 1999; McAllister and White 2017) to sub-Saharan Africa (Evans and Rose 2007, 2012; Asomah and Dim 2021) and South Asia (Farooq, Bouwhuis and Muis 2025).

Cross-national comparisons also suggest the relationship is stronger in more democratic countries (Kołczyńska 2020). Cultural explanations that locate support for democracy in, say, religious traditions, inherited values or psychological dispositions, are more mixed. The values, religion, and dispositions family is most often tested against democratic principles (53 of its 65 papers), although the corpus offers little support for the claim that particular cultural traditions are incompatible with democracy.

This is especially the case with religion, where the evidence points away from blanket incompatibility with democracy (Tessler 2002; Jamal 2006). Where religion does matter, the dimension measured shapes the relationship: in priming experiments, private religious belief lowers support for democracy while communal religious practice raises it, and cross-national survey data show a similar split (Ben-Nun Bloom and Arikan 2013a, 2013b). The Asian-values debate runs along similar lines. Of seven dimensions of Asian values, only one, a dislike of adversarial politics, is associated with lower support for democracy (Park and Shin 2006), although work that isolates hierarchical and paternalistic strands of Confucian thought finds them more consistently at odds with liberal-democratic commitments (Chang, Wu and Weatherall 2017; Xiao and Zhang 2025; Lee and Tsai 2026). Among dispositions, authoritarianism is the one most often found to predict lower support (Canetti-Nisim and Beit-Hallahmi 2007; Gryniuk 2018; Kaftan 2026).

However, the most-cited diffuse-support work traces regime preference not to culture but to political experience. In new democracies, citizens who judge that democracy delivers political goods such as civil liberties and free elections prefer it more (Bratton and Mattes 2001; Evans and Whitefield 1995).

As concern about democratic backsliding has intensified, scholars have pivoted to asking what erodes diffuse support in established democracies. Today, later generations of Americans appear less supportive of democracy (Claassen and Magalhães 2023), perhaps because they have seen it strain under the weight of gridlock and polarization. In this vein, partisanship often renders citizens unwilling to punish undemocratic candidates (Graham and Svolik 2020; Kingzette et al. 2021); and ethnic antagonism and national collective narcissism are associated with antidemocratic attitudes (Bartels 2020; Marchlewska et al. 2022; Miller and Davis 2021).

Whither spillover?

Despite the academic distinction between specific and diffuse support, one remaining question in this literature that goes largely unanswered involves the relationship between the two kinds of support. Easton expected them to be linked over time: diffuse support can be, in part, the product of "spillover effects from evaluations of a series of outputs" (Easton 1975, 446). By implication, persistent dissatisfaction with outputs should eventually erode it. Yet Easton was also careful to note that the connection between political dissatisfaction and acceptance of the regime is unpredictable. In fact, the critical-citizens argument implies that the two can stay apart for long periods (Norris 1999). On this view, the gap between citizens' commitment to democratic ideals and their disappointment with how democracy performs, which Norris calls a "democratic deficit," need not signal erosion of diffuse support and may instead reflect rising expectations among citizens who value democracy more, not less.

Elections are the natural place to look for these relationships, since every contest produces a clear set of losers whose evaluations of democratic performance might fall. Indeed, whether an electoral loss spills into commitments to democratic principles is the central question of the losers'-consent tradition (Anderson et al. 2005). The winner-loser literature is the largest single cluster in the corpus, yet almost none of it can speak to spillover. Of 138 findings, 122 concern regime performance (almost always satisfaction with democracy). However, 15 papers relate a respondent's side winning or holding power to a principle-level outcome, and in eight of them it is winners, not losers, who become less enamored with democratic ideals. Supporters of winning parties value checks and balances less (Mazepus and Toshkov 2022), incumbent supporters are less willing to constrain the executive (van der Brug et al. 2021), and partisans tolerate norm-eroding policies and undemocratic candidates when their own side benefits (Simonovits, McCoy and Littvay 2022; Graham and Svolik 2020; Driscoll et al. 2025). The remaining papers qualify that pattern, producing effects that vary by country (Gidengil, Stolle and Bergeron-Boutin 2021), or party (Kingzette et al. 2021), or that disappear with controls (Sadowski 2024). Suggestively, the threat to democratic principles may come less from disaffected losers than from permissive winners (e.g., Cohen et al. 2023; Fjelde and Olafsdottir 2025; Teas 2026).

Direct tests of spillover outside elections are also scarce. Only 23 of the 442 included papers measure both a performance-level and a principle-level outcome. The handful that use performance evaluations to predict principles find a positive link (Evans and Whitefield 1995; Sarsfield and Echegaray 2006; Chasukwa 2019), although an older aggregate study found that poor state performance went with lower confidence in institutions but not with weaker support for democracy (Weil 1989). Nearly all of this evidence is cross-sectional, so it cannot separate spillover from common causes or establish which way the relationship runs. The context-dependent economic effects (e.g., Cordero and Simón 2016; Teixeira, Tsatsanis and Belchior 2014) and the thermostatic response of democratic mood (e.g., Claassen 2020) are perhaps the closest the corpus comes to observing how that reservoir of support drains or refills.

A literature of separate communities

To this point, I have highlighted important trends in measurement and modeling within the work researching support for democracy. What is less clearly understood, however, is how any given work or subsets of work are nested within the wider community of published literature on support for democracy.

One way of making sense of this literature, however, is assessing its networked structure. For this task, I built a within-corpus citation network to assess the shape of research communities within this work. In this network analysis, an edge runs from paper A to paper B whenever B appears in A's reference list. Papers were matched to OpenAlex by DOI or through a Crossref bibliographic search. Of the 498 keyword-search records, 472 works were matched, with 447 articles having a reference list in OpenAlex. The result is some 2,488 unique citations among corpus records. That total includes citations to and from records later excluded at screening; the tests below use only the 1,946 citations in which both papers were included and coded.

Limitations and shortcomings

Of course, no study such as this is immune from shortcomings. and Loken (2014) call the garden of forking paths. Of the 712 tested relationships, 637 (89%) are significant or mixed, that is, significant in at least some of the specifications the authors report. The rate barely varies from 90% in observational designs to 88% in experimental ones, and between 81% and 97% across the 13 predictor families. With many surveys, waves, countries, items and model specifications to choose from, analytic flexibility alone can generate significant results, so a covariate that is significant in one paper may say less than it seems. 15 Thus, statistical significance alone does little to parse "strong" from "weak" explanations in the literature.

These shortcomings notwithstanding, one glaring limitation produced by this project involves contemporary tensions borne from public support for unapologetically antidemocratic and illiberal parties and politicians (c.f. Druckman 2024). Positive affect towards and electoral endorsement of these groups and officials is a signal of (a lack of) public commitments to democracy. The closest the corpus comes to that analysis is the small set of studies on revealed support (n = 10), which ask whether voters punish candidates who behave undemocratically. Their answer is sobering.

In Graham and Svolik's (2020) experiments, only a small fraction of American voters were willing to abandon a preferred candidate who took an undemocratic position, presumably because polarization raised the price of doing so. But these designs largely rely on hypothetical candidates in forced-choice experiments. Observed support for real parties committing real harm against democracy is a simple and appropriate behavioral measure, but treating support for a particular party as anti-democratic imposes a normative judgment on that party, and political scientists studying mass attitudes are often reluctant to render such verdicts.

However, researchers need not feel such pressure because expert-coded data can pass judgment accordingly. The V-Party dataset scores nearly every relevant political party worldwide on an Anti-Pluralism Index: low commitment to the democratic process, demonization of opponents, encouragement of political violence and disrespect for minority rights (Lührmann et al. 2020; Medzihorsky and Lindberg 2024). By that measure, the U.S. Republican Party's rhetoric by 2018 more closely resembled ruling parties such as Turkey's AKP and Hungary's Fidesz than typical center-right governing parties in established democracies (Lührmann et al. 2020). Bolsonaro's Social Liberal Party (PSL) scored 0.95 on the 0-1 index in 2018, the election that brought him to power, above Fidesz (0.91) and close to the AKP (1.00 in 2015).

France's National Front, renamed the Rassemblement National in 2018, scored 0.49 in 2017: well below those parties, but above the British Conservatives (0.35) and far above Germany's CDU (0.05) (Lindberg et al. 2022). Linking vote choice or party identification in the CSES, ESS or national election studies to these scores would let researchers measure support for anti-pluralist parties as an outcome, while leaving the party judgments to external expert coding. Doing so would also connect to the measurement problem documented above in that respondents can endorse democracy in the abstract while voting for parties that dismantle it. Some of that gap reflects the partisan trade-off Graham and Svolik identify, not hostility to democracy itself. But that is precisely the point: if the outcome that matters for backsliding is whether citizens withhold support from parties that attack democratic institutions, then a literature built on abstract endorsement and satisfaction measures is studying the wrong thing, or at least the more comfortable part of the right thing. Ignoring this facet of support for democracy is done at the field's peril.

Looking ahead

Understanding support for democracy is a long-running project. As polling has become cheaper and more efficient, the proliferation of survey projects has followed suit. Yet despite the considerable lay and scholarly attention paid to these attitudes, integrative research has lagged. The SFD corpus is an attempt to close that gap by mapping 442 papers on what predicts support for democracy, how that support is measured, and who reads whom.

Moving forward, there are still questions left unanswered or evidence that is less straightforward than one might assume, which I organize into four broad areas.

Spillover. First, does sustained dissatisfaction with democratic performance erode diffuse support?

How fast, and, critically, for whom? Panels that measure both kinds of support before and after elections, economic crises and changes of government could answer this question. However, because specific support maps so cleanly onto the winner-loser framework, investigations of spillover must likely account for the asymmetric nature of performance evaluations.

Representation. Next, are election winners, rather than losers, the main electoral threat to diffuse support? To what undemocratic ends might losers go to reestablish power? To what extent are electoral winners willing to restrict their opponents' rights once they obtain power? Measures of democratic support that focus predominantly on abstract regime preferences likely miss how illiberal behaviors may be permitted among winners and losers at different rates. Measurement. Third, scholars have made much of (the failure of) norms in the intervening years since 2016. How should these commitments be operationalized? Can survey measures reveal democratic commitments, or is behavioral research necessary to illustrate when and under what circumstances the proverbial "rubber meets the road" when it comes to support democracy? In a similar vein, under what conditions can people be encouraged to punish norm-breaking politicians? Much of the SFD corpus uses easy-to-measure attitudinal measures like support for army rule, but many citizens of democracy have virtually no experience with such conditions. What does it mean for citizens to endorse anti-democratic forms of government? To what extent does the discipline's reliance on long-running survey measures obscure how citizens really feel about democracy? Generalizability. Finally, how far do findings from Europe-centered survey programs travel to the regions those programs cover thinly? Massive survey research programs like the ESS and WVS have permitted cross-national survey research to flourish, but to what extent are the relationships between covariates and support for democracy idiosyncratically related to (institutional) context? Do the results of studies conducted in western, educated, industrialized, rich, and democratic (WEIRD) countries travel outside the boundaries of this context? To various degrees, the papers included in the SFD corpus tackle some of these questions. Yet, there are substantial holes for future research to plug. Of course, no single study can answer all these questions; the process of literature building is, after all, iterative and prevailing events can shift research foci in unexpected ways. The support for democracy literature has grown large and productive, but it has yet to become a cumulative one.

Using simulations, we can show whether the boundaries are real rather than an artifact of group size. For each grouping, I compare the share of citations that stay within a group with the share we would expect if papers cited each other without regard to group. To get that expected share, I shuffle group labels across papers 2,000 times while holding the citation network fixed, which accounts for how large each group is. Because the labels move across papers, this baseline does not account for differences between groups in how much their papers cite or are cited. The ratio of observed to expected citations is 1 when papers cite across groups as often as chance predicts and 2 when they stay within their own group twice as often. By outcome family, the ratio is 2.1: 85% of citations stay within the citing paper's family, against 40% expected. When labels are shuffled only among papers with similar citation activity (terciles of citations made and received), the ratios shrink but remain well above 1: 1.45 for outcome family and 1.44 for principles versus performance (1.29 and 1.28 when region is also held fixed; all p < .001). I treat these as lower bounds, since the activity strata partly absorb the divide itself.

These boundaries largely hold up against the obvious objections. They do not depend on how citations were collected, on authors citing themselves, on a handful of highly cited papers, or on when papers were published. Under each of these checks, the outcome-family ratio stays between 2.0 and 2.2 and the principles-versus-performance ratio between 1.8 and 1.9.

Comparing papers only within the same journal weakens that contrast further (1.45). That is to be expected: journals specialize, and specialized venues contribute to the formation of separate reading communities.
