We found 4 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Positive well-being outcomes are associated with prosocial behavior. Understanding how cortical activity relates to changes in prosocial behavior can guide the development of interventions for those with neural deficits in prosocial behavior and support measurement when self-report may not be possible. This pilot study explored how a communal music-based intervention may relate to improvements in prosocial behavior and its relationship with cortical activity to establish feasibility and generate hypotheses for larger trials using hypothesis-driven and exploratory analyses. 37 children aged 4-6 were randomly assigned to a control group (visual art-focused, passively listened to music) or treatment group (communal music engagement) for 10 sessions of 45 minutes each. Hemodynamic responses were assessed using functional near-infrared spectroscopy collected pre-and post-intervention while participants listened to communal vs. unknown music. Prosocial behavior was measured with parental reports via the Strengths and Difficulties Questionnaire. We found initial differences in cortical activation, particularly in the superior frontal gyrus, between the control and treatment groups using bootstrap resampling methods (b = -11.470, 95% CI = [-21.202, -2.126]). We also found that the same area was correlated with changes in prosocial behavior (ρ(28) = 0.490, p = .008).

Correcting for multiple comparisons eliminated some significant findings. These pilot study findings suggest a need for further exploration with refined hypotheses to better understand relationships between communal music engagement, improvements in prosocial behavior, and cortical activity. There is an opportunity to expand on this research with a larger sample and a longitudinal design.

Introduction

Music has been proposed to play a central role in fostering social bonding across human cultures (Savage et al., 2021; Tarr et al., 2014; Trehub et al., 2015). While mechanisms are still being explored, some researchers suggest that social bonding through shared music engagement may also relate to prosocial behavior (Good & Russo, 2016; Kirschner & Tomasello, 2010; Savage et al., 2021; Tarr et al., 2014). Building on this, Music-Based Interventions (MBIs), structured programs involving music engagement through listening or activities, have shown promise for improving developmental outcomes in children, such as executive function and prosocial behavior (Muir et al., 2023; Piccolo et al., 2026). However, most of this evidence is behavioral. Little is known about whether such interventions change how the developing brain responds to music, or whether those changes relate to gains in prosocial behavior. Understanding how neural activity changes can elucidate how or why these interventions may be beneficial.

This understanding could then support future hypothesis generation, lead to targeted mental health interventions, and support measurement in future interventions, particularly in situations where self-report may be difficult, such as in early childhood. The present study aims to identify changes in cortical activity in response to music in children following a communal MBI and to understand the relationship between those changes and prosocial behavior.

Prosocial Behavior

Prosocial behavior represents actions performed to assist or provide benefit to others, often including terms like kindness, altruism, instrumental helping, and/or comforting, as well as actions like providing emotional support, donating, volunteering, cooperation, and acts of heroism (Malti & Speidel, 2023; Roth-Hanania, 2025; Wu & Hong, 2022). Such actions can have both individual and collective benefits. When cultivated, prosocial behavior is associated with positive outcomes and appears to protect against negative ones (Memmott-Elison et al., 2020).

For example, higher prosocial behavior scores were related to lower externalizing (e.g., aggression, substance use) and even internalizing (e.g., depression, low self-esteem) behaviors, compared with those with lower prosocial behavior scores (Memmott-Elison et al., 2020).

Additionally, higher prosocial behavior scores during elementary school predicted academic success and stronger social ties during teenage years (Caprara et al., 2000) and have been found to relate to outcomes in adulthood, including education, employment, and mental health (Jones et al., 2015). Poor prosocial behavior skills as early as preschool have been linked to poorer mental health and behavioral outcomes during adolescence (Donohue et al., 2020; Memmott-Elison et al., 2020). Collectively, prosocial behaviors are associated with higher trust (Simpson & Willer, 2015), and some research suggests societies higher in trust are generally associated with improved economic growth (Bjornskov, 2012; Knack & Keefer, 1997). As a result, interventions that support improvements in prosocial behavior can therefore benefit individuals and society as a whole.

Although prosocial behavior may have evolutionary roots (Decety & Holvoet, 2021; Eisenberg et al., 2015), these behaviors do not appear to naturally grow throughout one's entire lifetime; instead, research suggests they may stabilize, for example, during childhood to adolescence (Flynn et al., 2015; Nantel-Vivier et al., 2009). For example, Flynn and colleagues (2015) conducted a longitudinal study, starting with children at age 10 and following up yearly through age 18. The researchers explored prosocial behavior ratings from their teachers across these ages and found that prosocial behavior remained essentially stable (Flynn et al., 2015).

Given that prosocial behaviors are directly associated with the development of empathy and emotion regulation (Decety & Holvoet, 2021; Malti & Speidel, 2023; Roth-Hanania, 2025), identifying ways to intentionally enhance them, especially at an early age, is of utmost importance.

Various interventions have been explored over the years to identify how prosocial behavior can be promoted, encouraged, or further developed, including creative art interventions, such as through dance and music (Malti & Speidel, 2023; Muir et al., 2023; Murano et al., 2020; Piccolo et al., 2026). However, studies in this space are often behavioral. For example, if people have deficits in prosocial skills due to lesions or brain damage, understanding the relevant neural mechanisms may help explain those deficits and inform alternative interventions or therapies.

Although behavioral studies have arguably shown some improvement in prosocial behavior and related outcomes, questions remain regarding how MBIs relate to neural activity and how that activity may relate to prosocial behavior, as MBIs are considered more accessible and costeffective (Golden et al., 2021; Savage et al., 2021). MBIs may support continued growth in prosocial behaviors.

Communal Music-Based Interventions

Communal MBIs, MBIs conducted with a group rather than in individual sessions, have shown promising effects among children and may support and improve emotion regulation and prosocial behavior growth. In one study, researchers explored group singing, group art, and competitive gameplay with children around 7-8 years old and found that cooperation increased in the singing group across trials, whereas the group art and competitive gameplay groups either appeared to decrease cooperation or remained stable (Good & Russo, 2016). One meta-analysis suggests that MBIs may support improvements in internalizing symptoms, such as depression, for children and adolescents, calling for more research to better understand the phenomenon (Geipel et al., 2018). Another meta-analysis found medium-to-large effects on clinical outcomes, such as externalizing or behavioral diagnoses, when music therapy was used (Gold et al., 2004).

Music therapy uses interventions that involve interaction with music to communicate, for example, emotions, nonverbally (Gold et al., 2004). Another study comparing a music engagement group (singing and dancing) with a non-music group (speaking and non-dance movement) found that 4-year-old children in the music engagement group were more helpful or cooperative in subsequent tasks than the non-music group (Kirschner & Tomasello, 2010). Taken together, these studies suggest that communal music engagement may support emotion regulation and cooperative outcomes from young ages. Additionally, research suggests that communal MBIs offer cost-effective avenues for mental health support and may drive additional benefits such as exposure to the arts and, when conducted in groups, social connectivity (Golden et al., 2021; Piccolo et al., 2025).

Attempts to better understand the links between communal MBIs and prosocial behaviors have faced challenges and limitations. Previously, many studies focused on interventions, but perhaps did not implement full randomized controls across groups (e.g. Good & Russo, 2016), introduced interventions that lasted only a few hours as opposed to several sessions over a longer period of time (e.g. Cirelli & Trehub, 2018; Kirschner & Tomasello, 2010; Piccolo et al., 2025), did not blind assessors to participants' condition (e.g. Good & Russo, 2016), or focused on measuring specific outcomes like cooperation or stress hormones, but did not include prosocial behavior or cortical measures (e.g. Bainbridge et al., 2021; Good & Russo, 2016). Thus, research in this space may benefit from a different methodological design. An assessor-blind randomized controlled trial, over a longer period of time, taking pre-and posttest measurements not only behaviorally, but also in terms of cortical activity, would be a step in that direction. A study could potentially link behavioral outcomes to changes in cortical activity, which could lead to improved interventions for children, support future hypothesis generation, and perhaps provide avenues for improved outcome measurement.

In a recent pilot, researchers attempted to link communal MBIs to prosocial behavior, while addressing methodological limitations of some past studies. Over 10 separate sessions in a pilot randomized controlled trial, two groups proceeded through a study exploring changes related to experiencing communal music (i.e., music that has been experienced by a group of people for or related to some context) as opposed to familiar music (i.e., music that one has heard before, but not experienced in a group context) or unfamiliar music, that is, music that has not been experienced before at all (Piccolo et al., 2025 (Piccolo et al., , 2026)). Participants in the communal music engagement group engaged with certain music as a group, referred to as communal music, which included aspects like singing and moving synchronously (Piccolo et al., 2026). The control group also heard music similarly to the communal music engagement group, but instead participated in other art-related activities that sometimes emphasized behaviors like individual expression and sometimes cooperation but did not require synchronicity or active engagement (Piccolo et al., 2026). One finding of large effect size from this pilot was that improved prosocial behavior scores from pretest to posttest were associated with the communal music engagement group (Piccolo et al., 2026). Yet, behavioral observations did not clarify how cortical activity relates to these findings.

Brain Activity

Focusing on the frontal lobe, several cortical areas may activate in relation to cognitive control (which supports emotion regulation or compliance with social norms), functions that research suggests relate to prosocial behavior (Decety & Holvoet, 2021; Orui et al., 2025). Of particular interest are Brodmann Area 10 (BA10) and BA46; two areas that may relate to prosocial behavior. Starting with the latter, the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (dlPFC), which includes BA46, is implicated in cognitive control, executive functioning, and altruistic decisionmaking (Decety & Holvoet, 2021; Orui et al., 2025; Rhoads et al., 2021). Additionally, some researchers indicate BA46's role in cognitive control and working memory (Ardila et al., 2016; Petrides, 2005), which may be relevant when given a music-listening task. One functional Magnetic Resonance Imaging (fMRI) study found that increased music exposure was related to activity in the dlPFC, including BA46 (Green et al., 2012), which researchers suggest relates to working memory and episodic memory retrieval. Other researchers have indicated left lateralization when observing social approach behaviors (Orui et al., 2025), and BA46 sits on the lateral sides of the brain. It is possible that episodic memory retrieval during music listening may call to mind episodes of social interactions with others that occurred while that music was heard.

Moving anteriorly, we next examine BA10. The frontopolar cortex (FPC), which includes the anterior Superior and anterior Middle Frontal Gyri (SFG and MFG, respectively) and generally relates to BA10, also plays an important role in executive function (Burgess & Wu, 2013; Koessler et al., 2009). Researchers have found relationships between BA10 and multitasking, goal tracking, and social cognition, including Theory of Mind (Roca et al., 2011). Additionally, researchers suggest the FPC is involved in social norm compliance, which plays a role in cooperation (Zoh et al., 2022). While more cortical regions may be implicated in these cognitive functions, we focus on these few as we consider the accessibility of measurement.

Functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) uses absorption and reflection of nearinfrared light waves in cortical tissue to assess hemodynamic response (Almajidy et al., 2020).

Light waves are absorbed by hemoglobin at different wavelengths depending on whether it is oxygenated (HbO₂) or deoxygenated (HbR; Almajidy et al., 2020), allowing the detection of relative temporal variations of these chromophores. It is non-invasive, arguably lower-cost and portable, and offers better temporal resolution than some other imaging techniques (Almajidy et al., 2020), facilitating experimentation with children. However, few randomized controlled trials have been conducted with children on communal MBIs, with the goal of understanding not only the relationship with prosocial behaviors but also how it may manifest in cortical activity. Recent research has identified a special association between communal music and affect change (Piccolo et al., 2025). However, these experiments focused on the immediate effects of communal music exposure on affect and did not examine longitudinal changes in neural responses following an MBI.

Present Study

We aimed to answer questions about how cortical responses of preschool-aged children differ to communal music across pre-and posttest and across groups and to investigate correlations between communal music response and prosocial behavior change, preregistered with the Open Science Framework. Given that BA10 and BA46 were implicated in various aspects of executive function and social cognition, we hypothesized that H1) cortical activity in these BAs would differ between pre-and posttest for the communal music engagement group.

Additionally, given the left lateralization found in the literature on some aspects of social cognition (Decety & Holvoet, 2021; Orui et al., 2025), we hypothesized that H2) differences between pre-and posttest for the communal music engagement group would appear in the left BA46 as a region of interest. Looking across groups, we hypothesized that H3) cortical activity in BA10 and BA46 would differ between the communal music engagement and control groups at posttest, and H4) differences in cortical activity between the communal music engagement and control groups would appear in the left BA46 region of interest. Finally, H5) changes in cortical activity in BA10 and BA46 would correlate with changes in prosocial behavior. Given that the study is a pilot, we also conducted exploratory analyses across all available channels to validate the study design and generate hypotheses for a future, more robust study.

Methods

Participants

The original study was conducted in two locations in Brazil: Universidade Federal do 1). The objective was to cover selected regions of the frontal cortex, including Brodmann Area 6 (BA6), BA8, BA9, BA10, and BA46, encompassing anterior dorsal and lateral regions (Fig. 2).

These areas overlap with portions of the SFG, which spans parts of BA6, BA8, BA9, and BA10, and parts of the MFG, which includes parts of BA6, BA8, BA9, BA10, and BA46 (Koessler et al., 2009). Subcortical and ventral regions were not accessible with the fNIRS setup.

The fNIRS caps were fitted to the children and contained eight pairs of optodes (sources and detectors), which created 28 channels (20 long-distance and 8 short-distance). The room had only artificial lighting, and a black cap was used on top of the optodes to protect against environmental light interference.

Prosocial Behavior

Prosocial behavior was measured using the Strengths and Difficulties Questionnaire (SDQ; Goodman, 1997). The SDQ was validated in Brazil by Fleitlich and colleagues (2000).

Cronbach's alpha for the Portuguese version was 0.80, and validity was adequate (Woerner et al., 2004). This study focused on the five-item prosocial subscale of the SDQ. Internal consistency for the subscale at pretest was questionable (alpha = 0.63), but was acceptable at posttest (alpha = 0.73). Items were rated on a three-point Likert scale from 0 to 2, where 0 represents not true, and 2 represents certainly true (Goodman, 1997). The prosocial subscale includes the following items: "considerate of other people's feelings", "shares readily with other children (treats, toys, pencils, etc.)", "helpful if someone is hurt, upset or feeling ill", "kind to younger children", and "often volunteers to help others (parents, teachers, other children)" (Goodman, 1997). Parents completed this instrument and other pre-and posttest instruments. For more on the other measures from the original study, which are not a focus of this study, see Piccolo and colleagues (2026).

Music Stimuli

During this pre-and posttest period, the children were also exposed to three types of music, some of which would be used during the intervention itself: unfamiliar (songs they had never heard before, some created explicitly for this study), familiar music (music they had heard before), and communal music (music familiar to them prior to the test, but theoretically became communal for one condition after the intervention; Piccolo et al., 2025). The pretest unfamiliar, posttest unfamiliar, familiar, and communal music stimuli were recorded by the research team at UMFS for this study. This allowed the experimenters to maintain consistent tones and styles across all music types. There were two songs representing each type of music: familiar (F1, O Sapo Não Lava o Pé and F2, Borboletinha, from popular children's repertoire), communal (C1, História da Serpente and C2, Peixinhos do Mar), pretest unknown (U1, Jennie-Mamma and U2, Jimba-Jimba), and posttest unknown (U1, Parlenda and U2, Vagalumes). Three random sequences were generated (Sequence A: C1, U1, F1, U2, C2, F2, F1, U1, C1, U2, F2, C2; Sequence B: U1, C1, F1, C2, U2, F2, C1, F1, U1, C2, F2, U2; and Sequence C: F1, U1, C1, U2, F2, C2, F1, U1, C2, C1, U2, F2), and each participant was randomly assigned a sequence. The same sequence was maintained at pre-and posttest. Music files can be found in the Supplemental Materials.

Procedures

Prior to the intervention, children in the study began with a desensitization procedure and were given an opportunity to habituate to the fNIRS cap. First, the children spent time playing in a room (see Fig. S1 in Supplemental Materials). Bath caps with wires attached were placed on the child while they continued to play to help habituate them to the process for approximately five minutes. Following the habituation procedure, a child and their parents were taken into a different room for the actual fNIRS data collection, where the child donned the fNIRS cap. The child was allowed to sit on their parents' laps, and both the parent and the child were asked to be as still as possible.

The children listened to the three types of music, with fNIRS caps on during pre-and posttest periods. Headphones were not used to reduce disruption with the fNIRS caps. For data collection, six excerpts were played, 30 seconds each, in one of the three sequences, maintained during pre-and posttest, with each song ending in a fade-out. The sequences were played twice during pre-and posttest. There was no pause between the songs to minimize time on task. During the music listening, an abstract neutral visual, developed by Inscapes, was displayed without audio to help focus the children without being distracting (Vanderwal et al., 2015). During the posttest period, the data collection process was repeated without the habituation period.

The intervention consisted of children being randomly assigned to either a visual art control group (n = 17) or a communal music engagement group (n = 20). The program included 10 sessions, each 45 minutes long, delivered over 5 (UFABC) or 10 (UFMS) weeks. Pretest and posttest data were collected at UFMS during the week immediately before the intervention sessions and the week immediately after. The communal music engagement condition group focused on music-related activities, including communal music-making (singing certain songs together), while the visual art control group created visual art through drawing, painting, and other means. The music sung together in the communal music engagement condition served as background music for the visual art control group. For more details on the intervention procedures, see Piccolo and colleagues (2026).

Analyses fNIRS Data Preprocessing and General Linear Model (GLM) Analysis

Preprocessing was completed in early 2025, initially using Satori software (Brain Innovation, 2025). Raw optical intensity measurements obtained from the detectors were converted into changes in oxygenated (HbO₂) and deoxygenated hemoglobin (HbR) concentrations using the modified Beer-Lambert law (Boas et al., 2001; Delpy et al., 1988). To improve signal quality and minimize unwanted fluctuations, two filtering procedures were applied: a high-pass Butterworth filter with a cutoff frequency of 0.01 Hz and a low-pass filter based on Gaussian smoothing with a cutoff frequency of 0.40 Hz. The high-pass filter was used to attenuate low-frequency components, such as slow signal drifts and physiological fluctuations, whereas Gaussian smoothing was applied to reduce high-frequency noise and rapid signal variations (Pinti et al., 2019).

Following data preprocessing, a General Linear Model (GLM; Greene, 2012) was modeled for each channel using Satori software (Brain Innovation, 2025) to investigate changes in oxygenated (HbO₂) and deoxygenated (HbR) hemoglobin concentrations across the different experimental conditions throughout the task. The model was fitted individually for each participant to estimate the hemodynamic response associated with each condition (Huppert et al., 2009). The dependent variable of the GLM was the observed hemodynamic signals, and the regressors were the experimental conditions convolved with the canonical two-gamma hemodynamic response function. The baseline condition with which the others were contrasted was the unfamiliar music. In other words, the betas from the GLM refer to the listening to familiar and communal music activations, considering the unfamiliar one as the baseline.

Subsequently, after model fitting, beta coefficients (β) were extracted for each channel and participant. These coefficients represent the estimated magnitude of the hemodynamic response associated with each experimental condition and were subsequently used for the statistical analyses as described below.

Statistical Analyses

Given the small sample sizes, normality was not assumed, and tests were run using nonparametric methods, and sometimes parametric ones for exploration. Data were loaded, and participants with missing fNIRS data were removed (n = 2). This left 35 participants for the fNIRS analyses. Both site and condition data points were coded from strings to either -1 or 1.

UFABC was coded as -1. The communal music engagement condition was coded as -1. Deltas were calculated by subtracting pretest from posttest values across all fNIRS channels for both HbO₂ and HbR signals. Separate total prosocial scores were generated from pre-and posttest data by summing the prosocial scale items at those time points. A prosocial delta was generated by subtracting the pretest from the posttest.

Several pre-analysis checks were conducted to assess data contamination from systemic artifacts. Short-distance channels were assessed using an ANCOVA and bootstrapped resampling to determine whether pretest scores on those short-distance channels could predict posttest scores. Any significant results may indicate feedback from the cap that could affect the longdistance channel results. In addition, correlations between the deltas (posttest minus pretest) of HbO₂ and HbR signals were checked. It was expected that correlations would be negative, as a positive correlation would indicate that the two hemodynamic signals were moving in the same direction, which is generally considered unlikely.

Hypotheses were analyzed using parametric and non-parametric methods. Linear regressions provided an efficient, initial exploration of trends. Due to the small sample sizes, bootstrap resampling methods were used as the main form of analysis, as they are robust to nonnormality. Depending on the hypothesis, the analyses accounted for factors such as time (through pre-post differences or baseline adjustment), experimental condition (visual art control or communal music engagement), and site (the location where children participated in the program). This study, which includes the hypotheses and the analysis plan, was preregistered with the Open Science Framework (see https://osf.io/kah5s/overview). Departures from preregistration, along with rationale, are described in the Supplemental Materials.

A priori Hypothesis Analyses. H1, H3, and H5 focused on BA10 and BA46. This included both hemodynamic signals, HbO₂ and HbR. BA10 overlaps with the following channels: S2D3, S5D3, S5D6, S8D6, and partially overlaps with S2D1, S3D3, S5D4, S6D6, and S8D7. BA46 partially overlaps with S1D1, S2D1, S3D1, S6D7, S7D7, and S8D7 (see Fig. 1 and Fig. 2). For H1, data were filtered to the communal music engagement condition with complete indirect measures of cortical activity (n = 17). A linear regression was conducted on the delta for each combination of hemodynamic signal and channel, with site as a covariate. Bootstrapped analyses were performed with the same linear model. H2 used the same analysis pattern as H1.

However, analyses were performed using an ROI representing left BA46. The BA46 ROI was created by summing the beta values of the channels that overlap left BA46 (S1D1, S2D1, and S3D1). In H3, an ANCOVA was used to test whether posttest signals could be predicted based on condition and included both pretest signals and site as covariates. A separate ANCOVA was conducted for each hemodynamic signal and channel combination. A similar model was fit using linear regression with bootstrap resampling. H4 followed the same pattern as H3, with the BA46 ROI described above. H5 was analyzed using a Spearman correlation for participants with complete fNIRS and prosocial behavior scores (n = 28).

Exploratory Analyses.

Given the small sample size and pilot nature of this study, the same analyses for H1, H3, and H5 were completed across the remaining channels and both hemodynamic signals for exploratory purposes and to support future hypothesis generation. This includes the following channels: S1D2, S3D4, S4D2, S4D4, S4D5, S6D4, and S7D5 (see Fig. 1).

Results were also reviewed at p < 0.1 to support future hypothesis generation. R version 4.5.3 (2026-03-11 ucrt) was used for the analysis. Linear regressions used the lm() function or the more specific aov() for ANCOVAs (R Core Team, 2026). Correlations were tested using cor.test (R Core Team, 2026). Parametric tests and correlations were applied with p < 0.05 to define significance and were corrected via Benjamini-Hochberg tests, using the p.adjust function (R Core Team, 2026). Bootstrap resampling used the boot and bootci functions (Canty & Ripley, 2025; R Core Team, 2026). All bootstrapped analyses used 2000 resamples and started with a seed set to 123. Bootstrap resampling used 95% confidence intervals with the biascorrected and accelerated (BCa) method to define significance and was corrected by using a stricter 99.85% confidence interval in H1 and H3. H2 and H4 used a 99.17% confidence interval based on the number of comparisons.

Results

Across all analyses, given the small sample size, results should be interpreted with caution. Data checking for contamination by systemic artifacts for the short-distance channels showed no significant results. Any significant result may indicate that systemic artifacts could affect the long-distance channel results. Bootstrap resampling did not yield any significant results. No positive correlations between HbR and HbO₂ deltas were statistically significant.

Given these results, the data were considered adequate for further analysis. Significant results from all exploratory parametric tests can be found in the Supplemental Materials, Tables S1-S3.

Discussion

Research suggests that MBIs may offer opportunities to improve mental health. This study aimed to explore the relationship between changes in prosocial behavior and superficial frontal brain activity for children in a pilot communal MBI randomized controlled trial. Data analyses revealed changes in cortical responses to communal music over time within the communal music engagement group, particularly in channels overlapping BA10. However, within-group differences did not remain statistically significant after correction for multiple comparisons. This was seen in differences in indirect measures of cortical activity before and after the intervention for participants in the communal music engagement group, when listening to communal music. Significant results were found in BA10 and BA46 (H1), including the left BA46 ROI (H2), and changes in two channels in BA10 survived correction. One possible explanation is that the changes detected in these areas may relate to focus, that is, executive function and cognitive control in the children, as they recognized the music posttest in a way they had not prior to the test. That said, given that several weeks had passed and other changes in the children's lives and the school year may have occurred, these could have likewise resulted in changes in these areas.

Between-group differences were no longer statistically significant after correction for multiple comparisons. Indirect measures of cortical activity in the communal music engagement group posttest initially appeared to differ from the visual art control group while listening to communal music, where unfamiliar music set the baseline, in a few channels across BA10 and BA46 (H3). This might suggest that areas of executive and cognitive control in children in the communal music engagement intervention are related differently to communal music than those in the visual arts intervention. Prior studies suggest that people relate to communal music in special ways; for example, it can improve affect after negative experiences (Piccolo et al., 2025).

The differences seen with fNIRS may reflect another mechanism to detect relationships with communal music, and further study is needed to explore this hypothesis.

Although correlation analyses between changes in cortical activity and prosocial behavior showed no differences in BA10 and BA46 (H5), exploratory analyses revealed an uncorrected significant channel in BA9, suggesting communal music engagement may relate to changes in prosocial behavior, and that these changes may be detectable in indirect measures of cortical activity in this area. In fact, this channel in BA9 also showed an uncorrected significant finding in the between-groups exploratory analysis (H3). Stated differently, the HbR S3D4 channel had uncorrected significant findings in two key analyses. First, the intervention group (communal music engagement group or visual arts group) predicted posttest cortical activity in HbR S3D4 while listening to communal music, controlling for pretest cortical activity and site. Additionally, when listening to communal music, changes in cortical activity in HbR S3D4 correlated with changes in prosocial behavior scores. Taken together, these results hint at the possibility that group differences in prosocial behavior change may be reflected in cortical activity when listening to communal music. BA9 relates to the dlPFC, and research suggests it may be implicated in altruism (Orui et al., 2025), further hinting at a connection between these findings.

Other studies have linked communal music listening to affect change, even when the communal music experience itself was not recent (Piccolo et al., 2025), although no brain responses were investigated in that study. Here, the results may point toward communal music listening and activation of cortical areas that may make prosocial behavior more likely. That said, BA9 and the dlPFC have also been indicated in studies on memory retrieval (Green et al., 2012), which could suggest another explanation for some of the changes. Group effects may be explained by stronger memories associated with the communal music. That is, the communal music engagement group may have stronger or more vivid memories of these songs, which may explain the group effects seen in the exploratory H3 HbR S3D4 findings. Further study may help clarify these results.

Some of the inconclusive results may be explained by the study's limitations. The sample size was small, as this was a pilot study, and a larger study population would be needed for generalizability. fNIRS may not offer the detailed depth of other methods (only 1-3 cm), and caution is needed, as sweat and hair can obstruct the optical nodes and disrupt measurement (Almajidy et al., 2020). This may have impacted this study, as positive correlations were found between the deltas of the hemodynamic signals on five long-distance channels, though none were statistically significant. That said, four of the channels were all located in close proximity on the anterior right of the head and could indicate a poor-fitting fNIRS cap or some other disturbance with the optodes in this area. Several differences did not survive corrections for multiple comparisons, and thus further investigation is needed. For more on the limitations of the larger pilot study, see Piccolo and colleagues (2026).

This pilot provides data for future hypothesis generation while corroborating the direction of the initial pilot. These results suggest value in further investigation. The pilot showed that some changes could be detected, and the breadth of the analyses and the small sample size warrant larger studies. Further studies with a larger sample size could provide further evidence of changes in the brain and further substantiate correlations. Prosocial behavior was measured only through parental reports before and after the interventions. Future studies may include opportunities to measure prosocial behavior during the sessions themselves. Cortical activity in response to the music was indirectly measured pre-and posttest, but it remains unclear how long these changes may last. Follow-up studies may include measurement of cortical activity sometime after the study has concluded to understand the duration of lasting effects. Prior research indicates several mechanisms that may influence prosocial behavior development during communal MBIs, such as synchronicity (Savage et al., 2021; Tarr et al., 2014). Future studies can explore this link. Future studies with larger samples, repeated cortical and behavioral assessments, and designs aimed at investigating potential mediating processes may help clarify the mechanisms linking communal music engagement to prosocial development.

Conclusion

Communal MBIs can provide cost-effective means to support mental health for participants and may even provide benefits such as opportunities to connect to the arts and social connectivity (Golden et al., 2021; Piccolo et al., 2025). Developing interventions that also support improvements in prosocial behavior can benefit individuals and society alike. Prosocial behaviors are linked to positive individual outcomes while protecting against negative ones (Memmott-Elison et al., 2020). Similarly, some researchers suggest that prosocial societies, which are associated with higher levels of trust, also fare better economically (Bjornskov, 2012; Knack & Keefer, 1997). Understanding the cortical activity that underpins prosocial behaviors can provide benefits of its own. It may advance neuroscience by elucidating the relevant neural mechanisms that explain deficits and inform alternative interventions or therapies for those with brain damage. It may also provide support as an additional form of measurement to augment self-reported prosocial behavior. Additional research can further clarify the interaction among communal music engagement, prosocial behaviors, and brain activity, supporting positive outcomes for individuals and perhaps even society.
