Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. Some differences come from the ground truth: it lacks p-values with a correction name (“p Bonf = .061”), and counts partial eta squared written with its subscript after it (“η 2 p = .05”), “p = .05”, and some table notes and figure legends (“† p < .10, *p < .05”), which this module leaves out, as non-significant p-values. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. Full text

Abstract

The use of computational modeling has facilitated substantial advances in our understanding of how people make decisions. However, previous applications of models rely predominantly on the parameters generated by these models to test for the presence of effects, rather than using models as a means by which to directly test hypotheses derived from theories. As computational modeling becomes ever more vital in the field, developing a framework of practices to catalyze theory building in the decision sciences becomes ever more important.

Here we advance a framework which comprises four practices: axiomatization, explication, identification, and extrapolation. These practices, which inform how computational models are developed and evaluated, offer the potential for the decision sciences to realize the full benefits of computational modeling to hone, refine, and integrate theory.

Introduction

In recent years, computational models have emerged as an analytical workhorse of decision-making research (Konovalov, Hu, & Ruff, 2018; Roberts & Hutcherson, 2019; Tusche & Bas, 2021; Vahed, Galván, & Sanfey, 2024; Wu & O'Doherty, 2025).

By representing the causal structures underlying choice behavior, computational models endeavor to approximate the psychological processes by which we decide between different potential options. Via this approximation, models have allowed for a direct conceptual bridge between choices observed in laboratory experiments and the so-called "black box" of cognition (see Morgan & Morrison, 1999). In other words, computational models enable us to propose, and rigorously assess, theoretical models of the psychological processes which produce specific behavioral outputs from associated external inputs. By virtue of this approach to instantiating and testing theory, the practice of computational modeling has both facilitated theoretical advances in decision-making, as well as promoted open scientific practices (Durstewitz, Koppe, & Toutounji, 2016; Wilson & Collins, 2019; Guest & Martin, 2021; Guest, 2024).

The utility of computational models in the broader cognitive sciences can largely be attributed to their potential to express formal accounts of cognitive processes.

When applied to decision-making, computational models are as diverse as the types of choices we encounter, from low-level perceptual decisions regarding allocation of attention (Peelen, Berlot, & de Lange, 2024), to higher-level processes involving perspective-taking (Krach et al., 2008; Tusche et al., 2016; Konovalov et al., 2021). Here, we focus on decision-making as a behavior arising from considered, intentional, deliberation between multiple choice options, with attendant choices representing the most valued option (Busemeyer & Johnson, 2004; Krpan, Fasolo, & Scheider, 2025). In this context, computational models are particularly valuable because they offer insights into not only how individuals interact with their environments and choice options, but also why decision behavior can differ from person to person, even when encountering the same choice scenario. By mediating between theory and data, computational models can help build explanations that go beyond prediction and correlation (Guest & Martin, 2021; Guest, 2024; Morgan & Morrison, 1999).

Within the decision sciences, the widespread adoption of computational modeling has been a relatively recent phenomenon. Though recent work in this domain has leveraged computational models to advance the development of theories of human decision-making, many have suggested that the benefits that computational modeling can deliver are not being fully reaped (Roberts & Hutcherson, 2019; Eckstein et al., 2022). Because computational models can illuminate latent, implicit, or underdeveloped aspects of theories, they offer great potential for facilitating the development, refinement, and integration of theory (Guest & Martin, 2021). However, many, if not most, current approaches using modeling in the decision sciences have treated computational models as tools to characterize decision-making behavior, which, we believe, reflects a primary use of computational models as "parameter generators". While this has undoubtedly been useful, we believe that research on decision-making can benefit from also adding a focus on proposing, testing, and refining models, which can facilitate the integration of decision theories and produce more informative and generalizable models of decision-making. Here, we propose and outline a four-pillared framework for the use of computational modeling to facilitate theory building in the decision sciences.

A Modeling Framework to Promote Theory Building

The goal of this framework is to promote theory building in the decision sciences by advancing a set of practices which describe how models can be both developed and evaluated. This framework represents somewhat of a departure from the typical practice, particularly with reference to the predominant view of computational models as parameter generators.

The rapid adoption of computational models into the study of decision-making has been, in a large part, facilitated by the assimilation of computational modeling into the philosophy of experimental human behavioral research. Here, experimentalists endeavor to neutralize all additional factors that can potentially influence the decisions that people make, with the exception of the relevant factors of experimental interest (usually no more than three such factors) in order to justify a causal inference (Aronson & Carlsmith, 1968; Friedman & Sunder 1994; Anderson-Cook, 2005; Campbell & Stanley, 2015). The goal of experiments, therefore, has been to identify whether there is a systematic relationship between several orthogonalized predictors and the decisions that people make: to characterize the strength of a relationship between an external factor and choice behavior rather than the decision-making process itself.

Computational modeling in the decision sciences has largely been grafted onto this "effect testing" tradition: models have been used to assess the effects of experimental factors rather than to facilitate testing integrative accounts of how people make decisions (i.e. theories).

Although computational modeling is not constrained by the same statistical limitations and considerations that linear modeling operates under, experiments in the cognitive sciences are often designed to test effects of manipulations on cognition, rather than theories of cognition themselves (van Rooij & Baggio, 2021).

Transplanting this "effect testing" perspective into the computational modeling of decision-making has led to a view of computational models as "parameter generators" -that models are functions to estimate parameters, which are then used to test hypotheses. Importantly, even when experiments are designed to test models rather than effects, theory building is often undermined because models are often either computationally overspecified or because model parameters do not explain the same phenomenon across different paradigms (Roberts & Pashler, 2000; Cooper & Guest, 2014; Palminteri, Wyart, Koechlin, 2017; Eckstein et al., 2022). In either case -that is, whether models are used as "parameter generators" or models are instead "paradigm-bound" -a change in how computational modeling is practiced in the decision sciences can usefully help to build better theories about how people make decisions.

The current work outlines a framework for computational modeling of decisionmaking, which we will illustrate using the example of choices regarding the allocation of resources. Allocation behavior is typically studied in socioeconomic tasks such as the one-shot Dictator Game. The Dictator Game is a dyadic task, wherein one player (the proposer) is given a monetary endowment which they then distribute between themselves and another player (the receiver); this second player has neither agency nor recourse, which means that the first player's decision about how to distribute the money is binding. When played in a standard one-shot format, behavior in the Dictator Game is not constrained by either past history or by strategic considerations for future interactions and, thus, is typically considered to elicit fundamental distributive preferences. Behavior in the Dictator Game is typically studied in an incentive-compatible way -that is, with actual financial consequences to game decisions. Thus, it is economically advantageous to be selfish and, conversely, economically costly to act in a prosocial way. Using this example of resource allocation in the Dictator Game, we will illustrate how our proposed modeling framework can facilitate theory building. This framework involves four complementary principles, namely, axiomatization, explication, identification, and extrapolation (Figure 1).

Axiomatization and explication inform how models are developed, while identification and extrapolation are concerned with how models are evaluated in the service of refining theory. Note.

We propose that computational modeling can pursue theory building in decision science by following the proposed 4 pillared approach. These pillars are not sequential steps, but interconnected practices which, if followed, will best enable the decision sciences to build and refine theory.

Model Development

Axiomatization Clarifies Conceptual Claims

Here we define axioms as theoretical claims about the structure of cognition. In a rudimentary sense, axioms in our framework serve the same purpose as mathematical axioms: they are claims which are initially taken to be true in the service of establishing the logic of a system, from which conclusions about the behavior of that system can be derived. Analogous to mathematical axioms, which define the rules from which theorems are derived, axioms in our framework define the cognitive architecture of the decider from which behavioral predictions are derived. However, unlike sets of mathematical axioms which are not themselves falsifiable (because, together they define the logical fabric of a theoretical system), in our framework, axiom sets can -and in fact should -be falsified.

In computational models of decision-making, axioms thus represent conceptual hypotheses about how people arbitrate between different choice options to select the (subjectively) best one. These hypotheses include the kinds of information people consider when making decisions, how this information is processed in decision-making, and what the basis could be for heterogeneity in choice behavior (i.e. interindividual or contextual). Axiomatization, therefore, is simply the practice of referring to computational models as singular, specific, instances of a more generic set of axioms. Because axioms are theoretically precise but uncommitted to formal operationalization, one single axiom set can be formally instantiated in many different possible ways -in other words, axiom sets are multiply realizable (Guest & Martin, 2023). Axiomatization is a powerful approach to theory building because it maps computational models' claims onto a set of theoretical claims in the service of clarifying the usefulness of those claims for characterizing decision-making.

One particularly revealing example of the one-to-many relationship between axioms and models are the two most influential computational models for describing decision-making in the Dictator Game: Inequity aversion (Fehr & Schmidt, 1999) and ERC Theory (Bolton & Ockenfels, 2000). These contemporaneous models are formally distinct, reflecting different operational hypotheses about how information is processed with respect to choice valuation and decision-making. Crucially however, both models make the same general claims about how people make distributive decisions. These shared axioms are, namely, that (a) distributive choices are subjectively valued, based in how they accord with certain rules, and that (b) people differ in the extent to which they prioritize each rule. Axiomatization shifts the focus of computational modeling from the descriptive accuracy of individual models towards an inference about what an individual set of modeling results means for the broader question of interest. Axiomatization therefore asks "are the claims which a given class of models advances sufficient to characterize how people make decisions in a specific case?"

The axiomatic approach construes models as one-of-many possible instances of a discrete axiom set and, by extension, views model testing (Box 1) as a means by which to test axioms about choice behavior on a case-by-case basis. Although formalized models of resource distribution were not proposed until more than a decade later, the origin of the Dictator Game itself is a consequence of what we define as an axiomatic approach to modeling decision-making (Kahneman, Knetsch, & Thaler, 1986). Earlier work on the Ultimatum Game -a variant in which the receiver has the option to veto the proposers' offer thereby leaving both players with nothing -had found that proposers typically made near-equal divisions, which deviated from the prevailing economic notion of rational selfinterest (Güth, Schmittberger, & Schwarze, 1982). Two differing explanations were put forth to explain the ubiquity of near-equal proposals: either proposers were merely attempting to strategically maximize their own material payoff by making offers that their partners were more likely to accept, or proposers were also considering non-economic motivations such as fairness or equality in addition to their own financial payoff. The Dictator Game was thus proposed as a minimum test of these two competing models of social choice, with empirical data consistently supporting the notion that people consider both material benefit as well as broad social preferences when making these decisions (Camerer, 2018; Engel, 2011; Umer, Kurosaki, & Iwasaki, 2022). With respect to the context of distributive behavior, the axiomatic approach has allowed us to rule out the entire class of models which embrace the axiom of self-interest.

Axiomatization is a powerful approach from a theory-building perspective because it enables the exclusion of entire classes of models which contain properties or aspects that contradict or contravene the axiom. Philosophically, all models are by definition 'wrong' because they are underspecified or vague (Box, 1979; Morgan & Morrison, 1999; Guest & Martin, 2021) and, thus, model testing serves to assess whether the specificity of the model is appropriate to characterize the data; essentially, this answers the questions "Is this fictive simplification sufficiently useful?" and "Is the complexity of this model necessary?". For example, the broad class of models which adhere to the axiom of rational self-interest do not typically usefully characterize how people make decisions in the case of resource distribution though, in some exceptional casessuch as specific patient populations -it does (Krajbich et al., 2009). More to the point, bargaining behavior in a repeated Ultimatum Game shows that rational self-interest is often a useful assumption to operate under (Wells & Rand, 2013; Pulcu & Haruno, 2020). Thus, although an axiomatic approach does facilitate the falsification of entire classes of models, it does not necessarily make the therebyfalsified models obsolete. Rather, axiomatization clarifies the usefulness of a set of axioms for describing choice behavior using case-switch logic, such that individual studies represent one of several cases in which the axioms are tested.

In light of the assumption that "all models are wrong, but some are useful" (Box, 1976), axiomatization helps us to clarify under which circumstances a class of models is useful for describing how people make decisions.

Two issues which have emerged in research on human behavior are the seeming exponential growth of research papers and the interpretation and synthesis of contradictory and null findings. By contextualizing these studies as cases defined by a conjunction of factors such as the task paradigm, experimental protocol, and sample characteristics, the axiomatic approach offers an overview or an organizational lens through which to pursue theory building. When applied to cases which are relevant to the study of a given phenomenon, axiomatization imposes a hierarchy which clarifies the extent to which a set of broad hypotheses usefully characterize observed decision data for said phenomena in each case, thus providing "conceptual clarity" (Cushman, 2024) or in Guest's (2024) terms, metatheoretical commitment and empirical interface. In this regard, the axiomatic approach is intended to make metatheoretical commitments and empirical interfaces explicit beyond the level of single hypotheses or single theories. By construing existing findings as cases to be explained by a defined set of explanatory mechanisms, axiomatization can lead to the development of more robust and powerful theories of decision-making (Guest, 2024).

Explication Facilitates Integration

In principle, computational models of decision-making function to reify verbal or conceptual models which often describe how features in the environment are encoded, represented, and mechanistically processed in order to produce the decisions that people make. Consequently, one often noted advantage of computational modeling is its potential to bridge the gap between artificiallycontrolled laboratory environments and the complex ecology or human ethology in which people actually make the decisions we seek to understand (Calder et al., 2018; Guest & Martin, 2021; Carvalho & Lampinen, 2025). Explication is a key practice which enables the realization of this goal; it is the bidirectional process encompassed by specification and abduction. Specification is the transcription of a theory into a formal model (Morgan & Morrison, 1999; Guest & Martin, 2021). Regardless of the extent to which a model purports to characterize the information processing mechanisms underlying choice behavior, a theory about how such processing proceeds is a prerequisite for the development of such models. For instance, although few (if any) modelers who developed utility models of distributive social choice -such as inequity aversion and ERC Theory -putatively acknowledge it, the formal specification of these models reflect a claim about cognition. This is, namely, the intuitive notion that decision-making in such scenarios cognitively unfolds via (1) the quantification of multiple attributes of each choice according to various rules, (2) the subjective weighting of these attribute values based on personal preference, (3) the combination of these attribute values into a single scalar value per choice (i.e. utility or subjective value), and (4) the selection of the choice with the highest utility. The transcription of this four-step conceptual model into a computational model reflects that the development of such models requires theory, de facto.

Explication builds upon specification, as a unidirectional practice, by considering the converse: abduction. Abduction is the inference of cognitive claims from the formal specification of a computational model (Park, 2016). As implied above, the specification of computational models often does not explicitly seek to make claims about the auxiliary processes which ultimately enable choice valuation and selection (i.e., the encoding of perceptual information, choice identification, metacognition).

However, the formal specification of models logically entails answers to these questions which can be inferred. In these two cases (and often) we can, for example, infer the modelers' claim of veridical perception because inputs to the choice arbitration function (i.e., a utility function) match the actual experimental stimuli (i.e., are not numerically transformed, stochastic, etc.). This claim is both biologically implausible and demonstrably false (Beck, Latham, & Pouget, 2011; Louie, Khaw, & Glimcher, 2013; Coen-Cagli & Solomon, 2019) though the extent to which this inaccuracy situationally "matters" depends on whether specific experimental paradigm elicits variability in choice behavior which can only be explained by more realistic models of perception. Thus, explication forces the modeler to explicitly consider what theory or hypothesis the model should test and to engage with the logical implications for cognition that are entailed herein.

We will conclude this section by outlining the current typical use of computational modeling in decision-making, and argue for the importance and necessity of a shift in perspective with regard to its role in experimental decisionmaking research.

The view of models as "parameter generators" has facilitated the widespread use of computational modeling as a tool in decision-making research, which has undoubtedly aided in the building and refinement of theory. By lowering the barrier to entry, this perspective has enabled researchers to conduct the same experiments they would have otherwise conducted but allow for the derivation of more psychologically meaningful conclusions (i.e., rather than X condition impacts Y behavior, modeling can show that X condition impacts Y behavior by changing Z preference). It has also aided work aiming to axiomatize choice arbitration (i.e., Engel, 2011) by providing cases from which to glean the conditional usefulness of various model classes. As we will see, this perspective can also enable these models to be evaluated in line with the principles of identification and extrapolation. The parameter-fitting perspective is, however, incompatible with explication within the decision sciences: explication, with its attendant benefits, can only be pursued under two conditions. First, computational models must be used to express verbal and conceptual theories which relate environmental inputs to behavioral outputs (i.e., Chen & Fischbacher, 2020; Teoh et al., 2020); such verbal and conceptual theories can sometimes be inexact or underspecified, and explication can aid in revealing or clarifying what needs to be further developed (Guest & Martin, 2021 , 2023; Guest, 2024). Second, experiments must be designed to test models rather than effects (i.e., Cappelan et al., 2007; Chen & Li, 2009; van Baar, Chang, & Sanfey, 2019). With the possibility to unify and integrate models of various cognitive processes which underlie decision-making, explication offers the promise to both facilitate theory building and approach ecological realism.

Model Evaluation

Identification Substantiates Mechanisms

Of the four pillars in our proposed framework, identification is most prevalent in current uses of computational modeling. Identification here refers to the practice of evaluating models in terms of the weight of evidence for the model's mechanistic implementation, rather than the model's explanatory ability alone.

Computational modeling can be used to test hypotheses about which criteria are used to evaluate potential choice options, and where variation in choice behavior arises. With this definition in mind, the goal of identification can thus be understood to independently corroborate claims about how choice options are represented and arbitrated between, and why decision tendencies might vary from context-to-context or person-to-person. An important caveat herein is that assessing models via identification is not typically done to demonstrate that aspects of the computational model have a demonstrable mechanism per se.

Rather, the goal is to establish that the model's claims are substantiated: that is, that there is an empirical basis in reality (but see Guest & Martin, 2023). Broadly, identification proceeds by either establishing evidence in support of a computational model's implications for either structure or function.

The practice of identification has clear import with respect to models' claims about structural factors underlying decision-making: one forthcoming example of this is recent work on Moral Preferences Theory in the context of redistribution (Galván & Sanfey, 2026). Moral Preferences Theory is based on the two axioms previously mentioned: that there are multiple moral principles which guide behavior, and that interindividual heterogeneity dictates the prioritization of these principles. While demonstrating that these two axioms empirically capture behavior is important, the verisimilitude of these axioms begs the question "Where do these different dispositional values originate from?". This questionwhich pertains to structure as it assesses which intrinsic, static factors underlie redistributive behavior -was addressed by employing a variety of methodological and analytical approaches (Galván & Sanfey, 2026; Tsilogianni, Galván, & Sanfey, 2026). This work broadly revealed that Moral Preferences Theory's structural claim was buttressed by the practice of identification: these analysis revealed candidate determinants of these preferences in neuroanatomical, trait, demographic, and institutional factors.

In a similar vein, identification is also a valuable practice for corroborating functional claims advanced by computational models. These functional claimswhich often become apparent when explicating models -address how environmental information is represented in the brain (i.e., encoding), how this information is transmitted within the brain (i.e., connectivity), and where this information is integrated culminating in choice selection (i.e., decoding). Evincing these claims, several previous functional neuroimaging studies have suggested that environmental information and choices are encoded in frontotemporal regions (Nakatani et al., 2017; Gao et al., 2018; Vavra, Galván, & Sanfey, 2024), that utility-like signals can be decoded in the ventromedial prefrontal cortex and ventral striatum (Soutschek et al., 2021; Gao et al., 2024), and that there is connectivity from encoding regions to value integration regions during choice (Makwana et al., 2015; Wang et al., 2017). Similarly, prior work has proposed that temporal dynamics also govern these behaviors, with different principles emerging at different points during the decision-making process (Rand, Greene, & Nowak, 2012; Chen & Krajbich, 2018). Computational models, such as drift diffusion models, have been utilized to evidence this claim in behavioral data via model testing, however, the practice of identification is crucial to explicitly and independently corroborate this notion, which prior work has done via attentional and neuroimaging data (Hutcherson, Bushong, & Rangel, 2015; Teoh et al., 2020; Chen et al., 2024).

The practice of identification in the computational modeling of decision-making can promote theory building by clarifying how the mechanisms of the model are implemented (i.e., function) and why interindividual variability arises (i.e., structure). By increasing our knowledge of the neurobiological substrates underpinning choice behavior, identification will enable mapping of behaviorally-grounded theories of decision-making onto neurally-grounded theories of cognition (i.e. Rumelhart & McClelland, 1986; Schulz, Dayan, & Montague, 1997; Marr, 2010; Friston, 2010; Varela, Thomson, & Rosch, 2017; Kriegeskorte & Douglas, 2018; Collins & Shenhav, 2022). Likewise, identification's focus on investigating the psychocultural factors which produce interindividual heterogeneity will connect this knowledge with both psychometrically-grounded theories of individual differences and sociologically-grounded theories of culture (i.e., Weber & Hsee, 2000; DeYoung, 2010; Falk et al., 2018).

Extrapolation Justifies Generalization

In a general sense, there is a twofold function for theory in the cognitive sciences.

Theories are used to integrate a myriad of findings into a coherent account of cognition (see Guest, 2024), and this integrated, coherent, account can be used to predict how people will behave (Palminteri, Wyart, & Koechlin, 2017) but, more importantly, to explain why they behave in a certain way. These predictions are, therefore, forward-inferences: conclusions drawn from a particular data generation process that predict what that process will produce in a given (different) situation. Experiments are, in this regard, conducted with aspirations for generalizability: results inform the development or refinement of larger theories which facilitate this forward-inference in a broader set of situations. The goal of pursuing extrapolation is to simply substantiate the assumption that these claims do, in fact, generalize.

Extrapolation proceeds by evaluating models in terms of their ability to generate accurate and meaningful predictions beyond the situations that these models are explicitly tested in. We identify two primary means of pursuing extrapolation: ecologically (i.e., to a real-world behavior or attitude of interest) and experimentally (i.e., to a novel task condition or paradigm). Although examples of extrapolation in general are to date relatively sparse in the literature on distributive choice, ecological extrapolation is the more prevalent practice.

Ecological extrapolation, to some degree, requires a conceptual map which relates together real-world behaviors of interest, the underlying computational model, and the exhibited experimental behavior. Figure 2 illustrates such a map with respect to the Dictator Game: at a theoretical level, the psychological preferences which social utility models (i.e., Fehr-Schmidt Inequity Aversion, ERC Theory, etc.) capture are hypothesized to govern both (a) behavior within the experimental task itself, and (b) behaviors relevant to distributive fairness in the real world, such as views on governmental redistributive policies. In terms of how computational models are implemented and estimated, pro-social behavior observed in the Dictator Game is the basis for a reverse-inference about the preferences that people have for their own material self-interest versus the material interests of other people. In this situation, ecological extrapolation thus involves evaluating computational models' ability to generate forward-inferences as to the real-world behavior of interest: that differences observed in the lab predict meaningful distinctions about real beliefs or behaviors. A few previous studies have indeed demonstrated this link in the context of distributive choice, for example showing that task-elicited preferences predict views on redistributive policies (Epper et al., 2024; Galván & Sanfey, 2026). Note. The logic of conducting experiments where task behavior is studied is to learn more about a real-world phenomenon of interest -in the case of distributive choice, this might be views towards redistributive policies. With computational modeling, the conceptual map is that psychological preferences motivate both task behavior and policy views. Operationally however, this forward inference does not necessarily apply to both: instead, task behavior is used to make a reverse inference about the psychological preferences (i.e., parameters).

Thus, to substantiate the forward inference in the behavior of interest, ecological extrapolation is necessary.

Experimental extrapolation is a distinctly different endeavor with respect to ecological extrapolation; rather than seeking to assess a model's ability to yield useful inferences beyond the laboratory, experimental extrapolation seeks to assess a model's ability to yield useful inferences in novel experimental settings.

Thus, ecological extrapolation can be construed as testing generalizations from axioms (to the intended phenomenon of interest), while experimental extrapolation can be considered testing generalizations from the formal structure of the model.

Experimental extrapolation can proceed in two ways: the first is by testing the ability of the model to predict behavior in a novel task, given known preferences in a task which studies a similar psychological phenomenon. Here, the benefits of pursing experimental extrapolation is two-fold: firstly, it demonstrates that the claims derived from the model are not "paradigm-bound" but are robust to specific design choices made by the experimenter. From a theory building perspective, the second is benefit is of crucial importance: experimental extrapolation demonstrates that a model can produce forward inferences that are individual-specific and which are usefully predictive in novel contexts. This means, namely, that the biases or preferences that parameters represent are psychologically stable biases or preferences which reliably guide choice behavior (or not, as in Eckstein et al., 2022).

The second way that experimental extrapolation can be pursued is by testing the ability of the model to predict the modulatory effect of a novel treatment condition on decision-making, given known preferences in analogous conditions.

Computational models can be explicated in such a way that they predict how different treatment conditions would modulate a known decision process, such as monetary allocation (e.g., Li & Chen, 2009). This approach uses novel hypothetical conditions as new inputs on a continuous scale, rather than as another discrete case. Experimental extrapolation is the means by which to validate this conditionto-condition generalization, because it explicitly tests the forward-inference that such models offer. That is, given our reverse-inference about the parameters which generated the data observed in conditions A through F, that we know how a given person will behave in condition G (or not, as in Galván et al., 2026).

Using computational models to facilitate theory building in the decision sciences has, to date, been predicated upon the fundamental assumption that models produce results that generalize beyond the situations that they are empirically tested in. Our proposed framework allows for the corroboration of this assumption via the principle of extrapolation. Assessing computational models in terms of their ability to extrapolate from one situation to another -be it from one experimental condition to another or from the laboratory to the real-world -can serve the key function of justifying the generalizations which theories create and which, thus, make them useful.

Conclusion

In the decision sciences, computational models have enabled researchers to directly, and precisely, derive insights into how we decide between different alternatives. Utilizing computational models to extract parameters to compare across experimental conditions has been instrumental for facilitating the wider adoption of computational modeling in the field. However, as utilizing these models is becoming a standard analytical technique for the study of decisionmaking, we believe that computational modeling can be better leveraged to additionally test and refine theory. In the current opinion, we argue for the importance of this goal and outline a framework for achieving this. By embracing the practices of axiomatization, explication, identification, and extrapolation, we believe that computational modeling can play a valuable role in theory building in the decision sciences. This theory building can, in turn, facilitate the development of integrated models which are better able to predict what decisions people will make, and also better explain how these decisions are made.
