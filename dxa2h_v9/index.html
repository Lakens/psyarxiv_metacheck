Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Full text

Abstract

Environmental preferences vary between individuals, yet little is known about how these preferences relate to memory for information encountered in different environments. We investigated this relationship in 60 participants using four virtual environments varying in environmental type (urban vs. natural) and spatial regularity (rhythmic vs. irregular). Participants completed immediate and delayed recall tasks, including two tasks assessing context-supported memory, and reported their general environmental preferences and preferences for the environments they experienced. General preferences for nature and spatial structure were not associated with memory performance. However, contextsupported recall was higher for objects encountered in environments that participants subsequently identified as preferred. These findings highlight the relevance of individual evaluations of specific environments for understanding memory in everyday settings, suggesting that the cognitive implications of environmental design may depend not only on environmental characteristics but also on how individuals experience and evaluate them.

1. Introduction

Research on environment and cognition examines how characteristics of the physical environment relate to cognitive processes and behavior (Berman et al., 2019; Bratman et al., 2015; Djebbara et al., 2025; Fich et al., 2014; Hussein et al., 2026). Among the most extensively studied environmental distinctions is that between natural and urban settings, with research reporting differences in mental health and cognitive outcomes across populations and timescales, as well as in affect, stress, attention, and working memory following brief exposures (Dadvand et al., 2015; Engemann et al., 2020; González-Espinar et al., 2023; Schertz et al., 2022; Sudimac et al., 2022). Some of this research has been informed by Attention Restoration Theory (S. Kaplan, 1995), which proposes that natural environments can support the recovery of directed attention through different mechanisms. However, evidence for this, following nature exposure remains mixed, with outcomes varying across cognitive measures, environmental conditions, and study designs (Huynh et al., 2025b; Trammell et al., 2024). Beyond these empirical inconsistencies, the theoretical mechanisms proposed by Attention Restoration Theory have also been questioned (Joye & De Block, 2026; Joye & Dewitte, 2018; Ohly et al., 2016; Prugger & Kühn, 2026; Stevenson et al., 2018). Such variability has increasingly directed attention toward the specific characteristics of environments rather than their classification into broad categories (Huynh, Hussein, Behzadi, et al., 2026b; Meidenbauer et al., 2020; Schertz & Berman, 2019).

Beyond differences between environments, individuals may also differ in how the same environmental characteristics are perceived, experienced, and evaluated. Environmental responses vary with prior experience and individual characteristics (Coutrot et al., 2022; Kuliga et al., 2019; Newcombe et al., 2022), while the experience and evaluation of natural environments also differ between individuals (Koivisto et al., 2022; Meidenbauer et al., 2019; Tost et al., 2019; Twedt et al., 2019; Wilkie & Delicato, 2026). Consequently, environments sharing similar physical characteristics or belonging to the same environmental category may not be experienced or evaluated equivalently across individuals. Understanding relationships between environments and cognition may, therefore, require consideration not only of environmental characteristics, but also of individual differences.

Environmental preferences may reflect evaluations of multiple environmental characteristics rather than responses to a single objectively defined feature. Research has demonstrated that preferences vary with characteristics of environmental configuration (Coburn et al., 2020; Gregorians et al., 2022; Huang et al., 2018; Weinberger et al., 2021). Kaplan et al. (1989) demonstrated that environmental preference was associated with multiple domains of environmental characteristics, with perception-based attributes, particularly openness and smoothness, providing important predictors. Preferences for natural and built environments may similarly reflect differences in their underlying visual and perceived characteristics (Kardan et al., 2015; Van Den Berg et al., 2016). Fractal structure provides one example, with preferences for patterns that repeat across spatial scales varying according to their complexity and fractal dimension (Hagerhall et al., 2008 (Hagerhall et al., , 2015;; Taylor et al., 2011). More broadly, rhythmic and recurring patterns constitute important dimensions of environmental organization, with potential implications for perception and neural processing (Behzadi et al., 2026; Charalambous & Djebbara, 2023). Environmental preference may, therefore, reflect an integrated evaluation of multiple characteristics rather than a response to any single environmental feature.

Individuals may also evaluate specific environments differently, even when they share a general preference for the same environmental category. Research has shown that preferences for natural environments vary with people's conceptions and associations of nature (Beute & De Kort, 2019; De Groot & Van Den Born, 2003), while Purcell (1987) examined how landscape preferences relate to discrepancies between encountered environments and existing expectations or schemas. Lamb and Purcell (1990) further investigated how vegetation structure relates to perceived naturalness, while Purcell and Lamb (1998) demonstrated that preference varied with vegetation type, structural integrity, foliage density, and extent of view, despite all evaluated scenes containing vegetation. Similarly, Mangone et al. (2021) found differences in preferences across natural biomes, with familiarity and individual nature relatedness accounting for some, but not all, of this variation. Mascherek et al. (2025), additionally, found that although natural environments were generally evaluated more positively than built environments, agreement in individual evaluations was not greater for natural environments. This indicates that broad patterns of environmental preference do not necessarily imply uniform evaluations of specific environments.

Individual characteristics may also shape how particular environments are evaluated, as Tang et al. (2015) demonstrated associations between personal connection to nature and the evaluation of perceptual qualities of natural landscapes. Wilkie and Stavridou (2013) and Wilkie and Clouston (2015) investigated environmental preference-type congruence in relation to perceived restorative potential and restoration outcomes, while Wilkie and Delicato (2026) further examined how place preference and environmental characteristics relate to perceived restoration. These studies, together, suggest that individual environmental preferences may be relevant to how environments are evaluated and experienced beyond their classification as natural or urban, although the observed relationships vary across environmental conditions and outcomes. General environmental preferences and preferences for specific experienced environments may, therefore, represent related but distinct levels of evaluation, with the latter reflecting responses to the configuration and experience of a setting. This distinction aligns with calls for more specific characterization of environmental exposures and experiences beyond broad environmental classifications (Huynh, Hussein, Fich, et al., 2026a; Kühn et al., 2023; Mascherek et al., 2026; Sander et al., 2024; Zähme et al., 2026).

Beyond the characteristics of an environment, individuals' evaluations of their surroundings may also be associated with cognitive performance. Joye et al. (2022) proposed that the aesthetic qualities of natural environments may contribute to cognitive benefits through motivational processes rather than necessarily through attentional restoration, while subsequent experimental work examined the natureas-reward hypothesis in relation to task performance (Joye et al., 2024). Although these studies do not directly address episodic memory, they suggest that positive evaluations of environmental stimuli may be relevant to cognition. More directly, research has linked subjective evaluation to memory for spatial information in which Babo-Rebelo et al. (2022) found that aesthetic experience was associated with enhanced spatial representations of artworks encountered in a virtual exhibition. Momenzadeh et al. (2026), similarly, found that preference for architectural interiors predicted delayed incidental recognition memory, with preference accounting for memory differences beyond environmental distinctiveness. Preference and memory may, therefore, be associated, but that their relationship is not necessarily uniform across memory outcomes.

Episodic memory involves not only information about an event itself, but also the spatial and temporal circumstances in which it was experienced (Bellmund et al., 2018; Huynh, Hussein, Fich, et al., 2026b; Sugar & Moser, 2019). Environmental context can contribute to remembering when aspects of the original experience are encountered again during retrieval (Bramão et al., 2017; Robin & Moscovitch, 2014), while spatial characteristics and ongoing interactions with architectural environments can shape how places are experienced and remembered (Djebbara et al., 2019 (Djebbara et al., , 2021;; He et al., 2019; Huynh et al., 2025a; Iggena et al., 2023). However, whether individuals' preferences for specific environments are associated with memory for information encountered within them, and whether this relationship differs across retrieval conditions, remains unclear.

2. Methods

>Methods

2.1 Study paradigm

The present study examined associations between environmental preferences and episodic memory performance differing in environmental type (natural vs. urban) and spatial organization (rhythmic vs. irregular). The study, thereby, employed a 2 × 2 within-subject design in which participants experienced four environments. We considered environmental preference at two levels of specificity. First, we examined whether general preference for natural environments was associated with memory performance across natural and urban environments, and preference for spatial structure versus variation was associated with memory performance across rhythmic and irregular environments. We then examined whether memory performance differed according to participants' preferences for the specific environments they had experienced. Episodic memory was assessed through Immediate and Delayed Free Recall and two context-supported Guided Recall tasks, allowing us to investigate whether preference-memory associations varied across retrieval conditions. Particular attention was given to the Guided Recall tasks, which involved retrieving object-environment associations in complementary directions, to examine whether environmental preference was associated with memory when retrieval involved the original environmental context.

All participants completed each experimental condition, with exposure order counterbalanced across participants. The experiment was conducted in virtual reality (VR), allowing participants to physically move and explore the environments. All the environments were matched in overall spatial dimensions, while their visual composition and spatial organization varied according to the experimental manipulations (Figure 1). Rhythmic environments were characterized by regular, symmetrical arrangements of environmental elements, whereas irregular environments contained greater variation in element orientation and placement. In the urban-irregular environment, this variation also extended to the orientation of surrounding buildings. The terms rhythmic and irregular, thus, describe the experimentally manipulated spatial organization rather than participants' subjective evaluations of the environments. Experimental objects occupied identical spatial locations across all four environments, ensuring that object position was held constant across conditions. At the beginning of each condition, participants freely explored the environment for one minute before completing an object-search task. Using a handheld VR controller, participants searched for eight objects distributed throughout the environment and interacted with each by pointing at and selecting it with the controller (Figure 2). The objects within each environment belonged to different semantic categories to minimize semantic associations between them. After locating all eight objects, participants remained in the same environment and completed an Immediate Recall task, recalling as many of the encountered objects as possible. This procedure was repeated across all four environments, with oneminute breaks between conditions. Following the encoding and Immediate Recall procedures across all four environments, participants completed a series of additional memory tasks. First, in a neutral virtual environment, they completed a Free Recall task, recalling as many objects as possible across all conditions, irrespective of their original environmental context. Participants then completed two context-supported recall tasks. In Guided Recall 1, they returned to each experimental environment and recalled the objects previously encountered there. In Guided Recall 2, previously encountered objects were presented individually, and participants identified the environment in which each object had appeared. One-minute breaks separated the memory tasks. Following a 30-minute break which was used to fill out the environmental preference questionnaire, participants completed Delayed Recall task, recalling objects from all four conditions irrespective of their original environmental context.

2.2 Environmental preference measures and statistical analysis

General environmental preference captured participants' preference for natural vs. urban environments. Preference for spatial organization assessed the extent to which participants preferred orderly and structured environments vs. environments characterized by variation and spontaneity, reflecting the regularity dimension manipulated in the experiment. Participants were also asked to select which of the four experimental environments they preferred overall and, separately, which they would prefer for concentration and learning. For these measures, observations from the selected environment were coded as preferred and observations from the remaining three environments as non-preferred, allowing memory performance in the subsequently preferred environment to be compared with performance in the other environments. Overall environmental preference constituted the primary preference measure. Participants additionally reported the perceived complexity of their childhood environment to capture previous environmental experience. Perceived safety was assessed but, due to limited variability, was included only in supplementary analyses (Figure 3). The illustrations are schematic representations intended to convey the spatial organization and underlying principles of the experimental conditions rather than their exact visual appearance. Participant measures captured general preferences, preferences for the specific environments experienced, and environmental background. Preferredenvironment status indicated whether memory performance corresponded to the environment subsequently selected as preferred. Memory outcomes included free recall, in which objects were recalled without explicit support from the environmental context, and context-supported recall, in which retrieval required objectenvironment associations.

The statistical analyses examined whether memory performance was associated with environmental preference while accounting for environmental condition, presentation order, and repeated observations within participants. Linear mixed-effects models were used as the primary analytical approach, with participant included as a random effect. The main outcome was a context-supported recall index, constructed to capture memory performance when retrieval explicitly depended on the relation between objects and their environmental context. Guided Recall 1 required participants to recall objects within their original environment, whereas Guided Recall 2 required participants to identify the original environment associated with each object. Although the tasks differed in retrieval direction, both therefore depended on object-environment associations. To provide a common measure of contextsupported recall, Guided Recall 1 and Guided Recall 2 were z-scored across observations and averaged, with higher values indicating stronger context-supported recall.

The main predictor of interest was preferred-environment status, indicating whether an observation came from the environment that the participant subsequently identified as their preferred environment. Preferred-environment status was coded separately for each observation by comparing the current virtual environment with each participant's selected preferred environment. Each participant therefore contributed one preferred-environment observation and three non-preferred-environment observations. 2.3 Participants

Sixty participants (f = 33) were recruited from the local university community through social media and university recruitment portals. Participants had a mean age of 24.15 years (SD = 1.96, range = 21-30 years). All participants had normal or corrected-to-normal vision and reported no known psychological diagnoses. Participants received financial compensation for their participation. All experimental sessions were conducted in the afternoon to maintain consistency in the timing of testing and environmental light exposure. The study received ethical approval from the university's ethical review board and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (World Medical Association, 2025). Participants received written information about the study via email before participation, including information concerning the study procedures, potential risks, benefits, and data handling. Participation was voluntary, and participants were informed of their right to withdraw at anytime. Data were treated confidentially and stored securely in accordance with the approved data-management procedures.

4. Discussion

>Discussion

4.1 Specific environmental preference is associated with memory

The present findings suggest that context-supported recall was higher in environments that participants subsequently identified as preferred, whereas general preferences for nature and spatial structure were not associated with memory performance. This association remained evident after accounting for the experimentally manipulated environmental characteristics and presentation order. The findings suggest that evaluations of specific environmental experiences may be more informative about memory performance.

The present findings extend previous evidence linking environmental evaluation and memory by distinguishing between what is remembered and the conditions under which remembering takes place. Whereas Babo-Rebelo et al. (2022) examined aesthetic experience in relation to spatial memory, and Momenzadeh et al. (2026) investigated preferences for architectural interiors in relation to incidental recognition memory, the present study examined memory for objects encountered within different environments. Importantly, the association with preference was not evident across all retrieval tasks. Momenzadeh et al. ( 2026) observed an association with delayed, but not immediate, recognition, whereas the present study found the clearest association during context-supported recall. Although the tasks differ, it suggests that the relationship between environmental evaluation and memory may depend on the information being remembered and the conditions under which retrieval takes place. In the present experiment, this was particularly relevant when participants returned to previously encountered environments or identified the environment in which an object had been experienced. Rather than indicating a general memory advantage associated with preferred environments, it suggests that environmental evaluation may be particularly relevant when remembering involves drawing on relationships between encountered objects and their environmental contexts.

The distinction between broad and specific environmental preferences further qualifies these findings.

Although most participants selected one of the natural environments as their preferred setting, general nature preference did not moderate memory responses to natural vs. urban conditions. General preferences for nature may reflect influences extending beyond the immediate characteristics of an environment. Cross-cultural differences in landscape preferences have been documented by Hägerhäll et al. (2018), while Jensen and Olsen (2019) examined the relationship between childhood nature experiences and environmental preferences in adulthood. Similarly, van den Berg (2021) has argued that preferences for natural over built environments, and their restorative effects, may be influenced by learned associations and culturally transmitted expectations. Although the present findings cannot distinguish between these explanations, they illustrate that a general preference for nature does not necessarily correspond to episodic memory performance within natural environments.

Likewise, preference for spatial structure vs. variation did not account for memory responses to rhythmic vs. irregular environments. Thus, a strong group-level preference for natural environments coexisted with individual variation in the specific environment preferred, while broader preference measures provided little information about memory performance. This distinction is consistent with evidence that individual differences in preference extend to particular perceptual characteristics (Cotter et al., 2017). Morton et al. (2017), furthermore, found that cognitive and motivational responses to natural environments depended on whether those environments corresponded with a salient social identity, with benefits emerging when the environment was identity-consistent and reversing when it was not. Although identity congruence differs from environmental preference, it illustrates that responses to an environmental category may depend on the relationship between the individual and the setting. Knowing which of the four experienced environments a participant preferred was, therefore, more informative about context-supported recall than knowing whether they generally preferred nature or a particular form of spatial organization. This does not imply that broad environmental preferences are irrelevant. Rather, preferences measured at different levels of specificity may capture different aspects of environmental evaluation (Huynh, Hussein, Behzadi, et al., 2026b , 2026a), in which a general preference for nature reflects an evaluation of a broad environmental category, whereas preference for a specific environment reflects an evaluation of a particular configuration of characteristics as encountered and experienced by an individual. These levels of preference should, therefore, not necessarily be expected to correspond to the same outcomes.

Importantly, however, environment-specific preference was assessed only after participants had completed the memory tasks. Consequently, the direction of the observed association cannot be established. One possibility is that participants remembered objects more successfully because they experienced a specific environment more positively. Conversely, they may have evaluated an environment more positively because objects encountered within it were easier to remember or because remembering within that environment was more successful. These possibilities are not mutually exclusive. Preferences need not constitute fixed evaluations that precede an experience but may themselves develop through experiences available in memory (Weber & Johnson, 2006). Processing fluency may also contribute to positive evaluations, such that experiences that are easier to process are subsequently evaluated more favorably (Reber et al., 2004). The present findings, therefore, do not demonstrate that environmental preference caused better memory. Instead, they identify an association between subsequent evaluations of specific environments and memory for information encountered within them.

From this perspective, environment-specific preference may be understood as an overall evaluation emerging from the interaction between an individual and a specific setting. Such an evaluation may reflect multiple aspects of the experience, including familiarity, visual engagement, affective responses, perceived distinctiveness, and ease of interaction (Gregorians et al., 2022; Reber et al., 2004; Vartanian et al., 2021). Importantly, positive environmental evaluations do not necessarily correspond to more favorable physiological or cognitive responses. In an exploratory study, Schuller et al. (2026) found that softer, more homely environments were associated with greater physiological arousal, while participants' subjective accounts suggested potential tensions between comfort and the ability to focus. These evaluations may also be shaped by previous environmental experiences, with familiarity influencing responses to different environments (Cipriani et al., 2026), and subjective and physiological responses varying with how environments are experienced (Koivisto & Grassini, 2023 , 2024). Environment-specific preference may, therefore, be informative because it reflects aspects of the relationship between an individual and an environment that are not adequately captured by broad environmental categories or isolated environmental characteristics.

4.2 Environmental Engagement and Memory

The association between environment-specific preference and context-supported recall may also relate to how individuals encounter, explore, and engage with their surroundings. Rather than preference directly benefiting memory, both environmental evaluation and subsequent remembering may be related to aspects of the experience, including attention, affective responses, and engagement. Research has demonstrated that motivational significance can influence memory performance, with effects observed in relation to reward, curiosity, and choice (Adcock et al., 2006; Gruber et al., 2014; Knowlton & Castel, 2022; Murty et al., 2015). Although preference for an environment cannot be equated with these forms of motivational value, this literature provides a basis for considering whether the subjective significance of an environment may be related to how individuals engage with information encountered within it. In this sense, preference may reflect aspects of the individual's ongoing interaction with the environment.

In the present experiment, participants freely navigated the environments while encountering objects, allowing for potential differences in how they explored and attended to their surroundings. Previous research has demonstrated associations between patterns of visual exploration and subsequent memory performance (Celli et al., 2025; Kafkas & Montaldi, 2011), with Alderman et al. (2026) urther showing that the organization of gaze, including refixations and spatial distribution, was informative about scene memory precision. Similarly, studies of evaluative decisions have shown that gaze can become increasingly directed toward subsequently preferred stimuli (Shimojo et al., 2003; Simion & Shimojo, 2006). Although these findings concern different experimental contexts, they suggest that attention and exploration may be relevant to understanding the observed association between environmental preference and memory. The pattern of memory performance provides a further consideration. Although differences were evident in context-supported recall, differences in delayed recall were no longer evident after accounting for earlier memory performance. This suggests that differential retention alone is unlikely to account for the observed pattern. Instead, the association may relate to processes occurring during initial encounters with objects or subsequent retrieval opportunities, both of which can influence later remembering (Pastötter et al., 2011; Uncapher & Rugg, 2005). However, the present study cannot distinguish between these possibilities, nor did it measure gaze behavior or attentional allocation. The literature on attention and exploration, therefore, provides a direction for future investigation rather than an explanation established by the present findings. However, the present study cannot distinguish differences arising during initial learning from those arising during subsequent retrieval, nor did it measure gaze behavior or attentional allocation. These studies, therefore, provide a possible direction for future investigation, rather than an explanation directly supported by the present data, particularly regarding how visual exploration, attention, and object-environment associations may contribute to the relationship between environmental preference and memory.

A further, exploratory finding concerned participants' previous environmental experiences. Childhood environmental complexity was associated with one Guided Recall measure, although this relationship was not consistent across the other Guided Recall task or Delayed Recall. A related association was observed in a separate study of architectural rhythm and episodic memory (Huynh, Fich, & Djebbara, 2026). Although these findings should be interpreted cautiously, they raise the possibility that previous experience with different forms of spatial organization may be relevant to how individuals engage with and remember information in subsequent environments. Research has linked childhood exposure to street-network complexity with adult navigation performance, particularly in environments resembling those experienced during upbringing (Coutrot et al., 2022), while other studies have similarly associated previous spatial experience with navigation abilities (Yavuz, He, et al., 2024; Yavuz, Manley, et al., 2024). Although these findings concern navigation rather than episodic recall, they suggest that previous environmental experience may be relevant to investigating individual differences in environment-memory relationships.

4.3 Environmental context and context-supported retrieval

The association between environment-specific preference and memory was most evident when retrieval involved the environmental context in which objects had been encountered. Whereas preference was not associated with Immediate Recall, it was associated with both Guided Recall tasks, suggesting that the relationship was not uniform across memory outcomes. This distinction is consistent with research demonstrating that the contribution of environmental context to remembering depends on retrieval conditions. Barak et al. (2013) found that reinstating environmental context influenced free and guided recall but not recognition, while Cox et al. (2023) demonstrated that reinstating elements of the original context can facilitate episodic retrieval. In the present study, the association with preference was particularly evident when participants either returned to the environment in which objects had been encountered or were asked to identify that environment from an object. This suggests that the relationship between environmental evaluation and memory may be particularly relevant when remembering involves drawing on relationships between objects and their surrounding environments.

One possible explanation concerns how objects and environmental cues become associated through experience. Hayes et al. (2007) demonstrated that changes in scene context can impair subsequent object recognition, illustrating how remembering objects may depend on the contexts in which they were originally encountered. Masís-Obando et al. ( 2026), additionally, examined how differences in the neural reliability of spatial contexts related to later memory. Their findings suggest that spatial contexts may differ in how they support subsequent remembering, with individual differences in neural responses also playing a role. Related research has further shown that architectural aesthetics and spatial complexity are associated with activity in brain regions involved in memory and environmental experience (Gregorians et al., 2026). In the present experiment, participants encountered objects while exploring distinct environments, and the association between preference and Guided Recall may, therefore, be related to differences in how objects and environmental cues were experienced together or subsequently drawn upon during retrieval. However, the present study did not independently measure the development or strength of object-environment associations, and the findings cannot establish whether such differences account for the observed relationship.

A related possibility concerns the distinctiveness of environmental contexts, as distinctive contextual information can facilitate recognition when reinstated during retrieval (Park et al., 2006). Although the environments in the present experiment differed in spatial organization, including rhythmic and irregular arrangements, these differences cannot be equated with perceived contextual distinctiveness, which was not independently assessed. The contribution of environmental context may also depend on retrieval goals. Elward and Rugg (2015) demonstrated that retrieval goals influence which aspects of a previous experience are recovered, while Gardette and Bastin (2026) showed that information treated as a focal item may instead function as context depending on what is being retrieved. This is particularly relevant to the present experiment, in which Guided Recall 1 required participants to recall objects while revisiting their original environment, whereas Guided Recall 2 required them to identify the original environment associated with a presented object. Unlike these tasks, Immediate Free Recall did not explicitly require participants to draw on environmental context. The association with preference across both Guided Recall tasks is, therefore, consistent with the possibility that environment-specific evaluations relate to remembering when object-environment relationships are relevant to retrieval, although the underlying contribution of contextual distinctiveness remains untested.

Taken together, these findings suggest that the relationship between environmental preference and memory may be better understood in relation to the circumstances under which environmental context contributes to remembering, rather than as a general advantage of preferred environments. Contextual associations, perceived distinctiveness, and retrieval demands offer complementary perspectives on why environmental contexts may contribute differently to recall. However, the present experiment cannot determine which of these processes, if any, accounts for the association with subsequently reported preference. What the findings demonstrate is that this association was particularly apparent when remembering involved the environments in which objects had been encountered.

4.4 Limitations

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings. Most importantly, environmental preference was assessed after completion of the memory tasks rather than measured independently beforehand. Consequently, the observed association between preferred-environment status and context-supported recall does not establish that preference improved memory. Participants may have engaged differently with environments they subsequently preferred, but their preference judgments may also have been influenced by their experiences during the experiment, including their perceived success in remembering objects, even though no feedback on memory performance was provided. Although the analyses accounted for repeated observations within participants, presentation order, and the experimentally manipulated environmental conditions, they cannot resolve the direction of this relationship. Furthermore, each participant identified only one preferred environment among four alternatives, resulting in one preferred and three non-preferred observations per participant. This comparison captures the distinction between the most preferred environment and the remaining alternatives, rather than a continuous relationship between degrees of preference and memory. It, therefore, remains unknown whether memory performance varies systematically across different levels of environmental preference. The processes underlying the association also remain unresolved.

Although the study examined memory while participants actively explored virtual environments, it did not independently assess attention, visual exploration, affective responses, motivational engagement, or the strength of object-environment associations. Consequently, the potential contributions of these processes cannot be distinguished, and the mechanisms discussed in the preceding sections should be understood as possibilities rather than explanations demonstrated by the experiment. Future studies incorporating these elements could help clarify how preference and memory become related during environmental encounters.

The specificity and measurement of environmental preference also warrant consideration. General preference for nature and preference for spatial structure vs. variation were not consistently associated with memory, whereas preference for the specific environment was associated with context-supported recall. However, these measures differed in their content, scale, and operationalization. Environmentspecific preference was expressed as a choice among four experienced settings, whereas the broader measures assessed more general environmental preferences. Their different associations with memory, therefore, cannot be attributed to the specificity of preference alone. Furthermore, perceived suitability for concentration and learning was not significantly associated with either Guided Recall measure, despite the association observed for overall environmental preference. Research suggests that preferences for learning environments may reflect perceived suitability for particular activities (Beckers et al., 2016), while positive environmental evaluations do not necessarily correspond to improved cognitive performance (van den Bogerd et al., 2020 (van den Bogerd et al., , 2021)). Nevertheless, the absence of a significant association should not be interpreted as evidence that perceived suitability is unrelated to memory. Moreover, preference for a particular environment was assessed at a single time point, providing no information about how evaluations might develop through repeated encounters. Research on favorite places suggests that environmental preference, personal experiences, memory, and place attachment can become intertwined over time (Korpela et al., 2001; Ratcliffe & Korpela, 2016; Subiza-Pérez et al., 2021). Although familiar and personally meaningful places differ substantially from the novel virtual environments examined here, this literature highlights the importance of examining how environmental evaluations and memories may develop together rather than treating preference as a stable characteristic preceding experience.

5. Conclusion

The present study suggests that the relationship between environmental preference and episodic memory depends on both the specificity of the preference and the conditions under which memory is assessed. General preference for nature and preference for spatial structure vs. variation showed little association with memory performance. In contrast, context-supported recall was higher for objects encountered within the environment that participants subsequently identified as their preferred one. This association was evident after accounting for the experimentally manipulated environmental characteristics, suggesting that evaluations of specific environments may capture aspects of individual experience not adequately described by broad environmental categories alone. These findings highlight the importance of considering environmental characteristics alongside individual evaluations when investigating how environments relate to cognition. Although natural environments were generally preferred, this did not translate into a corresponding association between general nature preference and memory. Moreover, the relationship between environment-specific preference and memory was not uniform across retrieval tasks, suggesting that environmental evaluations may be particularly relevant when remembering involves drawing on the context in which information was encountered. Importantly, because preference was assessed after the memory tasks, the direction of this relationship remains unresolved. The findings, therefore, do not establish that preferred environments improve memory, nor do they identify a particular environmental configuration as universally beneficial. For the study and design of learning and working environments, the findings suggest that environmental characteristics and individual experience should be considered together rather than assuming that the same spatial configurations will support cognition equally across people and tasks. This does not imply designing environments solely around stated preferences, but instead points toward examining how different environmental characteristics are encountered and evaluated in relation to cognitive activities.
