Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). Statcheck might still miss one-sided tests that are described in another sentence, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. Some differences come from the ground truth: it lacks p-values with a correction name (“p Bonf = .061”), and counts partial eta squared written with its subscript after it (“η 2 p = .05”), “p = .05”, and some table notes and figure legends (“† p < .10, *p < .05”), which this module leaves out, as non-significant p-values. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. Introduction

When we use a measure 𝑋 for some hypothesized variable of the natural world 𝑇 𝑋 that is not directly observable (i.e., latent), then that measure will depend on some function 𝑔 of one or more indicators from that natural world 𝐼 𝑋 . For example, when using an infrared ear thermometer, the goal is to measure core temperature 𝑇 𝑋 as indicated by infrared light 𝐼 𝑋 , which is transformed within the thermometer by a known function 𝑔 to yield the measure of temperature 𝑋. When there is little error, such a procedure may yield measures that are very close to the true values (𝑋 ≈ 𝑇 𝑋 ), and that make it more or less unproblematic to use those measures as if they directly correspond to the true variables. However, if the indicators 𝐼 𝑋 only capture 𝑇 𝑋 imprecisely, so that the measure 𝑋 has some substantial error, then that will bias the estimates of the latent variable, and, in turn, that variable's relationship with other variables, unless the measure is defined in a way so that error is accounted for.

Errors are often unaccounted when psychological researchers take their measurements. For example, if peoples' perception or cognition, or the measurement instrument itself, only has the precision to report a truly continuous trait (𝑇 𝑋 ) through some discrete categories (e.g., "a lot", "strongly agree"), then even in the best-case scenario the indicator (𝐼 𝑋 ) can only specify a range of true values to which each indicator value is associated. Therefore, there will be some error, however 𝑔 is specified. Depending on the properties of the errors, this may bias various statistics.

In contrast, accounting for errors is regularly considered in sciences such as physics. When a measurement is to be made, properties of the measurement situation and the measurement instrument are considered, and the instrument is fine-tuned in each specific situation to ensure errors are small enough for purposes of the measurement.

There are certainly many statistical methods for handling various errors. However, these methods are often specialized to a particular type of application, which means a more general consideration of errors can be beyond the scope of most applied researchers, beyond the methods in which they have had special training. These methods also often treat measurement and statistical modelling of errors separately, which may explain why measurement without statistical modelling of errors remains common. Contrast this with the procedure above, where it is recognized that modelling and handling errors is required before measurements are taken, and that this is to affect how measurements are scored. Developing a general model of how to define functions 𝑔 from a set of indicators 𝐼 𝑋 , based on an error model, can hopefully aid psychological researchers in incorporating error modelling into their general analysis procedure with greater regularity.

The goal with this paper is to develop principles for functions 𝑔 and corresponding scorings for 𝑋 that avoid bias in latent linear models for a wide category of errors. In particular, also for errors for which it is not possible to define a measurement decomposition as in Classical Test Theory (CTT): 𝑋 = 𝑇 𝑋 + 𝜀 𝑋 such that 𝐸(𝜀 𝑋 |𝑇 𝑋 = 𝑡) = 0 for all values 𝑡 of 𝑇; and for errors for which the Item Response Theory (IRT) assumption about local independence is not fulfilled. This will lead to a novel way of estimating regression parameters under various error structures through the same general steps. Returning Example: Tetrachoric correlations (setup)

To illustrate the different steps, we use tetrachoric correlations (see Olsson, 1979) as a returning example throughout the article (we focus on tetrachoric rather than polychoric correlations as we will compare our model against a particularly fast existing method). The tetrachoric model concerns a type of discretization where the true variables (𝑇 𝑋 , 𝑇 𝑌 ), neither of which is observed directly, follow a bivariate normal distribution with some correlation 𝜌 to be estimated. That is, we observe (𝐼 𝑋 , 𝐼 𝑌 ), where each of these variables have two ordered scores (𝑥 0 , 𝑥 1 , 𝑦 0 , 𝑦 1 ), so that 𝐼 𝑋 = 𝑥 0 if 𝑇 𝑋 ≤ 𝑐 𝑋 and 𝐼 𝑋 = 𝑥 1 if 𝑇 𝑋 > 𝑐 𝑋 , and correspondingly for 𝐼 𝑌 , where (𝑐 𝑋 , 𝑐 𝑌 ) are some unknown thresholds. Without loss of generality, we may assume the marginal distributions of (𝑇 𝑋 , 𝑇 𝑌 ) are standard normal, in which case 𝜌 is also the slope when 𝑇 𝑌 is regressed on 𝑇 𝑋 , or vice versa. The most common method to estimate tetrachoric (and polychoric) correlations is to estimate the thresholds in a first step, and then to estimate 𝜌 in a latter step through maximum likelihood (see Olsson, 1979). When we return to this example after Theorem 1, we will illustrate how (𝑋, 𝑌) should be scored to retain the linear relationship from this information. After Theorem 2, we will then show how to apply this information for estimation.

We now discuss how two special methods can be understood as prediction decompositions aimed at preserving the linear relationship. We present these as seeing them can help with understanding the broad applicability of this type of scoring.

First, Berkson (1950) considered how repeated manipulations of a predictor (measurably) set to the same level may produce random errors around this level when that exact level is not perfectly controlled for.

As shown by Berkson, when the errors around the measurement values for a single predictor are truly randomand so, when a prediction decomposition for that sole predictor is fulfilledthen the linear relationship will be retained. As an example, say that researchers study the effect of a nutrition compound on plant height using two groups, one not given the nutrition, and one given it. Say there are n plants in each group and that k grams of the nutrition are given to the experimental group. Now, if the effect is linear (for the included range of nutrition), then even if some plants in the experimental condition happen to get more of the nutrition than other plants then this will not bias the estimate of the effect, which is still 𝛽 0 in the control group, and 𝛽 0 + 𝛽 1 * 𝑘 𝑛 ⁄ in the experimental group.

Combined measurement and prediction decomposition

In the above examples, we focused on a single predictor. In latent factor regression models with continuous measures, Skrondal and Laake (2001) showed how joint regression scores could be used for the predictors, while Bartlett scores could be used for the outcome, to asymptotically retain the linear relationship. In an extension to IRT-model scoring of categorical items (under the usual assumption of conditional independence given the latent trait), Lu and Thomas (2008) in turn showed how the linear population relationship could be retained under an IRT model (so that measures fulfilled the usual local independence assumption conditional on the true/latent variables) when using joint expected-a-posteriori (EAP) scores for the predictors, and "conditionally unbiased" scores for the outcome, together with a further assumption about lack of correlation between errors of the predictors and outcome. In the measurement-and prediction decomposition language, Bartlett scores and the conditional unbiasedness scores of Lu and Thomas (2008) each correspond to an unbiased measurement decomposition, in that the expected value of the measures equal the true values for each value of the true variable. Meanwhile, the regression-and EAP scores correspond to a joint prediction decomposition, in that for each value of the measurement, the expected value of the true variable. The joint structure ensures that errors between predictors are uncorrelated.

Specifically, Lu and Thomas (2008) make the four assumptions below, which we have translated to our notation. We start with these before presenting the assumptions of Skrondal and Laake (2001), as Lu and Thomas relate their conditions to this earlier paper, which we will do as well.

1. (L&T.1): The indicators for the outcome 𝐼 𝑌 , and for the predictors 𝑰 𝑿 represent vectors of responses to 𝑞 and 𝑝 test items, respectively. The result of the different test items for 𝐼 𝑌 are locally independent given the true level of the trait 𝑇 𝑌 . Similarly, the result of the different test items for 𝑰 𝑿 are locally independent given the true levels of the traits 𝑻 𝑿 . This assumption comes from the assumed IRT model with local independence. 2. (L&T.2): 𝑰 𝑿 is independent of the error term 𝜖 in the linear relationship. 3. (L&T.3): 𝐼 𝑌 |𝑇 𝑌 and 𝑰 𝑿 |𝑻 𝑿 are independent. 4. (L&T.4): 𝐼 𝑌 |𝑇 𝑌 is independent from 𝑻 𝑿 , and 𝑰 𝑿 |𝑻 𝑿 is independent from 𝑇 𝑌 . This too follows from the IRT model with local independence, although not specified in (L&T.1). Lu and Thomas (2008) then show how joint EAP scores for the predictors and conditionally unbiased scores for the outcome will retain the correct linear relationship on the population level. Skrondal and Laake (2001) do not list their assumptions in the text in a similar way, but they consider only continuous measures and note that they take as their starting point the latent regression model, a special case of the full LISREL model of Jöreskog (1973 Jöreskog ( , 1977)).

Among these assumptions is one that the measurement errors of all variables are truly "random", meaning they are independent of all other sources of variance (i.e., other true variables and other errors). These assumptions are used instead of (L&T.1). The assumption about random errors directly implies (L&T.3)-(L&T.4), as the indicators are just equal to the true variables plus such random errors in their case. (L&T.2) is fulfilled by the same assumption plus the assumption that the relationship is truly linear with independent errors (so 𝜖 is independent of 𝑻 𝑿 ). Skrondal and Laake (2001) then showed that under their latent regression model, Bartlett scores for the outcome with joint regression scores for the predictors retained the linear relationship on a population level. See also the comparison in Lu and Thomas (2008, p.474-475) between their assumptions and the derivation in Skrondal and Laake (2001).

In our derivation, in contrast, other than some general OLS assumptions (which must also be implicitly assumed for estimation in the previous papers), we will only require a weaker version of (L&T.2) (we do not assume strict independence). None of the other (L&T) assumptions, nor the previous ones by Skrondal and Laake (2001) are required for our scoring methods, which, with our assumptions, always allow the linear relationship to be retained. Such scores can in principle be used in all cases allowed by Skrondal and Laake (2001) and Lu and Thomas (2008), but also much more broadly (although, in practice, our ability to apply them will also depend on what information is available in the data). We now turn to some preliminary results in this regard.

Structured prediction decomposition for all variables

We will base our extensions in part on the results above (Lu & Thomas, 2008; Skrondal & Lake, 2001) and derive conditions that ensure the multivariate linear relationship is retained even beyond continuous measures and IRT models. In the terminology above, this will consist of creating a structured type of joint regression scores both for the predictors and for the outcome (joint because the outcome's scores also depend on the predictors). Or, in other words, scores for both predictors and outcome that fulfill a prediction decomposition.

The utility of deriving a prediction decomposition for the outcome is hinted at by Lu and Thomas (2008). Namely, they say that "there are no IRT predictors available that are exactly conditionally unbiased" (p.473), meaning that the types of conditionally unbiased scores they define for the outcome generally cannot be used for their stated purposes without at least some bias. 2x2 Em algorithm

Motivated by Theorem 2, we define a 2x2 Em estimation algorithm below. Note that we call this an Em algorithm as it is similar to (and might be possible to incorporate into the framework of) Expectation-Maximization (EM) (see Dempster et al., 1977). But while such algorithms maximize the likelihood, the Em algorithm minimizes the (least) squares.

Step 1.1: Estimate nuisance parameters 𝜽 outside of the OLS regression that affects the expected values. If it is possible to estimate such parameters without taking into account the OLS relationship, then these parameters can be estimated by any existing method. Step 1.2: Estimate the conditional expectations of the 𝑿-variables through plugging in the related parameters from Step 1.1. This estimation may be done in several ways (as may estimating the expectations for 𝑌 below). If an analytical formula is available, that can be used directly. Alternatively, expectations may be solved numerically, or estimated through simulation by repeated draws from the population model implied by the parameters. Step 2.1: Estimate further nuisance parameters for 𝑌 that need to take into account the predictor 𝑿-variables, if necessary (note that we can do this if needed, as we only required that 𝜽 could be estimated independently of the outcome, not the predictors). Step 2.2: Estimate the conditional expectations and OLS parameters iteratively by plugging in relevant parameters from Steps 1.1 and 2.1, and some initial guess at the 𝜷parameters. Then regress 𝑌 onto the 𝑿-variables from Step 1.2, update the 𝜷-estimates in some appropriate way, calculate new expected values for 𝑌, and repeat until convergence. If (43) is not true, the choice of how values are updated becomes particularly important to avoid divergent estimates across iterations.

Sampling error

Theorem 2 justifies estimation by the 2x2 Em iterative plugin procedure above. While this may yield asymptotically correct estimates when the assumptions are fulfilled, we should also consider how to estimate the sampling error of the estimation procedure.

In general, the procedure above contains two sources of increased sampling error compared to if variables could have been measured without any error: (I) the extra residual error from the 𝜀 𝑌 , 𝜺 𝑿 errors themselves, and (II) the extra sampling error from having to estimate the expected values and nuisance parameters.

The model in Theorem 1 is expressed very generally, so we should also consider general ways to estimate this sampling error. Large-sample errors could be possible to derive, potentially from earlier methods when the methods can be shown to converge. Estimation should also be possible to accomplish by utilizing Bootstrapped standard errors (Efron, 1979), where the Em estimation procedure is resampled with replacement, and where the variation across samples is used to estimate the standard error. Using Bootstrapped standard errors has one advantage related to each point (I)-(II) above. (I) The errors 𝜀 𝑌 , 𝜺 𝑿 are unlikely to be independent and normally distributed, even if this is true for 𝜖, so the total residual error for the measurements will not have a normal distribution. Bootstrapping allows accounting for this non-normality. (II) When the expected values and nuisance parameters need to be estimated in the sample (as they usually need to be), the usual residuals from the OLS regression will not capture this extra uncertainty as it is only applied once those estimates are plugged in. Discussion

Here we derived a population model about errors that allowed latent linear relationships to be retained (Theorem 1), and a corresponding estimation method (Theorem 2). We showed how this can be used both to estimate such latent linear relationships, or to estimate nonlinear relationships between observed variables through the use of pseudo-latent variables. We also showed variance ratios that, like reliabilities, can function as measures of the precisions of our measures, and that can be used to quantify the change in explained variance between latent and observed variables, and to evaluate the usefulness of modelling that error.

The model we derived is also more general than prior models for retaining latent linear relationships (Lu & Thomas, 2008; Skrondal & Laake, 2001), as indicated by Lemma 1-2, as we also score the outcome by an unbiased prediction decomposition. This scoring also meant that we were able to derive these scores under fewer assumptions than in these earlier papers.

As a result, we can fit a greater range of measurement issues into our population model. In part because some variables cannot fulfill an unbiased measurement decomposition, while variables can always fulfill an unbiased prediction decomposition, in part because of the fewer assumptions required to ensure our scoring retain the linear relationship. In particular, we do not require that the error of the outcome does not depend on the predictors (as is required by the L&T.3-4 assumptions).

The model and its related Em estimation method also has several practical advantages.

First, when estimating the 𝜷-parameters the 𝑿 𝑇 𝑿 matrix technically only needs to be inverted once. This may speed up estimation when this matrix is large. The reason for this is that for each OLS iteration, we are estimating with (𝑿 𝜽 T𝑿 𝜽 ̂)-1 𝑿 𝜽 T𝑌 𝜽 ̂,𝜷 ̂, and 𝑌 𝜽 ̂,𝜷 ̂ the is the only part that needs to be updated based on the new estimates of 𝜷. Although it may of course be advantageous to introduce an iterative weight matrix into these estimates, especially when the variance of 𝑌 𝜽 ̂,𝜷 ̂|𝑿 𝜽 ̂=𝒙 varies a lot for different 𝒙.

The fact that we define the scores for the predictors and the outcome in a corresponding way may also yield certain benefits. Consider if the scores need to be estimated in a chained way. That is, where scores for predictors also depend on other predictors. If those scores can be estimated through the Em algorithm, then we can start with the estimates that need the least predictors. Once we have 𝜷-estimates in that relationship, this gives us a complete expression for the joint distribution of those 𝑿-variables, say for the first 𝑗 predictors. We can then use this directly in a second step, where these variables predict the 𝑗 + 1:th predictor.

This is then repeated until we arrive at predicting the outcome 𝑌.

The fact that we can use similar steps (by the Em algorithm) to estimate various types of errors is also advantageous, in that it can aid researchers in considering a larger variety of errors with more regularity. In all cases, we start with a measurement model, consider if, and if so, how nuisance parameters 𝜽 can be estimated separately from the 𝜷-parameters, evaluate and potentially modify the measurement and/or the number of predictors so that the measured predictors should not depend on the regression error term 𝜖 (this concerns dependence both for the true predictors and for their indicators), estimate 𝜽-parameters that do not depend on any joint predictors, calculate the expected values of the predictors (possibly in a chained way) and potential further 𝜽-parameters that require considering the joint distribution of the predictors (including intercepts and slopes between the predictors), and then estimate the expected values for the outcome and the 𝜷-parameters iteratively. The steps always concern ensuring Assumption 1-4 are fulfilled, finding expected values for the predictors, and then using those to estimate expected values and OLS parameters for the outcome.

As we split up the estimation into parts, this may further enable researchers to derive models for their errors, as they can first consider a model of the nuisance parameters, then the predictors (which itself can be broken up into steps), and then the outcome, which may be simpler than modelling a joint distribution all at once. Additionally, as multiple errors can be considered through the same steps, it can hopefully aid researchers in considering joint types of errors in the same model, as in Example 3.

The focus on expected-value scores also enables estimating scores by simulation even in cases where deriving those expectations is otherwise difficult. This will not be as quick as otherwise, but it can be utilized as a brute force approach even in complicated models.

The method that has been presented here thus appears highly applicable and flexible.

However, some limitations of the current treatment should be noted. First, the flexibility of the method means that it can be applied in different ways even for the same problem. This can be an advantage (e.g., one method may be possible to apply under conditions when another cannot), but it also means that new applications should check so that the proposed method has good properties compared to other methods. In the logistic regression part of Example 2, we used two different methods, and showed that they had converged on the right relationship. However, if one of the methods yields estimates with consistently lower standard errors than another, then that first method should clearly be preferred. Both may be versions of the method we apply, so, certainly, checks should also be applied to ensure each particular application works satisfactorily.

In a similar vein, our Theorem 2 provides conditions to ensure the iterative updating will asymptotically move the conditional expectations in the correct direction for each iteration.

However, any new application should modify the updating and check so that estimates converge as expected. The updated expectations may differ very little, making the iterative updating very slow unless some speedup is included, or (if (43) is not fulfilled), differ too much unless the updating is slowed down. Theorem 2 provides a flexible result about the direction in which expectations should be updated, how far that each iteration should update in that direction should be tailored to each problem at hand.

It should also be studied how some of our assumptions could be relaxed. Assumption 1-2 are fairly open, and Assumption 3-4 also holds under many cases, but there are also cases where they do not. Assumption 4 might be relaxed to an assumption that the covariances 𝐶𝑜𝑣(𝑿 1:𝑝,𝜽 ̂, 𝑌 𝜽 ̂,𝜷 ̂) (and, if necessary, the expectations) move in the correct direction when estimates are updated. Because even if expectations for some 𝒙 change quicker than indicated by (43), it may be that others change slower, and that this balances out in the covariance as a whole.

Conclusions

The model of measurement errors presented in Theorem 1 provides a broad analytical model for considering and handling various errors. As shown by Lemma 2, the scores of Theorem 1 can be applied for a wide variety of errors.

The analytical model derived in Theorem 1 is useful on its own in that it can be used to understand the consequences of errors. Even where expectations are not possible to estimate, or where the estimation procedure suggested here is less efficient, it can provide understanding about the optimal scores that would be required to avoid bias, and how much this could differ from the scores used by researchers. It can also hopefully enable communicating the consequences of a broader set of errors.

Finally, we derived a novel way to estimate OLS parameters through an Em algorithm in Theorem 2 for the multiple models that fulfill or can be made to fulfill the structure in Theorem 1. Future studies should further examine the general behavior of the algorithm, and how to specify the algorithm to estimate expected values and nuisance parameters most effectively for various measurements. The model also provides the possibility of deriving flexible code for estimating these models using the same general steps under various errors structures and distributions.
