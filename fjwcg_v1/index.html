How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Reaction-time variability (RTV) is a well-established indicator of ADHD, but we still do not fully understand its cognitive roots in adults. We analyzed Continuous Performance Task (CPT-II) data from 99 adults from the HYPERAKTIV dataset (50 ADHD, 49 controls). Analysis combines behavioral analysis, drift diffusion modeling (DDM), and interpretable machine learning classification in a single framework. Average reaction time did not differ between groups (0.39 s vs. 0.39 s, p = 0.8820), but RTV was significantly higher in the ADHD group (0.130 s vs. 0.098 s, p = 0.0026, d = 0.62), which indicates attentional instability rather than overall slowing. DDM analysis indicated differences in two components of the modeled response process: ADHD participants showed lower drift rates (4.499 vs. 4.782, p = 0.0224, r rb = -0.241), which reflects less efficient evidence accumulation, and shorter non-decision time (0.145 s vs. 0.172 s, p = 0.0033, r rb = -0.346), suggesting altered non-decision processes. Boundary separation did not differ between groups. Drift rate was correlated with childhood symptom severity on the WURS after FDR correction (r = -0.302, p = 0.016), and non-decision time was the only DDM parameter that independently predicted diagnosis (β = -15.064, p = 0.0073). Classification primarily depends on behavioral features (SVM-RBF: ROC-AUC 0.784, 74.2% accuracy), which outperformed DDM-only models (best ROC-AUC 0.694, 72.2% accuracy). A Random Forest model combining behavioral and DDM features achieved the highest ROC-AUC overall (0.808), but at lower accuracy (71.1%). To evaluate feature selection without selection-induced optimism, we performed nested five-fold cross validation across all 511 non-empty subsets of nine behavioral and DDM-derived features. The selected feature subset varied across outer folds, with mean reaction time and accuracy selected most frequently. Overall nested cross-validation yielded 72.2% accuracy, 72.3% balanced accuracy, and ROC-AUC of 0.772. These findings suggest that adult ADHD is characterized more by response inconsistency than generalized slowing, with DDM providing complementary information about altered evidence accumulation and nondecision processes, while behavioral measures remain the strongest basis for classification.

1 Introduction

If you walk into any adult attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) clinic, you will hear a common complaint that it doesn't fit the usual picture of hyperactivity: I am not slow, I am just inconsistent. Some days I am sharp, while some days I struggle to hold a thought. This everyday observation has a clear behavioral pattern in the lab. Over decades of research, one of the most consistently found results in ADHD is not that affected individuals respond slowly, but that they respond unevenly. Their reaction times vary from trial to trial in a way that clinical control participants do not. Castellanos et al. (2005) were among the first to highlight this and show that intra-individual reaction-time variability (RTV) is a strong and repeatable marker across ADHD subtypes. This suggests that RTV is a more fundamental characteristic of the disorder than average response speed itself. Karalunas et al. (2014) continue on this, stating that RTV serves as a transdiagnostic trait; they found it not only in ADHD but also in autism spectrum disorder, showing a shared issue in attentional stability rather than being specific to any one diagnostic category.

What remains unclear, despite nearly two decades of research on RTV, is why it occurs. Reaction time is the result of a series of cognitive steps, which include encoding a stimulus, evaluating evidence, deciding on a response, and carrying out the action. An issue at any stage of this process could result in a slower or more variable reaction time. Conventional metrics such as RTV, accuracy, or average reaction time are unable to identify the source of these problems. This is the primary gap that our study looks at and attempts to close. It has already been established that behavioral instability is not a result of ADHD. Rather, we focus on the particular decision-making stage that is responsible for this instability. About 5% of children and adolescents and 2.5% of adults worldwide suffer from ADHD, one of the most common neurodevelopmental disorders (Faraone et al., 2015). It is caused by a combination of environmental and genetic variables; no single element is sufficient or required on its own (Faraone et al., 2015). This likely explains the varied ways it presents in individuals and over time. Importantly, ADHD does not just continue into adulthood as a milder version of its childhood form. According to Volkow & Swanson (2013), adult ADHD has its own unique set of attentional, impulsive, and executive issues.

It is becoming more widely acknowledged that ADHD is a neurodevelopmental condition that lasts a lifetime. Nearly half of those affected continue to show significant symptoms during adulthood (Faraone et al., 2015). Adult ADHD is linked to persistent issues with impulse control, executive function, and attention.

Education, employment, relationships, and general quality of life can all be adversely affected by these problems (Volkow & Swanson, 2013). The majority of research has focused on children (Gupta & Kar, 2010 , 2009; Gupta et al., 2011 Gupta et al., , 2006)), despite the growing importance of ADHD in both clinical and societal contexts (Baijal & Gupta, 2008). This oversight leaves the cognitive processes behind adult ADHD largely unexplored (Onandia-Hinchado et al., 2021). Understanding these mechanisms is essential for creating practical computational markers and enhancing precision psyc hiatry (Huys et al., 2016).

Moreover, the rapid growth of AI-powered digital platforms and social media shaped by algorithms has raised concerns about their effects on attention and self-control. According to recent research, excessive use of highly tailored digital information may increase impulsivity and inattention, particularly in susceptible people like those with ADHD (Hassan et al., 2025). These issues highlight how important it is to comprehend how adults with ADHD pay attention in a society where artificial intelligence is be coming more and more prevalent.

1.1 ADHD and Attentional Dysfunction

The way ADHD is understood has changed a great deal since the earlier years. It is currently recognized as a disorder of distributed neural systems, specifically one in which multiple interconnected networks fail to coordinate effectively, rather than a weakness confined to a particular area of the brain or even a lack of self-control. Konrad & Eickhoff (2010) examined the structural and functional connectivity evidence in support of this systems-level explanation and found that ADHD is linked to altered communication between networks rather than to damage in any one particular area (Gupta et al., 2009). This new way of viewing the disorder is important since it helps to account for the great variety of behavioural symptoms. The difficulties with attention, impulsive behaviour, and physical restlessness do not arise from a single defective mechanism but are due instead to a more general instability in the way the attentional networks function together over time.

One influential version of this systems account is the default-mode interference hypothesis proposed by (Sonuga-Barke & Castellanos, 2007). They do this by connecting short-term, spontaneous variations in default-mode network activity to short bursts of sustained focus. According to this theory, RTV is not random noise in the neural system but rather an outward sign of an attentional system that occasionally falters, veering toward self-generated, task-irrelevant thoughts before returning to the current task. Fassbender et al.(2009) provided convergent neuroimaging evidence for this idea, linking elevated RTV in ADHD to a failure of default-mode suppression during sustained attention tasks, with greater distractibility tracking the degree of this network failure.

1.2 Reaction-Time Variability as a Cognitive Marker

If RTV reflects something real and mechanistic about ADHD rather than only measurement noise, it should behave consistently across measurement approaches, and largely, it does. Beyond the standard-deviationbased RTV measure used in most studies (including ours), distributional approaches converge on the same conclusion. Bella-Fernandez et al. (2024) conducted a meta-analysis of ex-Gaussian parameters fitted to RT distributions in ADHD, finding a consistently elevated tau parameter (the exponential tail of the distribution) across studies. This is important because it indicates that the additional variability in ADHD is not uniformly distributed across all trials. Instead, it is clustered within a subset of unusually slow trials (presumably reflecting attention lapses) that are superimposed on an otherwise typical response distribution.

The clinical relevance of this variability extends well beyond laboratory task performance. Munch et al. (2025) found that RTV in children with ADHD independently predicted parent reported social difficulties, suggesting that the same attentional fluctuations measured in a sterile CPT task also surface in the much messier domain of everyday social interaction. More recently, Tamm et al. (2025) examined the neural correlates of RTV at the single-trial level in a large pediatric fMRI sample drawn from the ABCD study (n = 5,719), reporting weakened reaction time-BOLD coupling in executive and default-mode networks among children with ADHD. This discovery provides a neurological as well as a behavioral foundation for RTV.

Few studies have examined whether the same patterns and underlying mechanisms apply to adults, despite the abundance of pediatric evidence. A systematic study of cognitive deficiencies in adult ADHD also highlights the gap and specifically advocates for additional computational modeling work in this population (Onandia-Hinchado et al., 2021).

1.3 Computational Modeling of Attention

Although behavioral measurements like RTV show that instability exists, they don't make clear what kind of instability it is. Nowadays, several academics are employing computer models of decision -making to better understand the mechanisms involved. One important example is the Drift Diffusion Model (DDM) (Ratcliff & McKoon, 2008; Ratcliff, 1978). The DDM views every simple decision as a noisy accumulation of evidence over time that approaches one of two decision boundaries. The single reaction time value that researchers usually measure is broken down into separate components: the efficiency of evidence accumulation (drift rate), the degree of caution shown when setting the decision criterion (boundary separation), and the time spent on processes unrelated to the decision, such as encoding the stimulus and carrying out the motor response (non-decision time). Voss et al. (2013) systematically verified this method for use in cognitive neuroscience and developed the parameter-estimation techniques that have since been applied in various clinical settings, including ours.

Using this framework for ADHD has already shown significant results in children. Huang-Pollock et al. (2017) discovered that a significant amount of the variability in response time and performance deficiencies observed in pediatric ADHD can be explained by a lower drift rate, indicating less effective evidence accumulation. This reframes RTV from being only an observation to an explanation of underlying reasons.

Recently, Hlutkowsky et al. (2026) employed diffusion modeling to investigate task -switching tasks in children with ADHD. They showed that deficiencies in more fundamental perceptual decision-making, which is represented in a lower drift rate, are really linked to deficiencies in executive function, which is usually regarded as a separate cognitive area. This represents a significant conceptual change since it suggests that many aspects of "executive dysfunction" in ADHD may actually result from disturbances in the basic process of accumulating evidence.

These results are less evident in adults. Fasmer et al. (2016) found that the temporal dynamics on the CPT-II can differentiate adult ADHD from mood and anxiety disorders, aiding in diagnosis, but their analysis did not fully implement diffusion modeling. In general, studies employing machine learning on behavioral CPT data in the late 2010s and early 2020s have used features like RTV to obtain a reasonable classification accuracy of 70-80%. The inability of this research to explain the cognitive processes underlying the discriminative signals, however, remains a chronic problem. Computational psychiatry seeks to close this gap. According to Huys et al. (2016), computational psychiatry serves as a link between clinical symptoms and latent cognitive mechanisms, such as those assessed by the DDM. The objective is to move psychiatric research away from classification and toward a more profound comprehension of mechanisms. In support of this, Lohani et al. (2025) examined machine-learning techniques for ADHD detection using MRI and EEG data, emphasizing the same problem: great classification performance with little understanding of the underlying causes. Furthermore, even greater classification accuracy has been attained by deep learning techniques applied to resting-state fMRI (Firouzi et al., 2024), but they still face the problem of limited interpretability.

Even while adult ADHD is becoming more widely recognized as a serious public health concern, there are still considerable gaps in our knowledge of its cognitive underpinnings. Children and teenagers have been the focus of the majority of studies connecting reaction-time variability (RTV) to Drift Diffusion Model (DDM) parameters. This raises the question of whether adult attentional issues are caused by the same covert decision-making mechanisms, particularly ineffective evidence of collection. Meanwhile, existing machinelearning studies have mainly focused on diagnostic classification. They frequently provide little understanding of the mental processes underlying model predictions. To the best of our knowledge, no prior research has integrated clinical symptom severity, behavioral measures, DDM-derived latent factors, and interpretable machine-learning analysis into a single computational framework for adult ADHD. Because of this lack of integration, it is difficult to determine if computational characteristics are primarily useful for describing the cognitive processes behind attentional problems or if they offer diagnostic information beyond typical behavioral tests. This study combines interpretable machine learning, cl inical evaluation, behavioral analysis, and decision modeling into a single framework to address these issues. In addition to comparing group-level behavioral and DDM measures, we use multivariable logistic regression to see if individual latent cognitive parameters can predict ADHD diagnosis independently while accounting for demographic factors. We also conduct thorough feature subset evaluation along with feature importance analysis to pinpoint the behavioral and computational markers that contribute mos t to classifying adult ADHD. This combined method aims to improve diagnostic predictions and offer a clearer understanding of attentional dysfunction in adults.

1.5 Study Objectives and Hypotheses

The primary objective of this study was to investigate the cognitive mechanisms underlying attentional dysfunction in adult ADHD by integrating behavioral measures, Drift Diffusion Modeling, and machinelearning classification within a single analytic framework, using Continuous Performance Task data from the HYPERAKTIV dataset (Hicks et al., 2021). Specifically, we asked whether the well -documented elevation in RTV reflects identifiable alterations in latent decision-making processes, and whether those processes carry clinical and diagnostic value beyond what conventional behavioral measures already provide.

Based on previous findings in the ADHD and computational psychiatry literature, we formulated the following hypotheses: • H1: Adults with ADHD will show significantly greater reaction-time variability (RTV) than clinical control participants, consistent with the broader literature on attentional instability (Castellanos et al., 2005; Karalunas et al., 2014).

• H2: Adults with ADHD will show reduced drift rates (v), reflecting less efficient evidence accumulation during decision-making, consistent with pediatric findings (Huang-Pollock et al., 2017).

• H3: DDM parameters, particularly drift rate (v) and non-decision time (t), will be significantly associated with ADHD symptom severity, as measured by the Adult ADHD Self-Report Scale (ASRS) and the Wender Utah Rating Scale (WURS).

H4:

We hypothesize that behavioural metrics and DDM-derived parameters will each contribute significantly to ADHD classification, and behavioural measures are expected to provide strong predictive performance. We further hypothesize that non-decision time may carry greater diagnostic information than the other DDM parameters, consistent with prior evidence linking non -decision processes to attentional and response-initiation irregularities in ADHD.

By testing these hypotheses within a single adult sample, this study makes three contributions. First, it extends computational modeling of attentional instability (long studied in children) into an adult clinical population. Second, it applies the DDM specifically to isolate which stage of decision-making (evidence accumulation versus non-decision processing) drives the behavioral instability long observed in ADHD.

Third, it integrates behavioral data, computational parameters, clinical symptom measures, a nd crossvalidated interpretable machine-learning classification within one coherent computational phenotyping framework, allowing a direct comparison of explanatory mechanisms against diagnostic utility. This is a comparison that the existing literature has rarely made explicit.

The significance of these results for understanding computational psychiatry and attentional dysfunction, as well as the study limitations and future directions, are discussed in Sections 4 and 5, respectively. 3.1 Behavioural Performance

Behavioral performance during the Continuous Performance Task (CPT) is summarized in Figures 1 and 2 and

Table 2. Reaction-time variability (RTV) is considerably higher in ADHD participants than in clinical controls (ADHD: 0.130 ± 0.061 s; Control: 0.098 ± 0.037 s; p = 0.0026, Cohen's d = 0.62), suggesting larger trial-to-trial variations in attentional performance. Mean reaction times were computed over five consecutive trial quintiles to assess vigilance throughout task performance (Figure 2). Individuals with ADHD showed somewhat more variation between quintiles. There is minimal indication that the groups' overall trajectories differed in terms of declining vigilance.

These results imply that the overall pattern of vigilance change throughout task performance was similar between ADHD and control participants, despite the fact that persons with ADHD showed more behavioral variabili ty.

3.3 Associations Between DDM Parameters and Clinical Measures

Associations between DDM parameters and clinical symptom measures are shown in Figure 4. For all clinical scales, higher symptom severity was often linked to lower drift rates and shorter non-decision times.

After adjusting for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini-Hochberg method, a significant negative correlation appeared between drift rate (v) and WURS scores (r = -0.302, FDR-corrected p = 0.016). This means that greater past ADHD symptom severity was related to less efficient evidence of accumulation.

Other than this, no correlations were significant after correcting multiple tests.

To further examine the relationship between DDM parameters and clinical symptoms, we fitted multivariable linear regression models controlling for age and sex. The models explained 22.0% of the variance in ASRS scores (R² = 0.220, p = 0.0026) and 21.3% of the variance in WURS scores (R² = 0.213, p = 0.0035). For ASRS scores, boundary separation and non-decision time significantly predicted outcomes, while drift rate did not. For WURS scores, drift rate, boundary separation, and non -decision time were all significant predictors. The corresponding MADRS model was not statistically significant (R² = 0.105, p = 0.149). These results indicate that various elements of the modeled decision-making process have different associations with symptom dimensions related to ADHD.

3.4 Independent Prediction of ADHD Diagnosis

To examine whether DDM parameters uniquely predicted an ADHD diagnosis, we conducted a multivariable logistic regression including drift rate (v), boundary separation (a), non-decision time (t), age, and sex as predictors. The model was statistically significant (χ2 = 12.38, p = 0.0268). The only significant independent predictor of ADHD diagnosis among the DDM parameters was non-decision time (β = -15.064, SE = 5.62, p = 0.0073). However, drift rate (β = -0.63, p = 0.155) and boundary separation (β = -0.84, p = 0.178) did not significantly predict diagnostic status after adjusting for other factors. Furthermore, neither age (p = 0.960) nor sex (p = 0.377) significantly correlated with diagnosis.

Collectively, these results show that even after controlling other latent decision-making metrics and demographic factors, shorter non-decision periods were specifically associated with having an ADHD diagnosis. This pattern is consistent with the idea that disturbances in non-decision components-such as stimulus encoding and the execution of motor responses-may represent a promising computational marker of adult ADHD above and beyond broader changes in evidence accumulation or response caution.

3.5 ADHD Classification Performance

To assess how well the behavioural measures and the DDM-based parameters performed, we used three machine learning classifiers: logistic regression, support vector machine with an RBF kernel, and a random forest. Each model was evaluated using stratified 5-fold cross-validation, and the ROC-AUC, accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, precision, negative predictive value (NPV), F1 -score, and the bootstrap-derived confidence intervals were calculated. The results are given in Figures 5a and 5b.

The SVM (RBF) classifier based on behavioral features was the best-performing of the single featureset models, obtaining a mean ROC-AUC of 0.784±0.088 (95% CI [0.576,0.990]), an accuracy of 74.2%, and an F1-score of 0.747. For DDM-based feature models, logistic regression performed the best, attaining a ROC-AUC of 0.694±0.124 (95% CI [0.432,0.917]) and an accuracy of 72.2% a figure only slightly lower than that of the behavioural models and indicating the limited predictive power of the DDM parameters alone.

The strongest overall discriminative performance was achieved when behavioural and DDM features were combined: the random forest trained on these joint features obtained an ROC-AUC of 0.808 ± 0.051 (95% CI [0.761,1.000]), which was higher than for any other model tested, despite its relatively modest accuracy of 71.1% and an F1-score of only 0.708. This suggests that, while behavioural features alone are the most effective single predictors of accuracy, adding DDM parameters to behavioural measures improves the model's capacity to rank and distinguish ADHD patients from controls (as reflected in AUC), even if this does not necessarily translate into higher overall classification accuracy.

Based on the sensitivity and specificity values (with 47 controls and 50 ADHD participants), the behavioral SVM correctly classified 35 controls and 37 individuals with ADHD. The DDMbased logistic regression model correctly identified 31 controls and 39 ADHD participants. The random forest model using combined features correctly classified 35 controls and 34 ADHD participants. Taken together, these results indicate that the behavioural task measures provide the strongest and most reliable basis for classification, while the DDM parameters, when combined with the behavioural data, contribute additional, complementary information that enhances overall discriminability (AUC) rather than boosting raw accuracy.

4 Discussion

By integrating behavioral data, drift diffusion modeling, and interpretable machine -learning categorization into a single analytical framework, this study aimed to describe the cognitive architecture that causes attentional problems in adults with ADHD. The most obvious and trustworthy finding is also the most straightforward: adults with ADHD did not respond more slowly than controls; rather, their performance varied more across time (Castellanos et al., 2005; Karalunas et al., 2014; Kofler et al., 2013). The ADHD group's trial-to-trial variability was substantially higher (0.130 vs. 0.098s, p = 0.0026, d = 0.62), although the groups' mean reaction times were almost identical (0.39 vs. 0.39s, p = 0.882, d = -0.03). This pattern excludes a simple theory involving general slowing in adult ADHD and instead suggests a more specific description-that is, a system which generally functions at an average level but sometimes becomes unstable (Tamm et al., 2025; Kofler et al., 2013), thus extending to adults a well-established phenomenon observed in children since the empirical evidence for adults has always been more limited.

The overall pattern of behaviors supports this explanation. Adults with ADHD made significantly more commission errors (0.55 as compared to 0.34, p < 0.001, d = 0.90) and showed a lower degree of detectability (d': 1.90 versus 2.70, p = 0.0002, d = -0.79), although there was no difference between the groups in terms of omission errors, response rate, or overall accuracy. Rather than a general deterioration in performance, these data suggest a specific impairment in inhibitory control and the capacity to distinguish between signals and noise (Faraone et al., 2015; Willcutt et al., 2005). This interpretation is supported by patterns of vigilance throughout the session: neither ADHD status nor its interaction with trial number attained statistical significance, although reaction time varied systematically during the task duration in both groups ( β = -0.003, p = 0.003). Thus, the processes of disrupting performance do not reflect a more rapid decline of attention over time. Adults with ADHD showed moment-to-moment inconsistency and disinhibition, not greater fatigue.

Drift diffusion modeling provided a mechanistic decomposition of the behavioral differences that could not be obtained from reaction-time variability alone. ADHD participants showed lower drift rates (4.499 vs.

4.782, p = 0.0224, rrb = -0.241), and shorter non-decision times (0.145 vs. 0.172 s, p = 0.0033, rrb = -0.346), while boundary separation did not differ between groups. The lower drift rate is consistent with less efficient evidence accumulation (Huang-Pollock et al., 2017; Shapiro & Huang-Pollock, 2019), whereas shorter nondecision time is consistent with altered processes outside evidence accumulation, potentially involving sensory encoding and motor-response preparation (Ratcliff & McKoon, 2008; Voss et al., 2013), and may reflect a more impulsive response style. These findings suggest that the elevated RTV observed in ADHD is associated with alterations in more than one component of the modeled response process, rather than with generalized slowing.

These two parameters also showed distinct relationships to the clinical profile. After correction, drift rate correlated with childhood symptom severity on the WURS (r = -0.302, p = 0.016), and jointly the two parameters accounted for a modest proportion of variance in ASRS (R² = 0.220, p = 0.0026) and WURS (R² = 0.213, p = 0.0035) scores. However, in a logistic regression predicting current diagnosis, only non -decision time remained an independent predictor (β = -15.064, p = 0.0073); drift rate's association with diagnosis disappeared after adjustment, even though its association with symptom history persisted. The drift rate shows how ADHD-like an individual's pattern of development appears, while the non -decision time indicates whether or not they currently meet the diagnostic criteria: these are two different mechanisms that serve separate clinical roles, not a single combined biomarker.

It is easy to misinterpret the reduced non-decision time. When combined with higher commission errors and poorer detectability, a shorter interval outside the decision-making process may seem advantageous, but it actually indicates responses that are started before stimulus analysis and motor planning are completeda pattern more consistent with impulsivity than with efficiency. It is more difficult to discount the result as an artifact of either method alone because two independent indicators-one behavioural and the other generated from a latent process model-converge on this same interpretation.

The classification analyses clarified the relative diagnostic and interpretive value of behavioral and DDM-derived measures. Behavioral features alone provided stronger classification performance than DDMonly features, whereas combined behavioral and DDM models showed modest improvements in discriminability without improving overall accuracy. Importantly, the nested exhaustive feature-subset analysis provided a more conservative estimate of diagnostic performance than the original non -nested search. Across 511 possible subsets of nine candidate features, the selected feature combination varied across the five outer folds. Mean reaction time and accuracy were selected most often, while omission rate, commission rate, and non-decision time were also selected repeatedly. The strongest individual outer-fold result was obtained for a four-feature behavioral subset (mean reaction time, accuracy, commission rate, and d ′ ), which achieved 85.0% accuracy and an ROC-AUC of 0.890 in that held-out fold. However, this performance was not consistent across folds. When the outer-fold predictions were combined, nested crossvalidation yielded 72.2% accuracy, 72.3% balanced accuracy, and an ROC-AUC of 0.772. Thus, the results support the presence of discriminative information in behavioral and computational features, but do not support a single stable optimal feature combination in this sample. Relative to pediatric literature, this adult pattern represents a divergence rather than a straightforward replication. (Huang-Pollock et al., 2017) reported that reduced drift rate largely accounted for RTV and performance deficits in children with ADHD, and (Hlutkowsky et al., 2026) similarly tied pediatric executive dysfunction in task-switching to lower drift rate; in both studies, the accumulation process itself bears the hallmark of the disorder. In the present data, drift rate was reduced and clinically associated with symptoms, but non-decision time (not drift rate) emerged as an independent predictor of diagnosis. Whether this reflects a genuine developmental change in the cognitive architecture of ADHD, or instead the specific go/no-go design of the CPTII compared with the forced-choice paradigms used in most pediatric diffusion studies, cannot be determined from this dataset. Nonetheless, the discrepancy is substantial enough that it should be acknowledged, not smoothed over.

Taken together, these findings clarify the complementary roles of behavioral and computational measures. Behavioral measures provided the strongest diagnostic information, whereas DDM parameters contributed primarily to interpretation of the processes associated with response variability. The association of drift rate with childhood symptom severity and the independent association of non -decision time with diagnostic status suggest that these parameters may capture different aspects of the ADHD phenotype.

However, the variability of feature selection across nested cross validation folds and the modest overall classification performance indicate that these computational measures should not yet be considered standalone diagnostic biomarkers (Huys et al., 2016).

At the same time, these results, while offering new understanding of the computational mechanisms underlying attentional dysfunction in adult ADHD, must be viewed in the context of several methodological constraints, which in turn highlight important avenues for future work.

5 Limitations and Future Directions

Several limitations qualify these findings. With N = 99, statistical power was insufficient to reliably detect small effects, which likely contributed to some clinical associations not surviving FDR correction. The data also come from a single sample assessed under one diagnostic protocol, so an independent adult cohort is needed before treating the drift-rate/non-decision-time dissociation as broadly generalizable. Furthermore, we fitted the DDM to response outcomes on Go trials, with omissions represented as the non-target response outcome. Although this is consistent with earlier research (Huang-Pollock et al., 2017), parameter estimates derived from go/no-go paradigms are often less reliable than those derived from specific forced-choice tasks.

Lastly, the study's crosssectional design makes it unclear if drift rate and non-decision time represent stable features or change with transient state factors, and it did not include neuroimaging or electrophysiological markers.

These limitations point the way towards important areas for future research. If the results are replicated in larger adult cohorts based at multiple sites, this would provide a more rigorous test of the dissociation of robustness and generalizability. It would also be possible to find out whether the reductions in non -decision time and drift rate correspond to particular neural markers, for example disruptions in the default-mode network, or whether they instead simply reflect behavioural effects. Moreover, longitudinal studies could ascertain whether these parameters indicate stable traits or whether they vary with the severity of symptoms, the use of medication, or the level of task engagement. Moreover, because the diagnostic utility of DDM metrics in this study was concentrated almost entirely in non-decision time, a logical next step is to test whether this parameter can delineate clinically meaningful ADHD subtypes, potentially shifting its role from an auxiliary classification feature to a candidate tool for individualized assessment and treatment planning.

6 Conclusion

The study looked at attentional dysfunction in adult ADHD by combining behavioural analysis, drift diffusion modelling, and machine-learning classification within one framework, using CPT-II data from the HYPERAKTIV dataset. Adults with ADHD were not slower than the controls but were considerably less consistent, and the drift-diffusion modelling showed that this instability was associated with two distinct components of the modeled response process: less efficient evidence accumulation and altered non-decision processes. Only non-decision time, and not the drift rate, was able to predict the diagnosis independently, whereas the drift rate instead reflected the severity of childhood symptoms, which means that these two computational parameters have distinct clinical roles and do not function as a single combined marker.

Behavioral measures provided stronger diagnostic information than DDM-only features. The nested exhaustive analysis of 511 feature subsets yielded 72.2% accuracy and an ROC-AUC of 0.772, with the selected feature combination varying across outer folds. Thus, no single feature subset can be regarded as a stable optimal classifier in the present sample. The primary contribution of the DDM is therefore not superior to diagnostic classification, but the decomposition of behavioral variability into interpretable components of the modeled decision process. Larger independent samples and external validation will be necessary to determine the generalizability and clinical utility of these computational markers.
