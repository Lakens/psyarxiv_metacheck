Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. Full text

Abstract

Although notions of a 'winning mindset' are repeatedly put forth in self-help books, podcasts, and sport psychology publications, there appears to be little to no compelling evidence that such a mindset exists. The aim of the current study was to investigate the beliefs elite athletes hold about the nature of winning and to examine whether these beliefs may suggest a winning mindset. Using a theoryinformed qualitative design, semi-structured interviews were conducted with nine athletes who had represented New Zealand across seven sports. Data were analysed using reflexive thematic analysis with a hybrid inductive-deductive approach. Results suggest that athletes conceptualise winning in two markedly different ways. Winning-is-Proving comprises beliefs that winning means beating others and not losing, whereas Winning-is-Improving comprises beliefs that winning means self-betterment and realising one's potential. These beliefs were associated with coherent patterns of goals, expectations, and attributions, providing support for their interpretation as a mindset about winning. To our knowledge, these results provide the first preliminary evidence for the existence of a winning mindset and, in doing so, lay the groundwork for research currently underway to develop and validate winning mindset as a novel psychological construct. Implications for mindset theory and sport psychology, as well as for coaches and support staff working with elite athletes, are considered.

Introduction

One of the most common pieces of advice that elite athletes receive is to 'have a winning mindset'. Over the years, an abundance of books, articles, and commentary have disseminated this rhetoric (e.g., Brat et al., 2025; Hughes, 2016; Niethe, 2024; Seversen, 2021). Yet, despite its widespread use, there is little to no convincing evidence that a distinct winning mindset exists in athletes or other populations.

Moreover, little is known about the beliefs athletes hold regarding the nature of winning itself.

Mindsets are core beliefs that people hold about a domain, topic, or category (Zion et al., 2025). Although the terms mindset and belief are often used interchangeably, mindsets are a particular class of beliefs that have motivational value and orient people to specific sets of expectations, attributions, and goals (Dweck, 2019).

In essence, mindsets help people organise and simplify complex information in ways that produce meaning (e.g., Why is this happening?), create predictions (e.g., What will happen next?), and drive action (e.g., What should I do next?). As such, their effects are not inconsequential, and mindsets can shape how people feel and act, with consequences for health, wellbeing, and performance (Crum & Zuckerman, 2017).

Mindsets have been extensively studied across a wide range of domains, from intelligence and personality (Dweck & Yeager, 2019) to stress (Crum et al., 2013), exercise (Zahrt & Crum, 2020), and even success (Allan & Rong, 2026). In the context of sport, research has focused primarily on growth and stress mindsets (McNeil et al., 2024; Spray & Warburton, 2025). Growth mindsets have been associated with greater performance and more constructive responses to success and failure (Gontijo et al., 2023; Shaffer et al., 2015). Similarly, stress-is-enhancing mindsets have been associated with more adaptive coping, greater psychological wellbeing, and lower burnout (Mansell & Turner, 2023; Shipherd et al., 2024), with intervention studies also reporting improvements in perceived performance and psychological functioning (Mansell et al., 2024; Mansell et al., 2026).

Although existing research on growth and stress mindsets in sport provides helpful insights, it does not address athletes' beliefs about the nature and meaning of winning itself. This distinction is important because an athlete may believe that ability is not fixed or that stress can be beneficial while holding quite different beliefs about winning, which could conceivably evoke changes in emotion, attention, motivation, and outcomes (Walton & Wilson, 2018).

Recent evidence suggests that the growing popularity of mindset as a concept has led to concerns about oversimplification, confusion, and inconsistent use, prompting calls for greater conceptual clarity and consistency in how mindsets are defined (see Gasteiger & Crum, 2025). These concerns are particularly relevant to the winning mindset, for which there is neither an agreed-upon definition nor clear and compelling empirical support.

More specifically, winning mindset appears to be conflated with a variety of established concepts, such as emotional intelligence, self-talk and visualisation (Nicholls, 2021), self-confidence (Lee & Hwang, 2025; Mansell, 2021), mental preparedness (Cabanda, 2021), and mental toughness (Sheard, 2012), to name a few. To further confuse the issue, several publications define winning mindset as an assortment of traits and behaviours (e.g., Beswick, 2015). For example, Niethe (2024) describes a winning mindset as comprising fearlessness, the ability to endure criticism, and a strong self-image. This description is similar in formulation to that of Bolckmans et al. (2023), who define winning mindset as an assemblage of effort, discipline, will to win, motivation, dedication to sport, and perseverance. Such definitions, while potentially inspiring, ignore the well-established body of research on mindsets (Gasteiger & Crum, 2025).

Crucially, these inconsistencies are both scientifically and practically problematic. Without alignment of terminology, winning mindset cannot be reliably distinguished from existing constructs or appropriately operationalised. Furthermore, it is difficult to establish shared practices or cohesive discourse, while claims that athletes should possess or develop a winning mindset remain unsubstantiated.

As a first step towards addressing these issues, the present research aimed to explore, in detail, the beliefs elite athletes hold about winning and to examine whether these beliefs orient their expectations, attributions, and goals, thereby suggesting a winning mindset. The study was guided by the following two research questions: RQ1: What beliefs do elite athletes hold about winning? RQ2: How do these beliefs orient athletes' expectations, attributions, and goals, and does this suggest a winning mindset?

Methods

Study design and philosophical underpinning

To address the research questions, a theory-informed qualitative design, underpinned by a critical realist ontology and an interpretivist epistemology, was selected (Sparkes & Smith, 2013). A critical realist ontology assumes that reality exists independently of human perceptions, while recognising that knowledge of reality is inevitably mediated by social structures, values, and sociocultural contexts (Braun & Clarke, 2021a).

Critical realism further posits a stratified reality in which individuals' experiences and interpretations provide partial access to underlying processes that may help explain them (Bhaskar, 2013). An interpretivist epistemology complements this position by asserting that knowledge is situated, partial, and shaped by the meanings individuals assign to their experiences (Atkinson, 2011). Together, these commitments call for an approach that is interpretive, context-sensitive, and reflexive. Correspondingly, semistructured interviews were selected for their suitability in exploring athletes' beliefs about winning while providing flexibility to probe participants' responses in depth (Kallio et al., 2016; Kim et al., 2017). Data were analysed using Braun and Clarke's (2022) reflexive thematic analysis with a hybrid inductive-deductive approach. This aligned with interpretivism by recognising the researcher's active role in interpreting participants' comments and interview responses, and with critical realism by treating those accounts as necessarily partial accounts of athletes' beliefs about winning and how those beliefs function. In addition, the hybrid approach allowed for the incorporation of the theoretical framework described below while affording the flexibility to interpret participants' responses inductively (Swain, 2018).

Theoretical framework

The Meaning System component of the generic mindset framework developed by the Stanford Mind & Body Lab (Figure 1) provided the theoretical basis for this investigation. This framework 'can be applied to a wide-variety of different mindsets'

Researcher's positionality

The research team was composed of two researchers with experience in sport and psychological research. The lead author had expertise in mindset science and peakperformance psychology, while the second author, an Honours researcher and lifelong athlete, conducted the interviews. The researchers therefore occupied different 'insider' and 'outsider' positions (Guba & Lincoln, 1994; Pezalla et al., 2012). Overall, the team's positionality enabled reflexive practice through ongoing discussion, critical examination of assumptions, and consideration of alternative interpretations throughout the analytic process.

Participants

This study was reviewed and approved by the Human Research Ethics Committee of the University of Canterbury (Ref: HREC 2023/05/BL). Athletes were recruited through purposive sampling from professional networks and via snowball sampling. Elite athletes were defined as those who had represented New Zealand in international competition (adapted from Elwood et al., 2021). Eligible participants were thus current or retired athletes aged 18 years or older who met this definition. Data were collected from N = 9 athletes. The sample comprised six female and three male athletes, with one participant from rowing, running, rugby, motocross, and triathlon, and two from netball and cycling. Pseudonyms are used in the results to protect participant anonymity.

Data collection procedure

Prior to taking part, participants received detailed study information and provided written informed consent. Each participant completed a single online, audio-recorded interview via Microsoft Teams™, lasting 45-65 minutes, and received a $20 supermarket voucher as a token of appreciation.

Consistent with the study's critical realist and interpretivist positioning, the Verbatim transcripts were produced for each interview and returned to participants for review. No participants requested changes or withdrew after completing an interview. In keeping with reflexive thematic analysis, data adequacy was judged by the richness of the dataset and its capacity to address the research questions (Braun & Clarke, 2021b).

Data quality and trustworthiness

Three pretest interviews were conducted before the main interviews to assess the clarity, relevance, and sequencing of the questions. Minor refinements were subsequently made to the interview guide, and the pretest data were excluded from the analysis.

Trustworthiness was supported through transparency and reflexivity throughout the research process. Credibility was strengthened by allowing participants sufficient opportunity to elaborate on their accounts and presenting quotations that illustrate the relationship between participants' reports and the researchers' interpretations.

Dependability was promoted by maintaining records of methodological and analytic decisions. Confirmability was addressed by making the researchers' interpretive roles and assumptions explicit and critically reflecting on their influence throughout the analysis. Transferability was facilitated by reporting relevant participant characteristics, alongside the research context, data-collection procedures, theoretical framework, and methodological approach, enabling readers to judge the relevance of the findings to other contexts.

Data analysis

Transcripts were analysed using Braun and Clarke's (2006, 2022) six-phase process for reflexive thematic analysis, with a hybrid inductive-deductive approach. The six phases comprised familiarisation with the data, producing initial codes, generating themes, reviewing and developing themes, defining and naming themes, and producing the report. Importantly, these phases were treated as iterative and recursive (Braun & Clarke, 2022).

Familiarisation involved repeatedly listening to each interview recording and reading the corresponding transcript. Brief notes capturing initial impressions, responses, and emerging ideas were recorded to support reflexivity. The second author conducted sentence-by-sentence coding in Microsoft Word, with codes considered in relation to the research questions. RQ1 was addressed predominantly inductively, with codes organised into themes. For RQ2, the components specified by the theoretical framework provided the deductive structure for analysis, while the specific subthemes were developed inductively from participants' reports (Swain, 2018). In both cases, interpretations were rooted firmly in participants' accounts. The first author reviewed and refined the codes and developing themes by combining overlapping ideas, distinguishing conceptually different patterns, and considering alternative interpretations. The authors discussed the analysis as part of the reflexive process, critically considering assumptions and interpretations rather than seeking coding agreement. Finally, themes and subthemes were defined and named, and representative quotations were selected to illustrate their meaning and relevance to the research questions (Braun & Clarke, 2006 , 2022).

Results

Throughout the interviews, athletes articulated different beliefs about the nature of winning and what it meant within their lives. We first present four beliefs about winning: realising potential, not losing, self-betterment, and beating others. We then examine how these beliefs were reflected in athletes' goals, expectations, and attributions, before considering whether these findings are indicative of a mindset about winning.

Findings

Winning is realising potential

For some athletes, winning was about realising one's potential. For those who articulated this belief, winning meant expressing their abilities, talents, and capabilities as fully as possible: We used to just try to win everything . . . and it sort of becomes more motivation to do more, and then it sort of becomes a cycle. Later on down the track . . . it's just a relief, I guess, sometimes, to not lose.

For other athletes like Isabella, the belief that winning is not losing appeared to influence her performance, such that her focus shifted from her own game towards preventing others from winning: In this respect, winning meant maintaining one's position at the top and preventing others from winning, rather than necessarily improving one's own performance.

Winning is self-betterment

Many of the athletes interviewed expressed a belief that winning is self-betterment.

Those who communicated this belief saw winning as becoming better over time. Here, improvement itself could constitute winning. For instance, Amelia expressed this belief as follows: 'I think [winning] is very much always bettering myself, whether it's athletically or academically or things like that.' Amelia went on to say that 'I feel like it's a never-ending cycle where you're bettering yourself and then you just keep moving the goal posts.' A similar view of winning was given by Zoe, who said: '[Winning] is achieving growth, I think. So you want to get the growth, and that is the win in itself, experiencing the growth and just, yeah, getting better with everything you do. Don't want to go backwards.' Winning, in this sense, means becoming better, with the experience of growth itself representing a win.

Winning is beating others

Several athletes articulated a belief that winning meant beating others and coming out ahead of the competition. For example, Liam said: 'It [winning] means you're the best.

. . You just want to win, you just want to see your name on the trophy and get the prizes. I used to race and beat people so I could get the win.' Similarly, Isabella stated that winning meant coming out ahead of others: 'If I'm honest, I think winning is coming out ahead of others, whether that's, um, finishing first or placing . . . in the sense [that], for you to win, other people have to have lost.'

In this respect, winning was defined by defeating others: to win was to come out ahead of someone else, such that someone else had necessarily lost.

Shared facets of beliefs about winning

Several recurring facets of beliefs about winning were evident across athletes. Winning was consistently described as positive and desirable; winning extended beyond sport into other areas of life; and, for some athletes, their beliefs about winning changed over the course of their careers.

Winning is inherently positive and desirable

One thing stressed by all athletes was that winning itself was inherently positive and desirable. Athletes consistently described winning as a positive, valued experience that should be pursued. When asked if she had ever experienced negative or unexpected consequences as a result of winning, Isabella said: 'No . . . not at all . . . I think winning has, in my history, been a pretty, yeah, positive experience.' A similar response was expressed by Zoe, who described winning as 'globally positive.' Sophia described it as 'really cool. I still think the feeling that you get from winning is the same as what you got when you were a kid. I still have a desire to win in what I'm doing.' This belief was also described by Steve, who said that 'For me personally, I see it [winning] as inherently positive. I think winning reinforces all the good things about sport.' Likewise, Amelia stated that '[Winning] is something I always strive for, and I don't think it should be avoided.'

Beliefs about winning extend beyond sport

Many athletes emphasised that their beliefs about winning went beyond sport, suggesting that their beliefs about winning were not confined to sport but extended into other areas of life. For example, Steve felt that his view of winning was the same outside of his sport: 'I guess that yeah it applies completely across work and sport. So whether it be winning a game or completing a project, I think that winning is the same.' Likewise, Zoe explained that her notion of winning extended to her career: I think from a young age I've always wanted to win, so it's kind of been a wanting aspect in everything I do as well. Could I keep being better at that? Whether that be my desk job, I kind of want to be the best at what I do in that aspect, but also in high performance sport.

Going further, Isabella highlighted how winning was an organising principle in her life: I think about it not just as a competition or something, but it can also be just winning in, I'd say, winning in life. Winning in life always orientates me . . . I have goals and right across all the types of things you do, career, personal life, acquisition, yeah, all of the financial things, all of that type of thing. It's how I think about winning is the same.

Shifting beliefs about winning

Several athletes explained how their beliefs about winning shifted over their careers.

Importantly, these shifts in beliefs were described as fundamental changes in how winning was conceptualised, pursued, and evaluated.

When asked if her beliefs about winning had changed over the course of her career, Chloe said: I definitely [did] not always believe that winning was about achieving my potential. I think when I was [in sport] in school, I felt like I had to win. In terms, like, I wanted to be the best and then, I had a [sport] partner who kind of made the point that doing your best is the best that you can do anyway, and so then I kind of shifted my mindset to like, I actually just want to enjoy this and do my best. And whatever that result is, will be. So I definitely shifted from winning to be better than others to if I win, then that's a result of the hard work I've put in, but it doesn't matter.

A shift in beliefs about winning was also identified by Amelia:

My belief about winning has definitely changed. Um, winning was always kind of high pressure in terms of you need to get to the final placing to get to the next competition or to keep a contract, always fighting to qualify or fighting to be seen as the winners . . . So I guess it moved . . . It [became] more how far can I go in terms of international racing? How fast can I go? Like what is my physical limit?

And it shifted your outlook, and I guess your expectation shifts with that.

Similarly, Steve explained how his belief about winning changed over time as a result of experience: Yeah, it used to be sort of the, yeah, want to win at all cost, but now it's as I've got a bit older, and experienced [how I view] winning has changed. It's setting out to develop and achieve different things throughout the game . . . I think, yeah, the development is winning.

Hence, it appeared that when athletes' beliefs about winning changed, so too did the goals and expectations associated with winning.

Goals, expectations, and attributions

Athletes' beliefs about winning were reflected in differences in the goals they pursued, the outcomes they expected, and the attributions they made. In this section, we detail the key contrasts between those who expressed proving and improving beliefs.

Goals

Those who communicated proving-oriented beliefs described goals directed towards proving dominance and continuing to win. Several athletes noted that the goal of proving dominance was pursued even when superiority over competitors was apparent.

Harrison explained: 'There'd be times where you go to races and that you're overly dominant, and you still, instead of just taking it easy, you still go and win, to obliterate the competition . . . nothing less than complete domination was acceptable.' Harrison's quote suggests that the goal was not simply to perform well enough to win, but to emphasise his dominance over the competition.

For others, the goal was not simply to win, but to continue winning. Liam explained: 'When you're winning, you're at the top . . . the goal is to keep winning. I wouldn't care how it affected anyone else, or how I did it, or anything.' Hence, winning once was insufficient; being at the top created a position that subsequently had to be defended.

In addition, athletes who reported proving-oriented beliefs described pursuing goals relating to meeting others' expectations. When asked about her goals, Lucy said:

I'd say I would probably-this is awful-but I probably would care more about having a really shit race and what people would think about that than potentially winning. You know, when you're representing your country, you want to do that proud . . . we also had it from the coach, you know, saying, well, if you don't perform or you don't win, then that's you. You're not at the next competition, and you could not get your funding for the next year.

Goals therefore included not only winning, but also meeting the expectations of others and avoiding the consequences associated with failing to meet them.

In contrast, those who expressed improving-oriented beliefs described goals focused on growth and meeting their own expectations. As Amelia explained:

My goal, for sure. I think [is that] I'm always trying to grow, to better myself in terms of what could I improve on to then make me go better. I never really celebrated my wins at the time, more focused on what I could improve on if I'm honest. Sophia described how, later in her career, she developed different goals for winning when playing in a less successful team and, in turn, appeared to adopt a wider definition of winning: I was playing for a [sports] team [later in career] that wasn't that successful. So, as I say, I didn't get to experience a lot of winning, but I had my own intrinsic goals, and I was still winning in other ways . . . you sort of try and have not performancerelated goals.

Expectations

In speaking about expectations, athletes commonly discussed expectations extending beyond anticipated sporting outcomes to include how they expected to think, evaluate their performance, and feel in relation to winning and losing.

Those who communicated proving-oriented beliefs expected to evaluate their performance through comparison with others. Harrison explained: You have to feel like you're always fighting someone. I used to compare myself to [name of rival] a lot, in training, in the performance . . . he wanted to win badly, I want to win badly. So it's kind of you constantly compare.

Performance was therefore expected to be evaluated relative to other athletes.

On the other hand, those who expressed improving-oriented beliefs expected to evaluate performance against their own standards and to experience satisfaction with performance even without placing first or securing a podium finish. For example, Chloe explained:

My expectation going in is I just want to live up to my own standards and being my best . . . I think just if we, like, go out and have a race that we have executed really well, and performed how we knew we were capable of performing, I think is probably the most gratifying thing that I would experience. Then if you win with that, it's really special.

Satisfaction was therefore not contingent on coming first or obtaining a podium finish; athletes could experience gratification from meeting their own standards and performing to their perceived capabilities, with outcomes of this kind representing an additional rather than necessary condition for satisfaction.

Those who expressed proving-oriented beliefs, by contrast, expected satisfaction with performance to be contingent on winning. For example, Liam said: Yeah, I think there's that you start winning. There's the expectation that you're going to keep winning from yourself, and from sponsors and coaches and stuff. So that, and then when you do [win] you're gonna be happy, when you don't, yeah, it can be upsetting for everyone. Furthermore, losing was expected to decrease motivation. Lucy explained that failing to win could lead to reduced motivation and questioning the purpose of continuing: I kind of really struggle when I've lost or not medalled, and find myself in quite a kind of a dark hole kind of thing [and] question why I'm doing it . . . after each loss it gets harder and harder I'll lose motivation and start thinking, what's the point and [I] have considered quitting a number of times.

Those who expressed improving-oriented beliefs also expected losing to have positive psychological effects. For example, Sophia emphasised:

Because there are all these things that people talk about that losing will give you, you know, resilience, the ability to keep going when the going is tough, and, you know, being motivated by things other than winning, like all of that sort of stuff.

So, like, I definitely expected all of that.

All athletes expected to take lessons from losing. Zoe described how losing makes one a better athlete: Similarly, Harrison also felt that learning was an expectation even when the goal was to prove dominance: 'Every single race, irrespective of if you do win or lose, there's always a quite big diagnostic process . . . you can't keep winning if you don't keep learning how to be the best.'

Learning from losing therefore did not distinguish proving-oriented from improving-oriented beliefs. Rather, the difference lay in the purpose that learning served: in improving-oriented accounts, learning was valued as part of development itself, whereas in proving-oriented accounts, it could serve the continued pursuit of preeminence.

Attributions

Athletes who reported proving-oriented beliefs attributed losing to weakness and not being good enough. Liam explained, 'If I lost, I'd think it was a weakness, either mentally or physically'. Likewise, Isabella stated that 'You're basically not as good as them . . . sometimes you're just not good enough'. In this sense, losing was attributed to perceived capability, rather than to particular aspects of their performance that could be identified and addressed.

Attributions of losing also involved assigning blame to other people. Lucy explained how losing could lead to blame being directed towards teammates or referees:

When you're losing, people are down, generally blaming your teammates, or perhaps blaming the referee for not winning, or the [position] for missing, or whatever. Whereas when you win, you forget about all those things and you just remember the outcome. Conversely, those who reported improving-oriented beliefs attributed losing to specific performance gaps that needed to be addressed. For instance, when asked about her response to losing, Amelia said: 'I think it certainly shows your gaps or mistakes you've made. That you've gotta take forward to the next race or in your training. So I definitely think not winning has been helpful.'

Zoe described this process in greater detail: So there's lots of little gems that can come from a loss . . . it might just be one little micro skill or one little moment in the game that was the breakdown . . . and usually I don't sleep the night of a game, so I would rewatch the game again and then I'd clip stuff that I'm like, I can be better at that next time. This was not an absence of recognition on the part of the athlete that she had lost. Zoe still identified shortcomings in her performance; however, the attribution was Sophia highlighted the role of others: 'I wasn't winning on my own. These shared attributions indicate that preparation, effort, execution, and support were common across both sets of beliefs; what differed was the meaning of winning itself and the purpose these efforts served.

Decoding the winning mindset

Taken together, the ways in which beliefs about winning were connected to athletes' goals, expectations, and attributions support our interpretation that these beliefs functioned as a mindset about winning (see Table 1 for a synthesis). Within this mindset, two contrasting sets of beliefs were evident. Winning-is-Proving (WIP) encompassed beliefs that winning meant beating others and not losing. While the former emphasises dominating the competition and the latter maintaining one's position at the top, both beliefs about winning are more akin to victory, which, in this sense, can be defined as a decisive triumph over an enemy, rival, or competitor (HarperCollins, n.d.-a, n.d.-b). Associated goals and expectations emphasised demonstrating dominance, continuing to win, meeting others' expectations, and evaluating satisfaction in relation to winning, while losing was attributed to weakness, not being good enough, or the actions of others and external forces.

In contrast, Winning-is-Improving (WII) encompassed beliefs that winning meant realising potential and self-betterment. While the former emphasises expressing one's capabilities and the latter ongoing improvement, both beliefs about winning are more akin to accomplishment, which, in this sense, can be defined as something successfully achieved through repeated effort, focus, and dedication (Cambridge University Press, n.d.), without necessarily requiring an adversary (HarperCollins, n.d.b). Associated goals and expectations emphasised development, personal standards, and satisfaction that was not contingent on winning a competition or placing in an event, while losing was attributed to identifiable gaps in performance that could subsequently be addressed.

Importantly, learning from loss was evident across both sets of beliefs, as were attributions of winning an event or competition to preparation, effort, execution, and the support of others.

These shared features suggest that, in themselves, learning from loss and explanations for winning did not distinguish WIP from WII. Rather, the distinction lay in what athletes believed about winning itself and the goals, expectations, and attributions associated with those beliefs. In this sense, beliefs about winning constituted a mindset, while together this mindset and its associated goals, expectations, and attributions formed the broader meaning system through which athletes experienced winning and losing. winning rather than being confined to sport (Crum et al., 2013).

A particularly noteworthy finding was that several athletes recalled that their beliefs about winning had shifted over the course of their careers through experience, reflection, or the influence of others, with associated changes in their goals and expectations. This finding is consistent with prior research suggesting that mindsets can change through exposure to new information, messages from influential others, or conscious choice (Gasteiger & Crum, 2025), lending further support to the interpretation that these beliefs about winning are indicative of a mindset.

Another noteworthy finding pertains to the goals athletes pursued. Existing studies of athletes' goal orientations emphasise task and ego orientations, two main types of goals related to Achievement Goal Theory (AGT; Nicholls, 1984), which reflect how athletes judge whether they have demonstrated competence or feel successful in sport. Task-oriented athletes feel successful when mastering challenges, whereas ego-oriented athletes feel successful when they perform better than others (Roberts et al., 1998).

Not surprisingly, given this conceptual proximity, WIP beliefs were more closely associated with ego goals, whereas WII beliefs were more closely associated with task goals. However, our data indicate that mixed associations are also possible.

For example, an athlete may feel successful when overcoming challenges or mastering a skill (i.e., task orientation), while simultaneously believing that winning means defeating a rival and proving they are the best (Winning-is-Proving). Consistent with this logic, at least one participant made explicit how training, execution, and effort served as a means to win: to beat the competition and prove they were the best.

One possible explanation for this mixed pattern is that winning mindset operates at a higher level of abstraction than achievement goal orientation, capturing broad, organising beliefs about the nature and meaning of winning (Yielder et al., 2026; Zion et al., 2025). Accordingly, research is underway to develop a measure of winning mindset and to empirically test its relationship with task and ego goal orientations.

Practical and theoretical implications

Although preliminary, our results may be valuable to coaches promoting task-involving motivational climates that encourage learning and continual improvement (Sarrazin et al., 2001). Winning mindset may, for reasons described above, offer a complementary and potentially valuable framework for coaches and support staff to understand what winning itself means to the athlete and how that mindset may orient goals and behaviours.

Moreover, our findings advance mindset theory by providing evidence for a new mindset category: mindsets about winning. Further, our findings add to the growing body of sport psychology literature demonstrating the importance of beliefs, mindsets, and other psychological factors in how elite athletes approach winning (Hoffmann et al., 2026; Ittlinger et al., 2025; Kreiner-Phillips & Orlick, 1993). More particularly, our results are the first to shed light on elite athletes' beliefs about the nature of winning in general and provide initial empirical support for the existence of a distinct winning mindset.

Limitations and future directions

One key limitation of this research is that our sample was largely female and comprised nine elite athletes representing seven sports from New Zealand. Thus, we are currently unable to establish the prevalence of WIP or WII or determine whether the same sets of beliefs occur across countries, competitive levels, sports, or other domains. Future research is therefore needed to test the proposed winning mindset in larger and more diverse samples and determine whether other populations hold similar or different beliefs about winning.

Another limitation lies in the study's single-interview qualitative design.

Although conducive to addressing our research questions, it cannot establish temporal or causal relationships between an athlete's winning mindset and their responses to winning and losing. It will therefore be important for future research to determine whether WIP and WII predict affective, motivational, attributional, and behavioural responses following actual wins and losses, while longitudinal work examining whether an athlete's winning mindset can change across seasons, career stages, and major sporting transitions is also warranted.

Conclusion

Notions of a 'winning mindset' abound in popular talks, self-help books, podcasts, and sport psychology publications, yet despite its widespread use, there is little to no compelling empirical data to support the existence of such a mindset. We suggest that the present study provides the first preliminary evidence of a winning mindset among elite athletes and, in so doing, establishes the foundation for research currently underway to develop and validate winning mindset as a novel psychological construct.
