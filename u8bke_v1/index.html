To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. Abstract

This study investigates the acquisition of strong pronouns in English by French and German speakers. Adopting a feature reassembly approach to the acquisition task, we examine differences in the difficulty of adding and removing features from transferred feature bundles. Additionally, we explore the effects of (lexical and construction) frequency and (general and specific) variability in the input on the acquisition process. In an acceptability judgement task, participants were tested on their acceptance of nominative and accusative 1 st and 3 rd person pronouns in three types of fragment response. The results provide evidence for the preemption problem: removing a transferred feature was more difficult than adding a feature. Additionally, lexical frequency (person), but not construction frequency (fragment type), impacted the reassembly process, with higher frequency associated with faster reassembly. Finally, specific variability in the input (fragment type), but not more general variability (variability in strong pronouns across environments), increased the difficulty of the acquisition task.

Introduction

In recent years, the Feature Reassembly Hypothesis (Lardiere 2008 (Lardiere , 2009) ) has emerged as one of the primary ways of accounting for learnability issues in second language (L2) acquisition. That is, it provides predictions and explanations for which learning situations are likely to present particularly challenging tasks for the L2 acquirer by comparing the featural makeup of lexical items in the first language (L1) to those in the L2. A significant body of support has accrued for this approach, particularly regarding the difficulty of removing a feature from a transferred feature bundle compared to adding a novel feature (e.g., Cho and Slabakova 2015; Guo 2022; Smeets 2024; Su 2019; Jimenez et al. 2024). However, there are outstanding questions regarding the effects of frequency and input variability on the reassembly process.

In this paper, we address how the type of frequency (lexical vs. construction), as well as the degree of variability, affect the reassembly acquisition task. To do so, we examine the acquisition of strong pronouns in fragment responses by advanced L1 German and French learners of English. To determine the effect of variability and construction frequency, we compare participants' behavior in three fragment contexts that differ in frequency and variability of case assignment. We also examine the effects of the underlying position of the fragment response (subject vs. object), for interpreting transfer and reassembly, and person (1 st vs. 3 rd ), to investigate lexical frequency effects.

Second Language Feature Reassembly

Acquiring the functional morphology of an L2 has been argued to be (one of) the most difficult aspect(s) of the acquisition process (Lardiere 2007; Slabakova 2008 Slabakova , 2013 Slabakova , 2019)). The reason that functional morphology proves to be so difficult is because cross-linguistic syntactic differences can be reduced to the different feature make-ups of functional categories, i.e. these differences are due to the sets, or bundles, of features that are encoded by a given lexical item. The task for the L2 learner, then, is to reconfigure the features associated with lexical items from the feature bundles associated with the L1 lexical item to the configuration appropriate for the L2 (Lardiere 2008 (Lardiere , 2009)). Therefore, feature reassembly takes as its starting point (full) transfer (Schwartz and Sprouse 1996; Sprouse 2006). That is, the initial state of L2 acquisition is the current state of the L1 grammar at the onset of acquisition. As such, the features associated with individual lexical items are transferred without the phonological matrices that label them, and learners map the lexical items in the L2 input to those that were transferred. Inevitably, this transfer/mapping leads to non-target-like feature bundles, which must be reassembled in response to positive input to match the L2 patterns.

A growing body of research has addressed the predictions of the Feature Reassembly Hypothesis, with one of the primary findings being that the difficulty of fine-tuning the feature bundles depends in large part on whether a feature needs to be added or removed from the transferred representation, as well as whether the feature that needs to be added is used elsewhere in the L1 system (e.g., Cho and Slabakova 2015; Guo 2022; Smeets 2024; Su 2019; Jimenez et al. 2024; and others). Thus, the difficulty hierarchy in (1) surfaces, where acquisition tasks to the left are easier than those to the right: (1) Add a feature not used in L1 > Add a feature used elsewhere in L1 > Remove a feature

The heightened difficulty of removing a feature can be linked to the well-known problem of preemption (Trahey and White 1993; White 2003); that is, how an L2 acquirer can remove some property from the grammar that is initially posited, especially due to transfer, if nothing in the target input rules it out (i.e., no negative evidence) and the property is not subject to instruction. As a seminal example, Trahey and White (1993) investigated adverb placement by L1 French learners of English. In French, the finite verb moves to TP, resulting in adverbs following the verb and preceding any direct object. In English, however, the finite verb stays in VP, so adverbs precede the verb. Trahey & White found that learners provided with positive evidence for the lack of verb movement in English successfully accepted and produced pre-verbal adverbs. Nevertheless, they continued to accept and produce post-verbal adverbs as well. That is, while the learners were able to add the grammatical word order to their grammar, they were unable to remove the ungrammatical option. The same principle holds for contexts involving feature reassembly.

In addition to the hierarchy above, other learnability considerations can further affect the difficulty of feature reassembly. For example, Slabakova (2019) identifies redundancy, opacity, and (construction) frequency as factors that additionally affect the difficulty of acquiring the functional morphology of an L2. "Detectability" has also been argued to play a role in the difficulty of the reassembly process (Lardiere 2017). For example, acquiring the correct feature bundles when the input displays variability and/or optionality is much harder than when there is no variability, and deeper features within featural co-occurrence hierarchies that need to be added to a feature bundle are harder to detect (Hwang and Lardiere 2013; Lee and Lardiere 2019). Here, we focus on frequency and input variability.

Only a few studies have examined frequency effects in the context of feature reassembly, with mixed findings (Smeets 2024; Thane 2023; Wang and Chan 2021). Smeets (2024) examined the acquisition of clitic left dislocation by L1 Romanian/L2 Italian and L1 Italian/L2 Romanian speakers. In both learner groups a feature needed to be added and removed from the transferred feature bundles, [+specific] and [+anaphor], and vice versa. Smeets additionally considered the role of construction frequency, between topic and focus fronting, in the reassembly process, finding that preemption of a transferred feature from a bundle was harder than adding a new feature, and frequency did not affect this process. Others, however, do find that frequency affects the reassembly process, particularly when examining lexical frequency (Thane 2023; Wang and Chan 2021). For example, Wang and Chan (2021) investigated the addition of the [±realis] feature associated with negation and aspect markers in L2 Chinese by L1 English speakers. They find that learners can reassemble this feature correctly, but they display more difficulty with less frequent aspect markers. Based on these studies, it is possible that there are different effects for construction frequency on the one hand and lexical frequency on the other. However, there are still too few studies to make a definitive conclusion. Moreover, it is important to examine the effects of both types of frequency for the same property, among the same learners, to control for potential confounds that arise from comparing multiple (unrelated) properties.

Less work has addressed the effect of variability and/or optionality in the input on the feature reassembly process. As evidence that input variability results in a more difficult reassembly process, Lardiere (2017) discusses the acquisition of Turkish accusative case marking and scrambling by L1 English speakers (e.g., Gürel 2000; Papadopoulou et al. 2011; among others). In Turkish, accusative case is marked on the direct object when it is [+specific], but there is no overt morpheme when it is [-specific]. This featural makeup contrasts with languages like English and Greek which do not have morphemes that encode specificity, and where case can be present, it is required. That is, from the perspective of the learner grammar, accusative appears to be optional prior to establishing specificity as the licensing feature. Therefore, the apparent, from the view of the L1, optionality of the Turkish accusative case morpheme might present a learnability challenge, and indeed, learners of Turkish have been found to display lower accuracy in the use of this morpheme, even at higher proficiencies.

True, as opposed to apparent, optionality/variability in the L2 input has also been found to affect feature reassembly. Hwang and Lardiere (2013) investigated the acquisition of plural marking in Korean by English learners. While plural marking is required for both human and non-human quantified nouns in English, plural marking is impossible for non-human nouns and optional for human nouns in Korean. They found that advanced learners had acquired the restriction on the pluralization of non-human nouns, but learners had also overgeneralized that restriction to human nouns, despite the possibility of such plurals in the L1. As such, the variable nature of the input may have increased the difficulty of the reassembly task. Still, variability/optionality can occur at several levels of analysis, from very narrow syntactic environments to broadly throughout the system. No studies have investigated to what extent there are differences between the effects of different degrees of variability within a particular grammar on the reassembly process.

Given the discussion above, several factors have been argued to affect the feature reassembly process. First, preemption is more difficult than adding a feature, all other factors being equal. Second, lexical frequency, but not construction frequency, affects the ease of reassembly. Third, variability/optionality in the input increases the difficulty of the task, but it is not clear whether the level of variability associated with a given property changes the degree of difficulty for the reassembly process. However, more research is needed to explore these findings. Additionally, research is needed that examines all three factors during the acquisition of the same property to determine how these factors interact. In this study we address these gaps by examining the acquisition of features encoded in strong pronouns in the L2 English of L1 French and German speakers.

Strong Pronouns and Environments

Languages often partition pronouns into various types, such as clitics, weak/deficient pronouns, and strong pronouns (Cardinaletti and Starke 1995; Grohmann 2000; cf. Zwicky 1977; Zwart 1991). One approach to the distinction between these types of pronouns is morphosyntactic, where weak/deficient pronouns lack projections that are present in strong pronouns, and clitics lack even further projections than the weak/deficient pronouns (Cardinaletti 1994; Cardinaletti and Starke 1999). We follow Caha (2024) who translates this morphosyntactic account to feature assemblies of individual pronouns, where strong pronouns contain additional features. Furthermore, we are only concerned with the distinction between strong pronouns on the one hand and weak/deficient pronouns on the other here.

An important analytical question, then, is how strong pronouns can be distinguished from the other types. First, strong pronouns may be lexicalized by a unique form, though not all languages make use of such a mechanism. Second, certain environments cross-linguistically require the use of strong pronouns (henceforth, strong environments). Finally, in some languages strong pronouns can occur with more word order options than weak and clitic pronouns. For example, in German a strong pronoun can occur before or after an adverb, as well in a focused position, but weak and clitic pronouns may only occur in a subset of these positions (2a-c; Grohmann 2000, 5-6). We turn now to the acquisition task for L1 German and L1 French learners of L2 English. Assuming Full Transfer (Schwartz and Sprouse 1996) and/or Relexification (Sprouse 2006), the feature bundles will be transferred initially, minus the phonological labels associated with them. The learner then tries to map these bundles to forms heard in the input. We expect that German ich and er would map to English I and he, and that mich and ihn would map to me and him. For French, we expect that initially je and il map to I and he, that me and le map to me and him, and that moi and lui remain unmapped but available. However, after encountering the use of me and him in strong environments, moi and lui will be mapped to those forms as well (i.e., either homophonous lexical entries are created or the two feature assemblies are merged).

Following the transfer and mapping phase, the transferred feature bundles will prove nontarget-like, so they will need to undergo feature reassembly (Lardiere 2009). For the L1 German learners, reassembly will consist of removing the [ROB] feature from I and he. For the L1 French learners, reassembly will consist of adding the [STR] feature to I and he. Neither group is expected to require reassembly for me and him. Additionally, following the microparametric approach to strong pronouns (Caha 2024), this adding and subtracting of features will need to be completed for each pronoun individually, rather than in one step. Based on previous research, we predict that the L1 French learners should converge on the target grammar faster than the L1 German learners, because adding features is typically found to be easier than removing features (e.g., Smeets 2024; Su 2019). This prediction is in line with the problem of preemption in L2 acquisition, where positive evidence alone would not rule out an initially posited feature assembly (e.g., Trahey and White 1993; White 2003). However, note that the task for the French learners is also difficult, based on the hierarchy in (1), because the feature that must be added is used in the L1 in other lexical items. Moreover, additional factors, such as frequency, may also be expected to interact with this addition/subtraction process (e.g., Slabakova 2019; Thane 2023; cf. Smeets 2024).

Finally, we return to the acquisition task for L2 English learners. While both L1 German and L1 French learners of English would initially assume that all strong environments select robust pronouns, consistent with their L1s, upon being confronted with the use of gracile pronouns in some strong environments, the learners then need to determine which type of strong environment fragment responses are in English, i.e., robust-selecting, gracile-selecting, or variable-selecting. Additionally, once the learners notice that embedding a strong environment within a robust-selecting environment can create a variable-selecting environment, learners would also then need to determine whether 'only' and 'not' are the types of strong environments that allow variation when embedded in other strong environments. While all the evidence will be that fragment responses, including with embedded 'only', are robust-selecting environments, the evidence for embedded 'not' will primarily consist of robust pronouns, despite its variableselecting nature. Still, both L1 German and L1 French learners have the same acquisition task regarding the various facets of strong environments; that is, transfer from their L1s will lead learners to expect all strong environments to be robust-selecting, but the learners will need to learn that (some) strong environments can select other types of pronouns.

Materials and Methods

In this study we address the acquisition of strong pronouns in fragment responses by L1 French and German learners of L2 English. We address the following research questions: 1.

Is preemption more difficult than adding a feature found in other lexical items? In line with previous studies (e.g., Smeets 2024; Su 2019; Jimenez et al. 2024), we predict that preemption will be more difficult than adding a feature. If so, L1 German learners of English will be less accurate than L1 French learners. That is, L1 German learners will display greater sensitivity to the underlying position (subject/object) of the fragment response than L1 French learners or L1 English speakers, evidenced by the L1 German learners accepting the use of nominative case with subjects in illicit contexts.

2. How does frequency (lexical vs. construction) affect the acquisition process when feature reassembly is necessary? The findings in the literature are mixed regarding the effect of frequency (e.g., Smeets 2024; Thane 2023; Wang and Chan 2021). One possibility is that the mixed findings are due to distinct effects for lexical frequency and construction frequency. If lexical frequency plays a role in the feature reassembly process, we expect to see differences according to person among the pronouns. Specifically, because 1 st person strong pronouns occur more frequently than 3 rd person strong pronouns in the input in general, reassembly for the 1 st person pronouns may occur more quickly, resulting in greater accuracy. If construction frequency plays a role, we expect to see effects of the type of fragment response (basic vs. containing 'only'). That is, since basic fragments are more frequent than fragments containing 'only', participants should be more accurate with basic fragments.

3. How does variability (general vs. specific) in the input affect the acquisition process when feature reassembly is necessary?

Strong environments display variability along two dimensions. First, there is general variability in that some environments select robust pronouns, others select gracile pronouns, and still others optionally select either type. If this general variability affects convergence on the target grammar, strong subject pronouns with nominative case may be harder to acquire for both L1 German and L1 French learners; that is, both groups should be less target-like than the L1 English speakers regardless of the type of fragment. There is also context-specific variability when considering the environments that select either type of strong pronoun. If this type of variability affects the acquisition process, acquiring the use of strong pronouns in fragments containing 'not' should be more difficult than in basic fragments and fragments containing 'only', which never display variability. It is possible that this difficulty in the 'not' fragments will surface as rejection of nominative subjects due to some type of overgeneralization, as was found in Hwang and Lardiere (2013).

Participants

We collected data from 30 L1 English, 35 L1 German/L2 English, and 35 L1 French/L2 English participants. The participants were recruited via Prolific and provided compensation for their time. At the time of testing, participants lived in the United States or United Kingdom (L1 English), Germany or Austria (L1 German), and France (L1 French). To participate in the study, they were required to be at least 18 years old. Moreover, participants in the L1 German and L1 French groups were required to have acquired English as their L2, i.e., as the first language after their L1; they were not, however, restricted from having further sequentially acquired languages after the onset of English acquisition. We did not include simultaneous bilinguals (multiple L1s). The L1 English group did not have restrictions on languages acquired sequentially after the L1 (i.e., L2+), but again, simultaneous bilinguals were excluded. Materials

We conducted a contextualized acceptability judgement task (AJT) testing acceptance of fragment responses in four conditions: two cases (nominative, accusative), three contexts (pronoun (i.e., Basic), only+pronoun (i.e., Only), and not+pronoun (i.e., Not)), two underlying positions (subject, object), and two persons (1 st , 3 rd ). These conditions were fully crossed, though contexts were manipulated for variety and to facilitate the use of fragments in the corresponding contexts. For example, 'only me' as a response is more felicitous when there is a possibility for multiple potential referents already present in the question. All items consisted of a two-turn dialogue. In test items the first speaker asked a wh-question, and the second speaker responded with a fragment containing a pronoun. Responses were rated on a scale of 1-4, where 4 indicated 'Completely Acceptable' and 1 'Completely Unacceptable'. Labels were always presented with the scale. An example test item is provided in Figure 1. 8 items were developed for each cell of the 2x3x2x2 cross, resulting in 192 test items. These test items were then divided into two lists of 96 test items each. This division was done in such a way that only one person was presented with each case in the same context (i.e., 1 st nominative and 3 rd accusative OR 1 st accusative and 3 rd nominative could be presented with the same context in one list). Each list was supplemented with 40 filler items testing other types of fragment responses. Thus, each list contained 136 total items. Participants were randomly assigned to one of the two lists. Participants completed all items in that list, and the presentation of items was randomized for each participant.

After completing the AJT, participants completed the LexTALE vocabulary assesment to collect a measure of English proficiency. Finally, participants completed a demographic background questionnaire designed to collect information on dialect exposure and use in English. The background questionnaire was supplemented with an adapted version of Linford's (2016) questionnaires. In total, it took about 30-40 minutes to complete the entire experiment. Participants provided informed consent prior to beginning the experiment, and they were compensated after completing all tasks. Materials, data, analysis scripts, and supplemental materials are available on Zenodo (https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.24196567).

Results

The data were entered into an ordinal logistic mixed model using the clmm function for ordinal data from the 'ordinal' package in R (Christensen 2025). The ordinal dependent variable was Rating (1-4), and the independent variables were L1 (L1 English, L1 German, L1 French), Case (Nom, Acc), Person (1 st , 3 rd ), Context (Base, Only, Not), and Position (Subj, Obj). The L1 variable was reverse Helmert-coded such that the model compared (a) the L1 French group to the L1 German group, and (b) the L1 English group to the mean of the L1 French and L1 German groups. The Case variable was sum-coded, with Nom as 1 and Acc as -1. The Person variable was sum-coded, with 1 st as 1 and 3 rd as -1. The Context variable was reverse Helmert-coded such that the model compared (a) Only to Basic, and (b) Not to the mean of Only and Basic. The Position variable was sum-coded, with Subj as 1 and Obj as -1. Random intercepts for Item and Participant were included. In order to build the optimal model and avoid overfitting of the data, the buildclmm function from the 'buildmer' package in R (Voeten 2025) was applied to the following model structure with direction = c('order', 'backward'):

Rating ~ Position * Case * Context * L1 * Person + (1 | Item) + (1 | Participant) Direction specified in this manner ensures that more important terms, assessed by likelihood ratio tests, are added to the model first until the maximal model that can converge is found. Then, from the maximal model, the program determines which predictors can be removed through likelihood ratio tests. Based on this procedure, the five-way interaction, as well as two four-way and one three-way interaction were removed. A likelihood ratio test comparing the maximal model to the null model (i.e., a model containing the random effects but no fixed effects) was significant: LR(55) = 1030.80, p < .001, McFadden's R² = .054. Coefficients of fixed effects for the model are provided in Appendix 1.

The model produced five significant main effects and 16 significant interactions. Because several of the significant effects were contained within higher-order significant interactions, only a subset of these effects will be discussed. First, there was a significant four-way interaction between Case, Context, Position, and L1. This interaction indicates that the L1 German group tended to rate nominative case higher when the underlying position was a subject, and accusative higher when the underlying position was an object, across all contexts. However, the size of the difference due to underlying position is smaller in the Not + pronoun context than in either of the other two contexts (for both nominative and accusative case). The L1 French and L1 English groups tended to make a smaller distinction between underlying subject and object positions when rating nominative and accusative case regardless of the context. Discussion

In this study we examined the reassembly of features on pronouns in English by L1 French and German learners. Specifically, we investigated differences in the acquisition task due to adding vs. preempting features associated with strong pronouns. In doing so, we addressed the role of underlying position (i.e., subject vs. object) in the acceptance of the two types of strong pronoun. Additionally, we examined the effect of differences in the frequency of case choice by person and construction (i.e., fragment type), while also considering the variability that is found in the selectional requirements of strong environments in English.

Returning to our research questions, we first asked whether the preemption of a transferred feature was more difficult than the addition of a feature found in other lexical items. Our results suggest that preemption is more difficult because we find more residual evidence of transfer for the L1 German group than the L1 French group. Specifically, the L1 German group was more affected by the underlying position (subject/object) of the pronoun than either the L1 French or English groups. The lexicalization of pronouns in German maps case features strictly to the argument role of the pronoun even in fragments or elliptical structures. Therefore, the stronger preference to align case with argument role is indicative of cross-linguistic influence. Moreover, the fact that French speakers' judgements are also affected, though less strongly, by argument role, suggests that the variability in the input interacting with transfer leads to different patterns of feature reassembly. That is, register and dialectal variation permit gracile I in some strong environments, resulting in French speakers tentatively allowing this option in fragments despite an initial mapping from moi to me and no need for reassembly in this context because strong me is always possible. Thus, our results are consistent with previous findings of the difficulty of preemption compared to addition (e.g., Smeets 2024; Su 2019; Jimenez et al. 2024). Unfortunately, given the general non-acceptance of nominative case with fragments containing 'not' by all groups, the evidence for successful reassembly of the [STR] feature is not as strong as it would be if nominative case had been accepted. That is, while we have evidence for the addition of [STR] to the nominative pronouns because the L1 French learners are affected by underlying position, we do not have the stronger evidence that acceptance of nominative pronouns in 'not' contexts would provide.

Next, we asked how (the type of) frequency affects the acquisition process. First, we find that lexical frequency plays a role during feature reassembly. Specifically, both L2 groups rate 1 st person higher than 3 rd person, and they tend to do so more in those contexts where positive evidence that would induce reassembly of the transferred L1 would be found. For example, the L1 German group made a larger distinction than the L1 French group when rating accusative subjects, but the L1 French group made a larger distinction than the L1 German group when rating nominative case generally. Additionally, the fact that 1 st person is rated higher in these contexts indicates that more frequent lexical items are reassembled faster than less frequent items, consistent with previous studies (Thane 2023; Wang and Chan 2021) While lexical frequency was found to affect reassembly, construction frequency was not. We found no differences between ratings for pronouns in basic fragments compared to fragments containing 'only' by either L2 group in any context. The fact that there are no differences between these contexts, despite basic fragments being more frequent in the input and the L1 German group being non-target-like in both contexts in at least one case/position combination, suggests that construction frequency may not affect acquisition during feature reassembly, consistent with previous findings (Smeets 2024). While Smeets suggested that the lack of construction frequency effects was due to the low frequency of the tested constructions in the input, a similar argument could likely not be made here, as basic fragments are frequent in speech. If the lack of an effect of construction frequency is not due to those constructions' low frequency, there remains an outstanding question of why lexical frequency, but not construction frequency, would affect the acquisition process. This issue is left to future research.

Finally, our third research question asked how (the level of) variability affects the acquisition process during feature reassembly. At a more general level, we predicted that if the variability in pronoun selection across strong environments in English affected the acquisition process, both L2 groups would display reduced accuracy when rating nominative subject pronouns across all three types of fragment response. However, we did not find this effect. Instead, while the L1 German learners were less accurate with nominative subjects, the L1 French group were not. As discussed above, the L1 German behavior is likely due to residual transfer and the difficulty of preemption, rather than only variability, though this task is likely made more difficult by the variability which reinforces the transferred grammar to some extent. Still, it is possible that general variability across the strong environments does affect the learners but that our participants were too advanced to see any effects.

Regarding (context-)specific variability, both the L1 French and L1 German learners rated nominative case lower in fragments containing 'not' than in the other two contexts. This rating is despite the fact that nominative case is possible only in the 'not' context (of these three contexts). While it is true that the L1 English speakers also did not accept nominative case in this context, they did not distinguish 'not' from the other two contexts, unlike the L2 learners. One possible interpretation of the L2 groups' behavior in distinguishing fragments containing 'not' from the other two types is due to the variability precisely in this context. That is, the learners were potentially sensitive to the variability in the input in this context to some extent, and because of that variability, they rejected a possible (but less frequently produced) variant. This result would then be similar to the finding in Hwang and Lardiere (2013) that optionality/variability in a specific context, i.e., human quantified nouns, resulted in overgeneralization and rejection of one of the available options, i.e., the use of the plural morpheme. However, this interpretation can only be tentative due to the L1 English speakers' behavior in this context. One could address this interpretation in the future by retesting these contexts using a bimodally-presented AJT. As fragments containing 'not' tend to carry a marked intonation contour, it is possible that a bimodally-presented task would increase acceptability for the L1 English speakers and therefore allow for stronger conclusions regarding the behavior of the L2 learners.

Because we could not clearly interpret the effect of context-specific variability due to our control group's response pattern, future research could examine acquisition contexts where effects of both general variability and context-specific variability can be examined simultaneously. Additionally, by comparing multiple contexts where there is variability, but differences in the frequency of that variability, such as other variable strong environments in English, one could better understand any potential interactions of the two types of frequency (construction and lexical) with the two levels of variability (general and specific).

Additionally, our study only examined the acquisition of strong pronouns by advanced English learners. Future research could compare learners at different proficiency levels to understand the timeline of effects stemming from each of these factors. It is possible that lexical frequency plays a larger role at lower proficiency levels (as suggested in Thane 2023), but construction frequency and (both types of) variability's effects are stronger at different times in the acquisition process.

Finally, incorporating sociolinguistic variation into research on feature reassembly and effects of variability could provide a more complete understanding of the acquisition task. Previous studies of variability/optionality and their effects on feature reassembly have only considered variability that occurs within the context of a broader language. However, for a given property, different amounts of variability can often be found between dialects and registers (and other sociolinguistically relevant contexts), as for example in the use of strong pronouns in English. Examining such differences in the degree of variability could provide a more nuanced picture of the effect of variability on feature reassembly, as well as learners' sensitivity to that variability. Therefore, adopting a sociosyntactic approach (Adger and Trousdale 2007; Cornips and Corrigan 2005; Duncan and Robinson 2025), which seeks to unite formal linguistic mechanisms with variationist sociosyntax, could provide a fuller understanding of the L2 acquisition task.
