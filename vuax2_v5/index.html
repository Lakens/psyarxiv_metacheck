Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

1 Introduction

A central goal of psychological research is the measurement and interpretation of individual differences in thoughts, feelings, and attitudes. This task is inherently challenging because these phenomena are internal states that cannot be directly observed (Kim et al., 2008).

Although these internal states may, at times, manifest in observable behaviours -for example, sadness may lead to crying -this relationship is neither consistent nor specific. Not all sadness leads to crying, and not all crying comes from sadness. Since observable behaviour provides such an incomplete representation of internal psychological states, detailed theories have been developed to help link behaviour with unobservable psychological processes, attitudes, and emotions (hereafter referred to broadly as "latent states"). One of the most widely used approaches for this purpose is self-report measurement, including open-ended questions, forced-choice tasks, Likert-type rating scales, and visual analogue scales, which are used extensively across psychology and related disciplines to assess individual differences in latent states. However, before these states can be observed and analysed, they must be translated into a response, such as selecting an option on a Likert scale. Understanding this translation process is therefore essential for interpreting self-report data and ensuring that observed responses provide valid information about individual differences in the constructs of interest.

In this paper, we propose a theoretical account of self-report responding in which observed responses arise from two separable but psychologically meaningful sources of individual differences. Specifically, self-reported responses reflect both the latent states individuals intend to communicate and the response processes that govern how those states are translated into observable responses. Although these response processes may be influenced by contextual factors, such as who is asking or the perceived consequences of responding, our framework proposes that individuals typically exhibit characteristic and relatively stable patterns of responding across measurement contexts. These patterns reflect systematic individual differences in how internal experiences are mapped onto available response options. As a result, observed self-report scores contain information not only about the latent states being assessed but also about stable differences in the response strategies individuals employ. Understanding and distinguishing these two sources of variation is critical for the accurate measurement and interpretation of individual differences.

Though our theory can, in principle, be applied to any form of self-report, we focus here on Likert scale surveys because they are ubiquitous in psychological science (and beyond; Joshi et al., 2015). Likert responses also provide a tractable setting in which to study systematic individual differences in response behaviour and to quantify how response styles contribute to variation in observed scores. A typical Likert scale presents respondents with a series of statements or questions (called "items") and asks them to respond using an ordered set of "response options" (Batterton & Hale, 2017; Joshi et al., 2015; Likert, 1932). As an example, people may be presented with the statement "This is a fantastic paper" and asked to rate their agreement on a 3-point scale, where options are labelled "Strongly Agree", "Mostly Agree" or "Somewhat Agree". Each response option represents an ordered category rather than a precise numerical quantity, allowing respondents to express gradations of opinion or experience in a structured and interpretable way. This structured format makes them an excellent method for quickly, easily and affordably collecting data (Dillman et al., 2014) that is easy to analyse (Joshi et al., 2015) and may otherwise be difficult to obtain (Kim et al., 2008).

Despite their widespread use, it is commonly acknowledged that Likert scale surveys may be vulnerable to systematic biases (Paulhus & Vazire, 2007). One such source of bias arises from response styles (sometimes referred to as "response bias"), whereby respondents exhibit preferences for (or against) certain response options, regardless of item content (Paulhus & Vazire, 2007). Classic examples of response styles include extreme, midpoint, and acquiescent responding. Extreme responding refers to a preference for selecting the endpoints of a response scale (e.g., "strongly agree" or "strongly disagree"), reflecting a tendency towards emphatic rather than cautious evaluation (A. R. L. Costa & Hauck Filho, 2019). Conversely, a midpoint response style involves a disproportionate use of neutral or middle response categories (Nadler et al., 2015), which may reflect satisficing, uncertainty, or a strategic avoidance of expressing strong opinions, making responses difficult to determine (Kusmaryono et al., 2022). Acquiescent responding is a directional bias characterised by a tendency to agree with survey items regardless of their substantive content (Billiet & McClendon, 2000; Heerwegh & Loosveldt, 2011; Smith, 2004) and can be difficult to separate from genuinely high trait expression when survey instruments are not well-balanced.

Our proposed theory suggests that these response styles arise from underlying decision processes about how individuals choose to communicate their internal latent states. In responding to survey items, people do not simply report these states directly, but rather engage in a series of judgments about how best to map their internal experiences onto the available response scale.

Consequently, observed responses reflect a combination of the latent state(s) of interest and the decision-making processes involved in selecting a response. In this way, variability in survey data may stem not only from differences in underlying constructs, but also from systematic differences in how individuals interpret and use the response scale.

A growing body of empirical research indicates that response styles exhibit stable, trait-like properties over time. For example, Weijters et al. (2010) demonstrate that several canonical response tendencies show substantial stability over a one-year period. Extending this work, Danner et al. (2015) also confirmed that acquiescent responding remains somewhat consistent over time, while Wetzel et al. (2016) found extreme and acquiescent responding may remain consistent across intervals of up to eight years, providing strong evidence for long-term stability. Similar patterns have been observed in applied contexts where evidence suggests that response styles remain relatively consistent over a two-year period when individuals in a clinical population complete personality measures (Wu, 2025). Together, these findings suggest that response styles are enduring individual characteristics that can systematically influence observed responses over long periods of time.

In addition to temporal stability, research suggests that response styles generalise across tasks and measurement contexts. Though not universally accepted (see Ames, 2022 for item-level models of response style), the existence of cross-task transference suggests that response styles are unlikely to be tied to specific items, scales, or latent constructs. In establishing temporal stability, Weijters et al. (2010) employed a design in which respondents answered different, randomly selected items at each measurement occasion, demonstrating that response styles persist even when item content varies. Similarly, Wetzel et al. (2013) and Kieruj and Moors (2013) have both demonstrated that extreme response tendencies are stable across measures of varying length and are systematically associated with specific personality profiles. Similarly, Danner et al. (2015) concludes that acquiescent responding is consistent across different measures of personality. Taken together, these findings indicate that response styles may be domain-general tendencies rather than side effects of particular scale designs or construct domains, which is a principal component of our proposed theory.

Despite its apparent stability, however, research suggests that features of the survey task can systematically influence response styles, suggesting that some aspects of response style may be under conscious control. The number of response categories has been shown to affect internal consistency, validity, and the distribution of responses (Garland, 1991; Kusmaryono et al., 2022; Leung, 2011), with larger numbers of options potentially increasing cognitive demands and promoting behaviours such as status quo responding (Weathers et al., 2005). The use of a midpoint in scales with an odd number of options may reflect uncertainty, indifference, or social desirability rather than true neutrality, and respondents vary substantially in how they interpret this option (Chyung et al., 2017; Kulas et al., 2008; Nadler et al., 2015; Weijters & Baumgartner, 2012). The wording and labelling of response categories may alter perceived psychological distances between options and inducing systematic biases (Moors et al., 2014; Schwarz, 1999; Steinberg & Rogers, 2022), while familiarity with labels can increase extremity in responses (Weijters et al., 2013). Additionally, the presentation and structure of response options can encourage tendencies like left-side bias, extreme responding, or straightlining (Hartley & Betts, 2010; Moors et al., 2014; Robie et al., 2022). Collectively, these findings highlight that response styles are not solely properties of respondents, but are also systematically shaped by task demands which may influence how individuals map their internal states onto available response options. This apparent tension between evidence for stability and context-dependent variation in response behaviour mirrors the patterns observed in other decision-making literatures. For example, the speed-accuracy trade-off in the speeded choice literature describes a well-established phenomenon whereby individuals must balance the benefits of accumulating additional information (at the cost of time) against making quicker decisions based on less information (and therefore with reduced accuracy; Heitz, 2014). Research in this area demonstrates that individuals tend to exhibit relatively stable internal thresholds across tasks and over time, with systematic individual differences in the extent to which speed or accuracy is prioritised (Alfers et al., 2025; D. R. Johnson et al., 2008). These thresholds are not fixed -they remain under conscious control and can be flexibly adjusted in response to changing task demands or incentives (Heitz, 2014; Katsimpokis et al., 2020). Both these phenomena can be described by the speed-accuracy tradeoff, whereby people have default preferences for their information threshold settings but can adapt these settings to produce faster or more accurate choices without affecting the rate of information processing.

Hence, cognitive models of speeded choice provide accounts of decision-making which allow both stable preferences and situational flexibility to co-exist within the same theoretical frameworks, much as our proposed theory aims to do for self-report responding. Our proposed theory posits that individuals possess general tendencies in how they express their internal states, while also retaining the capacity to adapt these strategies to suit situational or contextual demands, without implying a change in the underlying latent state itself. The theory reconciles evidence of stability across time and tasks with the context-dependent variation documented in the response style literature by suggesting that thresholds for responding to particular response options on a Likert scale may be flexibly adapted.

Our theoretical framework enables the use of existing statistical techniques to quantitatively test our theoretical contribution in ways that were previously impossible. Although response styles have been shown to exhibit stability across time (Weijters et al., 2010; Wetzel et al., 2016; Wu, 2025) and tasks (Danner et al., 2015; Kieruj & Moors, 2013; Wetzel et al., 2013), prior approaches have largely been descriptive, characterising, and often attempting to control for, individual differences in response behaviour rather than formalising them within a quantitative, process-based framework. In contrast, our core assumption of stability treats these response styles as systematic and informative features of behaviour, which in turn allows us to use an ordinal-probit modelling approach to infer the idiosyncratic ways in which individuals map their internal states onto response scales (J. Grimmond et al., 2025). This architecture provides a principled, computational framework to explain how self-reports are generated from combining latent states with decision processes. By providing quantitative characterisations of both parts, our approach supports a more unified account of self-report behaviour than earlier approaches, which tend to prioritise either response styles or latent state expression. In many cases, response styles are treated as a nuisance to be controlled, with primary emphasis placed on recovering latent state information (T. Johnson et al., 2005) and little regard for the potentially informative response style behaviour. In other approaches (Weijters et al., 2008 (Weijters et al., , 2010;; Wetzel et al., 2016), response styles, themselves, constitute the variables of interest, and accurate latent state measurement becomes secondary.

In this paper, we present a theoretical account of self-report response style that advances the measurement and interpretation of individual differences. We conceptualise self-report response style as stable, multidimensional individual differences comprising distinct and psychologically interpretable processes that interact to produce a wide range of response patterns. This framework treats response styles not merely as sources of measurement noise, but as meaningful and informative characteristics of individuals that systematically influence observed scores. By representing response styles within a unified parametric framework, our theory provides a means of quantifying individual differences in response behaviour and generating testable predictions regarding their structure, stability, and influence on psychological measurement. In doing so, it offers a principled approach for distinguishing substantive variation in latent states from variation attributable to how individuals communicate those states through self-report.

2 A Quantifiable and Unified Theory of Survey Responding

As in J. Grimmond et al. (2025), the modelling approach presented in this paper formalises our proposed theory and describes responses as arising from the interaction between a respondent's latent state and a decision process. In this ordinal-probit approach, each latent state is represented as a continuous, normal distribution characterised by a mean parameter (µ), reflecting the individual's level of endorsement of the construct of interest. For example, µ might represent the true, latent level of stress which a self-report measure aims to assess. When responding to a self-report item, a value is sampled from this latent distribution and mapped onto discrete response options via a set of ordered thresholds that partition the continuum into ordinal categories. These thresholds capture how individuals use the response scale, effectively encoding response style, such that differences in their placement can produce different observed response patterns, even when latent states are identical (see J. Grimmond et al. (2025) for examples).

The theoretical account we propose here shifts the explanation of response style from the decision thresholds to underlying parameters which are directly interpretable at the psychological level; three parameters which govern the overall spacing, shift, and compression of the thresholds. Shifting focus like this allows us to develop and test psychological theory about the relative stability vs. manipulability of response styles in a way previously not possible.

The well-established body of literature devoted to the characterisation and measurement of response styles guided both the inclusion and specification of the response style transformations in our model (Batchelor & Miao, 2016; Clarke III, 2000; Kieruj & Moors, 2013; McIntyre, 2011; Wetzel et al., 2013). This literature has primarily focused on identifying systematic individual, group, and cultural differences in response patterns (Bachman & O'Malley, 1984; Batchelor & Miao, 2016; Chen et al., 1995; Meisenberg & Williams, 2008), as well as documenting the prevalence and correlates of canonical response styles (Paulhus & Vazire, 2007).

We draw on these insights in two important ways: our theory posits that an important dimension of response style is the continuum between extreme responding and midpoint responding, and another is the continuum between an average tendency to select response options presented on the right-hand vs those on the left-hand side of the display. While the latter usually maps onto the widely-discussed "acquiesent" response style (whereby right-side selection represents agreement; Danner et al., 2015; Meisenberg and Williams, 2008; Smith, 2004), we aim to provide a more general, scale-agnostic explanation of response behaviour which is not specifically tied to response option labelling in this way. The third dimension of response style in our theory is motivated by decision-making research which consistently shows that people use heuristics and psychological simplifications to guide their judgements and choices (Gigerenzer & Gaissmaier, 2011). In Likert scale responding, this can mean simplifying a scale with many response options into a simpler format by collapsing together some adjacent categories (for related theoretical ideas, see :Böckenholt, 2012; A. R. L. Costa and Hauck Filho, 2019; Weathers et al., 2005). This theoretical dimension represents a continuum between over-using vs under-using the end-plus-middle categories (for empirical evidence for 1-3-5 responding, see : Colombi et al., 2021; He et al., 2014).

To help ground our theory in observed data, an exploratory factor analysis (EFA) was performed on participants' threshold parameters, estimated using the model described in J. Grimmond et al., 2025 from the data reported in that study. Full details of this analysis can be found in the Supplementary Material. The factor analysis showed a two-factor solution as the most parsimonious and interpretable representation of the underlying response style structure, whereby the first factor reflected the psychological collapse of the 5-point scale into either 2-(even response options) or 3-(odd response options) point scales and the second factor reflected the general distance between all thresholds. Though no factor reflected asymmetry in responding, we concluded that the prevalence of acquiescent responding in the literature (Billiet & Davidov, 2008; Smith, 2004; Wetzel et al., 2016), alone, justified the inclusion of a parameter to capture systematic left-or right-biased responding such as acquiescence (Cronbach, 1946; Krosnick, 1991).

With this theoretical and empirical support, we propose the Latent State and Response Style (LASARS) model, which defines three parameters that provide intuitive links between psychological theory and observed behaviour, reflecting the preference for centre options, directional bias and psychological collapsing in survey responding. We label these parameters "centre preference" (α), "directional preference" (ω) and "odds preference" (γ), respectively. The centre preference parameter reflects the degree to which people choose (or not) the middle response options on the Likert scale. Larger values of the centre preference parameter indicate greater distance between the decision thresholds, increasing the likelihood of using middle options and decreasing the likelihood of using extreme response options. The directional preference parameter captures asymmetry in response option selection, with larger values shifting the distribution of responses up toward the right-end options (often, but not always, toward agreement options) and away from left-end options. Finally, the odds preference parameter reflects the use of end-plus-middle options, with the distance between the inner thresholds changing as a function of the outer, fixed thresholds. Though this parameter is mathematically generalisable to scales with more than five response options, the interpretation varies with different numbers of options (we return to this point in the Discussion). Given that 5-point scales are the most popular response format in survey research (Kusmaryono et al., 2022), we have labelled this parameter "odds preference" to describe how response patterns are affected by different values of this parameter in a 5-point format. Larger values of the odds preference parameter shrink the mid-level options ("Agree", "Disagree"), making 1-3-5 responding more likely in a 5-point scale. Thorough model validation confirmed the LASARS model's success in recovering known-generating parameters and its descriptive adequacy with observed data (see Supplementary Material for full details).

For the rest of this paper, we provide empirical tests of the proposed framework. These studies evaluate predictions arising from the interpretation of response styles as meaningful individual differences in self-report responding. We first demonstrate that response style is under conscious control and that different psychological dimensions can be selectively influenced by task demands without corresponding change in latent state scores. We then show that, in the absence of explicit instructions to the contrary, individuals exhibit remarkably stable response styles across time and tasks. Establishing response style stability in this way provides support for the theoretical assumption that response style can be shared across latent states to improve measurement and that response style constitutes a meaningful individual difference in its own right.

To conclude the paper, we identify and evaluate a novel theoretical prediction about response style expression in instruments with no reverse-coding within latent state scales.

Specifically, we confirm that a scale with no reverse-scoring within each latent state conflates directional bias (in this case, acquiescence) with latent state expression. We show this occurs with conventional, model-free scoring techniques, but that the LASARS model offers a solution to this problem by leveraging response style information across tasks to reduce the potentially confounding effects of response style. Together, the analyses in this paper provide a body of evidence that supports our proposed theory; decision styles and latent states are two distinct sources of information that can be separately but simultaneously measured to improve the fidelity and informativeness of survey measures.

3 Response Style Can be Manipulated

The LASARS framework casts response styles as a decision process which can, theoretically, be manipulated independently of latent state expression (which is generally considered to be stable in individual differences research; John et al., 2010). The LASARS model incorporates explicit response style parameters, making it possible to test this theoretical distinction by comparing response and latent state parameters before and after manipulation instructions. We asked participants to complete a survey with standard instructions (Phase 1).

They then received instructions that aimed to only change their response behaviour, and completed the survey task a second time (Phase 2). Extreme responding and its inverse, midpoint responding, were encouraged in this experiment because the extreme response style is widely discussed in relevant literature (Batchelor & Miao, 2016; Baumgartner & Steenkamp, 2001; Clarke III, 2000) and directly maps onto the model's centre preference parameter. The LASARS model predicts that participants will have smaller centre preference estimates after they have been instructed to adopt an extreme response style than before, and that the reverse pattern will be present for participants who were instructed to adopt a midpoint style. No changes in any response style estimates are predicted for participants in a neutral instruction control condition.

Under the LASARS framework, there should also be no systematic differences in latent state estimates between Phase 1 and 2 for any response style manipulation condition.

3.1 Method

One hundred and ninety participants were recruited from Prolific Academic, where only participants with an approval rating of 90% or higher were eligible. Participants received £1.95 for their time. Of the 190 participants, 72 (37.9%) identified as female, 117 male (61.6%) and 1 (0.5%) preferred not to say. The mean age was 32.15 years old (SD = 10.34). Due to technical issues, data from two participants were not recorded correctly and were not included in the analyses.

Participants responded to the 50-item International Personality Inventory Pool (IPIP)

survey (Goldberg et al., 1999). The IPIP measures the Big Five personality traits -extraversion, openness, conscientiousness, agreeableness, and neuroticism. Each trait is measured with 10 items and responses given on a 5-point Likert scale with response options labelled "Disagree" (1), "Neutral" (3), and "Agree" (5). We also added two attention check items instructing participants to select a specific response option, resulting in a 52-item survey. In Phase 1, participants were directed to complete the survey by describing themselves as they currently are and in relation to people with similar demographic characteristics (standard instructions from the IPIP website - https://ipip.ori.org/New_IPIP-50-item-scale.html). They then completed the survey. In Phase 2, participants were randomly allocated to one of three conditions; extreme (n = 55), midpoint (n = 68) or neutral (n = 65). All participants were instructed to complete the IPIP survey a second time, but this time on behalf of a hypothetical friend. Since many people may not be consciously aware of their own survey responding styles/preferences, it was important that participants responded on behalf of somebody else in Phase 2, so response style could be more easily separated from personality constructs. Participants were told that this friend shares all the same interests and personality traits as they do, but participants in the extreme and midpoint conditions were additionally told that their friend usually responds to survey tasks by choosing the most extreme or midpoint response options, respectively. Participants in the neutral condition were not given any information about how their friend may respond and, in this way, served as a control group.

The full task instructions for each condition can be found in the Supplementary Material.

Since participants often ignore task instructions (Barnard et al., 1979), an attention check similar to that suggested by Oppenheimer et al. (2009) was also included to ensure participants had read the instructions completely. Participants were unable to begin the Phase 2 survey until they could successfully demonstrate they had read the instructions. At the completion of Phase 2, participants were again presented with the manipulation instruction and asked to indicate on a slider scale (ranging from 0 to 100) the degree to which they used these instructions to guide their responses. Both phases were completed in the same session, with an overall median completion time of 10 minutes and 27 seconds.

Parameters were estimated separately for the Phase 1 and Phase 2 surveys. That is, for each participant, we estimated separate latent state means for the five personality traits in Phase 1 and Phase 2, and three response style parameters in Phase 1 and Phase 2.

This resulted in a total of 16 parameters estimated per participant. We used the Particle Metropolis within Gibbs (PMwG) sampling approach (Cooper et al., 2024; Gunawan et al., 2020; Kuhne et al., 2026) to estimate the Phase 1 and Phase 2 parameters simultaneously, which has the advantage of providing covariance information about all pairwise combinations of the 16 parameters, allowing us to explore this in a principled, Bayesian way. We estimated the model independently for each manipulation condition (extreme, midpoint and neutral). The PMwG settings were the same for each of the three conditions' joint models. Each model sampled 3000 iterations of 75 particles in the "burn-in" and "adaptation" phase and 50 in the "sampling" phase. We used uninformative prior settings, whereby the prior for all 16 parameters was zero.

Two versions of the analyses were run. The first version, which we present here, was performed on a cleaned data set where participants were removed from the analysis if they failed two or more (of four possible) attention checks while completing the survey. They were also removed if their self-reported adherence to the instructions was less than 50/100 or if they failed to read the manipulation instructions correctly more than once. Eleven participants were removed from the midpoint condition and two from the extreme condition due to these failed attention checks, resulting in a final sample of 175 participants. A comparative analysis was performed on the full sample of 188 participants (results in Supplementary Material) to confirm that the model generates the same pattern of results when strict attention checks are not included. There were no notable differences in the patterns observed between the two versions, so we report the results of the cleaned sample for simplicity.

For inference, we took the difference of group-level posterior distributions, and summarised the difference distribution by its posterior mean and 95% credible interval. We also calculated Savage-Dickey Bayes factors to determine whether evidence indicates a meaningful difference between the Phase 1 and Phase 2 distributions or not (Dickey, 1971; Dickey & Lientz, 1970; Wagenmakers et al., 2010; Wetzels et al., 2010).

4 Response Style is Stable Across Time

Having established that response style can be influenced by conscious decision processes, it is also important to consider how such tendencies manifest in the absence of explicit manipulation. In typical survey contexts, respondents are rarely instructed to adopt particular response strategies and instead rely on their default or habitual approaches to answer questions.

Establishing that these tendencies remain consistent over time is an important step in theory validation, as our proposed framework considers response style to be a meaningful source of individual difference rather than a purely situational factor. If response styles are shown to persist across measurement occasions, our theory's prediction that they capture enduring decision-making preferences which systematically shape how individuals engage with rating scales will have empirical support.

We applied the LASARS model to longitudinal data collected and reported by Huang et al. (2024), where 1797 participants responded to various measures of impulsivity at two different time points, three months apart. Specifically, we use the data from impulsivity-related facet items from the Revised NEO Personality Inventory (NEO PI-R; P. T. Costa and McCrae, 2008).

The NEO PI-R is a 240-item questionnaire designed to assess the five broad personality domains described in the previous Section 3.1. Owing to its length, the NEO PI-R can provide fine-grained measurement at the facet level, with each of the five domains comprising six lower-order facets. The items used in this analysis were chosen by Huang et al. (2024) to reflect aspects of impulsive behaviour and were drawn from the impulsivity, self-discipline, excitement-seeking, and deliberation facets. Impulsivity is a facet of neuroticism and reflects difficulty resisting urges and regulating behaviour in emotionally charged situations, while excitement-seeking stems from the extraversion domain and reflects a preference for stimulation, novelty, and risk (P. T. Costa & McCrae, 2008). Self-discipline captures the capacity to initiate and persist with goal-directed activity despite distraction or discomfort, and deliberation reflects the tendency to think carefully and consider consequences before acting, both of which load onto conscientiousness (P. T. Costa & McCrae, 2008). Each facet is represented by eight items, resulting in a 32-item survey (henceforth referred to as simply "the NEO") with 15 reverse-scored items.

Responses are recorded on a 5-point Likert scale, with response options labelled "Strongly Disagree", "Disagree", "Neutral", "Agree", and "Strongly Agree".

To model responses to the NEO, we estimated four latent state distribution means (impulsivity, excitement-seeking, deliberation and self-discipline) and three response style parameters (centre preference, directional preference and odds preference). We estimated these parameters separately for both time points, resulting in a total set of 14 parameters. We again performed this estimation with the hierarchical Bayesian PMwG approach described in the previous Section 3.1, with the same uninformative priors and iteration and particle settings already described. Only the samples from the "sample" phase were used in this analysis. After converting the group-level covariances estimated in the PMwG analysis into correlation coefficients, we examined the relationships between all pairwise combinations of parameter estimates. As a comparison, conventional scores were also calculated by reverse-scoring items and summing responses. Table 3 presents the correlations between like-for-like latent state estimates obtained over the two sessions (Time 1 and Time 2). As predicted by the theory, response style parameters show consistently strong temporal correlations, indicating that response styles are relatively stable over time. Interestingly, the Time 1 and Time 2 estimates for all three response style parameters are more strongly related to one another than all but one of the latent state estimates across measurement occasions (excitement seeking). These findings align with previous research which shows that response style can be stable for years (Billiet & Davidov, 2008; Wetzel et al., 2016) and are particularly important because temporal stability is a defining feature of individual difference constructs. The observed consistency across measurement occasions, therefore, suggests that response parameters may be interpretable as trait-like decision tendencies which constitute meaningful individual differences in their own right.

As a secondary consideration, Table 3 also demonstrates that the model-based estimates produce stronger latent state correlations than conventional scoring approaches. Given that test-retest correlations are among the most widely used indicators of measurement reliability, this result has important implications for establishing temporal stability in individual differences research. Accordingly, the reliability of self-report measures (when improvement is theoretically appropriate) is improved when viewed through the lens of our psychological theory.

5 Response Style is Stable Across Instruments

In addition to temporal stability, response tendencies should generalise across instruments if they are stable individual differences as the LASARS model proposes. However, the extent of this generalisation requires exploration because it may be attenuated by individuals' capacity to adapt their response strategies to align with specific task demands. It is plausible that the observed temporal findings reflect consistent task demands that implicitly encourage particular response strategies across repeated administrations. Indeed, the earlier results showing that task instructions can systematically alter response behaviour highlight the sensitivity of response styles to contextual cues. As such, demonstrating cross-survey consistency is necessary to rule out the possibility that observed stability is merely a by-product of shared task structure and establish the true consistency of response style behaviour. The following analysis was performed on responses to three surveys measuring impulsivity through different theoretical lenses on both 4-and 5-point Likert scales with response options labelled "Strongly Agree" to "Strongly Disagree" and "Never" to "Always". Confirming that response styles persist across measures that differ in response format, item content, and theoretical framing in this way provides stronger evidence that they reflect stable, trait-like individual differences rather than context-dependent side effects of specific survey designs.

Establishing the stability of response styles across tasks also represents an important step in validating the LASARS framework's assumption that individuals exhibit consistent response styles across latent states; the model leverages information from multiple latent constructs to identify patterns of consistency, thereby enabling the separation of response style effects from genuine latent state expression. This assumption is supported by prior empirical work (Bolt & Newton, 2011; Wetzel et al., 2013), but it is not universally adopted in response style modelling approaches, many of which conceptualise response styles as varying at the item or trait level rather than as stable person-level tendencies (Böckenholt, 2017; Bolt & Newton, 2011). Because our theory assumes stability in both response behaviour and latent states, establishing the conditions under which response styles, themselves, are stable and assessing their robustness to changes in task demands represents an important step in further validating the theory and supporting its major assumptions.

In this analysis, we explore how response styles and latent states relate to one another across instruments with differential construct overlap and response formats. Because each survey instrument aims to measure the same conceptual domain (impulsivity), we expect to find consistent relationships between latent state scores, but the LASARS model additionally predicts that people will adopt similar response strategies for the three different surveys because they have innate preferences for how they communicate self-report information. In this way, LASARS suggests that, even with different response formats and wording, like-for-like response style parameters will be correlated across the three survey instruments because people's preferences for/against certain response options are consistent.

We again used the NEO data, along with the data from the Barratt Impulsivity Scale 11 Each of these three surveys provide a conceptual account of impulsivity, but are grounded in different theoretical frameworks and were designed to measure different aspects of the construct.

As shown in Table 4, these scales are also constructed differently, with different response option labels, item directionality, and number of response options. The BIS11 is a 30-item scale which measures three domains of impulsivity: attentional, motor, and non-planning impulsivity. Attentional impulsivity describes difficulty sustaining attention and cognitive instability, while motor impulsivity explains a tendency to act without thinking (Patton et al., 1995). Non-planning impulsivity reflects a lack of future orientation or forethought (Patton et al., 1995). The version used in this analysis is an English translation of the Chinese adaptation (Li et al., 2011). Since all attentional and non-planning impulsivity subscale items are reverse-scored, while the motor impulsivity scale is positively phrased, the BIS11 does not have a mix of reverse-and positively-worded items within each scale. This, in turn, makes the directional preference parameter mathematically unidentifiable. To overcome this, we simply do not estimate a directional preference parameter for this survey, resulting in three latent state parameters (attentional, motor and non-planning impulsivity) and two response style parameters (centre preference and odds preference) estimated for this survey.

The BIS-BAS survey contains 20 items which measure sensitivity to punishment and reward, as conceptualised within Gray's reinforcement sensitivity theory (Gray, 1981). The Behavioural Inhibition System (BIS) scale measures sensitivity to signals of punishment, threat, and novelty and is associated with avoidance motivation, anxiety, and cautious behaviour. High scores on the BIS scale reflect larger responses to potential negative outcomes (Carver & White, 1994). The Behavioural Activation System (BAS) Reward Responsiveness scale measures how strongly individuals react to the anticipation or receipt of rewards, while the BAS-Drive scale reflects persistent motivation toward desired goals (Carver & White, 1994). Finally, the BAS-fun-seeking subscale captures the tendency to act spontaneously in pursuit of enjoyment (Carver & White, 1994). As a 4-point scale, an odds preference parameter is not psychologically meaningful for the BIS-BAS, as odd-even responding can simply be captured by a combination of centre and directional preference, rather than reflecting a distinct psychological process.

Although the BIS scale includes five reverse-coded items, making a directional preference parameter theoretically identifiable, the limited information provided by the scale means that this parameter is not statistically identifiable in practice. As such, only five parameters are estimated for the BIS-BAS; four latent state parameters (BIS, BAS-reward-responsiveness, BAS-drive and BAS-fun-seeking) and the centre preference parameter.

While the NEO and BIS11 are both relatively explicit measures of impulsive tendency that measure similar aspects of the trait, such as poor self-control, rash behaviour and lack of forethought, they differ on how they elicit the information. The NEO's 5-point scale provides statements about impulsive behaviours (e.g., "I often do things on the spur of the moment") and asks people to rate their agreement. The BIS11, while also adopting a 5-point scale and similar items (e.g., "I do things without thinking"), requires people to rate how regularly they engage in these behaviours on a scale from "Always" to "Never". In this way, the two instruments may elicit different response behaviour because people conceptualise agreement and frequency differently.

The BIS-BAS measure differs not only in response option labelling (as with the NEO and BIS11) but also in response format altogether, employing a 4-point rather than a 5-point scale and omitting a neutral option. It also approaches the construct definition differently, approaching impulsivity indirectly through motivational sensitivity to reward and approach cues rather than through failures of self-control or deliberation per se. By simultaneously varying construct operationalisation, item framing, and response format, these surveys place a stronger test on the assumption that response styles reflect stable individual differences rather than task-specific behaviours.

We used the same prior and PMwG settings described in the previous analyses and report only analyses performed on the samples from the "sample" phase. The increased model complexity in this large joint model resulted in convergence concerns that were overcome by fixing the epsilon parameter of the PMwG algorithm to .8 (for more details, see Kuhne et al., 2026).

5.1 Relationships Between Latent State Parameters.

The correlations derived from the group-level covariance matrix are shown in Figure 2.

Across parameters, the latent state distribution means are correlated in the theoretically expected directions, providing an initial check on the validity of the latent state estimates. The strongest associations are observed among parameters within the same instrument. The NEO scales are all moderately positively correlated with one another, as are the BIS-BAS scales. Notably, the motor impulsivity scale of the BIS11, which is composed entirely of positively worded items, is negatively correlated with the other two BIS11 subscale means, which contain entirely reverse-scored items. As in the previous Section 4, these latent state correlations are consistently stronger when viewed through the LASARS framework than when conventional scoring methods are used, which has interesting implications for survey design and validation (conventional scoring analyses are reported in the Supplementary Material for brevity).

In contrast, correlations across instruments (shown in the green squares) are generally present but noticeably weaker (absolute values ranging from .01 to .61) than the relationships between latent states within the same instrument. This suggests that while the three surveys measure some overlapping aspects of impulsivity, they differ markedly in their operationalisation of the construct. The BIS-BAS latent state estimates also appear to have somewhat stronger correlations with the NEO scales than with most BIS11 scales. This may reflect differences in theoretical alignment or be a consequence of estimating fewer response style parameters for the BIS-BAS/BIS11 pairing (three), relative to the BIS-BAS/NEO pairing (four). Since the NEO model includes a richer set of response style parameters, it is better able to disentangle systematic response behaviour from genuine latent state expression, meaning that scales paired with the NEO are more likely to relate strongly.

5.2 Relationships Between Response Style Parameters.

Importantly, the response style parameters, seen in the green triangle in Figure 2, also show meaningful correspondence across tasks. In particular, the centre preference parameters exhibit strong, positive correlations between all three instruments (ranging from .63 to .72). As with the temporal stability analysis, these correlations exceed those observed for all but one within-survey latent state parameters and, without exception, those observed between latent state parameters across surveys. The BIS11 and NEO odds preference parameters show a more moderate, but still substantial (r = .43), association, likely reflecting differences in response option labels. Both instruments use 5-point scales, but the NEO asks respondents to indicate their degree of agreement, whereas the BIS11 asks how frequently people engage in the described behaviour. These conceptual differences in response framing likely encourage distinct interpretations of scale use across tasks, reflected in different degrees of psychological collapse.

Despite these framing differences, the correlation between the two odds preference parameters remains stronger than all but one between-instrument latent state correlations (the exception being BIS11's motor impulsiveness and BIS-BAS fun-seeking scales which have a correlation coefficient of .61). This provides strong evidence that response style is at least as stable (in most cases more stable) than the conceptually related latent state parameters. This provides further evidence that response styles represent a robust, task-agnostic source of individual differences, persisting even when constructs are only loosely related and highlighting the importance of explicitly modelling response styles.

One other interesting response style relationship is between the odds preference and centre preference parameters within the BIS11, which are negatively correlated. This pattern is theoretically interpretable because smaller odds preference estimates reflect a tendency toward even-category responding (i.e., responses of 2 and 4), which approximates midpoint responding (albeit with the loss of the neutral option), which would result in larger centre preference.

Although these response tendencies are not strict opposites, particularly given their differing implications for the unobserved midpoint response, they do reflect partially competing behaviours, accounting for the observed negative relationship.

5.3 Relationships Between Response Style and Latent State Parameters.

Additional links between response styles and latent state parameters further support the proposed model's theory. The NEO directional preference parameter shows relatively strong correlations with the BIS11 latent state parameters. Because BIS11 subscales are entirely positively or negatively keyed, any systematic directional responding must necessarily be absorbed into the location of the latent state distribution rather than being captured explicitly by a directional shift in thresholds. Importantly, the direction of these relationships is theoretically coherent; when scales are positively worded, higher directional preference estimates (which correspond with right-biased responding) will produce higher scale scores. Hence, the observed positive correlation for the entirely positively-framed scale (motor impulsivity) and the negative relationship with the two reverse-scored BIS11 scales (attentional and non-planning impulsivity) are consistent with expectations about directional response styles. Importantly, this also supports the inclusion of the directional preference parameter (where possible) as this kind of bias cannot be accounted for without it.

5.4 Response Style is Represented Separately From Latent State

To further examine the distinct contributions of response styles and latent states, an EFA was conducted on the correlation point estimates presented in Figure 2. This analysis was designed to assess whether parameters representing the two theoretically separable response style and latent state components would load onto distinct higher-order factors. Using the psych package in R (Revelle, 2025), a maximum likelihood extraction method was applied with the default oblimin rotation to allow for correlated factors. The Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin measure of sampling adequacy for the dataset was 0.76 and overall MSA above .5 for each item, suggesting this data was appropriate for factor analysis. According to the EFA, the best solution is represented by a four-factor structure. As shown in Table 5, the results suggest that each of the three survey tasks primarily defines its own factor, reflecting the task-specific nature of the latent constructs being measured, while the response style parameters, excluding the directional preference parameter, cluster onto a separate, fourth factor. This pattern is consistent with the theoretical expectation that response styles represent a consistent source of variation that can operate across tasks, but also highlights the extent to which their empirical separability depends on the structural properties of the measurement instrument. The directional preference parameter captures a tendency for individuals to preferentially select response options either above (right-biased) or below (left-biased) the middle of a scale.

However, the ability to estimate this parameter independently of latent state expression depends on survey design. In the case of the BIS11, the structure of the instrument limits this separability, as its subscales do not intersperse positively and negatively worded items; instead, one subscale (motor) is positively keyed and the remaining two are fully reverse-coded. Under these conditions, directional response tendencies cannot be mathematically disentangled from genuinely high or low latent state estimates, as both contribute to shifts in the location of the latent distribution. Consistent with this constraint, the EFA results indicate that the factor capturing the BIS11 latent states (Factor 2) is also the factor which reflects the directional preference in a more evenly balanced scale (NEO).

6 Knowledge of Response Style Reduces Bias Across Instruments

The proposition advanced in Section 5.4 -that both the directional preference parameter and the BIS11 latent states reflect directional responding -yields a novel, testable prediction within the LASARS framework. In this final section, we demonstrate how this LASARS provides a new perspective on the relationship between survey design and response style. We first establish that the predicted relationship is evident in the raw data and then apply our theoretically grounded modelling framework to reduce the confounding influence of response style. In doing so, we illustrate how our computational theory can leverage stable response preferences to disentangle latent state expression from decision-making strategies.

The LASARS theory predicts that it is impossible to distinguish true variation in the latent state from directionally biased responding when scales are not constructed with a balance of positively and negatively worded items. The implication of this finding is that simply knowing an individual's overall acquiescence tendency when responding to the NEO -where higher mean responses (shown on the x-axis of Figure 3) reflect greater agreement -strongly predicts apparent impulsivity on an entirely separate survey. This association is likely an unintended consequence of survey design, as neither theories of trait impulsivity nor theories of survey responding would predict such a strong relationship between impulsivity and decisions about how to use response scales.

Consequently, we argue that this problem would be considered resolved only if the correlation between mean NEO responses and BIS11 total scores is reduced toward zero.

Because the LASARS framework suggests that substantive latent state variation is independent of response style, the observed relationship between these two should attenuate once they are properly disentangled. In this way, the theory provides unique, testable predictions: that response styles systematically confound raw survey scores, and latent state scores will not be systematically related to directional responding when the two sources of information are separated. Although the influence of response styles on scale scores has been widely documented in empirical research (Baumgartner & Steenkamp, 2001; Schauberger & Tutz, 2022), the LASARS model goes beyond descriptive accounts by offering specific, quantifiable predictions.

These predictions can be directly evaluated (and, in this case, supported) using observed data, in contrast to purely statistical correction approaches.

To test these predictions, we first estimated the full set of LASARS response style parameters using solely the NEO data. We then estimated two variants of the LASARS model to the BIS11 responses. In a hybrid approach, we estimated centre preference and odds preference directly from the BIS11 data but imported each participant's directional preference parameter from the NEO-based model. This reflects scenarios in which it is desirable or necessary to estimate recoverable response parameters within the same data set used for latent state estimation, while supplementing them with externally identified components. In a second, more stringent test, we imported all three response parameters (centre preference, directional preference, and odds preference) from the NEO-based model and used them to adjust for response style in the BIS11 data, estimating only the latent state means of the BIS11. All PMwG and prior setting were the same as previously described. In line with our theory and the findings presented in this paper, this approach treats response style as a stable, cross-task characteristic and ensures that the parameters jointly function as an integrated system, though it imposes a stronger assumption of invariance across measures.

We next compared predicted BIS11 scale scores across the different model variants to determine whether the relationship between BIS11 scores and mean response selection on the NEO was attenuated after controlling for response style. To ensure that any differences in predicted scores reflected differences in latent state estimates rather than differences in response behaviour, we generated predictions under a common response style profile for all participants and models. The specific choice of response style parameters was largely arbitrary, provided they were held constant across comparisons. We, therefore, generated predicted responses using the group-level response style parameters estimated from the NEO model, together with the latent state estimates obtained from each of the two BIS11 models.

The middle column of Figure 3 shows a notable reduction in the correlation between scale scores and directional responding when the latter is explicitly accounted for, even when directional bias is estimated from an entirely separate dataset. This finding provides further support for the theoretical assumption that response styles are relatively stable across tasks and that information about them can be leveraged to mitigate the confounding effects of response style in other survey measures. Considered in isolation, and especially alongside the results in the right-hand column, this result underscores the importance of including a measure of directional responding within the model's response parameter set, even though it did not emerge naturally from the EFA performed on absolute threshold locations. This finding also demonstrates that borrowing information about directional bias from an external, well-designed scale can dramatically reduce its confounding influence on scale scores, relative to both model-free approaches and models that only partially estimate response style.

The right-hand column of Figure 3 illustrates the transferability of response style parameters across tasks, demonstrating that response parameters estimated entirely on one measure can be used to control confounding effects in another measure's latent state estimates.

Interestingly, this seemingly more stringent test of the LASARS model's stability assumption appears to reduce the association between scale scores and directional responding more effectively for the Motor and Non-planning BIS11 scales than the model that simultaneously estimates BIS11 latent states alongside centre preference and odds preference parameters (borrowing only the directional preference; middle column on Figure 3). This likely reflects the advantage of estimating response style parameters as a unified system..

7 Discussion

In this paper, we have introduced a theoretical account of self-report that conceptualises responses as arising from two separable but concurrent sources of individual difference: the latent state(s) the task intends to measure and the respondent's decision process governing how those states are communicated. By explicitly distinguishing between these components, the framework departs from traditional approaches that implicitly treat observed responses as direct reflections of underlying constructs, instead recognising that response generation also involves systematic, psychologically meaningful decision processes. Importantly, the proposed framework does not privilege either source of information; rather, it treats latent states and response processes as equally central, and jointly they generate observed data. We formalise this account for Likert-scale data using an ordinal-probit model and, in doing so, provide an interpretable account of how survey responses are generated. The model defines three conceptually distinct psychological processes which combine to characterise response behaviour, reflected by mathematical transforms governing the spacing and location of response thresholds. This quantifiable cognitive model represents a meaningful step toward a more psychologically grounded understanding of self-report responding. It also has the potential to improve the precision and interpretability of latent state estimates by explicitly accounting for variability attributable to response processes, which is a long-recognised source of bias in self-report data (Baumgartner & Steenkamp, 2001; Paulhus & Vazire, 2007).

This theoretical perspective is not, in itself, novel or surprising. The idea that self-reports are shaped by choices about how individuals disclose and convey information has long been acknowledged as a limitation of survey-based measurement (Paulhus & Vazire, 2007; Westland, 2022). What we contribute is a quantitative, theoretically grounded framework for separating these two sources of information. The LASARS model not only clarifies the nature of response behaviour but also generates novel, testable predictions about the interplay between response styles and latent state expression. In doing so, it offers a new lens through which survey data can be interpreted more informatively. The implications extend beyond the study of individual differences and response styles, providing value for any researcher or practitioner who relies on self-report measures to draw inferences about underlying psychological constructs.

Throughout this paper, we provide empirical support for the LASARS model's proposition that response style can be understood as a decision process under conscious control, while exhibiting a notable degree of stability across time and contexts when individuals choose their own response strategies. This dual characterisation aligns with broader perspectives in the decision-making literature, which recognise that response strategies may reflect both situationally contingent processes (Ames, 2022; Heitz, 2014) and more enduring individual differences (D. R. Johnson et al., 2008; Wetzel & Carstensen, 2015). We provide evidence that response styles and latent states are independent sources of variation and that, when this separation fails to occur, it is a consequence of poor scale design that confounds the two sources of information.

Importantly, our theory provides a principled solution to this confounding by allowing response style information obtained from well-designed external measures to be leveraged across tasks to mitigate the biasing influence of response style.

More specifically, our results indicate that relatively simple task instructions can induce systematic and targeted changes in response behaviour, particularly when respondents are asked to answer on behalf of another person. Instructions designed to encourage more or less extreme responding produced predictable changes in the model's centre preference parameter, which is psychologically interpretable as reflecting extreme and midpoint responding. Importantly, these shifts in response patterns were not accompanied by corresponding changes in latent state estimates, providing strong empirical support for the theoretical separation between substantive constructs and response processes.

In the absence of explicit instruction, we demonstrate that response style exhibits a high degree of temporal stability. Consistent with prior work suggesting that response styles possess a stable, trait-like component over extended periods (Danner et al., 2015; Weijters et al., 2010; Wetzel et al., 2016; Wu, 2025), our findings show that response style is generally at least as stable as theoretically-related latent states over a three-month interval. This is particularly noteworthy given that temporal stability is widely regarded as a defining feature of trait-like individual differences (McCrae & Costa, 1994). Hence, the persistence of response styles across measurement occasions suggests that they reflect enduring tendencies in how individuals engage with and use rating scales, rather than transient or purely situational decisions.

Similarly, we observed that response tendencies remained highly consistent across measures that differed in response format and theoretical framing. Notably, response styles were generally as stable, and in many cases more stable, than the theoretically-related psychological constructs they accompanied. This pattern aligns with prior research demonstrating that response styles generalise across tasks, instruments, and measurement contexts (Danner et al., 2015; Kieruj & Moors, 2013; Weijters et al., 2010; Wetzel et al., 2013), suggesting that they are not inherently tied to specific items, scales, or latent states. At the same time, this finding stands in contrast to item-level models of responding, which have shown that extreme and midpoint responding may not remain stable when task features, such as the number of response categories, change (Ames, 2022). Our proposed model helps reconcile these perspectives by demonstrating that, when response style is conceptualised broadly as a combination of multiple interacting response characteristics rather than isolated tendencies, it exhibits a high degree of cross-context stability.

In this sense, the proposed framework provides a more nuanced and integrative account of response behaviour, capturing consistent individual differences that extend beyond narrowly defined stylistic patterns and offering a clearer understanding of how response styles manifest across diverse measurement contexts.

Finally, we showed that poor scale design confounds the influence of directional response bias with latent state expression, and we tested a unique prediction derived from the LASARS framework: that response style information from one task can be used to compensate for design deficiencies in another, owing to the stability of response styles across contexts. Consistent with this prediction, we demonstrated that the observed relationship between directional bias and latent state scores is substantially attenuated when information about directional bias obtained from a well-designed scale is incorporated into latent state estimation. Importantly, this is not a prediction that purely statistical adjustment approaches can make. Rather, it is only through the integration of a theoretical account with a formalised quantitative model that this hypothesis can be articulated, tested, and ultimately supported, highlighting the benefit of our proposed framework.

Taken together, these findings provide direct empirical support for the theoretical assumption that response style operates consistently across different latent states. A key implication of these results is that further simplification may be both feasible and advantageous;

If response style is indeed stable across time and constructs, it may be reasonable to estimate a common set of response style parameters that generalise across measurement contexts. This simplification provides a more parsimonious account of response style while retaining improved latent state measurement precision. As we have demonstrated in Section 6, information about an individual's response behaviour obtained in one instrument can be leveraged to improve measurement precision in another, particularly in settings where direct estimation of response style is impractical due to suboptimal design or perhaps limited scale length. For example, an organisation seeking to assess employee satisfaction using a brief five-item questionnaire may lack sufficient information to reliably estimate response styles within that instrument alone; however, if employees have previously completed a more comprehensive measure (e.g., a personality inventory during intake), response style parameters derived from that context could be incorporated to improve the accuracy and interpretability of the shorter satisfaction survey.

In this way, the theory supports a more integrated and cumulative approach to survey measurement, where response style is treated as a transferable source of information that can enhance inference across otherwise disparate data collections.

While previous research has established that response styles can exhibit consistency across time and tasks (Weijters et al., 2010; Wetzel et al., 2016; Wu, 2025), an important contribution of the present work lies in the model's ability to simultaneously estimate and directly compare response styles and latent states within a unified framework. Given that our findings provide convergent evidence that response styles reflect systematic, domain-general tendencies in how individuals translate internal attitudes into observed responses, these processes should no longer be treated merely as nuisance variance to be controlled. Moving beyond simplistic categorisations such as "extreme" or "acquiescent," the characterisation of more complex response style signatures may offer additional predictive utility, for example in explaining variability in behaviour or patterns of latent state expression. Accordingly, the proposed modelling approach enhances the informational value of survey data by treating response style as an additional stream of signal, enabling richer inference and opening new avenues for understanding both how people respond and what their responses represent.

The LASARS framework also offers a new lens through which to interpret individual and group differences: are observed differences driven by true variation in latent states, or by systematic differences in how those states are communicated? Prior research indicates that factors such as age, sex, and cultural background can influence response tendencies (Batchelor & Miao, 2016; Chen et al., 1995), raising the possibility that observed group differences may be partially confounded by response style. For example, women self-report substantially higher rates of depression than men (Boerma et al., 2016); however, Sigmon et al. (2005) demonstrate that men self-report fewer depressive symptoms when task instructions indicate that they may be required to elaborate, relative to women. This finding suggests that gender-based differences in response behaviour may plausibly inflate or obscure true group-level differences in depression. Similarly, age-related changes in risk-taking (Duell et al., 2018; Rolison et al., 2014) and sensation seeking (Evans-Polce et al., 2018), which increase during adolescence and early adulthood before declining with age, closely mirror age-related variation in extreme responding (Batchelor & Miao, 2016). This correspondence raises the possibility that some proportion of observed differences in self-reported risk-taking and sensation seeking as a function of age may reflect changes in response behaviour, rather than genuine differences in underlying latent states. It is also possible that the same underlying psychological mechanisms that drive risk-related behaviours may also give rise to increased extreme responding. In either case, our unified theoretical framework's ability to distinguish between latent state expression and response behaviour provides a principled means of disentangling these effects and advancing understanding of the complex relationship between substantive individual differences and their expression.

The LASARS model predicts that these known group differences in response style may be reduced through explicit instruction when these populations are being surveyed in much the same way that task instructions can change people's speed-accuracy tradeoff settings in speeded decision-making tasks (Larson & Hawkins, 2023). This is an especially important consideration for use-cases where our proposed modelling solution cannot be appropriately applied, such as scales with only one latent state. It should be noted, however, that the manipulation we demonstrate aimed to induce a specific response style and, while it is plausible that instruction may serve to reduce the inverse pattern (i.e., instructions which encourage midpoint responding may neutralise some extreme responding), concrete conclusions about how task instruction may be used to minimise known group differences in response style require further investigation.

Improving the precision of latent state measurement by reducing the potentially biasing influence of response style may also help to improve the poor correlations between self-reported and objective and behavioural measures of the same constructs. Systematic reviews often conclude that correlations between self-report and behavioural measures fall within the weak to negligible range across many psychological domains, including inhibitory control (Saunders et al., 2018), self-regulation (Eisenberg et al., 2019), and emotional intelligence (Joseph & Newman, 2010). Similarly, self-reports often show limited agreement with objective measures or observer ratings (Vazire & Carlson, 2011), including assessments of personality (Vazire, 2010), pain experiences (Edwards & Fillingim, 2007) and academic ability (Dunning et al., 2004).

However, isolating the potentially confounding influence of response styles from true latent state expression may enhance the predictive validity of self-report measures, leading to stronger and more consistent associations with alternative indicators of the same constructs. In this way, self-report methods may regain some utility, as their considerable advantages -namely low cost, ease of administration, and straightforward analysis -are no longer undermined by systematic biases introduced by response styles.

It is important to note, however, that the present paper does not directly establish the model's ability to improve the construct or concurrent validity of survey instruments. During model validation (see Supplementary Material), we demonstrate that the proposed model can recover known generating parameters, showing that it is capable of recovering ground truth when the data-generating process is aligned with the model. However, evaluating whether this improves the ability of real-world survey instruments to recover an external ground truth lies beyond the scope of the present paper. We have shown that the model can enhance convergent validity across multiple simultaneously administered surveys, but this does not include comparisons with gold-standard measures (i.e., concurrent validity), nor does it examine predictive validity. Having established the theoretical validity of the framework and its capacity to disentangle response style from latent state information, an important avenue for future research is to quantify the extent to which the theory's bias reduction translates into gains in construct recovery, predictive performance, and overall measurement validity in applied settings.

It is also important to note that the LASARS model addresses systematic overuse or underuse of particular response options but does not extend to intentionally deceptive responding.

Although decisions about how to convey information that involve deliberate misrepresentation remain conceptually relevant, inasmuch as they still reflect choices about how information is communicated, they cannot be readily captured or disentangled within the present approach.

Deceptive responding effectively alters the latent state being expressed, meaning it cannot be identified as a response strategy that is separable from substantive latent states. Accordingly, while the present framework distinguishes between two sources of variation in self-report behaviour, it does not provide a complete solution in all cases. Just as the modelling approach cannot disentangle response style from latent states when poor scale design confounds the two, it is similarly limited when these components are intentionally conflated through deceptive responding.

Another general limitation of the current framing of response style is the lack of exploration of scales with more than five response options. Although the LASARS model is mathematically generalisable to survey instruments with any number of response categories, the interpretation of the odds preference parameter would necessarily shift under these conditions.

The odds preference parameter, which reflects a tendency to select (e.g. 1-3-5) or avoid (e.g. 2-4) odd response options on a 5-point scale, would no longer map cleanly onto the same behavioural interpretation in scales with a larger number of categories. For example, a high "odds preference" in a 6-point scale reflects high use of the first, third, fourth and sixth response options, changing the general interpretation and rendering the current parameter name misleading. This limitation is somewhat mitigated by the fact that 5-point Likert scales remain the most widely used format in practice (Kusmaryono et al., 2022), suggesting that the current formulation retains strong practical relevance in many situations.

8 Conclusion

This paper makes two central contributions to the study and measurement of individual differences. First, it advances a theoretical account of survey responding that treats response style as a psychologically meaningful and measurable source of individual difference, rather than unsystematic noise or a nuisance to be removed. Second, it specifies a flexible model-based framework that operationalises this proposal, allowing latent psychological states and response styles to be estimated jointly. By explicitly modelling response behaviour alongside latent state expression, the framework developed here offers a more complete representation of the processes that generate observed self-report responses than approaches that focus on either component in isolation. This theory, and the findings presented in this paper which support it, challenge the conventional view that observed survey responses reflect latent states plus random error alone, instead positioning response style as a systematic, stable and informative source of individual differences that contributes meaningfully to observed responses.

Importantly, the LASARS model achieves these gains without requiring a priori identification or classification of specific response styles. Instead, it estimates three-dimensional response style signatures directly from the data which, in turn, reflect a broad range of response tendencies, including complex or idiosyncratic patterns that are difficult to anticipate or label.
