Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). Statcheck might still miss one-sided tests that are described in another sentence, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

The COVID-19 pandemic provides a context for examining whether maladaptive personality traits-dispositions that can lead to distress and impaired functioning-respond to a lasting change in the shared environment. Using data from the GESIS Panel (N = 4,742), a probability-based sample of the German adult population, we examined maladaptive personality traits based on the Alternative Model for Personality Disorders in DSM-5. Traits were assessed four months before and eight months after the outbreak of the pandemic in Germany. Mean levels increased in antagonism (d = 0.20), the general factor of personality disorder (0.13), detachment (0.11), psychoticism (0.11), and disinhibition (0.10), but not in negative affectivity (-0.01). These changes are small but exceed the near-zero mean-level change expected over one year under normal conditions. Under assumptions we make explicit, we interpret them as a nonspecific effect of the pandemic. Increases were larger among younger and middle-aged adults than among older adults, for whom they were close to zero except in antagonism. Pre-pandemic trait levels predicted adverse life circumstances and negative perceptions of one's life. In exploratory analyses, romantic relationship conflict, psychopathological symptoms, and mistrust in public institutions were associated with small individual shifts toward higher trait levels.

Introduction

The Coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic disrupted the lives of millions and posed challenges to individuals' mental health and well-being. Some people adapted to the sudden upheaval, whereas others struggled (Anglim & Horwood, 2021; Benke et al., 2023; Hampshire et al., 2021; Kroencke et al., 2020; Proto & Zhang, 2021). For personality research, such a population-wide disruption raises two questions that are difficult to study under ordinary circumstances. The first is whether maladaptive personality traits-conceptualized as relatively stable dispositions-respond to a sudden and lasting change in the environment that affects an entire population at the same time. The second is whether the adverse circumstances that accumulate in such a period, and the way people perceive them, are conditions under which individuals shift toward more maladaptive functioning. Both questions bear on contemporary accounts of personality pathology as a dysfunction in person-environment transactions (Hopwood, 2018; Hopwood et al., 2022), and their answers matter for anticipating the mental health consequences of future crises. In this study, we address them using the Alternative Model of Personality Disorders (AMPD) featured in Section III of the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5; American Psychiatric Association [APA], 2013; Freilich et al., 2023; Zimmermann et al., 2019a), a framework of individual differences that has proven useful in clinical personality research.

We used data from the GESIS Panel (GESIS, 2021), a probability-based sample of the adult population in Germany, in which DSM-5 maladaptive personality traits were assessed approximately four months before and eight months after the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic. The survey waves conducted in between cover a variety of other constructs, which allowed us to relate maladaptive personality traits, as well as their change, to negative outcomes that individuals may have experienced during the first eight months of the pandemic. These outcomes included adverse life circumstances (e.g., inadequate housing, deterioration of the occupational situation, and absence of or conflict in a romantic relationship) as well as negative perceptions of one's life (e.g., mental health problems, lack of interpersonal support, and mistrust in public institutions). The aim of this study was to clarify (1) whether population-wide mean levels of maladaptive personality traits changed during the first eight months of the pandemic beyond what would be expected under normal conditions, (2) whether pre-pandemic levels of maladaptive personality traits predicted negative outcomes during the pandemic, and (3) whether individual differences in trait change were related to specific adverse life circumstances or negative perceptions of one's life.

Structure and Correlates of Maladaptive Personality Traits

Maladaptive personality traits refer to relatively stable dispositions of thinking, feeling, and behaving that can lead to negative consequences, such as subjective distress, harm to others, impaired functioning, or problems in living (Hengartner et al., 2018; Kerber et al., 2024; Leising & Zimmermann, 2011; Mullins-Sweatt & Widiger, 2010). These traits are integral to the characterization of personality disorders (PD) in the DSM-5 Section III (APA, 2013) and ICD-11 dimensional models (World Health Organization, 2021). The DSM-5 model organizes them into five domains that branch into more specific facets. Negative affectivity, detachment, antagonism (or dissociality), and disinhibition correspond to the pathological poles of emotional stability, extraversion, agreeableness, and conscientiousness (Suzuki et al., 2015 (Suzuki et al., , 2017;; Wright & Simms, 2014), whereas psychoticism is associated with a propensity toward unusual beliefs, perceptions, and thought patterns; whether it can be considered a maladaptive variant of openness is disputed (Widiger & Crego, 2019; for this view, see Blain et al., 2020). The domains share common variance, a pattern that is taken to indicate a core personality pathology construct and is typically modeled by a general latent factor representing PD severity (Asadi et al., 2022; Zimmermann et al., 2020), commonly referred to as the general factor of personality disorder (g-PD).

Maladaptive personality traits as featured in the DSM-5 AMPD are supported by considerable research on their reliability, validity, and importance as transdiagnostic factors in mental health problems (Freilich et al., 2023; Zimmermann et al., 2019a). For example, symptoms of depression and anxiety were found to be particularly associated with higher levels of negative affectivity and detachment (Zimmermann et al., 2014). Higher levels of maladaptive personality traits are also associated with negative perceptions of other aspects of one's life. In a daily diary study, higher levels of all trait domains were associated with more social stress in daily life, and detachment in particular was associated with fewer positive events (Zimmermann et al., 2019b). Other studies found that higher levels of maladaptive traits were associated with lower romantic relationship satisfaction, across all five domains (Wilson et al., 2018), most consistently for negative affectivity and detachment (Decuyper et al., 2018), and prospectively for DSM-5 Section III personality disorder types (Ingram & South, 2021). In a longitudinal study, negative affectivity, detachment, and disinhibition predicted lower satisfaction with life 1.4 years later (Wright et al., 2015).

With respect to outcomes during the COVID-19 pandemic, a meta-analysis by Ścigała et al. (2021) found that individuals high in aversive personality traits (e.g., the dark triad) evaluated guidelines aimed at containing the spread of the virus negatively and showed less prosocial and less health-related behavior such as social distancing, and a review (Starcevic & Janca, 2022) and an internal metaanalysis of five samples (Zettler et al., 2022) also found associations between maladaptive or aversive traits and lower compliance with health guidelines. Research on the DSM-5 AMPD traits suggests that high levels of all domains are associated with maladaptive responses to the pandemic (e.g., Sica et al., 2021; Tanzilli et al., 2022) and that some domains are associated with specific maladaptations. High levels of detachment were particularly associated with low use of social support, whereas the use of avoidance strategies was associated with all domains, most strongly with disinhibition (Sica et al., 2021), COVID-19-related anxiety was most strongly associated with negative affectivity (Zemestani et al., 2021; see also Bochicchio et al., 2021; Semeraro et al., 2023), pathological personality traits predicted fear of COVID-19 (Velotti et al., 2021), and psychoticism was associated with belief in conspiracy theories, also prospectively during the pandemic (Halama & Teličák, 2024; Swami et al., 2016). Taken together, these findings suggest that pre-pandemic maladaptive personality traits may predict adverse life circumstances and negative perceptions of various aspects of one's life during the pandemic.

Mean-Level Change in (Maladaptive) Personality During the Pandemic

Although (maladaptive) personality traits are viewed as relatively stable, it is now widely accepted that they can also change (Bleidorn et al., 2022). One form of change that is frequently studied in personality development research is change in the mean level of a trait across a population. To interpret a possible change in the mean level of maladaptive personality traits from four months before to eight months into the pandemic, and to attribute it to the pandemic as a nonspecific cause, an estimate of the change that would have occurred without the pandemic is needed. Several benchmarks are available. First, Bleidorn et al.'s (2022) meta-analysis of longitudinal studies found that mean-level change in personality traits varied with the time interval between assessments and with participants' age, with the largest changes in young adulthood. Their metaregression implies that normative mean-level change over one year at age 54.5, the mean age in our sample, is essentially zero (model-implied d between -0.04 and -0.01, depending on how age is modeled). True effects varied considerably across samples, however (τ ≈ .15). In the GESIS Panel, average year-to-year change in the Big Five was close to zero (Roemer et al., 2025). Second, two studies that assessed DSM-5 maladaptive personality traits repeatedly in clinical samples found only small mean-level changes, namely an average reduction of d = -0.11 over a 1.4-year period in patients with personality disorders who had access to mental health care (Wright et al., 2015) and increases in negative affectivity (d = 0.10) and detachment (d = 0.17) between subsequent admissions among patients repeatedly seeking partial hospitalization (Rodriguez-Seijas et al., 2020). Third, single life events typically change Big Five traits only slightly, with an average standardized effect of 0.06 across seven panel studies (Haehner et al., 2026) and significant event-specific effects ranging from about d = 0.09 to 0.28 in absolute value (Bühler et al., 2024), and collective disasters have produced even less change, as mean levels of the Big Five remained largely stable after the 2011 Christchurch earthquake (Milojev et al., 2014) and after Hurricane Harvey (Damian et al., 2021). Fourth, Rek et al. (2023) reported average reductions ranging from d = -0.38 (antagonism) to d = -0.89 (disinhibition) over an intensive seven-week psychotherapy course in depressed inpatients and day-clinic patients, consistent with metaanalytic evidence that psychological interventions can change personality traits (Roberts et al., 2017). Taken together, mean levels of maladaptive personality traits are unlikely to change within one year under normal circumstances, single life events produce small changes, and moderate to large changes have been observed mainly over the course of interventions.

To develop an expectation regarding possible changes in mean levels of maladaptive traits during the pandemic, we have to turn to results on Big Five traits, because no study has followed the maladaptive traits of the same individuals from before into the pandemic. One study compared the DSM-5 trait domains between two independent French samples, one assessed before and one during a lockdown, and found no differences, but such a design cannot capture change within individuals (Combaluzier & Bourgoise, 2025). Longitudinal studies of Big Five traits with a pre-pandemic baseline yielded small and partly inconsistent changes. Neuroticism decreased and extraversion increased slightly in a U.S. online panel assessed in February and March 2020 (d = -0.04 and 0.03; Sutin et al., 2020), emotional stability increased between 2019 and 2020 in the probability-based Dutch LISS panel (Manole & Curșeu, 2024), German employees assessed 11 times from December 2019 to December 2022 increased mainly in conscientiousness (d = 0.14) and emotional stability (d = 0.11; Rudolph & Zacher, 2023), and German students reported lower extraversion and neuroticism during lockdowns than before the pandemic (Krautter et al., 2022). Studies with repeated cross-sectional samples (Condon & Weston, 2021) or with a first assessment only after the onset of the pandemic (Kyle et al., 2025) are more difficult to interpret.

The most informative study for our purposes is that of Sutin et al. (2022), who used the probability-based Understanding America Study and compared each participant's assessment during the pandemic with up to two pre-pandemic assessments. In 2020, personality was largely unchanged except for a slight decrease in neuroticism. In 2021 and 2022, extraversion, openness, agreeableness, and conscientiousness had declined by about one tenth of a standard deviation, whereas neuroticism no longer differed from pre-pandemic levels. In terms of the DSM-5 model, these declines correspond to increases in detachment, antagonism, and disinhibition. Taken together, the evidence points to small changes whose direction and timing differ across studies, which makes it difficult to formulate definitive expectations.

Personality change in response to the pandemic might be moderated by age, as age could be associated with differences in stress exposure related to social roles and health risks (Birditt et al., 2021; Ioannidis et al., 2020). During the pandemic, younger and middle-aged adults could have been more likely to experience disruptions to work, family, and social life (Glowacz & Schmits, 2020), whereas older individuals may have faced elevated health risks or increased isolation (Ernst et al., 2022). In addition, personality traits tend to show greater stability at older ages (Bleidorn et al., 2022), which may constrain the extent of change in response to external stressors. Consistent with this possibility, Sutin et al. (2022) found that the declines in 2021 and 2022 occurred among younger and middle-aged participants, whereas participants aged 65 and older showed no significant declines in these traits (their neuroticism had decreased in 2020).

Individual Conditions of (Maladaptive) Personality Change

Another form of change that is frequently studied in personality development research concerns changes in the rank order of individuals with respect to trait levels (Bleidorn et al., 2022). Studies employing the DSM-5 trait model have evidenced high, albeit not perfect, rank order stability for maladaptive personality traits in clinical and non-clinical settings (Rodriguez-Seijas et al., 2020; Wright et al., 2015; Zimmermann et al., 2017), which implies that some individuals change more in a particular direction than others. These individual differences in change can be modeled directly with the latent change score (LCS) model (McArdle, 2009), which allows examining correlates or predictors of change. Rather than attributing change to the pandemic as a nonspecific cause by comparison with a benchmark, as for mean-level change, this approach identifies individual conditions of change in maladaptive traits. Previous research on Big Five traits typically considered life events in the domains of work, love, and health as catalysts for personality change (Wagner et al., 2020). While individual life events tend to exert small effects (Bleidorn et al., 2021; Bühler et al., 2024; Denissen et al., 2019), their accumulation may have a more substantial impact on personality (Dugan et al., 2024). Successful work transitions and experiences of social inclusion typically associate with shifts toward more adaptive personality traits (Lüdtke et al., 2011; Wagner et al., 2015) and toward higher self-esteem (Harris & Orth, 2020; Hutteman et al., 2015), whereas deteriorating health, particularly in late adulthood, can spur shifts toward more maladaptive traits (Wagner & Mueller, 2017). Beyond the objective characteristics of life events, their subjective experience in terms of perceived valence, impact, predictability, and challenge is important as well (Luhmann et al., 2021), and the perceived valence of events was associated with changes in Big Five traits, most consistently in neuroticism or emotional stability (de Vries et al., 2021; Haehner et al., 2023; Lüdtke et al., 2011).

Contemporary theories underscore the dynamic and reciprocal processes involved in establishing and maintaining maladaptive trait levels (e.g., Hopwood, 2018; Hopwood et al., 2022). Maladaptive traits often provoke recurring dysfunctional interpersonal cycles that are reinforced by negative responses of the environment. Mistrust, for instance, can trigger controlling behavior, prompting others to withdraw, which confirms and amplifies the initial mistrust. In daily life, negative emotions and interpersonal conflict fuel each other in a selfsustaining cycle (Vize et al., 2024), and personality dysfunction predicts more frequent and more persistent interpersonal conflicts as well as a tighter coupling between conflicts and mood (Kerber et al., 2026). Such feedback loops of maladaptive personality patterns and adverse life events can extend over many years (Jeronimus et al., 2014; Metts et al., 2021). Therefore, we expected that individuals high in maladaptive traits would experience more adverse circumstances during the pandemic, and that individuals who experienced such circumstances would shift further toward higher trait levels. A two-wave design can test these two expectations only separately and under assumptions that we make explicit below, and it cannot establish a reciprocal process.

The Present Study

The small and partly inconsistent changes found in previous studies on personality change during the COVID-19 pandemic, and the absence of longitudinal studies on maladaptive traits, call for additional research. Using two assessments from four months before to eight months into the pandemic in the GESIS Panel (N = 4,742), we examined mean-level change in maladaptive personality traits (Research Question [RQ] 1; "mean-level change"), prospective associations between pre-pandemic trait levels and negative outcomes during the pandemic (RQ 2; "prospective associations"), and the role of these outcomes as predictors of trait change (RQ 3; "predictors of change"). Neither the research questions nor the analyses were preregistered, and the analyses for RQ 3 are exploratory.

We treat RQ 1 as a causal question and state the assumptions on which this interpretation rests (Lawes et al., 2025; Rohrer & Murayama, 2023). The estimand is the average change in maladaptive trait levels in the German adult population between October/November 2019 and October/November 2020, relative to the change that would have occurred in the same population and period without the pandemic. Because every member of the population was exposed, this counterfactual change cannot be observed. We approximate it by the normative one-year change expected at the mean age of our sample under normal conditions, which is close to zero (Bleidorn et al., 2022). Attributing observed change to the pandemic therefore requires that (a) no other population-wide event changed maladaptive trait levels in Germany during the same year, so that the pre-pandemic assessment approximates the counterfactual trajectory; (b) the instrument functioned equivalently at both assessments, which we tested through longitudinal measurement invariance; and (c) differential attrition and panel conditioning did not shift mean levels. Assumption (a) cannot be tested directly, but a pandemic effect is more plausible if change occurred in the same direction across several trait domains and if it was larger in age groups that were more exposed to pandemic-related disruptions. RQ 2 asks whether pre-pandemic trait levels predict later outcomes. For RQ 3, we compare individuals who reported an adverse circumstance with those who did not; whether such comparisons can be interpreted causally depends on assumptions about unobserved confounders, which we address in the Method section.

Method Participants

Our study uses data from the ongoing GESIS Panel (GESIS, 2021), which at the time of this study comprised three cohorts totaling approximately 5,400 panelists drawn from the adult German population by probability-based sampling. Comparisons with census benchmarks show that the composition of the panel matches that of the German adult population well on most sociodemographic characteristics (Bosnjak et al., 2018). Waves are fielded every two months using either online questionnaires or paper-and-pencil surveys that are distributed and returned by mail. Panelists are assigned to one of the two modes at recruitment, and no participant in our sample changed mode between T1 and T5. For this study, we selected five waves of the GESIS Panel (labeled by GESIS as ge, hb, hc, hd, and he). The first wave (T1) was conducted in October/November 2019, several months before the COVID-19 pandemic was declared a public health emergency of international concern. The subsequent waves (T2-T5) were collected during different stages of the pandemic with different levels of public life restrictions in Germany. We included participants who completed the Personality Inventory for DSM-5, Brief Form Plus (PID5BF+; Kerber et al., 2022) with less than 25% missing responses at T1. This resulted in a sample size of N = 4,742, comprising 50% women and 50% men, with ages (at T1) ranging from 24 to 76 years (M = 54.5, SD = 14.3). Because this study is a secondary analysis of existing panel data, the sample size was not determined by an a priori power analysis but by the number of panelists who met this inclusion criterion, all of whom were included.

At later waves, we observed 283 cases of full nonresponse at T2 (6.0%), 430 at T3 (9.1%), 442 at T4 (9.3%), and 383 at T5 (8.1%). Small positive associations were found between PID5BF+ domain scores at T1 and full nonresponse at later waves (r ≈ .05 across domains), suggesting that individuals higher in maladaptive traits were slightly more likely to be absent at later measurement occasions. In addition, missing values may arise when participants do not respond to individual items. For 90% of included variables, item non-response ranged between 0.2% and 2.1%. Notable outliers were the four items assessing psychopathological symptoms in the past week, with item non-response of up to 5.5%, and the item assessing satisfaction with life in the work domain, which showed 8.3% item non-responses. Item non-responses for PID5BF+ items were consistently below 1%.

The GESIS Panel is run by the Leibniz Institute for the Social Sciences (GESIS). Participation is voluntary and based on informed consent, and the data are made available for scientific use in anonymized form. The present study is a secondary analysis of these anonymized data and did not require separate approval by an ethics committee.

Measures

Personality Inventory for DSM-5, Brief Form Plus (PID5BF+)

The PID5BF+ (Kerber et al., 2022) is a 34-item self-report instrument that measures maladaptive personality traits according to the AMPD in DSM-5 and is a short form of the Personality Inventory for DSM-5 (APA, 2013). Using a 4point scale, respondents rate statements from Very false (0) to Very true (3). Item responses can be averaged to create scores for five maladaptive personality trait domains (i.e., negative affectivity, antagonism, psychoticism, detachment, disinhibition) or used in confirmatory factor analysis to model trait domains as latent variables. The anankastia domain was not considered due to its low reliability (Kerber et al., 2022). We also did not include the 17 maladaptive trait facets (e.g., deceitfulness, manipulativeness, grandiosity), as each of these facets is represented by only two items in the PID5BF+. The PID5BF+ was administered at T1 (before the pandemic) and T5 (eight months into the pandemic).

Circumstances and Perceptions of One's Life During the COVID-19 Pandemic

We included outcome variables from different life domains as indicators of adverse outcomes during the pandemic and grouped them into two broad categories: circumstances and perceptions of one's life. The life circumstances category included variables related to living and working conditions (e.g., inadequate housing space, deteriorated occupational situation) and romantic relationships (e.g., absence of romantic relationship, romantic relationship conflict). The life perceptions category comprised variables indicative of personal wellbeing (e.g., low life satisfaction, psychopathological symptoms), quality of interpersonal relationships (e.g., perceived lack of interpersonal support, interpersonal mistrust), and attitudes toward public institutions and policymakers (e.g., mistrust in these entities, dissatisfaction with democracy, belief in COVID-19-related conspiracy theories). For statistical analysis, all variables were coded so that higher scores corresponded to more adverse outcomes. Statistical Analysis

The five trait domains of PID5BF+ and related versions of the Personality Inventory for DSM-5 are typically modeled in a joint measurement model that uses facet scores as indicators. Although this parceling approach has certain advantages, it overlooks item-level structure and cannot detect noninvariance of individual items (Little et al., 2013), which makes it less suited for evaluating measurement invariance (MI; Meredith, 1993). We therefore used items as indicators and specified separate measurement models for each trait domain. Each domainspecific measurement model included two latent factors that represented the trait domain at T1 and T5, with each factor loading on the six items assessed at the respective time point. We additionally specified correlated residuals for each item across time points (e.g., Item 1 at T1 and Item 1 at T5) and for item pairs belonging to the same facet, both within time points (e.g., Item 1 and Item 19 at T1) and across time points (e.g., Item 1 at T1 and Item 19 at T5). Residual correlations for corresponding pairs of same-facet items were constrained to equality.

The measurement model for the g-PD generally followed the same approach, except that composite scores (i.e., scale means) representing the trait domains served as indicators and only the residuals of the same domain score were allowed to correlate across time points. The composite scores for antagonism and detachment were computed without Item 2 and Item 4, respectively, because these items were not invariant across time (see Results). All analyses were conducted in R version 4.6.1 (R Core Team, 2026). Considering that PID5BF+ items are assessed on a 4-point scale and show some asymmetry in their frequency distributions, the decision between treating them as continuous or ordinal is not straightforward. Simulation studies suggest that robust continuous estimation performs acceptably with five or more response categories but less reliably with four or fewer (Rhemtulla et al., 2012). Robitzsch (2020), however, argued that the results of such simulations depend on the data-generating model assumed and therefore cannot determine the appropriate modeling strategy. The common ordinal approach (i.e., polychoric correlations with diagonally weighted least squares estimation, such as WLSMV) rests on its own assumption of normally distributed latent responses, which is also likely to be violated in practice. It is therefore not necessarily superior and, if anything, adds complexity to model estimation and interpretation. In this research, treating indicators as ordinal would complicate identification of MI models (Wu & Estabrook, 2016) and cannot yet be implemented within the LSEM framework available in the sirt package (Robitzsch, 2025). We therefore treated PID5BF+ items as continuous for all analyses. Models were estimated using maximum likelihood with a scaled (Yuan-Bentler) test statistic and robust (Huber-White) standard errors (MLR) with the R packages lavaan version 0.7-3 (Rosseel et al., 2026) and sirt version 4.2-133 (Robitzsch, 2025). LSEM models were estimated with standard maximum likelihood, as robust corrections are not implemented for the joint estimation procedure. To address missing data, we assumed missingness at random (Enders, 2010) and used full information maximum likelihood (FIML) estimation (Schafer & Graham, 2002). For detailed model syntax, readers may consult the analysis scripts and the knitted R Markdown document in the Zenodo repository.

Measurement Invariance of the PID5BF+ Across Time and Across Age

Tests of MI can help to determine whether inferences from measures to constructs hold to the same extent across time (Widaman et al., 2010) or across groups of individuals. The intended inferences in RQ 2 ("prospective associations") and RQ 3 ("predictors of change") assume that correlational patterns arise from the same constructs across time, which we tested through metric longitudinal MI of the PID5BF+. The inference in RQ 1 ("mean-level change") that observed differences between time points reflect changes in construct levels assumes scalar longitudinal MI, which we tested as well. Age-dependent mean-level change (part of RQ 1) assumes metric MI across age. Scalar MI across age is not required for our research questions; it would be needed only to interpret mean-level differences between age groups as substantive differences in maladaptive traits (Little, 2013).

To test the hypothesis that the PID5BF+ measures the same constructs on the same metric across time, we tested longitudinal MI using repeated measures confirmatory factor analysis (CFA). Using the identification strategy outlined by Widaman et al. (2010), we examined longitudinal MI for each trait domain of the PID5BF+ separately, using the configural MI model as a baseline (Panel A of Figure 2), and then gradually constraining model parameters across time points. We evaluated longitudinal metric MI (i.e., constraining factor loadings) and longitudinal scalar MI (i.e., constraining intercepts) by scrutinizing how constraints affected fit indices including Comparative Fit Index (CFI), Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA), and Standardized Root Mean Square Residual (SRMR). We interpreted a decrement in CFI for a more restrictive model relative to a less restrictive one as evidence of an MI violation when the ΔCFI exceeded the |.01| threshold proposed by Cheung and Rensvold (2002). This threshold is a convention, and fit index difference cutoffs do not generalize across model and data characteristics (McNeish, 2026); we therefore also report which items caused the misfit and what consequences imposing versus releasing the constraints had for the estimates. When a violation occurred, we identified local areas of strain using modification indices and released equality constraints for one item at a time to obtain a model with a ΔCFI smaller than |.01| (partial MI; Byrne et al., 1989). Whether and how to account for a noninvariant item is a modeling decision (Robitzsch & Lüdtke, 2023). We released constraints only when the misfit was concentrated in a single item and a change in that item's functioning during the pandemic was plausible.

To test the hypothesis that the PID5BF+ measures the same constructs across time and age, we extended the longitudinal MI model (Panel A of Figure 2) to LSEM (Hildebrandt et al., 2016) by including age as a non-linear continuous moderator with focal points between 24 and 74 in steps of 5 and a bandwidth factor of 2 (Hildebrandt et al., 2016; Liu et al., 2025). Using the joint estimation procedure (Robitzsch, 2023), we derived global fit indices for unconstrained configural models, wherein age was allowed to moderate all free parameters, and for models in which groups of parameters (i.e., loadings, intercepts) were constrained to equality across age. Each age-weighted subsample functions as a separate group in joint estimation, which makes the procedure analogous to a conventional multigroup CFA (Liu et al., 2025). Again, a decrement in the magnitude of ΔCFI greater than |.01| was considered an MI violation (Cheung & Rensvold, 2002), and equality constraints were released one item at a time to derive partial MI across age.

Mean-Level Change in Maladaptive Personality Traits (RQ 1)

First, we estimated average mean-level change using LCS models (Panel B of Figure 2), represented by the mean of the latent change factor. The variance of the latent factor at T1 was fixed to 1, so that the mean of the latent change factor equals the mean-level change in units of the latent SD at T1. We report it as Cohen's d, which corresponds to the metric used in the meta-analysis of Bleidorn et al. (2022). For comparison, we additionally report d based on observed scale means (mean difference divided by the observed SD at T1), both for the item sets of the latent models and for the full six-item scales. This is the metric of the clinical studies by Wright et al. (2015) and Rodriguez-Seijas et al. (2020). Second, we estimated age-dependent mean-level change by extending the LCS models to LSEM (Hildebrandt et al., 2016), using joint estimation with factor loadings constrained to equality across age (Robitzsch, 2023), so that the latent metric is identical at all focal ages. Local estimates of the change mean were divided by the latent SD at T1 in the total sample and are thus expressed in the same metric as the average d. Bootstrapped 95% confidence intervals (500 replications) were computed for the local estimates. A permutation test of the mean absolute distance (MAD) of the latent change parameter across focal ages was used to test whether mean-level change varied with age. We interpreted effect sizes against the benchmarks summarized in the introduction.

Prospective Associations Between Maladaptive Personality Traits and Adverse Outcomes (RQ 2)

We examined associations between pre-pandemic levels of maladaptive personality traits and adverse life circumstances and negative perceptions of one's life during the pandemic. To this end, we estimated correlations between the latent PID5BF+ variables at T1 and the observed outcome variables assessed between T2 and T4 within the LCS model (Panel C or D of Figure 2). We evaluated the effect sizes of correlation coefficients using the empirical benchmarks of Gignac and Szodorai (2016) that suggest interpreting a correlation near .10 as a small effect, near .20 as a moderate effect, and near or greater than .30 as a large effect.

Adverse Outcomes as Predictors of Maladaptive Personality Trait Change (RQ 3)

Latent change can be conceptualized via two approaches (McArdle, 2009): the difference score approach, implemented via the LCS model (Panel C of Figure 2), in which the covariate is correlated with the latent change score, and the residualized change approach, implemented via the latent change regression (LCR) model (Panel D), in which the association between covariate and change is estimated conditional on the initial latent level. The two approaches can yield different and even opposite conclusions (Lord's paradox; Lord, 1967), because they rest on different assumptions about unobserved confounders. The LCS estimate is unbiased if the change that would have occurred without the adverse circumstance does not depend on whether a person experienced it, whereas the LCR estimate is unbiased if experiencing the circumstance is independent of unobserved causes of change given the initial trait level (Lüdtke & Robitzsch, 2025). When an unobserved stable confounder affects both the circumstance and the trait in the same direction and the trait is less than perfectly stable, the LCS estimate is a lower bound and the LCR estimate an upper bound of the causal effect (Lüdtke & Robitzsch, 2025; see also Angrist & Pischke, 2009; Ding & Li, 2019). We therefore concluded that an outcome was related to change in a trait domain only when both estimates were significant at α = .05 and had the same sign, in which case the conclusion holds under either assumption set. Because this decision rule is a conjunction (intersection-union) test, its two constituent tests require no alpha adjustment (Berger, 1982; Rubin, 2021), and because each of the 66 combinations of outcome and domain is a separate inference rather than a test of the joint hypothesis that at least one outcome is related to change, no familybased adjustment across combinations is needed either (Rubin, 2021 (Rubin, , 2024)). Supplemental Note 3 nevertheless reports, as a sensitivity analysis, the results after a Holm (1979) adjustment across the 66 combinations, applied to the joint p value of each conjunction test (the larger of its two p values; Berger, 1982). Two limitations of this strategy apply to both models: the outcomes were assessed between T2 and T4, that is, within the interval over which change was measured, so that early change may have preceded the outcome; and the bounds do not hold if an unobserved vulnerability affected trait levels more strongly during the pandemic than before. We therefore describe RQ 3 results as associations between reported circumstances and trait change and discuss their causal interpretation with these caveats.

Longitudinal Measurement Invariance of the PID5BF+

We evaluated longitudinal measurement invariance of the PID5BF+ across T1 and T5 and report detailed results in Table 1. Configural MI models showed conventionally acceptable fit with CFI ≥ .988, RMSEA ≤ .037, SRMR ≤ .025. Full scalar MI was supported for four of the six PID5BF+ scales, namely negative affectivity, disinhibition, psychoticism, and the g-PD. For these scales, constraining factor loadings and item intercepts across time did not produce a meaningful decrease in model fit (ΔCFI < |.010|).

For antagonism, constraining factor loadings did not meaningfully reduce model fit but constraining intercepts did so with ΔCFI = -.015, which indicated a violation of scalar MI. The largest modification index (scaled χ² = 146.36) concerned the intercept of Item 2 ("I'm good at conning people"). Relaxing this constraint resulted in a partial scalar MI model whose fit did not differ meaningfully from the configural MI model with ΔCFI = -.003.

For detachment, constraining factor loadings produced a meaningful decrease in model fit with ΔCFI = -.013. This decrease reflected misfit associated with the equality constraint on Item 4 ("I keep my distance from people"; scaled χ² = 143.62). Constraining intercepts resulted in an even larger deterioration in model fit with CFI dropping from .981 to .879 (ΔCFI = -.103). Again, Item 4 was responsible for the misfit (scaled χ² = 966.99). Relaxing both equality constraints for Item 4 yielded an acceptable partial scalar MI model where the decrement in CFI relative to the configural model was ΔCFI = -.006.

Given that MI violations were confined to two scales and involved one item each, we retained partial scalar MI models in which the constraints on Item 2 (antagonism) and Item 4 (detachment) were released in all subsequent analyses. The parameter estimates of these items at T1 and T5 and the consequences of imposing the violated constraints are reported in Supplemental Note 2.

Over the one-year period from T1 to T5, the PID5BF+ domains showed latent correlations ranging from .78 to .83, and the g-PD a latent correlation of .78. Differences between middle-aged and older participants were pronounced, with estimates approaching zero at older ages for all scales except antagonism, which remained at d = 0.11 at age 74.

Discussion

Maladaptive personality traits, as defined in the DSM-5 AMPD, are considered stable dispositions that influence thoughts, feelings, and behaviors and can lead to a variety of adversities (e.g., Hengartner et al., 2018; Leising & Zimmermann, 2011; Mullins-Sweatt & Widiger, 2010), yet few longitudinal studies have examined these traits (e.g., Rodriguez-Seijas et al., 2020; Wright et al., 2015). We used two assessments of the GESIS Panel, a probability-based sample of the German adult population, to examine maladaptive trait change and its correlates across the first eight months of the COVID-19 pandemic. Three findings emerged. First, mean levels of antagonism, psychoticism, detachment, disinhibition, and the g-PD increased slightly (d = 0.10 to 0.20), whereas negative affectivity did not change; the increases were concentrated in younger and middle-aged adults and, except for antagonism, close to zero at older ages. Second, higher pre-pandemic trait levels predicted adverse life circumstances and negative perceptions of one's life during the pandemic. Third, individuals who reported romantic relationship conflict, psychopathological symptoms, or mistrust in public institutions shifted slightly more toward higher trait levels than those who did not. We discuss each finding in turn, together with the assumptions its interpretation requires.

Maladaptive Personality Trait Change During the COVID-19 Pandemic

Previous longitudinal research on Big Five traits found small and partly inconsistent changes during the pandemic (Krautter et al., 2022; Kyle et al., 2025; Manole & Curșeu, 2024; Rudolph & Zacher, 2023; Sutin et al., 2020 Sutin et al., , 2022)). The direction of our findings agrees with the declines in extraversion, agreeableness, and conscientiousness that Sutin et al. (2022) observed in a U.S. probability panel, which correspond to increases in detachment, antagonism, and disinhibition, and our null finding for negative affectivity fits the absence of a lasting increase in neuroticism at the sample level in that and other studies (Manole & Curșeu, 2024; Sutin et al., 2022), although younger adults in the U.S. panel increased in neuroticism in 2021 and 2022. In the U.S. panel, however, these declines appeared only in 2021 and 2022, whereas the increases in our sample were already present in late 2020. Unlike the pandemic studies reviewed above, we used latent variable modeling and tested longitudinal MI, a necessary condition for interpreting the observed increases as changes in the constructs rather than in the functioning of items across time (Widaman et al., 2010). The absence of change in negative affectivity suggests that, broadly speaking, the shift occurred at the behavioral and interpersonal level rather than at the affective level.

How large are these changes, and can they be attributed to the pandemic? Under normal conditions, mean levels of personality traits are expected to remain essentially unchanged over one year for adults around age 54 (Bleidorn et al., 2022). The increases we observed, d = 0.10 to 0.20, are small by conventional standards, but they are of the same magnitude as the between-sample heterogeneity in normative change (τ ≈ .15; Bleidorn et al., 2022) and as the larger effects of single life events on personality traits (Bühler et al., 2024; Haehner et al., 2026). They are smaller than the changes observed over intensive psychotherapy (Rek et al., 2023) and similar in magnitude to the small increases in negative affectivity and detachment among patients repeatedly admitted to partial hospitalization (Rodriguez-Seijas et al., 2020). Attributing the changes to the pandemic rests on the assumptions stated in the introduction. The two assumptions we could examine hold reasonably well: at least partial scalar invariance across time was established for all domains, and attrition was only weakly related to trait levels (r ≈ .05). The central assumption that no other population-wide event changed maladaptive trait levels in Germany between late 2019 and late 2020 cannot be tested with our data, although we are not aware of a plausible alternative event. Two features of the results are consistent with a pandemic effect but do not prove it: change occurred in the same direction in four of five domains, and it was concentrated in younger and middle-aged adults, who were more likely to face disruptions of work, family, and social life during this period, such as reduced occupational activity and social contact among young adults (Glowacz & Schmits, 2020) and school and childcare closures among working parents (Möhring et al., 2021), whereas older adults, whose traits are also more stable in general (Bleidorn et al., 2022), showed little change except in antagonism. In a U.S. sample of adults aged 60 and older, pandemic-related worries and hardships predicted unfavorable changes in personality profiles (Fu et al., 2024), which likewise suggests that exposure rather than age per se drives change. An alternative reading is that the changes reflect transient states rather than lasting trait change; with two assessments, we cannot distinguish the two (Lucas, 2023), and the second assessment coincided with the rapid rise of infections in autumn 2020.

These findings are consistent with the possibility that crises such as the COVID-19 pandemic pose extraordinary challenges for individuals to adapt to a changing environment, making it difficult for many to continue moving toward their personal goals and thus triggering maladaptive behavioral and experiential patterns (DeYoung & Krueger, 2018; Ringwald et al., 2021). These challenges include the pervasive threat of infection and serious illness, lockdown policies that limit personal freedoms such as seeing friends and family, or the increased burden of working from home or caring for children due to school and childcare closures (Möhring et al., 2021; Naumann et al., 2020). From the perspective of the TESSERA framework (Wrzus & Roberts, 2017), lasting trait change is expected when situations that trigger particular states recur over extended periods and the resulting state expressions are repeatedly reinforced. This distinguishes a pandemic lasting for many months from a single disaster such as an earthquake or a hurricane, after which trait levels remained largely stable (Damian et al., 2021; Milojev et al., 2014).

Our longitudinal MI analyses also illustrate how item meaning can shift in a changing environment. Item 4 of the PID5BF+ ("I keep my distance from people") violated both metric and scalar MI: its intercept increased and its loading on detachment decreased markedly from T1 to T5 (Supplemental Note 2). The item likely came to reflect adherence to social distancing norms rather than detachment as defined in the DSM-5, that is, maladaptive tendencies toward social withdrawal, avoidance of intimacy, and restricted emotional experience. Had we imposed the equality constraints, the estimated mean-level change in detachment would have more than doubled (d = 0.23 instead of 0.11), just as the manifest d of the full sixitem scale (0.29) exceeds that of the remaining five items (0.10). Removing the item from the model yields the same estimate as releasing its constraints (Supplemental Table S3). Item 2 ("I'm good at conning people") violated scalar but not metric MI, with a higher intercept at T5. One interpretation is that pandemic-related restrictions increased the prevalence of minor, socially sensitive norm violations (e.g., private gatherings exceeding permitted group sizes), so that endorsing the item partly reflected situational demands for concealment. We released the constraints for Item 4 on substantive grounds; for Item 2, the decision rested mainly on the statistical evidence, and retaining the constraint would have increased the estimated change in antagonism from d = 0.20 to 0.25.

Prospective Associations Between Maladaptive Personality Traits and Adverse Outcomes

Individuals with higher pre-pandemic levels of maladaptive traits were more likely to experience adverse life circumstances and to perceive their lives negatively during the pandemic, including inadequate housing, strained interpersonal relationships, and mistrust of public institutions. Higher levels in all trait domains were associated with lower well-being and more psychopathological symptoms, consistent with conceptualizing maladaptive personality traits as dysfunctions in person-environment transactions (e.g., Hopwood, 2018; Hopwood et al., 2022) that lead to problems in living (Hengartner et al., 2018; Kerber et al., 2024; Leising & Zimmermann, 2011; Mullins-Sweatt & Widiger, 2010). Most of these associations were small to moderate and resemble those found outside the pandemic. For instance, negative affectivity showed the strongest association with psychopathological symptoms (r = .51), which parallels the finding that neuroticism predicted distress throughout the pandemic in a U.S. probability panel (Angrisani et al., 2026), and detachment was most strongly associated with interpersonal outcomes such as perceived lack of support and interpersonal mistrust. The g-PD, indicative of PD severity (Zimmermann et al., 2020), predicted nearly the full range of negative outcomes, in line with evidence that PD severity, as assessed with the DSM-5 Level of Personality Functioning, is related to functional impairment (Sharp & Wall, 2021). In the same panel, pre-pandemic Big Five traits likewise explained only a small portion of the variance (0.6% to 3.8%) in responses to the pandemic, including trust in public institutions (Rammstedt et al., 2022).

Individual Conditions of Maladaptive Personality Trait Change

We examined which adverse life circumstances and negative perceptions of one's life were related to individual shifts toward higher trait levels, requiring that an association be present in both the difference score and the residualized change model. Whereas 47 of the 66 associations between outcomes and trait change were significant in at least one of the two models, only six associations, involving three outcomes, were significant in both. The effect sizes (LCS estimates of .04 to .07) are comparable to those observed for single life events on personality trait change (average b = 0.06; Haehner et al., 2026).

Participants who reported conflict in their romantic relationship shifted more toward higher levels of the g-PD and of psychoticism than participants who did not. This is consistent with research indicating a mutual amplification of interpersonal conflict and negative emotionality on a finer temporal scale (Vize et al., 2024) and with the view that interpersonal disturbances lie at the core of personality pathology (e.g., Hopwood, 2018; Wright & Ringwald, 2022). In a large ambulatory assessment study, personality dysfunction was associated with more frequent, more persistent, and more volatile interpersonal conflicts and with a tighter coupling between conflicts and mood, and the strength with which conflicts spilled over into subsequent mood was the dynamic feature that explained the most variance in personality dysfunction one year later (Kerber et al., 2026). Further, separation, the highest response option of the romantic relationship conflict item, is a frequent trigger of suicidal thoughts and behaviors (Kazan et al., 2016). Participants with greater mistrust in public institutions shifted more toward higher psychoticism, which is consistent with the associations of psychoticism with conspiracist beliefs (Halama & Teličák, 2024; Swami et al., 2016) and of conspiracist ideation with low trust (Bowes et al., 2023). Participants with more psychopathological symptoms shifted more toward higher negative affectivity, which reflects the conceptual interrelatedness of these constructs (e.g., DeYoung et al., 2022; Widiger et al., 2019).

These findings are exploratory and should be read as preliminary rather than established effects. Yet they are consistent with theoretical expectations, and each association was significant and directionally consistent across two models that rest on different assumptions about unobserved confounding, which provides converging evidence under the respective assumptions. Under the conditions described in the Method section, the two estimates bracket the causal effect (Lüdtke & Robitzsch, 2025), so that a causal effect of these three circumstances on trait change is a possible reading. Because these conditions cannot be verified with our data, the agreement of the two estimates is best understood as robustness to the choice between two sets of assumptions. Two threats remain. The circumstances were assessed between T2 and T4, within the interval over which change was measured, so that early trait change may have contributed to the conflict, symptoms, or mistrust that participants reported. And if a vulnerability that increased exposure to these circumstances also made people more susceptible to the pandemic's effects on their traits, both estimates would be biased in the same direction. Together with the results for RQ 2, the pattern is compatible with reciprocal personenvironment transactions (Hopwood, 2018; Hopwood et al., 2022), but a two-wave design cannot demonstrate such a process.

What do these results imply beyond the individual level? The rank-order stability of maladaptive traits over the year was high (latent r = .78 to .83), mean-level changes were small, and the associations with adverse circumstances were weak. In this sense, people largely remained who they were during the first eight months of the pandemic, which speaks for the robustness of personality under societal stress. At the same time, even small average shifts affect many people when they occur population-wide (Götz et al., 2022), and higher levels of maladaptive traits are associated with problems in interpersonal relationships and other life domains (e.g., Hopwood, 2018; Lazarus et al., 2014; Wilson et al., 2017). Whether the shifts we observed persisted, reverted, or accumulated over the further course of the pandemic is an open question that later waves of the GESIS Panel and comparable panels could answer.

Strengths and Limitations

Our study has three strengths relative to previous research: a probability-based sample that allows population-level estimates of mean-level change, assessments before and during the pandemic with the same instrument, and latent variable models with tested longitudinal measurement invariance. Its limitations qualify each of our conclusions, and we list them in the order of their importance. First, the causal interpretation of meanlevel change (RQ 1) rests on the untestable assumption that maladaptive trait levels would have remained unchanged in Germany between late 2019 and late 2020 without the pandemic. We consider this assumption plausible given the meta-analytic benchmark and the absence of other population-wide events, but a design with an unexposed comparison group cannot be realized for a pandemic. Furthermore, as noted above, two assessments cannot separate lasting trait change from transient state effects (Lucas, 2023), particularly because the second assessment coincided with a rapid rise in infections before vaccines were available. The effects should therefore be understood as change over the first eight months of the pandemic, not as lasting change.

Second, the two threats to a causal reading of RQ 3 described above, reverse causation within the change interval and a vulnerability that acted more strongly during the pandemic than before, cannot be ruled out. In addition, the analyses were not preregistered, and RQ 3 examined 66 combinations of outcomes and trait domains. We report all estimates in Table 3, but the associations we highlight were selected from this larger set, and none of them remains significant after a Holm adjustment across all combinations (Supplemental Note 3). They are exploratory and require replication. Third, several outcomes overlap in content with the PID5BF+ domains. We compared the content of each outcome measure with the content of the PID5BF+ items of each domain and identified the pairs in which at least one outcome item and one PID5BF+ item describe the same experience or behavior (Supplemental Note 1). Overlap in item content is confined to psychopathological symptoms with negative affectivity and detachment, and to perceived lack of interpersonal support with detachment. Such overlap may inflate the prospective associations of RQ 2 and, because initial trait levels are controlled, particularly the LCR estimates in RQ 3. Associations with outcomes that do not share item content, such as romantic relationship conflict, inadequate housing, or mistrust in public institutions, are not affected by this problem.

Fourth, several outcomes were assessed with single items or had low base rates (e.g., 6% inadequate housing, 9% occupational deterioration), and the reliability of the multi-item outcomes ranged from ω = .77 (low life satisfaction) to .93 (perceived lack of interpersonal support). Measurement error in the outcomes attenuates their associations with trait change, so that null findings, for instance for occupational deterioration, should not be taken as evidence of absence. Fifth, all measures were selfreports, so that common method variance may have inflated associations (Podsakoff et al., 2024), and the PID5BF+ items were treated as continuous with four response categories, which may bias parameter estimates (Rhemtulla et al., 2012). Finally, estimates of agedependent change were less precise below age 35, and age is confounded with cohort in a single panel, so that the contrast between middle and older adulthood is the most reliable part of the age pattern.

Beyond these limitations, three constraints on generality should be kept in mind. The results describe the adult population of Germany. Countries differed in the course of infections, in the stringency of containment measures, and in the social security provisions in place, and maladaptive traits may have changed differently under other conditions. Mean-level change also differed by age within the range covered by the panel (24 to 76 years), so that the average estimates should not be generalized across age groups or to ages outside this range. In addition, the results refer to the first eight months of the pandemic and do not speak to its later phases. Future research should examine whether the changes replicate in probability-based samples from other countries, use more than two assessments to separate lasting trait change from transient states, and assess adverse circumstances before the interval over which trait change is measured.

Conclusion

During the first eight months of the COVID-19 pandemic, mean levels of maladaptive personality traits in the German adult population increased slightly, most clearly for antagonism and among younger and middleaged adults, whereas negative affectivity remained unchanged and older adults showed little change except in antagonism. Under the assumptions we have made explicit, these changes are best interpreted as a small nonspecific effect of the pandemic, comparable in size to the effects of single life events. Pre-pandemic trait levels predicted how people fared during the pandemic, and, in exploratory analyses, romantic relationship conflict, psychopathological symptoms, and mistrust in public institutions accompanied individual shifts toward higher trait levels. Whether these changes persisted is unknown. The results support neither dramatic personality change during the pandemic nor complete stability; they suggest that maladaptive personality traits are sensitive, in small ways, to a profound change in the shared environment, and that monitoring such change belongs in the mental health response to future crises.
