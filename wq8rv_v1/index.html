How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

The RetractionWatch Check module compares the reference list against studies in the RetractionWatch database based on the DOI. If a study in the database is found, a reminder is provided that the study was retracted, has an expression of concern, or a correction.

The module requires that the reference has a DOI: the DOI in the paper’s reference list, or one looked up with add_bib_match() before the module runs (see ref_table()). References without a DOI are not checked.

It is possible the authors are already aware that a study was retracted, but the module can’t evaluate this.

The database can be manually updated with the rw_update function. For more information, see https://gitlab.com/crossref/retraction-watch-data.

How Replication Check works

This module checks references and warns for citations of original studies for which replication or reproduction studies exist in the FLoRA database.

The Replication Check module compares the reference list against studies in the FLoRA (FORRT Library of Replication Attempts) database based on the DOI. If a study in the database is found, a reminder is provided that a replication or reproduction of the original study exists, and should be cited. No reminder is given when the paper already cites that replication or reproduction (matched by its DOI).

The module requires that the reference has a DOI: the DOI in the paper’s reference list, or one looked up with add_bib_match() before the module runs (see ref_table()). References without a DOI are not checked.

It is possible the original study was cited for other reasons than the empirical claim tested, or that the replication/reproduction in the FLoRA database is for only one of the studies in the paper, and not the study the authors discuss.

The database can be manually updated with the FLoRA_update() function. For more information, see https://forrt.org/FLoRA/.

Abstract

Many technical texts exist on the potential problems of measurement error (i.e., variables are measured imperfectly) and the inclusion of interaction effects in linear regression. Such resources are typically rife with techniques that may or may not solve the situation (e.g, mean-centering) depending on the presence or absence of certain conditions (e.g., multicollinearity). Understanding such texts can be daunting to applied researchers, especially as visualizations of the potential effects of these are typically lacking. We provide a Shiny app to help users better understand and anticipate such situations. Based on user-specified input, the app allows users to compare a model that includes only linear effects to a model that additionally includes an interaction effect when such an interaction exists. It allows comparisons of the effects on regression weights, their bias and variance at different evaluation points, confidence regions for regression surfaces, model fit, and model differentiability. This information should ultimately help researchers to understand the problem at hand and align their substantive research objectives with their modeling choices better. Thus, the app is a useful tool towards both learning about and teaching about potential pitfalls of simple linear regression and is aimed at applied researchers in the social sciences.

The effect each type of measurement error has on regression and how it can possibly be corrected largely depends on the structure of the error itself (Innes et al., 2021; Schmidt & Hunter, 1996; Van Smeden et al., 2020).

In statistical models the purpose of an interaction is to capture data structures in which the effect of one predictor on the outcome is conditional on the level of a second predictor (Baranger et al., 2023; Rimpler et al., 2025; Venables, 1998). However, other definitions include any type of deviation from linearity and non-zero first partial derivatives. An overview of different conceptualizations along with their limitations can be found in Rimpler et al. (2026a) and Duncan and Kefford (2021). In the following, we refer to product-like interaction effects. If an interaction is not modeled, the regression estimates are subject to omitted variable bias, where the regression weights for the included variables adjust to partially make up for the omitted variable (Clarke, 2005; Feigenberg et al., 2025; Jeyaraj, 2024; Rimpler et al., 2025; Wilms et al., 2021). Simplifying the model in such a way means having to deal with the joint effects of classical measurement error and interaction omission. We have described these joint effects in an analytical manner in Rimpler et al. (2026b). The effects are non-additive and because of that it can be difficult to intuit the result of the combined modeling problem. Situations can exist where simple effects are positive for a model including an interaction term, but negative for a model excluding an interaction term, or vice versa. It is not possible to provide blanket rules for which modeling choice, to include or exclude an interaction term, is better, and this necessitates a proper understanding of the effects including an interaction term may have. Unfortunately, in our experience existing sources on these topics are either incomplete or inaccessible to applied researchers.

To this end, we provide an interactive Shiny app (Chang et al., 2021), available at https://interacterr.gmw.rug.nl/ and downloadable as R package on github at https://github.com/ARimpler/interactErr. The app allows the user to compare a model that includes only linear effects to a model that additionally includes an interaction effect when such an interaction exists. This tutorial focuses not only on the model which includes the interaction effect, but also on the effects a simplification has. The app allows users to investigate how modeling or omitting an interaction affects the bias and variance of regression weights, model fit, model differentiability, and prediction accuracy under different levels of measurement error. In addition, the app highlights the importance of evaluation points (due to regression on raw versus mean-centered data or simple slopes) and their link to the plausible range of data to evaluate the risk of sign flips (i.e., one or more simple effects turning from positive to negative, or vice versa). This information should ultimately help researchers to understand the problem of measurement error in combination with omitting interaction effects when they are present in the data-generating mechanism.

Before we present the different features of the app and how they relate to the challenges a researcher faces, we start with a concrete example that highlights some of the effects the choice of including an interaction effect may have on estimates of our simple effects. The parameters used in this example are also the default values in the Shiny app. We then introduce different features that can be used for the evaluation and comparison of linear regression models that include versus exclude an interaction effect. The topics we introduce can be roughly sorted into three different categories. The first relates to functional shape. Here, we provide the possibility to visualize different relevant 3D regression surfaces and how they relate to the range of the data. The second category relates to the regression weights, such as evaluation points of the model, the bias of regression weights and the variance of regression weights. The third category relates to the residuals of the models, such as model fit, differentiability, and prediction accuracy of the two competing models. It is important to note that all these different aspects are not independent of each other and we relate the following topics back to the previous when possible.

Leading Example

Let us assume we have infinite sample sizes ("populations") and a normally distributed predictor x with a mean of 5 and a variance of 1. We have a second predictor z with a mean of 3 and a variance of 1. Both variables were measured with a reliability of 0.8. These predictors are not correlated with each other and as such have a covariance of 0.

We assume that these two predictors relate to an outcome variable y. We model this relationship with a linear regression with an interaction effect with the weights b0 = 0 (intercept), b x = 1 (linear effect of x), b z = 1 (linear effect of z), and b xz = -0.75 (product interaction of both x and z). We suspect that there are other variables related to the outcome variable, however these are independent of our predictors. Additionally, the outcome is subject to classical measurement error. As a result, we have noise in the outcome which makes up roughly one third of the total variance of the outcome y, so that the true R² with these predictors is 0.66.

We have visualized example data (N = 10, 000) following these specifications as scatterplots between the observed predictors and the outcome variable in Figure 1. Now, if we want to analyze the data, we only have access to the noisy, observed predictors.

What happens if we analyze this data, both with a linear regression where we model the interaction, and with a linear regression where we do not model the interaction?

For the model with the interaction effect we get b0 = 1.15, b x = 0.44, b z = 0.20, b xz = -0.48 and R² = 0.52. For the simplified model without the interaction effect we get b0 = 8.35, b x = -1.00, b z = -2.20, and R² = 0.50.

As we can see from this, the model with an interaction has positive linear effects and a negative interaction effect. In contrast, the model without the interaction has negative linear effects (compare the signs of b x and b z for both models). One might expect these qualitative differences to be reflected in the R² values, but this is hardly the case here. The difference in R² is only 0.02.

In this example we see that even though the difference in R² is marginal, the difference in regression estimates can be drastic, even reversing the sign of the estimated effects. Given that the example is based on infinite samples, the behavior of these models for finite samples will in many cases be even harder to disentangle. Which model should a researcher favor? Should it be: (1) the model that aligns best with the visualization of the data; (2) the model with the better fit; or (3) the model without the interaction, because the fit is almost the same, and as such we should favor parsimony?

More generally, what does it mean that the different models have linear effects of opposite signs?

We provide a Shiny app (https://interacterr.gmw.rug.nl/) to facilitate a better understanding of these different features of regression models and how they interrelate. The app can also be run locally using R, importing the package can be done from GitHub at https://github.com/ARimpler/interactErr. The app can be started using the R code "interactErr::run_app()". In what follows, we explain all the different app features step by step.

Meet the App: Parameter Inputs and Properties of Predictor Variables

As previously mentioned, one of the assumptions in regression is that predictors in a linear regression model are measured error free. The reason behind this is that in regression vertical, but not horizontal, distances between regression line and data points are optimized, so that they are as small as possible. This means that in regression outcome variables are treated differently from predictor variables (van Ravenzwaaij, 2025; Xu, 2014).

Surprisingly, the predictor means also affect the substantive conclusions of a linear regression model. When we change the means of variables through a process like mean-centering, we potentially change the regression weights β, which can have implications for our interpretation of simple effects. We elaborate on this further in the sections "Visualization of Regression Surfaces" and "Evaluation Points".

For all results relating to infinite samples we assume normally distributed predictors.

However, all results can differ for predictor variables that are not normally distributed-as a result we provide users with the possibility to also obtain results based on skewed-normal predictors. Because of this they can differ from the infinite sample results.

App Feature 1: Functional Shapes for Models Including and Excluding Interactions

The most important difference between a data-generating process where only linear main effects are present and a data-generating process where both linear main effects and an interaction effect are present is the functional shape. In the following, we illustrate both functional shapes for a specific example.

If we theorize about a data-generating process, we define an outcome, here called y, and some variables on which this outcome depends, here two predictor variables called x and z.

Mathematically this translates to 'y is a function of x and z' or y = f (x, z). However, just defining y as a function of x and z does not mean we have characterized how y develops across the values of x and z. For example, we can say that y constantly decreases when x and z increase, and the respective decrease is constant, but y increases more when x increases than when z increases. Or we could say y increases up to a certain level of x, and then decreases again. Or y could increase when x increases and z increases, but the increase of x changes depending on the increase of z: After a certain increase of both x and z, y starts decreasing.

InfoBox 1: What is Classical Measurement Error?

Classical measurement error is a completely stochastic error. It is assumed that the error has a mean of 0, a constant variance, is independent of the true scores, other variables, other errors, and itself, and thus does not contain any information about any variable (Guolo, 2008; Nab et al., 2021). Classical measurement error only adds noise (variance) to the true variable (Carroll et al., 2006, p. 5), thereby lowering the reliability defined by classical test theory, the ratio of the true score to observed score variance (Cochran, 1968).

A kitchen scale can illustrate this. If we measure 1kg of flour and the measurement randomly fluctuates around 1kg, we have classical measurement error. This is independent of whether the fluctuation is in the range of 1g, 10g, or 100g-as long as the scale on average shows 1kg. It also does not matter whether is is sugar or flour that we are weighing! Classical measurement error is not error that is systematically related to other variables. For example, if a kitchen scale is put on a towel when weighing, the measured weight would be systematically lower than 1kg (the soft surface would absorb some of the pressure). Another example would be weighing different liquids such as water, and alcohol with the purpose of trying to establish the volume of these liquids. The scale is not wrong-the different liquids do weigh the same, but those weights translate to different volumes for each liquid: This is a conceptual error.

Generally, the linear effects b x and b z indicate the slope of the tangents of the interaction surface, at a specific evaluation point. For the raw, non-centered regression the evaluation points are both zero. For the mean-centered regression the zero value of the data is changed to the respective sample means, so that the regression surface gets evaluated at the x and z values equal to the sample means. We refer to this change in evaluation point as a re-parameterization. Neither parameterization is strictly better than the other: both have advantages and disadvantages. For example, mean-centering is often recommended for an easier interpretation of the linear main effects, because when mean-centered the regression coefficient represents the linear effect of one predictor at the mean level of the other predictor (Cronbach, 1987 Cohen et al., 2015 , p.261-267, Haslbeck et al., 2021) and it becomes the same as one would get by application of the linear effects only model. 3In many instances mean-centering can be a great choice, in particular when zero values are meaningless for the data. App Feature 4: Regression Estimates and Their Bias

The bias of estimates refers to the average difference between true effects and estimated effects. In practice, different sources of bias can arise-for example, bias due to misidentifying the functional relationship between variables (Ganzach, 1998; Rimpler et al., 2026a), bias due to omitting variables (Clarke, 2005; Rimpler et al., 2025; Simon, 1954; Westfall & Yarkoni, 2016), bias due to measurement error (Carroll et al., 2006; Fuller, 1987), or a combination of multiple bias sources (Rimpler et al., 2026b). While each source can be understood in isolation, their joint effects can be harder to intuit.

In the current tutorial we focus on two major sources of bias, bias due to omitting an interaction and bias due to classical measurement error. Importantly, in the case of interaction effects, if the true means of the predictors are non-zero, the error structure can be more complex and interact with the error in the other predictor due to the multiplicative nature of the interaction term (Rimpler et al., 2026b).

After we have learned the formal definition of regression estimate bias it might help to see what it refers to visually. First, we have to go back to Figure 4 and remember that the yellow curved surface in the middle panel represents the functional shape of the data-generating mechanism assumption-free. Now, the bias of a regression weights estimate is the difference in the slope of x or z at any specified evaluation point between the true surface and the surface stemming from a model. Important to keep in mind is that if we consider true interaction effects, we have to take the evaluation point into account when talking about bias.

App Feature 5: Regression Surfaces Implied by Confidence Regions

Regression estimates are typically reported alongside other metrics, such as standard errors or confidence intervals. These metrics are intended to indicate variability as an indicator of the certainty of the regression estimate across (hypothetical) repeated samples. Now, if we consider regression estimate b x ± se x , b z ± se z and b xz ± se xz -does that mean b x -se x and b xz + se xz as well as b x + se x and b xz -se xz are combinations that can occur across repeated samples? The answer is that they cannot, because the regression estimates are not independent of each other. We can report or visualize this dependence through confidence regions, which are multivariate generalizations of confidence intervals and for linear models typically visualized as 2D ellipsoids. In the app, we do something different and visualize regression surfaces that are implied by the confidence region following the Hotelling (1940) and Working and Hotelling (1929) procedure, based on the simulated data. The idea is that the variability in the estimated regression weights directly translates to the uncertainty in the predictions ŷ, which we again visualize in a 3D figure. Regression estimates are directly related to the predictions. The difference between the values y and the predictions ŷ which the different models make are also called residuals. In the following sections, we will focus on these residuals, as they are used to compute different metrics relating to model fit. These metrics can in turn be used to differentiate models.

App Feature 6: Residual Related Metrics

The residuals in linear regression are the difference between observed values of the outcome y and the predictions made by the regression model. Evaluation of residuals tends to rely on some type of visual inspection which tends to be difficult given that in multiple linear regression, these are multi-dimensional. The difference between observed and predicted values are used to calculate different metrics related to model fit, model differentiation, and prediction accuracy. The prediction accuracy refers to a model's ability to forecast unseen outcome scores as precisely as possible.

In the app we provide users with analytic, infinite sample results for the explained variance R² , the root mean squared error (RMSE), and the mean squared error (MSE). In statistical work on predictive modeling, R² is oftentimes not considered as a good measure for predictive fit, for example because it is typically an overestimate (see also Browne, 2000; Rocca & Yarkoni, 2021; Shmueli, 2010), because it cannot indicate whether the predictions are biased or heteroskedastic, and because its magnitude largely depends on the variance of the outcome variable. Nonetheless we provide R² alongside other metrics because of its familiarity. Additionally, the difference ∆R² for nested models is often used as an indicator of the value added by an interaction. We also provide the infinite sample MSE (for a detailed overview see Rimpler et al. (2026b)) because it forms the basis for many different fit and differentiation metrics under different assumptions and transformations, but also for the computation of the variance of regression estimates.

To get a better intuition for residuals and the different metrics, let us first have a look at data-generating process in yellow. On the right we see the differences between the true regression surface and the regression surfaces between the model including an interaction term (goldenrod) and excluding the interaction (dark green).

There are multiple observations one can make from the right-hand panel. First, the surface relating to the model including the interaction is still curved. This is because this model cannot fully capture the curvature due to the measurement error (if the reliability is 1 for both predictors, this surface will be flat). Second, the more curved surface in the panel indicating the difference between true and predicted surface (i.e., the green plane) relates to the model excluding the interaction. This is because linear effects cannot capture the curvature-also in the absence of measurement error. However, in this Figure it is clearly noticeable where the models predict larger or smaller values than the true values, simply by comparison to the 0 value on the y -ŷ scale.

The average difference between these two surfaces in the figure on the right directly relate to the different fit and prediction accuracy metrics. The average of the squared difference between these two surfaces (plus the error in the outcome y), constitute the difference in the mean squared error(MSE). In fact, the square of these surfaces directly constitutes the squared bias in the decomposition of the mean squared error into bias and variance, known from the bias-variance trade off (Hastie et al., 2009, pp. 159-160). Squaring the numbers related to the surfaces has two problems. First, because negative numbers squared are positive it is not possible anymore to see where the model over-or under-predicts. Second, the numbers are not on the same scale as the original outcome anymore-by taking the roots of the average of the squared difference between these two surfaces we get the difference of the root mean squared error (RMSE). This means the RMSE has the advantage over the MSE that it is directly on the outcome scale and gives an estimate for the average prediction error. But what does it mean if these values differ 0.07 (see example in Figure 13)? Because these values are absolute values on the scales of the outcome, we can also take the variance of the outcome itself into account to get a relative value. If we take the squared average difference between these two surfaces and divide it by the variance of the outcome, we get the R² difference. However, because of the standardization process, R² does not simply represent the squared bias from the bias-variance trade off anymore and, importantly, is largely influenced by the range/variance of the outcome itself.

For the model comparison based on fit metrics in Figure 13 we use the same layout and structure as those used in Figure 9, where we provide the results for infinite samples on the left and for finite, simulated samples on the right. The fit metrics for the infinite samples provided here are not subject to overfit in contrast to what one would obtain by sampled data, like the simulated data in the table on the right.

In Figure 14a we provide the difference for the R² , RMSE and MSE for the infinite samples across different reliability levels of the predictors. The black crosses within the figures indicate the chosen reliability levels. The reason behind the full grid of reliability for these metrics is that often we do not know the true reliability of psychological measurement instruments.

However, sometimes the fit difference between the reliability of say 0.6 and 0.8 might be marginal (this always depends on the exact data configuration and there is no general rule of thumb).

We also provide summaries for fit differences for the simulated, finite sample data. If only limited data is available (finite samples) statistical models tend to adjust to the particular data at hand and adding variables to a model will yield larger (over-)fit. For example, R² values cannot decrease if variables are added. However, this does not mean the model will also perform better on new, unseen data, or that this model is 'more' correct. Because this is a known issue, many different fit metrics, with different goals and assumptions, were developed which take these properties of samples and models into account.

Here, we provide the difference between three such measures: R² adjusted , AIC and BIC.

The R² adjusted simply corrects for the sample size and number of predictors, with the goal to get an unbiased estimate of the R² one would obtain on infinite samples. The AIC is a model selection metric, with the goal to minimize the out-of sample prediction error based on in-sample prediction error (Burnham & Anderson, 2004; Ding et al., 2018). The BIC is closely related, but in contrast to the AIC the idea behind the BIC is that if the correct model is part of a model comparison, it is the target of such a selection procedure (Burnham & Anderson, 2004). Empirical Example

In this section, we demonstrate with an empirical example that small fit differences, with meaningful interpretational differences, between a regression model with only linear effects versus a model that includes an interaction effect is not just a problem encountered for hypothetical data sets. Lenzo et al. (2022) were investigating the relationship between prolonged grief (PG) as outcome, with social support, attachment dimension(s) and the interaction between social support and attachment dimension(s) as predictor variables. Here, we focus on the reported results relating to the avoidant attachment dimension. The authors have reported a mean of 56.31 for avoidant attachment, and a standard deviation of SD = 11.22. The authors have also reported that within their sample the avoidant attachment ranged from 32 to 93. For social support, a single item measure with a range from 1 to 10, the authors have reported a mean of 7.73 and a standard deviation of 2.34. Both predictors have a covariance of -4.40.

The authors have reported a linear regression model Lenzo et al. (2022) which includes an interaction effect on mean-centered data with the estimates b intercept = 27.28(0.68), b support = 0.23(0.31), b avoidance = 0.23(0.06), b interaction = 0.05(0.02), and an R² = 0.10, where according to the paper adding the interaction yielded ∆R² = 0.02.

We have used this data to generate a regression surface, which we display in Figure 15a.

Because these findings are based on (noisy) empirical data, we do not take additional effects of measurement error into account, but stick to the implied surface based on the reported data.

The reported model was mean-centered, which we have to take into account when specifying the range. This is highlighted by indicator 1 in Figure 15. For the predictors we have to take the lower range minus the mean, to get the lower range value based on the mean-centered data, and the upper range minus the mean, to get the upper range of the data. If we focus on the arrow pointed to by indicator 2, we see that this arrow points downwards, whereas the arrow pointed to by indicator 3 points upwards. Because these arrows follow the direction of the tilt of the yellow surface, including the interaction, we see that whether we interpret the association between z and y as positive or negative depends on the evaluation point on x. This represents a sign flip of the association within the data range considering the curvature due to the product-like interaction. In 15b we indicate the exact number where this flip happens. The association for z changes at x = 4.6, which is well in the range of x, as the observed range goes roughly form -25 to 36 based on mean-centered data. The same is not the case for variable z-here the sign flip of z occurs again at 4.6. However, based on mean-centered data the upper limit is 2.3.

In contrast, if we inspect the linear effects only model (see both blue arrows), we see that both point upwards and as such represent a positive linear effect for both predictors, irrespective of the evaluation point. If we give this a substantive interpretation we see that the model with interaction specifies a positive relation between avoidant attachment and PG throughout the whole range of the data, whereas whether the relation between social support and PG is positive or negative depends on the level of avoidant attachment-for people high on avoidant attachment there is a negative association, for people low on avoidant attachment there is a positive association. This is in contrast to the model without interaction which specifies a positive relation between social support and PG throughout the full range of scores on avoidant attachment. This all happens, despite the small fit difference they found in the empirical data.

Summary

Throughout this tutorial we have aimed to demonstrate how a data-generating process which includes a product-like interaction effect and is subject to classical measurement error influences a regression model in which the interaction effect is modeled, but also the model in which this effect is omitted. Unlike existing tutorials, which focus exclusively on the statistical power of interaction effects (e.g., Baranger et al., 2023; Sommet et al., 2022) we have attempted to capture both competing models in their entirety. This includes expected regression surfaces, evaluation points and regression estimates, model fit and model differentiability.

We showed that while an increase in measurement error flattens out the curvature of the regression surface, this does not automatically imply attenuation of the regression coefficients as their estimates can also become more extreme. This effect is driven by the mean values of the predictor variables. If a noisy predictor is subject to measurement error, all regression estimates are influenced.

If only the linear effects are modeled, the resulting slopes are biased to differing degreesthe further we move away from the sample means, the more the slopes get biased. This does not necessarily have to be reflected in big differences in model fit, so that it is possible that slopes can change direction (i.e., from positive to negative) within the predictor range. These qualitatively different slopes are not necessarily accompanied by big changes in model fit and prediction accuracy. As such, very large samples may be needed to differentiate these models from each other.

We hope that the different criteria that can be obtained via the app can be used to further the understanding of linear regression with noisy predictors so that researchers can (1) better align research objectives with their modeling choices; (2) better interpret regression models which include product-like interactions; and (3) better assess the risks of modeling an interaction effect, but also the risk of not modeling one.

Correction Procedures

As we have established throughout the manuscript, empirically found effect estimates can be fragile proxies of true population effects. If possible, one could either use measurement error correction methods such as method-of-moments corrections (Busemeyer & Jones, 1983; Fuller, 1995), simulation extrapolation (Cook & Stefanski, 1994), regression calibration (Carroll & Stefanski, 1990), structural equation modeling (Klein & Moosbrugger, 2000; Marsh et al., 2004; Sánchez et al., 2005), or structural after measurement approaches (Rosseel et al., 2025; Vieira et al., 2026) to obtain more reliable approximations of the true population effects. See also Innes et al. (2021) and Nab (2023) for an overview of the different methods. However, if true error structures are not correctly identified in such methods, the correction itself can lead to more bias in the parameters (Rimpler et al., 2026b; Schmidt & Hunter, 1996; Van Smeden et al., 2020; Welsh et al., 2015) or increased uncertainty in estimated parameters (Ferron et al., 2002).

With an increase in the prediction error, the chances of different biases stemming from other omitted variables (Clarke, 2005) or failure to correctly identify non-linear effects (Belzak & Bauer, 2019; Rimpler et al., 2026a; Simonsohn, 2024) increase.

Limitations

Because we try to provide users with a simple possibility to compare the two competing models, we need to make multiple assumptions for the computations. These assumptions mimic idealized situations, which are not always and maybe even never true.

For one, we assume that the additional unexplained variance in the outcome (everything that is not captured by "True R² ") is independent of all predictors. If this assumption is not met, parameter estimates can flip from positive to negative or vice versa. Clarke (2005) showed how omitted, related variables can bias the estimates in both directions. In general it holds that the larger the additional unexplained variance, the more likely that regression estimates flip sign.

Similarly, we assume classical measurement error in the predictors. Given common measurement practices described in Elson et al. (2023) and Flake and Fried (2020) and little overlap for heavily investigated constructs such as depression (Fried, 2017) this seems unlikely to be typically true in practice. Van Smeden et al. (2020) described how different types of measurement error can have unexpected results beyond simple heuristics.

We also assume that there is indeed a true product-like interaction. This means that the conclusions based on the output produced by the app do not hold for other conditional effects as described in Rimpler et al. (2026a) or other non-linear effects that are easily mistaken for interaction effects (Belzak & Bauer, 2019; Ganzach, 1998; Rimpler et al., 2026a; Simonsohn, 2024).

Additionally, we refer to a product-like interaction between continuous predictors. The current findings do not apply in the same way to product-like interactions between a categorical and continuous predictor or two categorical predictors.

Beyond Regression

In the app we enable users to explore the impact of a product-like interaction at the population level, when resulting data is modeled with noisy predictors. We have learned that in general the regression surface flattens due to the measurement error in the predictors. However, we have also learned that properties of one predictor carry over to the other predictor-even under extreme conditions. We gave an example where the association of a predictor with the outcome became larger than the original association, because the other predictor was almost pure noise. This is a good reminder that the results of a regression are of descriptive nature, and that under noisy conditions associations can also appear stronger, due to artifacts in the data-generating process and measurement instruments. This is not only the case for linear regression. The foundations on which these findings are built relate to simpler but also more complex methods. This means that the problem cannot simply be avoided by going for simpler methods (e.g., zero-order correlations) or more complex methods. For example, Henry and Ye (2024) found classical results relating to measurement error and omitted variable bias from multivariate regression in network analyses. This suggests that noisy variables with large means that interact in a product-like fashion with other variables may increase the magnitude of unaffected edges in network analyses. Similarly, multilevel models may be affected (Butland et al., 2019) even though the exact mechanism and degree may differ (Huang, 2018). This is because regression is underlying many statistical techniques, and its problems can be found everywhere.

We believe that our Shiny app can help users understand how different bias sources can get intertwined, and we are confident that it provides a valuable tool for understanding how various aspects of regression relate to each other. We also believe that the app helps users recognize that linear models, which are simplifications for pragmatic reasons, do not need to have the same properties as a linear model which is based on a truly linear data generating process.
