Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. Some differences come from the ground truth: it lacks p-values with a correction name (“p Bonf = .061”), and counts partial eta squared written with its subscript after it (“η 2 p = .05”), “p = .05”, and some table notes and figure legends (“† p < .10, *p < .05”), which this module leaves out, as non-significant p-values. The Effect Size module checks if effect sizes are correctly reported in t-tests and F-tests.

The Effect Size check searches for regular expressions that match typical ways in which effect sizes are reported. It subsequently checks different ways in which Cohen’s d, g, ηp2, and ωp2 can be computed against the reported value. If effects are missing, or might be incorrect, you the module provides a warning. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

Abstract

This review synthesizes research on teacher achievement goals, highlighting teachers as a theoretically informative population within achievement goal theory. Unlike students, teachers occupy a dual role as both architects of learning and achievement environments for students and learners and achievers themselves. We review the historical development and taxonomy of the teacher goal concept, including teaching-specific refinements such as relational goals and the distinction between task and learning goals. The analysis extends to the antecedents, consequences, and temporal dynamics of these goals, with particular attention to professional learning, well-being, and instructional behavior. Drawing on research in schools and higher education, we examine the processes through which teachers' personal achievement goals may become relevant for student motivation and learning through their student-oriented goals, instructional behavior, and classroom goal structures. Finally, we discuss how considering the reasons underlying goal pursuit can advance research on teachers' professional learning. This goal-complex perspective connects teachers' aims with the broader meaning of their pursuit and provides a basis for investigating how goals and reasons relate to learning behavior and competence development.

1. Introduction

Why does one teacher welcome a difficult student question as an opportunity to learn, whereas another experiences it as a threat to professional competence? Why does one instructor use student feedback to revise a course, whereas another dismisses it, avoids it, or experiences it as a personal attack? Why do some teachers invest substantial effort in improving instruction, while others seek primarily to get through the lesson with little effort? Such differences are not merely differences in professional competence, knowledge, or beliefs, but may also reflect motivational differences in what teachers strive for in teaching contexts. Moreover, in the school and classroom context, teachers hold a pivotal and structurally distinctive position compared with students. They face not just a wide range of competence demands; they also create the conditions under which others experience competence, failure, and evaluation. Through their instructional behavior-in particular their tasks, feedback, assessments, directed classroom discussions, responses to errors, and comparison cues-teachers shape the achievement environments for their students.

Achievement goals for teaching are therefore highly relevant for understanding teacher behavior and teacher development, and teachers' contributions to educational success; beyond this, teacher goal research also challenges and enriches motivation theory itself. Teachers pursue goals across multiple, partly competing domains and under conditions of visibility, uncertainty, and conflicting demands. Their goals can manifest themselves in instructional behavior, classroom climates, and motivational messages for students, and thus become relevant for others' motivation. Here, teachers occupy a dual role as both architects of learning and achievement environments for students and learners and achievers themselves. Research on teacher populations thus contributes to clarifying how achievement goals are differentiated, contextualized, stabilized, and translated into social environments.

In this review, we examine research on teacher achievement goals with this dual focus. We first situate the field within broader achievement goal research and other teacher motivation research, then synthesize findings on the structure, antecedents, consequences, and dynamics of teachers' achievement goals. We identify relevant research gaps, particularly regarding the processes connecting goals with instructional behavior and professional learning, and close by discussing how a goal-complex perspective can help explain the combinations of aims and reasons that teachers bring to professional learning and their relevance for the development of professional competence.

2. Historical and Conceptual Foundations

>Historical and Conceptual Foundations

2.1 Origins of achievement goal research in general

Achievement goal theorizing emerged from attempts to explain why students respond differently to challenge, failure, and competence evaluation. Early formulations by Ames, Dweck, Maehr, and Nicholls distinguished between mastery or task-involving orientations, in which success is defined through learning, improvement, effort, and task mastery, and performance or ego-involving orientations, in which success is defined through demonstrating competence or superiority (Ames, 1992; Ames & Archer, 1988; Dweck, 1986; Maehr, 1984; Nicholls, 1984 Nicholls, , 1989)). These motivational orientations encompassed beliefs, interpretations of success and failure, and characteristic responses to achievement situations, and were thus broader than narrowly defined goals. As this general history has already been reviewed extensively elsewhere (Elliot, 2005; Elliot & Hulleman, 2017), we focus on key conceptual developments relevant for understanding teacher achievement goals. Based on the historical mastery-performance dichotomy, subsequent developments differentiated approach and avoidance valence, first within performance goals and later also for mastery goals, distinguishing striving toward positively valenced states (e.g., demonstrating competence, mastering tasks) from striving to avoid negatively valenced states (e.g., demonstrating incompetence, failing to master tasks; Elliot & Harackiewicz, 1996; Elliot & McGregor, 2001; Middleton & Midgley, 1997). Later, theorists refined competence standards further by distinguishing task-, self-, and other-based standards alongside their approach and avoidance valence (Elliot et al., 2011). At the same time, conceptual and meta-analytic reviews showed that achievement goal research is not just highly generative but also conceptually heterogeneous: Similarly labeled goals often differ depending on contexts, theoretical foci, competence standards, valence, and whether measures capture goals narrowly or broader motivational orientations (Hulleman et al., 2010; Senko et al., 2011; Urdan & Kaplan, 2020).

A central conceptual development in contemporary achievement goal theory was the move from broad goalorientation constructs toward a sharper understanding of achievement goals as competence-related aims, that is, what individuals strive for in competence-relevant situations (Elliot & Hulleman, 2017). Such a definition is useful because it increases conceptual clarity and helps pre-vent the conflation of goals with beliefs, emotions, reasons, and behavioral tendencies, while the standards used to evaluate competence help specify the aim itself. For teacher research, this distinction is particularly important because teachers' achievement goals can easily be confused with instructional objectives, student-oriented aims, professional values and beliefs, or teaching behavior and standards for evaluating student behavior and achievement. Throughout this review, we retain the aims-based definition, while recognizing that enduring preferences for particular goals-their goal orientations-can constitute relatively stable, personality-related characteristics.

At the same time, the aims-based definition of achievement goals does not cover the full breadth of historically earlier views on achievement goals. Earlier traditions, especially Maehr's and Nicholls' work, treated orientations more broadly as meaning systems that organize how individuals interpret achievement situations, define success, and regulate behavior (Maehr, 1984; Nicholls, 1989). Considering the reasons underlying an aim allows us to recover part of this broader meaning while preserving the distinction between what individuals strive for and why they do so. Our work with Korn and Elliot on the distinction between standpoints and standards-and how they can also converge in measures and experiences of learners, as well as in classic achievement goal theorizing-likewise draws attention to differences in what individuals seek to accomplish and how competence is evaluated (Korn et al., 2019). Moreover, and relevant for teacher research, Nicholls included work-avoidance goals, defined as striving to get by with little effort, which were later incorporated into teacher goal models. We therefore use goals for teaching as a broader label encompassing competence-related achievement goals as well as work-avoidance and relational goals, acknowledging that teaching also involves effort regulation and relationships with students.

The goal-complex approach offers a way to connect aims with the reasons underlying their pursuit (Liem & Senko, 2022; Sommet & Elliot, 2017). Two teachers may both aim to improve their instructional explanations, but one may do so out of interest in understanding students' learning and another because an impending evaluation creates pressure to improve. Although their stated aim is similar, the experience and regulation of its pursuit may differ depending on these reasons; the combination of a goal and a reason for pursuing it is what is meant by a goal complex. Examining such combinations allows us to address how and why an aim becomes motivationally relevant, an issue to which we return when considering professional learning and future research.

2.2 Development of Research on Teacher Achievement Goals

For a long time, motivation research in educational psychology focused primarily on students, while teachers' own motivation for teaching received much less attention. An exception was teacher self-efficacy research, rooted in social-cognitive theory, which had already established teachers' perceived capability to support instruction, manage classrooms, and motivate students as a central motivational resource (Bandura, 1997; Tschannen-Moran & Woolfolk Hoy, 2001; Zee & Koomen, 2016). Since the 2000s, teacher motivation research has broadened considerably, with expectancy-value theory used to inform work on career-choice motivations in the FIT-Choice tradition (Watt & Richardson, 2007), self-determination theory guiding research on autonomous motivation for teaching (Roth et al., 2007), and, in particular, achievement goal theory being extended to teachers' goals for teaching (Butler, 2007). The decisive conceptual step in the achievement goal tradition was to construe teaching itself as a competence-relevant achievement context for teachers, rather than merely as the setting in which students pursue achievement goals and acquire competences. Teachers continuously need to develop their own professional competence (Opfer & Pedder, 2011), and recent reviews place research on their goals within the broader challenge of explaining how teacher motivation becomes relevant for teaching and student outcomes (Lauermann & Butler, 2021; Lazarides et al., 2025).

Butler pioneered this extension. In her seminal work, she proposed that schools are achievement arenas for teachers, who strive to succeed but differ in how they define success (Butler, 2007). She distinguished mastery/learning, ability-approach, ability-avoidance, and work-avoidance goals, and her Goal Orientations for Teaching measure asked teachers "I feel successful when…", modeled after Nicholls' student measure. Empirical findings supported this four-factor structure over alternatives that collapsed ability-approach and abilityavoidance goals or combined ability-avoidance and workavoidance goals (Butler, 2007; Retelsdorf et al., 2010). Butler's early studies linked teacher goals to attitudes toward help seeking, help avoidance, instructional behavior, student help seeking, and student cheating (Butler, 2007; Butler & Shibaz, 2008; Dickhäuser et al., 2007). Her later introduction of relational goals became another important teaching-specific extension: Because teaching is inherently interpersonal and caring, striving to establish and sustain supportive relationships with students constitutes a distinct and meaningful goal for teaching (Butler, 2012; Noddings, 1992).

Parallel and subsequent work broadened teacher achievement goal research across countries, educational levels, and measurement traditions. Early work inspired by Butler's conceptualization included studies with Greek teachers by Papaioannou and Christodoulidis (2007), work by Retelsdorf et al. (2010) linking teachers' goals to instructional behavior, teaching interest, and burnout, and our own studies that refined the assessment of school teachers' goals through more direct goal-importance stems and different addressees and professional domains (Nitsche et al., 2011 (Nitsche et al., , 2013)). Two more studies conducted in our own lab further demonstrated the usefulness of achievement goal theory in explaining the associations between teacher motivation, instructional behavior, and students' motivation, as well as the professional development of teacher trainees (Dresel et al., 2013; Fasching et al., 2010). International work also expanded the field, including research in Korea on teachers' goals and their perceived school goal structures (Cho & Shim, 2013), in China on tertiary teachers' goals and teaching approaches (Han et al., 2015), in Australia on relations between teachers' goals and their self-reported classroom behavior (George & Richardson, 2019), and in Canada on longitudinal relations among teacher goals, classroom goal structures, and teaching-related emotions (Wang et al., 2017).

In our own later work, we extended this line of research systematically to teachers (lecturers) in higher education (Daumiller et al., 2019; Daumiller et al., 2016). The higher-education focus proved theoretically valuable not only because it broadens the range of teaching settings and professional demands under investigation but also because faculty work is distributed across multiple achievement domains, especially teaching and research. This makes higher education a particularly informative context for examining properties of achievement goals such as domain specificity and temporal stability, as well as their interplay across professional roles. Here, we draw on comparisons with the research domain where they help clarify the meaning and functioning of goals for teaching, while maintaining our focus on teaching and professional learning.

Teacher achievement goal research has evolved into a productive field, with the systematic review by Han and Gao (2023) documenting the range of goal models and outcomes investigated over 15 years of empirical research. The growing evidence base has also motivated our own meta-analytic work, initially presented at the 2021 EARLI conference (Butler et al., 2021). The published studies and reviews considered here suggest that learning and relational goals frequently show favorable associations, work-avoidance and performance-avoidance goals often show unfavorable associations, and findings for performance-approach goals are more mixed. However, the considerable differences in goal definitions, populations, and outcomes make it necessary to examine more closely which goals matter for which outcomes, in which contexts, and through which processes.

3. What Goals Do Teachers Pursue? A Taxonomy

Teacher achievement goal research was never a simple transfer of student goal classes to the teacher population. While teaching is undoubtedly a competence-relevant activity, it is also inherently relational, public in the classroom, and directed toward students' learning. Consequently, the goal classes studied in teachers mirror general achievement goal distinctions but are shaped by teachingspecific refinements, supported not only by scale-development work but also by qualitative interview studies that we conducted with teachers and instructors (Butler, 2007; Daumiller, Butler, & Dresel, 2025; Daumiller et al., 2019).

As we have outlined before, competence-related achievement goals are part of a broader set of goals that become relevant in teaching situations. Note that competence itself is a multifaceted construct: Teachers' professional knowledge includes content knowledge, pedagogical content knowledge, and pedagogical knowledge (Baumert & Kunter, 2006), on which they draw to perceive relevant classroom events, interpret them, and decide how to respond. These situation-specific skills connect dispositions with performance (Blömeke et al., 2015), but the competence domains themselves should be distinguished from the goals teachers pursue in relation to them. For example, a teacher may seek to develop diagnostic competence, demonstrate it to others, or avoid appearing deficient in it, while learning goals may concern several different competence domains.

In teaching contexts, where competence is simultaneously private during preparation and public during instruction, and where evaluation comes from multiple sources such as students, peers, and administrators, considering mastery and performance goals at a more finegrained level can help clarify their functioning (Daumiller et al., 2019). Accordingly, we proposed the Hexagon Model, which presents a hierarchical taxonomy of theoretically distinguishable and relevant goals teachers pursue and integrates competence-related aims with workavoidance and relational strivings (Figure 1). The model is based on a combination of the valence dimension (approach and avoidance) with the different definitional components of mastery goals (task goals, learning goals) and performance goals (appearance goals, normative goals), while considering relational goals and work-avoidance goals as further relevant types. These distinctions provide a framework for examining which goals are psychologically meaningful for teachers and help explain their experiences and behavior (while also extending back to student research as seen in our recent validation of the Hexagonal Achievement Goal Scale for Students that supported differentiated goals among lower secondary school students; Beck & Daumiller, 2025). The model combines approach and avoidance valence with task and learning goals and appearance and normative goals, while considering relational and work-avoidance goals as further relevant types of goals for teaching.

3.1 Mastery Goals

Mastery-related teacher goals concern strivings to develop or realize competence in teaching. In research on teachers, we distinguish between learning goals and task goals: Learning goals refer to teachers' aims to improve their own teaching competence, expand their instructional repertoire, and learn from professional challenges (Butler, 2007; Daumiller et al., 2019; Nitsche et al., 2011), whereas task goals concern the correct accomplishment of teaching tasks, such as explaining content accurately or conducting a lesson successfully (Daumiller et al., 2019; Mascret et al., 2017).

Although both are mastery-related, they imply different regulatory emphases. A teacher may want a lesson to run smoothly (task goal) without necessarily wanting to learn from it (learning goal); conversely, an instructional failure may be valuable if it becomes an occasion for professional learning. For instance, a teacher confronted with unexpected student misconceptions may use an established explanation to accomplish the current teaching task or use the difficulty as an occasion to develop a new explanatory approach and improve their own competence. Both goals can involve careful diagnosis, but they differ in the desired end state. Thus, learning goals are not simply equivalent to self-based goals in the restrictive sense of the 3 × 2 model, in which one's past performance or potential serves as the competence standard (Elliot et al., 2011). Although learning goals can involve such comparisons, they refer more broadly to professional growth and competence development, which need not explicitly be evaluated against past or potential performance (Daumiller et al., 2019).

This distinction seems particularly fertile in teacher research because, whereas students' primary task is learning, teachers' primary task is teaching; accomplishing the teaching task and developing one's own competence therefore need not be the same aim. In our questionnaire study of 1,066 German university instructors, task goals could be distinguished from learning and performance goals, with the differentiated goal structure holding across instructor status groups (Daumiller et al., 2019). Regarding their functional relevance, learning goals have been linked to favorable attitudes toward help seeking and professional development (Butler, 2007; Nitsche et al., 2013), whereas task-approach goals were particularly closely associated with subjective well-being in an international questionnaire study of 1,335 faculty members (Rinas et al., 2022). These findings support attending to both types of mastery goals, while their relative importance for instructional behavior remains a question to be addressed for specific outcomes.

Some work has also considered avoidance variants of mastery-related goals, such as striving to avoid failing at a teaching task or avoiding a lack of professional growth. These aims were detectable in our semi-structured interviews with 24 German school teachers, although learningapproach and task-approach goals were mentioned most frequently and avoidance variants occurred particularly following targeted prompts (Daumiller, Butler, & Dresel, 2025). The interviews thus support the occurrence and subjective meaning of these aims, while questionnaire findings additionally support distinguishing learningavoidance from learning-approach goals (Daumiller et al., 2019). Empirical evidence on their prevalence and relevance for teachers' cognition and behavior remains limited and varies across the outcomes considered, with some findings pointing to favorable links with learning-related processes (Hein et al., 2020; Hein et al., 2021). Masteryavoidance goals may be especially relevant when the motivational task shifts from acquiring competence toward maintaining expertise and avoiding professional stagnation (Senko & Freund, 2015), although this proposed role of career stage still requires direct investigation in teacher populations.

3.2 Performance Goals

Performance goals in teaching involve strivings to demonstrate competence or avoid demonstrating incompetence. A relevant theoretical refinement has been the distinction between appearance goals and normative goals, paralleling a similar distinction in research on student goals (Grant & Dweck, 2003; Hulleman et al., 2010). Appearance goals involve striving to appear competent or avoid appearing incompetent to students, colleagues, parents, supervisors, or evaluators, with empirical evidence indicating that these goals can be pursued to different degrees depending on the addressee (Nitsche et al., 2011).

Normative goals, in turn, involve striving to be better than other teachers or avoid doing worse than them (Daumiller et al., 2019).

These two types of performance goals should be distinguished because teaching is often highly visible but seldom explicitly competitive. Appearance and normative goals may differ not only in their associations with outcomes but also in the psychological processes through which they become relevant, depending on the surrounding context. For example, an appearance goal may make students' reactions or an evaluator's judgment particularly salient, whereas a normative goal may direct attention toward comparisons with colleagues. Examining such processes would help explain why the two goal types function differently, while also allowing us to assess whether their distinction reveals differences in regulation that would be concealed by a broader performance-goal category.

In student research, normative and appearance components help explain why performance-approach goals have shown different associations with achievement (Hulleman et al., 2010). Research on lecturers and faculty in higher education who are also involved in research alongside teaching adds a question of domain specificity. In our study of university instructors, we distinguished appearance from normative goals and examined their relations with positive affect, attitudes toward help, and selfreported teaching quality (Daumiller et al., 2019). A separate study of 439 German and Austrian higher education teachers linked appearance-approach goals with pride and appearance-avoidance goals with anxiety and shame (Rinas et al., 2020). The research domain, which is characterized by competition for resources and reputation, provides a different evaluative context; indeed, our fourwave study of 681 faculty members over two years revealed domain-specific relations between goals, burnout/engagement, and performance (Daumiller & Dresel, 2023). The relevance of performance goals therefore needs to be considered in relation to both the demands of the professional domain and the outcome under investigation.

Existing work suggests that performance-approach goals show mixed associations, accompanying positive experiences in some teaching contexts without consistently predicting better instruction. Performance-avoidance goals, particularly appearance-avoidance goals, have more often been linked to unfavorable experiences such as perceiving help as threatening and experiencing negative emotions (Butler, 2007; Dickhäuser et al., 2007; Rinas et al., 2020). The findings on help concern attitudes toward seeking it, however, leaving open how these evaluations translate into actual help-seeking behavior. More generally, differences in the goal facets and criteria studied need to be considered when interpreting the mixed findings, as a goal that is relevant for teachers' emotional experiences may have quite different associations with how they teach or learn.

3.3 Work-Avoidance Goals

Work-avoidance goals in teachers involve their striving to minimize effort in teaching or professional learning (Butler, 2007; Nicholls, 1989; Nitsche et al., 2013). Work avoidance should not be simplistically equated with laziness: In the teaching profession, characterized by high demands and limited resources, a wish to reduce effort may also reflect overload or attempts to conserve resources. Teachers must set personal priorities to manage the wide range of tasks and responsibilities at work, making some degree of effort regulation necessary. Such prioritization and recovery should nevertheless be distinguished from a work-avoidance goal, which concerns doing the focal activity with as little effort as possible.

Empirical work revealed the general pattern that work-avoidance goals are linked to less favorable teacher experiences and learning behavior (Nitsche et al., 2013; Retelsdorf et al., 2010). For example, in our study of 48 academics attending professional training courses, workavoidance goals assessed before the courses were negatively related to learning engagement assessed during them, with engagement statistically mediating associations with subsequent self-reported learning gains (Daumiller, Rinas, et al., 2021). In another study, involving 251 university instructors, 392 courses, and 9,241 student evaluations, work-avoidance goals were negatively associated with students' ratings of their learning (Daumiller et al., 2016). These associations suggest that a persistent wish to minimize effort is relevant for professional development and instructional quality, while the reasons for adopting such goals and the consequences of changing them require further investigation.

3.4 Relational Goals

Relational goals involve teachers' strivings to build close, supportive, respectful, caring, or positive relationships with students. This goal class was first introduced by Butler (2012) and is relevant because teaching is inherently relational. A relational goal concerns the relationship a teacher seeks to establish, which should be distinguished from a learning goal directed at improving the professional competence needed to do so. For example, a teacher may strive to build a trusting relationship with a particular student or, alternatively, to become more skilled at establishing such relationships across the students they teach.

Particularly for teachers who are responsible for the learning of others, there is a strong, job-inherent obligation to care about individual students and the relationships shared with them (Butler, 2012; Noddings, 1992). A series of questionnaire studies analyzing the factorial structure of teachers' goals, including samples of Israeli school teachers, German university instructors, and Chinese ter-tiary teachers, revealed that relational strivings form distinguishable factors (Butler, 2012; Daumiller et al., 2019; Han et al., 2015). These findings support the separability of relational goals across several educational and national settings, although measurement equivalence across cultures remains to be established. Butler's studies also incorporated student reports, connecting teachers' relational goals with students' experiences of caring and supportive instruction, and thereby documenting their relevance for the interpersonal conditions under which students learn.

4. Teachers as a Special Population for Achievement Goal Research

Throughout this review, we have already highlighted several particularities that distinguish teachers from students in achievement goal research-their dual role as learners and achievers, their role in designing learning and achievement contexts for others, and their navigation of multiple evaluative audiences. Here, we want to emphasize two additional points that underscore why teachers represent a theoretically informative population for achievement goal science and, conversely, why analyzing teacher goals is a valuable endeavor within broader teacher motivation research to explain and improve teacher behavior in the classroom and beyond (Lazarides et al., 2025).

4.1 Social Consequences and the Transmission Mechanism

Teachers' goals carry social consequences beyond the individual, as their striving may become relevant for others through instructional behavior, classroom climate, and ultimately student motivation and learning (Daumiller, Fasching, et al., 2022). A teacher seeking to avoid appearing incompetent, for example, may respond to a difficult student question in ways that shape how students experience asking questions in that classroom. Teachers construct motivational environments for others, making their goal striving a possible mechanism of social influence through which institutional achievement norms become relevant for classroom life (Dickhäuser et al., 2021). Although supervisors and coaches also shape others' achievement environments, teachers' continuing responsibility for students' learning makes this social function especially salient in the teaching profession.

Because of this social function, an important distinction is whether teachers' goals concern their own competence or what they aspire for their students to attain. Personal goals concern the teacher's own competence (e.g., "I aspire to improve my teaching competence"), whereas student-oriented goals concern their students' competence (e.g., "I aspire for my students to improve their competence"). The latter are goals held by teachers about their students, and are therefore conceptually distinct from the goals students themselves pursue. This distinction is essential for modeling how teachers' personal achievement goals translate into classroom goal structures and student experiences (Daumiller, Fasching, et al., 2022), with student-oriented goals specifying more closely what teachers seek to achieve for their students through instruction.

Consequently, an account of teacher achievement goals must consider whether they are focused on teachers themselves or their students, as the relations with instructional behavior and student outcomes may differ depending on this focus. We proposed a model according to which teachers' personal goals relate to student-oriented goals, which in turn more proximally shape instructional behavior and perceived classroom goal structures (Daumiller, Fasching, et al., 2022). We examined this model with 84 secondary school teachers and their 1,447 students, combining teachers' reports of personal achievement goals and student-oriented goals with students' reports of specific masteryand performance-oriented instructional behaviors and classroom goal structures. Two-level models supported indirect associations between teachers' personal goals and students' perceptions through student-oriented goals and instructional behavior. Although the nonexperimental design leaves their causal ordering open, these findings help specify intervening processes through which teachers' goals may become relevant for instruction, as we discuss in more detail in Section 5.2.

4.2 Teachers as Diagnosers of Student Motivation

Next, teachers also act as diagnosers of student motivation, continually assessing student understanding, motivation, emotion, engagement, help-seeking needs, and social dynamics to adapt their instruction. Accurate diagnosis is cognitively demanding and prone to bias, as illustrated by two vignette experiments in which Beck et al. (2025) examined how preservice and in-service teachers used information to judge student motivation. In the first experiment (N = 205), teachers judged eight fictitious students sequentially from information about mastery-approach and work-avoidance goals, either alone or alongside gender and academic achievement. They used the goal cues strongly even when the additional information was present, whereas in the second experiment (N = 213), in which the vignettes were presented together and judgments had to be made from memory without access to them, teachers relied more on gender and achievement information. The findings suggest that the conditions under which teachers make motivational judgments affect which cues they use, raising the further question of whether their own goals also contribute to how they perceive and interpret students' motivation.

Connections between teacher goals and the perception of classroom events can be studied through professional vision, which involves noticing relevant events and interpreting them using professional knowledge. A teacher noticing that a student stops participating, for example, may consider whether this reflects confusion, boredom, or concern about making an error. In an eye-tracking study with 51 preservice teachers, we examined how student-oriented goals were associated with noticing student interactions in an authentic video of an eleventh-grade mathematics lesson (Daumiller, Böheim, et al., 2025). After viewing the beginning of the video, participants described their goals for individual students and the class in an interview, and their eye movements were recorded during the remainder of the approximately three-minute video. Goals for individual students were associated with more and longer fixations on those students, whereas goals for the class were associated with shorter fixations on individual students, suggesting that the targets of teachers' goals are related to the distribution of their visual attention.

In a related study, 51 preservice teachers watched three classroom videos, described student-oriented goals beforehand, and explained noticed interactions afterward (Böheim et al., 2026). Longer fixation duration was associated with more elaborated reasoning, while student-oriented mastery goals were associated with deeper interpretations and classroom-management goals with more superficial interpretations. Taken together, these findings indicate that student-oriented goals are relevant to both noticing and interpreting classroom events. How teachers' personal achievement goals are implicated in these processes, and whether they contribute to more or less accurate judgments of student motivation, remains to be investigated through designs that directly connect the goals teachers pursue with the diagnostic judgments they make.

5. Consequences, Dynamics, and Transmission to Student Outcomes

While we have already outlined the nomological net of specific teacher goal classes and relevant contextual conditions shaping their adoption, this section examines the processes, dynamics, and transmission pathways through which goals may become relevant for professional experiences. Therein, we focus particularly on temporal dynamics regarding professional learning and well-being, and on the process by which teacher goals relate to instructional behavior and, ultimately, student motivation and learning.

5.1 Professional Learning and Well-Being: Temporal Dynamics

Teacher achievement goals are assumed to initiate regulatory processes that shape how teachers perceive challenges, engage in professional learning, and experience their work. Theoretically, goals act as lenses for evaluating achievement situations and organizing cognitive, affective, and behavioral responses (Elliot & Hulleman, 2017). Consistent with achievement goal research in student populations, learning goals are associated with favorable attitudes toward seeking help and participating in professional development (Butler, 2007; Nitsche et al., 2013), whereas performance-avoidance goals may make help threatening because it can expose difficulties and work-avoidance goals may make it seem unnecessarily effortful (Dickhäuser et al., 2007). These different evaluations of the same opportunity are one way in which teachers' goals may become relevant for their professional learning.

In our longitudinal work, we examined whether these goals relate to teachers' investment in learning, following 705 university scholars across two semesters (Hein et al., 2019). Learning goals predicted self-reported professional learning gains in teaching and research, with learning time mediating this association in the teaching domain. A more intensive study of 107 university instructors over four weeks examined their learning goals, learning time, and learning gains (Hein et al., 2020). Here, the expected indirect association through learning time was not supported for learning-approach goals but was supported for learning-avoidance goals, with autonomy relevant to the pattern. Thus, the relations between learning goals and perceived learning gains depend partly on the goal facet and the conditions under which teachers can invest time in learning.

Professional training provides a more bounded context for investigating these processes. In the study of 48 academics described above, goals were assessed before the training courses, engagement was assessed three times during them, and perceived learning gains were assessed afterward (Daumiller, Rinas, et al., 2021). Learning-approach goals were positively associated with engagement and work-avoidance goals negatively associated with it, with engagement in turn statistically mediating associations with self-reported gains. This temporal sequence is consistent with the assumption that goals are relevant through the quality of engagement during a learning opportunity, although the reliance on perceived learning gains leaves open how these processes relate to independently assessed competence development.

Student feedback offers another opportunity for professional learning. In a longitudinal study of 407 university instructors, 152 voluntarily used an online platform to evaluate their teaching (Hein et al., 2021). Learningavoidance and appearance-approach goals positively predicted voluntary evaluation use in multivariate analyses, whereas appearance-avoidance goals negatively predicted it and learning-approach goals were unrelated to this initial step. Among instructors who used evaluations, both learning-approach and learning-avoidance goals were positively associated with intentions to act on the results, but only learning-approach goals predicted the number of proposed teaching improvements. Neither learning-goal facet predicted the recorded time spent processing the feedback. These differences suggest that the relevance of a goal can change over the course of a learning opportunity, depending on whether the teacher is deciding to seek feedback, engaging with the information received, or considering how to act on it.

The studies reviewed above illustrate why professional learning deserves attention as an outcome in its own right, with participation in a learning activity, engagement during that activity, and the development of competence requiring separate consideration. A teacher may already endorse learning goals but lack time or suitable opportunities to act on them, just as participation in professional development need not entail intensive engagement or durable learning. Research on professional development therefore benefits from specifying the learning activity and assessing its consequences beyond participants' immediate evaluations (Desimone, 2009; Opfer & Pedder, 2011). Emerging longitudinal research further suggests that these processes are embedded in teachers' wider experiences of work, including their well-being, which may itself contribute to subsequent goal pursuit.

Reciprocal relations between goals and well-being represent a dynamic that challenges simple assumptions about goals as the starting point of professional experiences. Analyzing data from a two-wave longitudinal study of 489 university instructors during the COVID-19 pandemic using cross-lagged panel models, we found that positive affect and job satisfaction predicted subsequent mastery-approach and performance-approach goals positively and work-avoidance goals negatively over approximately three months, controlling for earlier goal levels (Rinas et al., 2023). A reciprocal association also emerged between job satisfaction and performance-approach goals, suggesting that feeling well may help sustain approach strivings alongside the possibility that goal pursuit contributes to well-being. As prospective associations, these findings provide grounds for investigating reciprocal processes, while their causal operation within individual teachers remains to be established.

In our four-wave study over two years, latent change score models likewise indicated that goals, burnout/engagement, and performance were linked over time, with positive reciprocal relations between mastery goals and performance and an association between burnout and subsequent reductions in mastery goals (Daumiller & Dresel, 2023). These findings encourage attention to both directions of influence when explaining teachers' professional development: The goals they pursue may shape their experiences of learning and teaching, while experiences of success or strain may, in turn, change which goals they pursue.

Domain-specific dynamics and goal facets further complicate this picture. In faculty members, we found goals in research to be more strongly implicated in the development of overall burnout/engagement than goals in teaching (Daumiller & Dresel, 2023), which is relevant when interpreting broad measures of occupational wellbeing among people whose work extends beyond instruction. Within mastery goals, differences between task and learning components also emerged: In our international questionnaire study of 1,335 faculty members, task-approach goals were particularly favorably associated with subjective well-being, whereas work-avoidance goals showed unfavorable associations (Rinas et al., 2022). Accomplishing professional tasks and developing competence may therefore have different relationships with how teachers experience their work, warranting closer investigation of how well-being, learning, and performance are connected over time.

The conditions under which academics pursue their goals also deserve consideration. In our mixed-methods study of 483 researchers from Germany, Norway, and South Africa, higher perceived academic freedom was associated with stronger mastery goal pursuit and, more strongly, with autonomy need satisfaction, higher job satisfaction, and lower work strain (Daumiller et al., 2026).

Participants most commonly described funding limitations and organizational barriers as restrictions on their academic freedom. Although these cross-sectional findings concern research rather than teaching, they suggest that understanding academics' goal pursuit and well-being requires attention to their perceived scope for pursuing scholarly questions and communicating their findings. Whether comparable relationships hold for freedom in teaching and teachers' professional learning remains an important question.

5.2 Teaching Behavior and Transmission to Student Outcomes

A theoretically significant yet conceptually less understood consequence of teacher achievement goals is their transmission to students. Understanding this pathway is central to explaining how teacher motivation matters for educational success, as it involves several intervening processes connecting teachers' own strivings with what students experience and learn in their classrooms.

Teachers' personal goals may become relevant for students and their motivation and learning through student-oriented goals, teacher appraisals and emotions, instructional behavior, and student-perceived classroom goal structures (Daumiller, Fasching, et al., 2022; Lazarides et al., 2025). One theoretically reasonable way in which this may occur is illustrated by our study with 84 teachers and 1,447 students. Two-level modeling indicated that mastery-oriented instructional behaviors were predicted by teachers' student-oriented goals and, indirectly, by their personal mastery goals positively and performance-approach goals negatively. Performance-oriented instructional behaviors were negatively predicted by teachers' personal mastery goals, while classroom goal structures were closely related to instructional behavior and indirectly predicted by teachers' goals (Daumiller, Fasching, et al., 2022). These findings support distinguishing teachers' personal goals from what they want for their students and connecting both with the instructional behaviors and broader goal structures students perceive. Student motivation and learning are subsequent outcomes to be explained through these processes. Supporting teachers in translating personal learning goals into student-oriented goals and instructional behavior has the potential to strengthen this connection, although the effectiveness of such an intervention has yet to be empirically shown.

More recently, we examined personal goals, studentoriented goals, and self-efficacy alongside teaching behavior in a study of 70 mathematics teachers in Grades 7-9, who reported their motivation at baseline and completed 345 lesson diaries over five weeks (Daumiller, Gaspard, et al., 2025). Personal performance goals were positively related to student-oriented performance goals, while student-oriented mastery goals were associated with teachers' self-efficacy and, in turn, positively predicted mastery-based teaching behavior. These findings suggest that considering what teachers want for their students helps explain their instructional behavior beyond their personal goals alone.

Our standardized lesson diary study with 134 teachers and 670 diary entries further examined goals in relation to ten specific instructional behaviors (Daumiller, Fasching, et al., 2023). By asking teachers how they had taught particular lessons, we brought the assessment closer to concrete classroom activity than is possible with a single global judgment. Cognitive stimulation, individualization, structuring, and other instructional behaviors need not have identical motivational antecedents, making it necessary to specify which aspect of instruction is relevant to a given goal. Although the diary reports retain the limitations of teacher self-reports, they allow us to examine these connections at the level of the lessons in which the goals are pursued and to formulate more specific questions for studies incorporating students' perspectives or independent observations.

Of note, motivational climate is experienced and interpreted by teachers and students, whose perspectives provide complementary information about intended instruction and professional reasoning on the one hand and students' experiences of that instruction on the other. Empirical evidence stemming from the higher education context suggests that agreement between teacher self-reports and student evaluations can be strong for overarching teaching quality: In a study of 97 instructors, 889 teaching sessions, and 15,503 student ratings, we found a latent correlation of .74 between the two perspectives (Daumiller, Janke, Hein, et al., 2023). Whether similarly strong agreement occurs for particular motivational dimensions or classroom goal structures remains an open question. Future research should therefore compare intended, enacted, observed, and perceived instruction to clarify where teachers' and students' perspectives converge and where they diverge. For instance, a teacher may intend to support mastery while using grading practices that students experience as competitive, suggesting that discrepancies between intended and experienced instruction may help explain why personal goals do not always translate into the classroom climate teachers intend.

Multi-perspective studies incorporating not only teacher questionnaires but also student perceptions of classroom instruction and classroom climates have been part of teacher goal research from early on (Butler, 2012; Butler & Shibaz, 2008; Daumiller et al., 2016; Dresel et al., 2013). However, associations between teachers' goals and students' experiences are not equally robust across designs. In two studies on students' learning experiences, we found that instructors' general goals did not predict the outcomes in a semester-level design with 166 instructors and 2,106 students, whereas performance-approach and performance-avoidance goals were relevant mainly at the session level in an intensive study of 96 instructors, 828 sessions, and 16,009 student assessments (Daumiller, Janke, et al., 2021). The level and timing of assessment thus appear relevant to understanding when teacher goals are associated with instructional experiences.

The "black box" between teacher motivation and student experience remains only partially opened, requiring multilevel designs to account for students' nesting in classrooms and longitudinal designs to examine how the proposed processes unfold over time. Reciprocal dynamics also deserve attention, as illustrated by research connecting teacher motivation with student-reported teaching and students' enjoyment and achievement that explicitly examined both directions of influence (Lazarides & Schiefele, 2024). For achievement goals specifically, we still need to establish whether and how students' responses reinforce or alter teachers' goals. A positive response to a mastery-oriented lesson might encourage a teacher to sustain this emphasis, whereas resistance might prompt a change; testing such feedback processes would help explain how teachers' goals develop through their experiences with students.

Peer processes should be further considered in this transmission pathway (Daumiller & Hemi, 2025). In our longitudinal study of 4,189 students in 169 classes, classmates' achievement goals predicted changes in students' individual goals; classroom-level goal structures were no longer predictive when these peer goals were included (Bossert et al., 2025). Related social network research linked students' mastery-approach goals with greater classroom centrality, while performance-avoidance goals were associated with more outgoing but fewer incoming peer nominations (Hemi et al., 2025). While they did not test transmissions from teachers, the findings indicate that students' peer environments should be considered when examining how teacher-created goal structures become relevant for individual motivation.

Moreover, classrooms are not the only motivationally relevant context, as the wider school environment is shaped by leadership, feedback, and collegial norms. In a study of 532 teachers in 40 schools, positive perceptions of schools' feedback culture at the school level were associated with stronger teacher learning-goal orientations, while a collaborative climate was associated with weaker performance-goal orientations (Dickhäuser et al., 2021). Research on perceived school goal structures and teacher self-efficacy likewise connects the organizational context with teachers' goals (Cho & Shim, 2013), indicating the need to consider personal goal pursuit within the professional environment in which teachers learn and teach.

6. Malleability, Intervention, and Future Directions

The preceding sections have shown why teacher achievement goals matter for professional learning, well-being, and students' instructional experiences. This raises a practical question with which we have also grappled in our own research: Can these goals be changed, and if so, how? 6.1 Can Teacher Achievement Goals Be Changed? Achievement goal pursuit is assumed to respond to contextual conditions, including the goal structures emphasized in an achievement environment (Ames, 1992; Elliot & Thrash, 2001), which forms a theoretical basis for supporting adaptive goal pursuit through interventions and environmental modifications. However, the conceptual and empirical picture reveals considerable complexity regarding the stability and changeability of teacher goals over time. Achievement goals are influenced by factors bound to dispositional characteristics as well as aspects of the context or situation, such that stable differences between individuals coexist with variability in goal pursuit within individuals. Enduring preferences for particular goals are what the concept of goal orientations seeks to describe, while the goals that become salient in a particular lesson may vary with the situation.

Specifically, achievement goal pursuit can be partly stable and partly variable over time, and partly general and partly context-specific, yielding two distinct dimensions: temporal stability and context specificity (Daumiller, Janke, Rinas, et al., 2023; Praetorius et al., 2014). In empirical investigations, these two sources are often conflated, for example, when studying retest correlations over time without considering whether the teaching context has changed. Yet they represent different aspects of variability that can be disentangled through an appropriate repeated-measures design and multilevel modeling.

Figure 3. Conceptual framework for disentangling temporal variability and context specificity of goal pursuit (Daumiller, Janke, Rinas, et al., 2023). The model is illustrated with the example of teachers whose goal pursuit may differ over time and between contexts, such as the courses they teach.

Longitudinal research demonstrated that teachers' achievement goal orientations are considerably stable over time, while also showing within-person variability (Praetorius et al., 2014). To address the conflation of stability and specificity, we proposed a framework that crosses temporal stability (stable vs. variable) with context specificity (general vs. specific), resulting in four distinct components of goal pursuit (Figure 2). Utilizing an intensive longitudinal design with 108 higher education teachers, 213 courses, and 949 consecutive course sessions across five weeks, we tested this model to examine variation in goal pursuit across time and contexts (Daumiller, Janke, Rinas, et al., 2023). Multilevel analyses indicated that between half and two-thirds of variability in goal pursuit could be attributed to a stable-general component, reflecting differences between teachers that extended across the observed weeks and courses. Further variability was attributable to a variable-general component (different weeks for a teacher), a stable-specific component (different courses taught by a teacher), and a variable-specific component (remaining session variance, which also includes measurement error).

This fourfold structure clarifies that, besides betweenperson differences, variation in goal pursuit can be associated with temporally variable and context-overarching aspects, such as workload or mood in a given week; temporally stable and context-specific aspects, such as course format or familiarity with a course topic; and temporally variable and context-specific aspects, such as experiences in a particular session. The examples in Figure 2 illustrate possible sources of variation, whose causal relevance would need to be examined beyond the variance decomposition itself. Although these findings provide grounds for considering concrete teaching contexts when developing interventions, they leave open which components of goal pursuit can most readily be changed, a difficulty we also encountered in our own intervention research.

Given that goals are, in principle, changeable, we also tried to change them through an achievement goal theorybased intervention approach, evaluated in a quasi-experimental control-group design with three measurement occasions (Benning et al., 2019). Forty teachers participated in the intervention and 29 teachers in the comparison group, with student assessments collected from 1,172 students. Teachers participated across three workshop days focusing on knowledge about achievement goals and implementation in teaching. They also received feedback on their teaching twice and kept weekly goal diaries to support reflection on personal goal setting. Results showed high subjective learning gains and perceived usefulness, but no treatment effects were detected on teachers' achievement goals, student-rated instructional behavior, or student motivation at posttest or three-month followup. This null finding despite high participant satisfaction illustrates the gap between teachers' perceived value of goal-oriented professional development and demonstrable motivational or behavioral change.

Several explanations for this difficulty are conceivable. High initial endorsement of learning goals may limit the room for upward change, while an intervention's intensity, its fit with teachers' situations, and difficulties in translating reflection into everyday behavior may also contribute to the absence of detectable effects. These possibilities require direct examination, as the available evidence does not support a general ceiling-effect explanation for unsuccessful teacher interventions. In addition to addressing the goals teachers pursue, intervention efforts may therefore need to focus on the conditions that help teachers act on those goals, particularly on supporting the translation of existing learning goals into consistent learning and instructional behavior. Moreover, it is not sufficient to tell teachers to adopt mastery goals; interventions should also consider evaluation structures, feedback climates, autonomy support, competence support, collegial cultures, and professional norms (Ames, 1992; Dickhäuser et al., 2021). Schoollevel motivational climate is associated with teacher goals, with positive perceptions of schools' feedback culture corresponding with stronger learning-goal orientations and a collaborative climate corresponding with weaker performance-goal orientations (Dickhäuser et al., 2021). Institutions that reward impression management or defensive performance might limit the effectiveness of individual goal reflection, suggesting that efforts to support adaptive goal pursuit need to consider the practices, policies, and procedures of the respective school or university alongside support for individual teachers. For higher education, this includes examining whether perceived restrictions on academic freedom constrain opportunities to act on learning goals, alongside the individual support provided to instructors (Daumiller et al., 2026).

6.2 Intervention Principles and Support Strategies

Changing goal pursuit remains challenging, and combining direct work on goals with support for the conditions under which teachers pursue them raises further questions about how such interventions should be designed. Here, we want to highlight three additional points.

First, professional development should provide a setting in which teachers can discuss difficulties and use feedback for learning. Supporting autonomy, competence, and relatedness is consistent with self-determination theory (Ryan & Deci, 2000) and with our findings on the interplay between need satisfaction and achievement goals among 1,410 faculty members in Germany, India, and the United States (Daumiller, Janke, et al., 2022). Avoiding unnecessary social comparison and making uncertainty, mistakes, and help seeking discussable may support constructive goal reflection, although the effectiveness of these design principles requires intervention evidence. In our longitudinal study of student evaluations, the tested moderation effects involving threat from negative feedback were not statistically significant (Hein et al., 2021), leaving open whether and under which conditions reducing perceived threat would help teachers use feedback for professional learning.

Second, situated tools should be considered, as video analysis, student feedback, and lesson diaries enable teachers to examine goal pursuit in concrete teaching situations (Daumiller, Böheim, et al., 2025; Daumiller, Fasching, et al., 2023). Standardized lesson diaries, in particular, can allow teachers to track goal-behavior links across specific teaching episodes, connecting what they wanted to accomplish with how they taught a lesson. The temporal variability and context specificity findings reviewed above provide a rationale for examining goals at this level (Daumiller, Janke, Rinas, et al., 2023), while comparisons with interventions addressing general orientations would help establish the value of a situated approach. Prospectively, AI-supported reflection may offer further possibilities for working with such records, but its usefulness would need to be evaluated in terms of changes in teachers' learning and teaching.

Third, goal interventions should address not only what goals teachers pursue but why they pursue them. Mastery goals pursued out of curiosity and authentic interest may be experienced differently from similar aims pursued under pressure (Sommet & Elliot, 2017). In our qualitative study of 24 teachers, both "wanting to" and "needing to" reasons appeared across learning-and task-related mastery goals (Daumiller, Butler, & Dresel, 2025), with two raters coding the interviews with substantial agreement (κ = .74). Teachers sometimes described the same aim in terms of both reasons, suggesting that the meaning of goal pursuit cannot always be captured by assigning a single reason to a goal.

Moreover, "wanting to" and "needing to" should not simply be equated with autonomous and controlled motivation: A teacher may personally endorse the necessity of learning how to support a struggling student, while another may experience the same demand primarily as external pressure. The interviews provide insight into the occurrence and subjective meaning of such goal complexes, on which further research can build to investigate their consequences for professional learning. Interventions that help teachers identify and reflect on the reasons underlying their goal pursuit, without necessarily changing the goals themselves, may offer a promising approach; examining whether changes in reasons are associated with changes in concrete learning behavior and its outcomes would be a necessary next step.

6.3 Future Directions

As outlined above, despite substantial progress in understanding teacher achievement goals, significant gaps remain that warrant attention in future research and intervention development. Here, we would like to highlight five areas, partly touched upon before, in which we see particular promise.

First, much existing evidence relies on cross-sectional teacher self-report data, limiting causal inference and obscuring dynamic processes. Future work should build on experience sampling, standardized lesson diaries, videobased observation, and multi-informant designs to capture the situated nature of goal pursuit (Daumiller, Fasching, et al., 2023; Daumiller, Janke, et al., 2021; Daumiller, Janke, Rinas, et al., 2023). The timing and frequency of assessment need to match the process under investigation, as questions about goals during a particular teaching episode require a different time scale from questions about professional development across a school year. Dynamic structural equation models and latent change models can help examine temporal ordering and reciprocal associations between goals, experiences, and behavior when the measurement schedule permits the proposed processes to be observed.

Second, specific attention needs to be devoted to fundamental questions about transmission to students. Which specific instructional behaviors mediate the associations of which specific teacher goals with students' experiences? How do student-oriented goals develop from personal goals, and can this pathway be supported through intervention? How do students' motivational responses feed back to reshape teacher goals over time, and how do cultural and institutional contexts moderate these connections? Answering these questions requires process-sensitive, multilevel, longitudinal designs that connect teacher motivation with instructional behavior and student outcomes (Daumiller, Fasching, et al., 2022; Lazarides et al., 2025). Combining the assessment of student-oriented goals with repeated lesson-level data would allow researchers to investigate successive parts of the proposed transmission process and identify where personal goals are, or are not, reflected in students' experiences.

Third, research should continue to broaden the educational and cultural contexts represented. Work in higher education and international comparisons has already extended the field beyond school teachers in a single national setting (Daumiller et al., 2016; Daumiller, Janke, et al., 2022; Han & Gao, 2023), providing a basis for further work with early childhood educators, vocational teachers, teacher educators, and special education teachers. Different educational contexts present distinct achievement structures, evaluation systems, and professional demands that may shape goal pursuit in different ways. Examining both the comparability of measures and the associations of particular goals across these contexts is therefore necessary to clarify which distinctions generalize and which depend on the demands of a particular teaching role.

Fourth, achievement goals should be connected more closely with complementary motivational frameworks, including self-efficacy research, self-determination theory, expectancy-value theory, and emotion research. Need satisfaction, beliefs about competence, and goals may jointly shape teachers' experiences and behavior, as illustrated by our study of 785 university instructors in which implicit theories of intelligence were related to achievement goals, although the hypothesized indirect relations with teaching quality were small (Stockinger et al., 2021). Our international work likewise links need satisfaction with goal pursuit (Daumiller, Janke, et al., 2022). Investigating these interconnections while retaining the conceptual distinctions between the constructs can help explain why teachers who endorse similar aims differ in their engagement and can inform interventions that address the conditions under which goals are pursued. Related work with early-career researchers further distinguishes need satisfaction, which was more strongly associated with satisfaction with work tasks, from need frustration, which was more strongly associated with work strain (Janke et al., 2026). Integrating these processes with achievement goals could help explain how academics sustain professional learning under demanding working conditions.

Finally, the field should continue to examine how teachers combine, activate, coordinate, and revise multiple goals in the professional contexts in which they work. Multiple-goal approaches have a long tradition in achievement goal research, and our multi-study analysis of 3,681 teachers and university instructors yielded six meaningful and broadly generalizable goal profiles (Daumiller, Janke, Butler, et al., 2023). However, these profiles explained less variation in self-efficacy and work-related distress than the individual goals, suggesting that the value of studying configurations depends on what we seek to explain and how the configurations are represented.

For professional learning, we see particular promise in the goal-complex perspective introduced earlier, which connects teachers' aims with their reasons for pursuing them. Building on our finding that mastery aims can be linked with wanting to, needing to, or both (Daumiller, Butler, & Dresel, 2025), a central next question is how these combinations relate to what teachers actually do when learning. Do they seek feedback, analyze errors, try out alternative explanations, consult colleagues, or reflect on the consequences of an instructional decision, and do these activities result in changes in professional competence? Content knowledge, pedagogical content knowledge, pedagogical knowledge, and situation-specific skills provide different, complementary criteria for examining such changes (Baumert & Kunter, 2006; Blömeke et al., 2015).

This agenda complements research on the motivational processes involved in current teaching by focusing on the development of the teacher as a professional learner. Assessing aims and reasons in a defined learning context, documenting subsequent learning activities, and examining competence before and after that activity would help connect the subjective meaning of goal pursuit with its consequences for professional development. Combining expertise in teacher motivation, workplace learning, and competence assessment is particularly valuable for this purpose, as it allows us to examine how teachers' motivation is expressed in their learning activities and whether the resulting changes extend beyond their perceptions of having learned.

7. Conclusion

The research reviewed here demonstrates that teacher achievement goals are important for understanding professional learning, teacher well-being, classroom experiences, and students' educational outcomes. Teachers offer fertile ground for achievement goal research because they are simultaneously learners and achievers, diagnosers, and architects of students' achievement environments, with their own motivation becoming relevant for the conditions under which others learn. Research in schools and higher education has helped differentiate goals for teaching and clarify their social, contextual, and temporal dimensions, while also revealing the difficulties involved in tracing their consequences and supporting changes in goal pursuit.

We believe that further progress depends on examining how teachers' goals operate in the situations in which they teach and learn, including the student-oriented goals that connect their personal motivation with instruction and the reasons that give meaning to their professional learning. Goal complexes offer a framework for investigating the latter without losing the distinction between what teachers strive for and why they do so. Connecting these aims and reasons within broader achievement goal systems with concrete learning behavior and independently assessed competence development would advance our understanding of teachers as professional learners and, through this, of the motivational processes underlying their dual responsibility for their own learning and that of their students. A further theoretical question concerns how individual teachers represent the connections among their goals and reasons, and whether these representations help explain why similar aims are pursued differently across teaching and learning situations. This calls for distinguishing teachers' understandings of their motivation from the processes through which it operates, and testing whether these understandings explain professional behavior beyond the goals and reasons considered separately.
