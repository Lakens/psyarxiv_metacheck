How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Marginal Significance works

List all sentences that describe an effect as ‘marginally significant’.

The marginal module searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The list of terms is a subset of those listed in a blog post by Matthew Hankins. The module returns all sentences that match terms describing ‘marginally significant’ results.

Some of the terms identified might not be problematic in some contexts, and there are ways to describe ‘marginal significance’ that are not detected by the module.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. Abstract

Music conveys emotion and meaning. Here, we assessed how performers' expressivity influence listeners' affective, aesthetic and social-cognitive processes, and how these processes are shaped by individual differences. A total of 275 participants completed an online experiment in either an audiovisual (n = 139) or audio-only (n = 136) condition, in which they viewed or listened to three randomized performances of the same piano work by different world-renowned pianists (Yunchan Lim, Khatia Buniatishvili, and Lang Lang). Following each performance, participants provided affective, aesthetic, and social-cognitive ratings and completed measures of individual differences related to music engagement and social cognition. We found significant Performer (Lim, Buniatishvili, Lang) x Modality (audiovisual, audio-only) interactions for social-cognitive (Connection, Theory of Mind) and specific emotional (Joyful Activation, Sadness) responses, with performer-specific differences being present only in the audiovisual condition. Listener familiarity with the score was the strongest modulator of listener's experience, whereas empathy, musical reward sensitivity, musical absorption, and Theory of Mind showed selective associations. Our results suggest that musical experience is a multimodal interaction of performer expressivity, sensory modality, and listener characteristics. Furthermore, it supports the idea that visual access to a performance amplifies how listeners construct musical meaning and connection from their interpretation of the performer's expression and intentions.

Introduction

Music permeates nearly every aspect of human life-from daily routines to social and cultural events-enriching our experiences with meaning and emotion (Schubert, 2009; Clayton, 2016; Mehr et al., 2019). Music fosters affective engagement and exploration, conveying emotions and ideas that transcend the musical domain, in turn allowing listeners to form personal and culturally grounded interpretations (Cespedes-Guevara & Eerola, 2018; Thompson, 2008; Groves et al., 2025; Steinbeis et al., 2011; Davies, 1994). These interpretations arise from the interplay between the listener's inner world-shaped by cultural and musical experience (Pearce, 2018; Mas-Herrero et al., 2026)-and the performer's unique interpretive vision, which translates internal imagery, understanding, and emotion into expressive nuances that guide the listener's experience (Persson, 2001). Recent research suggests that listeners are sensitive to the human qualities of musical performance, valuing expressive nuances such as individuality, intentionality, and imperfection (Lecamwasam et al, 2025; Anderton and Goodge, 2025; Brand et al., 2012). Yet relatively little is known about how performer-specific expressive differences influence the affective, aesthetic, and social-cognitive meanings that listeners construct from a musical experience. The present study addresses this gap by examining how performer expressivity serves as a conduit for emotion and meaning.

Previous research has extensively examined construction of musical meanings by measuring parameters such as tempo, phrasing, dynamics, articulation, and chord asynchrony, among others (Ayyildiz et al., 2025; Cancino-Chacón et al., 2018; Eitan, 2017; Margulis et al., 2017; Palmer et al., 2013; Vines, 2006; Gabrielsson et al, 1996; Palmer, 1989). Converging evidence indicates that music reliably conveys emotion through acoustic cues that listeners can accurately decode (Groves et al., 2025; Vuoskoski et al., 2022; Ma & Thompson 2015; Zentner et al., 2008; Juslin & Laukka, 2003). For instance, in Western music, musical sadness is associated with specific acoustic features such as lower pitch, slower tempo, minor mode, dark timbres, legato articulation, and less energetic execution (Juslin & Laukka, 2004). Extending these findings, other approaches consider sources of musical meaning beyond musical structure itself. For example, a constructionist account evaluates how the valence and arousal expressed by the music (i.e., its core affect; Russell, 2003) interact with the listener's personal and contextual information to shape emotion perception (Cespedes-Guevara & Eerola, 2018). In a similar vein, Thompson and colleagues (2023) proposed a framework for music appreciation with three interacting pillars: musical structure, self-oriented responses (e.g., music-evoked autobiographical memories, individual differences in music appreciation), and source sensitivity. Source sensitivity has received less research attention than the other two pillars and refers to the listener's awareness of the causal and contextual origins of music, including information about the performer, their gestures, intentions, and the music's cultural meanings.

In Thompson and colleagues' (2023) framework, source sensitivity interacts with the musical structure and the self-oriented responses to modulate how humans perceive music. For example, in live or recorded performance contexts, listeners may attend to visual cues such as facial expressions, gestures, and body movements, which in turn influence perceptions of musical structure and can amplify affect and meaning (Thompson et al., 2005; Thompson et al., 2008; Wald-Fuhrmann et al., 2021). Thompson et al. (2023) further suggest that this source-based interpretive process may also involve judgments of authenticity, including whether a performance is perceived as sincere and emotionally genuine. These source-based inferences may also contribute to broader aesthetic evaluation. Judgements of beauty, expressivity, pleasure, and liking may depend not only on acoustic features, but also on the performer's embodied expressions and whether these bodily cues are perceived as authentic. This view resonates with Brielmann and Pelli's (2017) claim that "beauty requires thought": beauty is not merely a direct sensory pleasure but depends on attentional and cognitive engagement with the stimulus. In the same vein, Margulis et al. (2017) demonstrated that contextual information about artist's expressive intent shapes audience's emotional interpretation and aesthetic responses.

The emergence of AI-generated music has intensified debates about authenticity and artistic meaning. While AI can emulate stylistic features of performers, they remain limited in the capacity for intentionality, spontaneity, and embodied expression due to its lack of self-awareness and emotional capacity (Watchus, 2024). Listeners consistently rate musical performances believed to be human as more emotionally engaging than those perceived as AI-generated (Ansani et al., 2025). In this sense, aesthetic and affective responses to music may be shaped not only by what listeners hear, but also by how they understand the person and expressive intention behind the performance, underscoring emotional resonance grounded in human connection (Launay, 2015).

Music can thus be viewed as inherently social and imbued with a sense of human agency (Launay, 2015), with listeners responding to music in ways that resemble how they respond to another person (Eerola et al., 2018). Livingstone and Thompson (2009) propose a relevant conceptual foundation for this dynamic human relationship linking music, social cognition, and Theory of Mind (ToM)-the human capacity to attribute emotions, beliefs, and intentions to others (Happé, 2003). The authors argue that musical expression serves as a proxy for emotional states, enabling listeners to engage ToM-like mechanisms to infer a performer's inner state. In support of this view, an fMRI study by Steinbeis & Koelsch (2009) showed that when listeners believed music was human made rather than computer-generated, activation occurred in brain regions associated with ToM, including the medial prefrontal cortex and temporoparietal junction.

Taken together, these findings illustrate how source sensitivity may contribute to listeners' inference of human agency, intention, and emotion from a musical performance. However, such source-based cues are unlikely to affect all listeners in the same way. Within the framework proposed by Thompson and colleagues (2023), source sensitivity interacts with self-oriented responses, suggesting that the influence of a performer's expressive cues depends partly on individual differences. In other words, the same performance may elicit different responses across listeners. Two particularly relevant sets of individual differences are social-cognitive skills and music-specific predispositions and experience.

Social-cognitive skills refer broadly to the processes through which individuals perceive, interpret, and respond to other people's emotions, intentions, beliefs, and behavior (Beaudoin and Beauchamp, 2020). These include empathy and Theory of Mind (ToM), two related but distinct processes that may be particularly important when listeners interpret a performer's expressive intentions and emotional state. Trait empathy -the capacity for empathic reactions as a stable feature of personality (Wiseman, 1996) -has been associated with stronger emotional engagement with music, with more empathic listeners experiencing music, particularly sad music, as more moving, pleasurable, and socially meaningful (Taruffi & Koelsch, 2014; Zickfeld et al., 2017; Eerola et al., 2016). Neuroimaging studies further suggest that trait empathy enhances the processing of musical emotion, with more empathic listeners showing stronger recruitment of neural systems involved in emotional contagion, sensorimotor simulation, reward, as well as more distinct neural representations of emotions conveyed through music (Wallmark et al., 2018; Sachs et al., 2018).

Social neuroscience research distinguishes empathy and ToM as complementary but dissociable mechanisms for understanding others that are supported by distinct neural systems.

Whereas empathy represents a socio-affective route involving sharing another person's emotional state, ToM represents a socio-cognitive route involving inferences about others' beliefs, intentions, and emotions (Preckel et al., 2018; Kanske et al., 2015). Experimental studies further suggest that music may modulate these processes differently. Instructing listeners to empathize with a performer and imagine their emotional experience has been shown to intensify music-induced emotions and associated physiological responses (Miu & Balteș, 2012), while music-induced mood has been associated with the participant's use of mental-state inferences during social reasoning (Converse et al., 2008). However, more recent work found that emotional music selectively enhanced empathic responding, compassion, and prosocial decisions while leaving ToM performance unaffected, suggesting that music may exert a stronger influence on socioaffective than socio-cognitive processing (McDonald et al., 2022). Together, these findings indicate that empathy has a well-established role in shaping musical experience, while the contribution of ToM remains comparatively less consistent and underexplored.

Regarding music-specific characteristics, previous research shows that individual differences in musical absorption, musical reward sensitivity, and musical expertise further contribute to shape musical experience. Absorption-the tendency to become deeply immersed in perceptual and imaginative experience-has been linked to greater engagement, enjoyment, and reward during music listening, suggesting that sustained immersive attention may amplify affective and aesthetic responses (Cardona et al., 2022; Hall et al., 2016). Greater sensitivity to musical reward is associated with stronger emotional, physiological, and neural responses to music, whereas specific musical anhedonia is characterized by reduced musical pleasure despite intact responsiveness to other rewards (Mas-Herrero et al., 2013; Mas-Herrero et al., 2014; Martinez-Molina et al., 2016; Mas-Herrero et al., 2026). The degree of musical expertise also modulates the way people experience music (Castro and Lima, 2014; Battcock and Schutz, 2021) For example, musicians-especially drummers-perceive expressiveness and emotion in solo drumming more strongly through visual cues than non-musicians (Di Mauro et al., 2018).

Taken together, these findings highlight the need for an integrative approach (such as that proposed by Thompson and colleagues, 2023) that considers how a performer's intentions and expressive cues (part of the source sensitivity) interact with individual differences in listeners' social-cognitive and music-related skills (part of the self sensitivity). However, these factors have rarely been examined together within a single experimental framework. This study addresses that gap by examining whether different interpretations of the same classical musical piece by different pianists elicit distinct responses, and whether these responses differ when presented with or without visual access to the performer. Classical piano performance provides a useful context for exploring this gap because the same piece can convey different emotions, aesthetic, and cultural meanings across performers through subtle variations in timing, dynamics, phrasing, articulation, and embodied expression (Repp, 1992; Bhatara et al., 2011; Bernays & Traube, 2014). Furthermore, by comparing audiovisual and audio-only conditions, the study aims to clarify the extent to which performer-specific meaning is conveyed through sound alone and how visual cues, such as facial and body movement, further shape the listeners' experience of human agency, authenticity, and emotional communication.

Two groups of online participants evaluated three recorded performances of the same musical work by different pianists. One group viewed audiovisual recordings of the performances, whereas the other listened to audio-only excerpts. Participants rated their affective, aesthetic, and social-cognitive responses to the different performances. The study also examined whether individual differences in demographical factors, trait empathy, musical absorption, musical reward sensitivity, musical expertise, and ToM-related affective inference ability modulated the effects of performer and presentation modality on listeners' responses. Based on previous literature, we hypothesized that the effects of performer expressivity would be more pronounced when visual cues are available. We also hypothesized that individual differences would predict the overall responses and differ depending on visual access to the performer, with trait empathy and ToM expected to show stronger associations with social-cognitive responses in the audiovisual condition.

Methods

Participants.

Using pilot behavioral data (N=52) and the R package mixedpower (Kumle et al., 2021), we estimated statistical power to find a main effect of performer across a range of sample sizes (50-150 participants). Power analyses were conducted for each dependent variable. A sample size of 100 participants achieved greater than 90% power to detect a significant main effect of performer among at least one of the chosen measures. We planned to collect data from at least 100 participants per group after exclusions. Between February 9 th and May 27 th , 2026, two groups of participants were recruited online through the New York University SONA participant pool, a credit-based recruitment system for students enrolled in psychology courses. One group completed the audiovisual condition of the experiment, whereas the other completed the audio-only condition.

Written consent was obtained online. All procedures were approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) at New York University, and participants were given course credit. Exclusion criteria were established to ensure valid auditory and perceptual data (e.g., attentional checks) and to avoid potential confounding effects from conditions known to reduce hedonic or affective responsiveness (e.g., music anhedonia; Mas-Herrero et al., 2013) and psychiatric or neurological conditions. Of 262 participants who completed the audiovisual version of the experiment, 123 participants were excluded, resulting in a final sample of 139 participants. Exclusions included 9 participants who had music anhedonia (i.e., scored below 60 on the Barcelona Music Reward Questionnaire, BMRQ; Mas-Herrero et al., 2013), and 95 participants who failed a headphone screening task. Additional exclusions were made for reported psychiatric diagnoses, including bipolar disorder or autism spectrum disorder (n = 12), non-normal vision or hearing (n = 2), and failure of two or more catch trials (n = 5). A second independent group of 219 participants completed the audio-only version of the experiment. Participants were excluded for music anhedonia (n = 8), failing the headphone screening task (n = 58), reporting a diagnosis of bipolar disorder or autism spectrum disorder (n = 9), reporting a hearing deficit (n = 1), and failing two or more catch trials (n = 7). This resulted in a final sample of 136 participants in the audio-only condition. Across both groups, the final sample included 275 participants.

Stimuli.

The experimental stimuli consisted of three professional audiovisual performance recordings of Franz Liszt's Liebestraum No. 3. This well-known work from the Romantic piano repertoire was selected for two primary reasons: (1) it has numerous commercially available recordings, and (2) its emotionally ambiguous character affords a broad range of expressive interpretations rather than a single, fixed emotional narrative. It was therefore ideally suited for exploring how performance-driven nuances shape listener's perception. Three interpretations of the piece were selected from performances by internationally acclaimed pianists Yunchan Lim, Khatia Buniatishvili, and Lang Lang, chosen for their distinct interpretive approaches: Lim's poetic and introspective style, Buniatishvili's passionate and sensual expressivity, and Lang Lang's virtuosic and theatrical performance manner. Using Music Information Retrieval (MIR) tools (McFee et al., 2015), we confirmed that the three specific performances displayed observable differences in key expressive parameters (e.g., tempo, dynamic range, timbre, loudness). To minimize visual confounds, all selected recordings shared similar visual contexts, including comparable camera framing of the pianists' faces, hands, and body movements, as well as similar stage settings. The recordings were also similar in length (Lim: 328 seconds; Buniatishvili: 302 seconds; Lang: 302 seconds). The original recordings used as stimuli are listed in Table 1 of Appendix B.

Procedure. The experiment was conducted online using Qualtrics. An overview of the experimental design is shown in Figure 1. Participants first read and signed the informed consent and then filled out a brief set of demographic questionnaires (age, gender, ethnicity, psychiatric diagnosis, vision, and hearing). Participants were then required to wear headphones and to complete a validated headphone screening test commonly used in online auditory experiments (Woods et al., 2017; Orpella et al., 2025). In this task, listeners were asked to identify which of three pure tones was the quietest, with one tone presented 180° out of phase between stereochannels. Participants completed six trials in randomized order, with each trial corresponding to one of the six possible presentation orders of the three stimuli. This task was easily performed when using headphones but difficult with loudspeakers due to phase cancellation. Data from participants who scored fewer than 4 trials correctly in this task was excluded. After the headphone test, participants proceeded to the main experimental task. The participants in the audio-visual group viewed three video recordings of the same piano work, each played by a different pianist.

The participants of the audio-only condition listened to the three audio recordings extracted from the same performances. To control for order effects, the three stimuli (Video/Audio A, B, C) were presented in one of six possible orders, with participants randomly assigned to one order. Materials Affective-aesthetic responses.

Affective-aesthetic responses were assessed using single-item 7-point Likert scales measuring arousal (e.g., how aroused (that is, physically activated) did you feel while watching the performance?), emotional intensity (e.g., how emotionally intense was your personal experience while watching the performance?), valence (e.g., how positive or negative did your emotional experience feel?), pleasure (e.g., how pleasurable was the experience of watching the performance?), and beauty (e.g., how beautiful did you find the performance?). Using single-item scales for aesthetic responses (i.e., subjective evaluations of, for example, beauty or liking; Brielmann & Pelli, 2017) is a standard and validated methodology in empirical aesthetics research (Juslin, 2021; Leder & Nadal, 2014). This approach captures a listener's explicit judgments about the performance, a process that correlates with but is distinct from felt emotional response (Brattico et al., 2013; Ripollés et al., 2025).

Emotional engagement.

Music-specific emotional engagement was measured using the 9-item Geneva Emotional Music Scale on 5-point Likert scales (GEMS-9; Jacobsen et al., 2024). The GEMS-9 is especially well-suited for this study as it was validated using primarily classical music stimuli and is uniquely sensitive to emotions commonly present in music: Wonder, Transcendence, Tenderness, Peacefulness, Nostalgia, Joyful Activation, Power, Sadness, and Tension, which are more complex and nuanced than basic emotions (Zentner et al, 2008). This instrument is specifically designed to quantify the intensity of felt emotional responses to music (e.g., please rate the intensity with which you felt each of the following feelings on a scale ranging from 1 (not at all) to 5 (very much)). We focused on felt emotions rather than perceived emotions (Schubert, 2013) as our aim was to capture listeners' direct affective experience rather than their judgments about the music's expressive content.

Social-cognitive responses.

Social-cognitive processing was assessed using an additional set of single-item questions measuring authenticity (e.g., how authentic did the performance feel to you?), meaning (e.g., to what extent did the performance feel personally meaningful to you?), connection (e.g., how connected did you feel to the performer?), and Theory of Mind (e.g., how well did you feel you understood the performer's emotional or expressive intentions?) on 7-point Likert scales. The ToM measure was designed to capture the content of the spontaneous mental state attributions that listeners engage in (Steinbeis & Koelsch, 2009). The question was adapted from Steinbeis and Koelsch (2009), whose fMRI results demonstrated that greater endorsement of this item was associated with increased activation in brain regions implicated in ToM processing.

Familiarity with the score. We also measured participants' familiarity with the score (Prior to this survey, how familiar were you with the piece of music performed in these videos?), using a 5point scale (Never -Vaguely familiar, cannot name -Somewhat familiar, know the piece or the composer -Very familiar, listened many times -Expert familiarity, performed or studied the piece).

Trait Empathy. The Interpersonal Reactivity Index (IRI) is a widely used scale with established reliability and validity for its subscales-perspective taking, empathic concern, fantasy, and personal distress (Davis, 1983). We used the brief IRI, a 16-item version of the questionnaire (four items per subscale) that has demonstrated adequate internal consistency and established both structural validity, via a clear four-factor structure, and construct validity, through theoretically meaningful associations with external criteria (Ingoglia et al., 2016).

Musical Absorption.

The Absorption in Music Scale (AIMS) was used to measure an individual's trait tendency to show strong emotional responses or become deeply engaged in music (Sandstrom & Russo, 2013). This 34-item scale has shown high internal consistency (Cronbach's alpha .92-.94) and strong test-retest reliability as well as solid criterion validity to successfully predict emotional responses to music.

Musical expertise.

The Goldsmiths Musical Sophistication Index (Gold-MSI; Müllensiefen et al., 2014) was used to measure musical expertise. The scale has demonstrated solid reliability (high internal consistency and test-retest correlations) and validity, reflected in moderate associations with objective listening ability tests and appropriate convergent patterns with theoretically related subscales.

Musical Reward Sensitivity.

The Barcelona Music Reward Questionnaire (BMRQ) was used to assess individual differences in musical reward sensitivity across five subdomains: musical seeking, emotion evocation, mood regulation, social reward, and sensory-motor integration (Mas-Herrero et al., 2013). The BMRQ has shown high internal consistency (Cronbach's alpha = .87) and strong construct validity, demonstrating robust associations with behavioral, physiological, neural, and genetic measures of music reward (Mas-Herrero et al., 2014; Martinez-Molina et al., 2016; Martinez-Molina et al., 2019; Bignardi et al., 2025; Mas-Herrero et al., 2026).

Theory of Mind (ToM).

The Reading the Mind in the Eyes Test (RMET; Warrier et al., 2020) was used to index individual differences in ToM-related affective inference ability. This 36-item multiple-choice test measures the ability to infer mental states from subtle facial cues in the eye region. It is brief, easily administered, and widely used in social cognition research, showing acceptable internal consistency (Yeung et al., 2024).

Statistical Analyses

To ensure that participants in the audiovisual and audio-only conditions were wellbalanced, we used JASP with default priors to examine group differences for demographic variables (age, gender, ethnicity), musical reward sensitivity (BMRQ), musical expertise (Gold-MSI), trait empathy (IRI), musical absorption (AIMS), and ToM-related affective inference ability (RMET). Continuous variables were compared using Welch's independent-samples t-tests and Bayesian independent-samples t-tests, while categorical variables were examined using frequentist and Bayesian contingency-table analyses. Frequentist tests assessed whether the two groups differed significantly. Bayesian tests assessed the relative evidence for no group difference versus a group difference, reported as Bayes Factors (BF₀₁). Values above 1 support the null hypothesis of no difference, whereas values below 1 support the alternative hypothesis of a difference. For example, BF₀₁ = 5 means that the observed data are five times more likely under the null hypothesis than under the alternative hypothesis.

To analyze the effects of performer, modality (audiovisual, audio-only) and individual differences in the 18 different dependent variables (5 affective-aesthetic, 4 socio-cognitive, and 9 music emotion measures) we used linear mixed modeling (LMM) in R (version 4.6.1) and RStudio (2026.06.24) with the lme4 package. All models included fixed effects of Modality (Audiovisual, Audio-only), Performer (Lim, Buniatishvili, Lang), and their interaction. Participant ID was included as a random intercept to account for repeated ratings across performers. Models also included control fixed effects for trait empathy, musical absorption, musical expertise, musical reward sensitivity, theory of mind, gender, race, age and familiarity with the score. To assess significance, type III Wald χ² tests were used via the car package, with false discovery rate (FDR) correction implemented to control for the multiple comparisons across the 18 analyses. Significant FDR-corrected interactions were further examined using the emmeans package with Tukeyadjusted pairwise comparisons and were plotted using the ggplot2 package.

Discussion

The present study shows how performer expressivity, sensory modality, and individual listener characteristics jointly shape emotional and social-cognitive responses to classical piano music. Consistent with our primary hypothesis, visual access to the performer led to more pronounced effects of performer expressivity in social-cognitive and music-specific emotional responses, shown here by significant Performer x Modality interactions for two of the socialcognitive responses (Connection, Theory of Mind) and two of the emotional responses measured (Joyful Activation, Sadness). Conversely, visual access to the performer led to significantly lower ratings of Nostalgia, shown here with the main effect of Modality with listeners reporting greater nostalgia in the audio-only condition. Also consistent with our secondary hypothesis, we show how individual differences modulated affective, aesthetic, and social-cognitive responses with familiarity with the score being the strongest modulator of listeners' experiences. Increased familiarity was associated with higher ratings of three affective-aesthetic responses (Emotional Intensity, Valence, Pleasure), two social-cognitive responses (Connection, Meaning), and four specific emotional responses (Nostalgia, Transcendence, Wonder, Tenderness). Greater trait empathy and musical reward sensitivity were both associated with higher ratings of Authenticity and Beauty while greater musical absorption was associated with higher ratings of Transcendence.

Contrary to our hypothesis that ToM would modulate social-cognitive responses, higher ToM scores were associated with lower ratings of Joyful Activation. Together, these findings support: (i) the idea that visual access to a performance amplifies performer-specific expressive communication; (ii) that musical meaning is constructed not only from acoustical cues and listener context, but also through social interpretation of the performer as an expressive, intentional agent; and (iii) that individual listener characteristics further shape how meaning and emotion are constructed from a performance.

These results also have implications for music pedagogy and performance training as well as for applications in music-based interventions aimed at enhancing social cognition, emotional engagement, and well-being (Sihvonen et al., 2017; Meissner, 2021; Schiavio, 2018).

After controlling for multiple comparisons across all 18 dependent variables, the strongest modality-dependent performer effects were observed in the social-cognitive responses.

Connection and Theory of Mind ratings across performers significantly differed only when participants had visual access to the performers. Moreover, despite identical acoustic signal, Connection ratings for Lim significantly differed between the audiovisual and audio-only conditions, suggesting that embodied visual cues play an important role in inferring performer's intentions and emotional state and in fostering feelings of connection. These findings align closely with Thompson and colleagues' (2023) framework, which suggests, in the context of source sensitivity, that listeners integrate visual information about performers into their interpretation of musical meaning. The authors further suggest that this interpretive process may involve judgments of emotionally genuineness, which may be supported by ToM inferences. The present findings also extend previous work by Livingstone and Thompson (2009) and Steinbeis and Koelsch (2009) which showed associations between ToM and musical experience. Here we show that listeners use visually available expressive cues to further distinguish between performers, highlighting performer expressivity as a socially meaningful signal that shapes listeners' inferences about intention and interpersonal connection. It is particularly intriguing that these effects were observed for both ToM and Connection -constructs that are theoretically distinct yet complementary. In this vein, detecting and inferring another person's mental state may provide the foundation for developing a deeper sense of interpersonal connection (Wheatley et al., 2012).

A similar pattern was observed for music-specific emotional responses. Joyful Activation and Sadness ratings differed significantly across performers only when participants had visual access to the performances. Furthermore, despite identical acoustic signals, Lang's performances elicited significantly different ratings on both emotions between the audiovisual and audio-only conditions, suggesting that embodied visual cues also shape listeners' emotional experiences beyond the information conveyed by sound alone. This finding is consistent with previous studies showing that facial expressions, body movements, and performance gestures contribute to emotional perception and communication (Thompson et al., 2005; Thompson et al., 2008; Vines et al., 2006; Wald-Fuhrmann et al., 2021). These findings suggest that embodied expressive cues do not uniformly amplify emotional responses but shape specific emotional qualities.

One of the most interesting findings was the robust influence of familiarity with the score, which modulated nine of the eighteen dependent variables across affective, aesthetic, socialcognitive, and emotional responses. This broad pattern supports previous research underscoring familiarity as a central determinant of listeners' musical experience (Freitas et al., 2018; Pereira et al., 2011; van den Bosch et al., 2013) and is highly consistent with Tompson and colleagues' (2023) framework, in which familiarity is considered a central self-oriented contributor to musical experience. This finding is also compatible with the constructionist account of musical emotion proposed by Cespedes-Guevara & Eerola (2018), which emphasizes listeners' prior knowledge and experience in the construction of emotional responses to music. From a predictive processing perspective, the results are likewise consistent with Huron's (2006) ITPRA theory, whereby greater familiarity may support richer predictive models of musical structure, enhancing emotional engagement through more refined processes of anticipation, confirmation, violation, and resolution.

Beyond familiarity, several measures of individual differences showed selective associations with listener's experiences. Greater musical reward sensitivity and trait empathy were associated with stronger perceptions of beauty and authenticity, suggesting that listeners who derive greater reward from music and those who are more empathic may be particularly sensitive to aesthetic quality and sincere expressive intentions. The association between trait empathy and aesthetic evaluations is broadly consistent with previous research showing that highly empathic listeners experience stronger emotional engagement and music-related reward during music (Eerola et al., 2016 (Eerola et al., , 2021)). Greater musical absorption was associated with stronger feelings of transcendence, consistent with previous research linking absorption to immersive and emotionally rewarding musical experiences (Cardona et al., 2022; Hall et al., 2016). Interestingly, higher ToM scores were associated with lower ratings of Joyful Activation. Although the present data cannot establish the mechanism underlying this relationship, it is broadly consistent with evidence suggesting that positive and negative affective states differentially influence mentalizing processes. Experimental studies have shown that sad mood can promote more deliberate Theory of Mind inference than happy mood (Converse et al., 2008), while emotional music does not necessarily enhance ToM performance (McDonald et al., 2022). Collectively, these findings suggest that individual differences (the self-pillar in Thomson and colleagues' framework, 2023) selectively shape specific dimensions of musical experience.

The present study has several limitations. First, participants consisted primarily of university students recruited through a psychology participant pool, limiting the generalizability of the findings to broader populations with different cultural and educational backgrounds, ages, and musical experiences. Second, the study examined only one musical work from the Western classical piano repertoire. Although using the same score allowed expressive differences between performers to be isolated, the findings may not generalize to other musical genres, instruments, or performance traditions. Third, only three world renowned pianists were included. While they represented clearly distinct expressions, a larger and more diverse performer sample would provide a more comprehensive understanding of performer-specific communication. Finally, although audiovisual recordings capture many important aspects of musical performance, they cannot fully reproduce the multisensory, interactive, and socially immersive nature of attending a live concert.

Conclusion

Here we demonstrated that musical communication extends beyond the acoustic signal to encompass the performer, the listener, and the dynamic interaction between them. Visual access to the performer enhanced performer-specific expressive communication for social-cognitive and music-emotional responses, highlighting the importance of embodied expression in shaping musical meaning. We further showed that listeners' prior familiarity with the score and individual characteristics influenced how performances are experienced, emphasizing that musical perception may be an active meaning construction that is highly individualized. Together, these findings support the view that musical experience is a multimodal, socially grounded, and individualized phenomenon, and place the human performer at the center of how listeners perceive emotion, infer intention, and form meaningful interpersonal connection through music.

Future

research could extend this work in several directions. Examining a broader range of musical genres, performers, and cultural traditions would determine whether the observed effects generalize beyond Western classical piano performance. Comparing recorded and live performances would further clarify how audience-performer interaction and physical co-presence influence social cognition.
