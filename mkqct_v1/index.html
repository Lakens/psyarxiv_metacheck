How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. 1. Introduction

The notion of discourse prominence is standardly diagnosed through referential form. In a context like "John chatted with Mary," speakers who continue by mentioning "John" are more likely to use a pronoun than a name, and this asymmetry has been taken to indicate that the subject "John" is more prominent in the discourse than the object "Mary" (e.g., Ariel, 1990; Brennan, 1995; Brennan et al., 1987; Grosz et al., 1995; Kehler et al., 2008). However, this definition faces a problem of circularity, as pointed out in Hert et al. (2024) and Bader and Portele (2025). On one hand, the notion of prominence is used to explain why speakers prefer to use pronouns in certain environments. On the other hand, the preference to use a pronoun is often taken as evidence for the prominence of a referent. To avoid this circularity, discourse prominence must be defined independently of pronoun use if it is to explain it. This paper examines the feasibility of one potential definition, i.e., discourse prominence as a reflection of the activation of an entity in memory.

This definition comes from one classic class of accounts of referential form choice, which defines the discourse prominence of an entity as a privileged status in memory, such as the psychological ease to retrieve it (accessible, activated) or the amount of attention allocated to it (in focus). This privileged status has been described as accessibility, topicality, or saliency (e.g., Ariel, 1990; Givón, 1983; Grosz et al., 1995), and more accessible, topical, or salient referents are more likely to be expressed with reduced forms, such as pronouns. Less salient referents, in contrast, require speakers to activate more information for production (Fukumura et al., 2011), or to provide more explicit descriptions such as names to facilitate listeners' interpretation of the referent (e.g., Ariel, 1990). This special cognitive status is often conceptualized as the gradient activation level of the antecedent in memory (e.g., Arnold, 2001; Arnold & Griffin, 2007).

For clarity, we consider the term discourse prominence to refer to the discourse status of a referent, i.e., how it has been treated in the discourse up to this point (Arnold, 2008; p.499), for instance, topical vs. non-topical, given vs. new. We adopt the assumption that the use of pronoun is an indicator of discourse prominence of the antecedent, such that entities favored by pronoun use are also more prominent in the discourse. However, we do not assume that every factor affecting pronoun use also affects discourse prominence, because referential form choice can also be influenced by independent constraints, such as ambiguity avoidance. We use the term cognitive prominence or antecedent activation to refer to the supposed corresponding special cognitive status in memory, and activation-based account to refer to this particular type of accounts that consider referential form choice as a result of cognitive status.

We argue that there are at least two versions of the activation-based accounts, although this distinction is not explicitly articulated in the literature. The strong version identifies discourse prominence with cognitive prominence. Under this account, reduced forms are used in a particular discourse because their referent is cognitively prominent (cf. Accessibility Hierarchy by Ariel (1990) and Givenness Hierarchy in Gundel et al. (1993)): For instance, the Accessibility Hierarchy considers "degrees of Accessibility correspond to different statuses within memory, where material may be extremely salient, i.e., highly activated, or, vice versa, unactivated or fading away" (Ariel, 1990; p.16). From this perspective, speakers use pronouns because their antecedents are cognitively prominent, that is, they are highly activated in memory. Thus, factors that promote pronoun use for a referent by affecting its discourse status should also increase its activation in the memory.

Note that the traditional view of the activation-based accounts (e.g., Gundel et al., 1993; p.275) considers an "addressee-oriented" notion of prominence: speakers choose referential forms based on the activation level of the referent in the addressee's memory, so the addressee can efficiently identify the antecedent. However, given the communal nature of the discourse, the set of entities that are prominent in the addressee's mental representation often overlaps with that in the speaker's mental representation (Arnold, 2008; p.504). This offers a "speaker-oriented" production account. Evidence has shown that reference production is affected by productioninternal constraints (see Arnold, 2008 for an overview) and speakers are in fact referring to their own discourse model rather than the addressee's. For those reasons, we consider the cognitive prominence of a referent as its activation level in the speaker's mind in this paper.

The weak version of the activation-based account assumes that cognitive prominence of an antecedent is merely one of the potentially many contributors to its discourse prominence.

Under this view, factors that do not affect cognitive prominence of a referent could still affect its discourse prominence, thereby affecting referential form choice (e.g., the informativeness of the antecedent; see Arnold & Zerkle (2019) for an overview). Consequently, a preference for using a pronoun under a certain discourse status does not necessarily indicate that the antecedent is cognitively prominent.

Studies have provided findings consistent with the possibility that antecedent activation influences referential form choice, although these findings do not distinguish between the strong and weak versions of the activation-based account (Arnold & Griffin, 2007; Fukumura et al., 2011). For example, Arnold and Griffin (2007) showed that speakers were less likely to use pronouns when referring to "Mickey" in "Mickey went for a walk with Donald" than in "Mickey went for a walk." Because this effect persisted even when the additional character was unambiguous in gender, the declining pronoun use was unlikely to be due to ambiguity avoidance. That is, speakers were not using more names to provide more details to help the addressee resolve reference. Crucially, assuming that attention is a limited resource and the use of pronouns is a reflection of antecedent activation, they argued that the declining use of pronouns was due to the decreasing amount of activation allocated to each antecedent under the presence of an additional character.

Yet, these studies address a different direction of the relationship from the one we test.

They manipulate factors expected to affect antecedent activation and observe consequences for referential form. Our question is the converse: whether factors known to affect discourse prominence, as diagnosed by referential form, also affect antecedent activation. The two are not equivalent. Even if activation influences form choice, it does not follow that every factor conferring discourse prominence does so by increasing activation. Establishing that discourse prominence can be identified with cognitive prominence, as the strong version requires, therefore demands evidence running in the direction we test here. Addressing this question requires an independent measure of antecedent activation that does not rely on pronoun use. In this paper, we address this question by using pronoun planning speed as a proxy for antecedent activation.

2. The present study

The present study aims to examine the relationship between discourse prominence and cognitive prominence by testing whether entities that are prominent in the discourse, which are consequently more likely to be referred to by pronouns, are also more highly activated in the memory during reference production. We specifically investigate whether linguistic factors that are argued to affect pronoun use via discourse prominence affect antecedent activation in reference production, as measured by the planning speed of the co-referring expression. Our investigation therefore does not concern constraints, such as ambiguity avoidance, that affect referential form choice independently of discourse prominence.

In four experiments, we tested how grammatical function (the major factor that uncontroversially affects discourse prominence) and discourse function of antecedents affects the planning speed, while controlling referential form using Binding Principle C (Reinhart, 1976; Chomsky, 1981), such that pronouns are the only possible form. Under the common assumption that a more activated item is retrieved from the memory faster than a less activated counterpart (e.g., Engelmann et al., 2019; Lewis & Vasishth, 2005; Anderson, 1996 for more general assumptions in memory; see Dell, 1986; Roelofs, 1997 for a similar assumption in production, despite not explicitly related to working memory objects), referring to a more activated antecedent should facilitate the retrieval of the antecedent, and thus the overall planning of the co-referring expression. We therefore treat the pronoun planning speed as a proxy for antecedent activation.

We designed stimuli like (2) to control the choice of referential form available to participants, in contrast with (3).

(2) Gary told Anna that he lost the job.

(3) Gary told Anna the news. Gary/He got the job.

Although (2) and ( 3) describe very similar events, the available referential forms for the antecedent differ. In (3), when referring to the subject antecedent "Gary," speakers could choose between the name "Gary" (though less natural) or the pronoun "he" as the referential form. But in (2), speakers are only allowed to use the pronoun "he." This difference also holds when referring to the object antecedent "Anna." The restriction on referential form in (2) has been formulated as Principle C of the Binding Theory (Chomsky, 1981). According to Principle C, a proper name or a definite noun phrase (or R-expressions more generally) cannot be used to refer to an antecedent that c-commands it. The term "c-command" refers to a relationship between constituents based on the hierarchical notion of dominance (Reinhart, 1976). A constituent ccommands its sisters and any constituents that the sister constituents dominate. In "Gary told Mary that he got the job", both "Gary" and "Mary" c-command the subject position of the second clause, so a pronoun "he" or "she" is the only possible form for "Gary" and "Mary."

The application of Principle C ensures that our measurement is not confounded by differences in the proportion of pronoun use across conditions. When speakers are free to choose between a pronoun and a full expression, as in (3), they may produce pronouns at different rates across conditions (e.g., 30% of pronoun use in the less prominent condition vs. 60% of pronoun use in the more prominent condition). In this case, the pronoun planning speed across the two conditions is not necessarily comparable. We thus compare the production speed of pronouns when speakers are strongly encouraged to use pronouns and pronouns only because of Principle C, as in (2). As will be seen, speakers indeed use pronouns in the majority of trials, and use full expressions very rarely (~3% across 4 experiments). This design therefore minimizes form choice as a source of variation, allowing a more comparable measure of pronoun planning speed across conditions. However, it also raises two questions: how planning speed can provide evidence about antecedent activation and its relationship to discourse prominence in reference production, and whether syntactically constraining referential form choice changes how antecedent activation contributes to planning speed. To address these questions, we outline a processing model specifying how antecedent retrieval and referential form selection contribute to planning speed. The model formalizes two assumptions about antecedent retrieval to address the first question, i.e., that planning speed indexes antecedent activation, and that discourse prominence is one potential source of that activation. Our design depends on the former to interpret planning speed as a meaningful measure at all, while our results, reported below, speak to whether the latter specifically holds. The model additionally specifies how referential form selection interacts with antecedent retrieval, addressing the second question by clarifying why constraining form choice via Principle C leaves the relationship between antecedent activation and planning speed intact.

2.1.A Processing Model Linking Discourse Prominence, Activation, and Planning Speed in Reference Production

Before introducing the model, it is worth briefly reviewing how activation relates to processing speed in single-word production. Two lines of evidence are relevant. First, pictures with high-frequency names are named faster than those with low-frequency names, a pattern attributed to easier word-form access (Jescheniak & Levelt, 1994). Second, under competitive accounts, picture naming is slowed by semantically related distractors or repeated naming of semantically related items because of increased competitor activation (Schriefers et al., 1990; Belke et al., 2005; Schnur et al., 2006). These findings motivate models in which access speed depends on target activation, whereas selection time depends on its activation relative to its competitors (Levelt et al., 1999). The same distinction motivates our model: activation of the conceptual and linguistic representations of an antecedent affects retrieval speed, whereas competition among referential forms adds selection time. What is more complicated than single word production in reference production is the restriction from syntactic relation, which should be checked regardless of if the syntactic constraint applies or not. This is why models for single word production cannot be directly extended to reference production.

Beyond single-word production, however, the relationship between planning time and reference form is less straightforward. Several studies have used latencies to begin speaking as an index of planning ease in referential contexts. For instance, Rosa and Arnold (2017) found that speakers both produced more reduced forms and began speaking sooner when continuing with a goal rather than a source referent. However, latency itself cannot be used to predict referential form: as reviewed by Arnold and Zerkle (2019), increases in onset latency were not accompanied by any change in the rate of pronoun or zero use across the experiments using this paradigm. For instance, Arnold and Nozari (2017) found a relationship running opposite to a simple accessibility account, in that speakers produced more pronouns and zeros on trials with longer latencies, and the strongest predictor of reduced-form use was the presence of a connector such as and or then. They interpreted the results as longer latencies afforded greater messagelevel pre-planning and stronger discourse connectedness, rather than easier retrieval of the referent. Zerkle and Arnold (2019) likewise found no support for the hypothesis that predictability affects reference form by way of planning facilitation using eye movements.

Durational measures present a somewhat more consistent picture: Zerkle et al. (2017) found that onset latency predicted the duration of subsequently produced character names, and that latency was itself sensitive to thematic-role predictability, indicating that planning difficulty can surface in the acoustic properties of the utterance.

However, the absence of mapping between planning time and referential form does not by itself reject the possibility that planning time can reflect activation. These studies measured planning difficulty in contexts where speakers were free to choose among referential forms.

However, the speed of form selection is not solely determined by the activation level of the antecedent and thus could confound planning speed as a measure of it. For example, if a speaker chooses a pronoun for a less activated antecedent, the pronoun may be selected quickly despite the antecedent's low activation. Planning speed would then reflect lemma activation rather than antecedent activation. This is the confound our use of Principle C is intended to remove: when only one form is grammatically available, variation attributable to selection among competing forms is minimised, and the remaining variation can more plausibly be attributed to retrieval.

The processing model developed below makes this reasoning explicit.

The model draws on widely accepted accounts of language production (Bock & Levelt, 1994) and memory architecture in sentence processing (Lewis & Vasishth, 2005); its predictions therefore do not hinge on idiosyncractic assumptions. According to Bock and Levelt (1994), the production process start with a generation of a pre-linguistic 'message' (Levelt, 1989), where the conceptual representation of the referent is presumably prepared. Then, in the subsequent stage of grammatical encoding, an intended referent is grammatically encoded to an appropriate type of referential expression via the process of lemma selection, and obtains corresponding morphological and phonological information in the process of lexeme selection. Finally, the grammatical encoding is mapped to a phonological representation for articulation. For each step, we specify the potential role of discourse prominence and discuss what determines planning speed in reference production.

During the message formulation stage, speakers retrieve the conceptual representation of the referent. The activation level of the conceptual representation should determine the speed of retrieval, which is the major determinant of planning speed at this step. If discourse prominence contributes to the activation of the conceptual representation, more prominent referent in the discourse should also be planned faster at this stage.

In the subsequent stage of grammatical encoding, speakers select the lemma as the referential form. This at least consists of two steps: First, the syntactic relation between the antecedent and the current position should always be checked to confirm the range of available referential forms. If the antecedent c-commands the position of the current referent like in (2) such that Principle C applies, then pronoun is the only available referential form. Second, if multiple forms are available, speakers should select from one of them. The time course of these two steps is not specified and is not central to the model: they may occur simultaneously or sequentially. Regardless of their time course, we assume that Principle C restricts the candidate set before or during referential form selection, such that grammatically unavailable forms do not compete with the pronoun to the same extent as available forms.

The first step is omitted by existing models on reference production such as Schmitt et al. (1999) and Bader and Portele (2025), but necessary given our manipulation using Principle C. The implementation of syntactic relation is not the major concern of this paper, so we adopt proposals in sentence comprehension and assume that it is determined by multiple linguistic cues encoded in the linguistic representation (Kush, 2013; Kush et al., 2015). The checking of linguistic features indicates that the linguistic representation of the antecedent should be retrieved at this step. Following the existing proposal in memory architecture (Lewis & Vasishth, 2005), the retrieval speed of the linguistic representation would be the major determinant of planning speed at this stage. Like in the retrieval of conceptual representation, a greater activation of the linguistic representation of an antecedent should facilitate their retrieval and thus planning time. If a prominent discourse status is used as a retrieval cue in this process, or promotes the base activation of the representation, it could also facilitate the retrieval and thus planning time of this step.

Up to this point, the activation of conceptual and linguistic representations of an antecedent is the major source of variant of planning time regardless of whether Principle C applies. Therefore, when other components of planning are held constant across conditions, differences in planning speed can serve as an index of differences in antecedent activation. If discourse prominence is a source of variation in antecedent activation, its effect should be reflected in planning speed. Yet, when selection among referential forms is required, selection time becomes a potential confound because the observed planning time reflects both the retrieval speed of the antecedent representations and referential form selection. We assume that referential form selection is a process similar to lemma selection, in which speakers select a lemma from multiple activated lemmas. Under a competitive view of lemma selection (Levelt et al., 1999), as adopted in Bader and Portele (2025; but see Mahon et al., 2007 for a non-competitive view), the most activated lemma will be chosen, and the time for selection here is determined by the activation of the target form and its competitors. If the competitors are strong, lemma selection will become slower. Under an activation-based account of referential form choice, since discourse prominence makes the conceptual representation of the referent and the linguistic representation of the antecedent more activated, this may in turn allocate more activation to the pronoun lemma, leading to the more frequent pronoun use when referring to these antecedents. However, it is unclear how activation is distributed among all candidate lemmas, and thus how the selection time reflects the activation of the referent. Principle C minimizes this competition because alternative referential forms are grammatically unavailable, which allows planning speed to provide a cleaner measure of antecedent activation.

Lastly, after the pronoun lemma is selected, the speaker selects the corresponding lexeme and phonologically encodes it for articulation. While the activation of the chosen lemma flows to its lexeme and phonological form, and word form access can affect production latency (Jescheniak & Levelt, 1994; Levelt et al., 1999), we do not expect these later processes to be substantially affected by discourse prominence because the same pronoun form is produced across conditions. Lexeme selection and phonological encoding contribute to overall planning speed but should not substantially alter any effect of discourse prominence arising from antecedent retrieval.

Taken together, we argue that planning speed is a feasible measure of relative antecedent activation when Principle C minimizes the confound in planning time from referential form choice and other contributors to planning speed are kept constant. According to the proposed processing model, the activation level of the referent is a major source of variation in planning speed when Principle C applies, and the relationship between discourse prominence and antecedent activation, if any, should hold in both the single-sentence constrained context and the two-sentence unconstrained context.

2.2. Experiment paradigm

Since the antecedent and the co-referring expression are in the same sentence but different clauses in our stimuli, we implemented the picture description task by separating the two clauses onto two screens and presenting them one-by-one. The first screen introduces the characters and their names, as well as the verb used in the item (Figure 1a). Participants were asked to familiarize themselves with the characters and start to structure the first clause of the sentence using the given verb ("Gary informed Anna that…"), but not yet speak it aloud. The second screen (Figure 1b) then replaces the first screen. It has the same setting as the first screen, except that it includes a "speak-aloud" icon and the content of the second clause. The content of the second clause includes the figure of the character and a relevant event in the text as the predicate of the target clause. Participants were asked to produce the whole sentence ("Gary informed Anna that he got the job") as soon as possible once they saw the "speak-aloud" icon.

We chose this split-clause design to minimize the planning of the first clause from affecting our measure, which measures planning that occurs before the second clause. By varying the verb in the two screens and the referent in the second screen, we could manipulate the properties of antecedents that participants refer to and see how they affect the planning speed of the pronoun. The two screens seen by participants in the experiment for an item (left: 1a; right: 1b); Target response: Gary informed Anna that he got the job.

Participants' speech was recorded. We manually checked the referential form used in the second clause and extracted three intervals from the speech using Praat (Boersma & Weenink, 2026): the duration of the name of the second character (N2), the duration of the pre-pronoun complementizer (that), and the duration of the pronoun itself (pro). We mainly considered the duration of the pre-pronoun complementizer as our critical measure reflecting our process of interest (the activation of conceptual representations of antecedent), assuming that the planning of the upcoming referent is done shortly before it is articulated. Evidence that pronoun planning difficulty can be reflected in speech timing immediately before a pronoun comes from Kandel et al. (2024). They measured whether speakers produced a silent gap between the preceding verb and a pronoun, and considered this measure as evidence that interference during pronounnumber agreement can delay production before the pronoun is articulated. Although their measure concerns the presence of a silent gap rather than word duration, their findings suggest that processing difficulty associated with an upcoming pronoun can be reflected locally in the immediately preceding speech interval. In the present study, we examine the pre-pronoun duration as a measure of local planning difficulty.

The results of Experiment 1 were used only to identify complementizer duration as a potentially informative pre-pronoun measure; they do not independently establish that this measure reflects antecedent activation. In Experiment 1, we also analyzed the total duration of the complementizer and the preceding name (e.g., the duration of "Mary that" in "Gary informed Mary that") because it was not a priori clear which pre-pronoun regions would best reflect the process of interest. Thus, we should be cautious in interpreting the results of Experiment 1 due to the multiple comparison problem. However, the analysis of all subsequent experiments, including the replication of Experiment 1 (Experiment 4) was decided before the data collection, based on the results of Experiment 1.

The duration of the complementizer is measured from the offset of the preceding word (the name) to the onset of the upcoming word (the pronoun). For example, in "Gary informed Anna that he got the job," the duration is measured from the offset of "Anna" to the onset of "he." While multiple ways of partitioning this interval are possible, the present approach was selected a priori, as no prior work establishes an optimal method. As this partition worked well in the first experiment, we adopted the same approach in the subsequent experiments.

Also, the complementizer "that" is optional (Ferreira & Dell, 2000). To take nocomplementizer trials into account, we explored other measures, such as using a longer interval of name + complementizer (if it was produced) as the critical measure for all trials. Across all four experiments, these measures exhibited the same trend with the pre-pronoun interval that excludes the trials without a complementizer. For consistency, we report results only based on trials with a complementizer. Importantly, we did not observe a systematic relationship between complementizer omission and planning latency.

2.3.Stimuli design

In all four experiments, we elicited responses in which speakers were strongly encouraged to use pronouns due to Principle C, as in "[Character A] informed [Character B] that he/she [the informed event]." Referent predictability has been argued to influence form choice (e.g., Arnold, 1998 Arnold, , 2001;; Arnold & Zerkle, 2019). It is in principle possible that our manipulations affect referent predictability, potentially confounding our design. To address this concern, we conducted an independent off-line forced-choice norming study for each experiment to measure referent predictability for each stimulus. Participants were asked to read incomplete sentences like "Gary informed Anna that ____ got the job", and select the more probable referent between the two choices provided, e.g., "he (Gary)" or "she (Anna)". This measure provides an estimate of the predictability of each referent given the context in each condition across all experiments.

2.5. General analytic approach

The same general analysis procedure was used across all four experiments, unless otherwise noted. We fit Bayesian mixed-effects regression using the R (v4.2.3; R core team, 2023) package brms (Bürkner, 2017). We used a logistic regression model for binary outcomes in the off-line forced-choice experiments that measure referent predictability of the stimuli used in the main experiment. We also implemented a linear regression model with a log-normal distribution for the pre-pronoun complementizer interval measured in the main experiment.

All models were fitted in the maximal random effect structure justified by design (Barr et al., 2013). They were fitted using 10 chains, each with 4000 iterations. The first 1000 were warm-up to calibrate the sampler. This results in 30000 posterior samples. Priors were specified as follows: Logistic regression models were specified with a weakly informative prior using the Cauchy distribution with center 0 and scale 2.5 for intercepts. For lognormal linear regression models that analyze the length of intervals, the prior for intercepts was 𝒩 (5.5,0.5). This reflected the expectation that interval durations would be centered at approximately 245 ms, with a 95% prior interval of approximately [101, 721] ms. For all other parameters, we selected weakly informative priors. For populational-level fixed effects, the prior was 𝒩 (0,0.5). The prior for sigma and the standard deviations of the group-level random effects (SD) was 𝒩 (0,0.1) and 𝒩 (0,0.5) respectively. Lastly, for both logistic and linear regression models, the prior on the correlation matrix was a LKJ regularizing prior (Lewandowski et al., 2009) with the shape parameter nu = 2. Setting its shape parameter to 2.0 downweighs extreme correlation values like ±1. We verified the convergence of the model by ensuring that there were no divergent transitions, all the Rhats were close to one, the number of effective sample size was at least 10% of the number of post-warmup samples, and visually inspected the traceplots to ensure that the chains were mixed properly.

We draw inferences from the statistical models by considering the 89% highest density interval (HDI) of the posterior distribution, and its relation to a region of practical equivalence (ROPE) around the null value (Kruschke, 2014; Kruschke, 2018), i.e., the HDI+ROPE decision rule. The 89% HDI is considered because it is argued to be more stable than the 95% frequentist convention (Kruschke, 2014). When the entire ROPE of a parameter value lies outside the 89% HDI of the posterior distribution of that parameter, the null hypothesis is rejected.

Since this study examines a novel measure that does not have any prior references for its effect size, we follow the default setting of ROPE suggested in Kruschke (2018) to define the ROPE, i.e., a range from -0.1 to 0.1 of a standardized parameter for linear models, and ±0.1× π/√3 for logistic regression. This is also the default setting in the R package bayestestR (Makowski et al., 2019). For each analysis, we report the posterior estimates, the 89% HDI, and the percentage of the 89% HDI that falls into the ROPE.

Previous studies using offline (untimed) completion tasks found that speakers used more pronouns for subject than non-subject antecedents, and passive subject antecedents than active subject antecedents, likely due to the higher topicality associated with passive subjects (Kaiser et al., 2011; Rohde & Kehler, 2014). Given that this is because establishing the non-agent as the topic is one of the primary functions of English passives (Shibatani, 1985), we assume that passive subjects are also more prominent in the single-sentence context we used. Assuming that these factors increase the use of pronouns due to their effects on the discourse status (i.e., topicality) of the antecedent, we expect that the passive subject antecedent is the most prominent antecedent in the discourse, followed by the active subject antecedent, then the active object and the passive object antecedent.

Although the subject and passive subject biases were established in a multiple-sentence context, which is different from our stimuli, we argue that discourse prominence established there can still be extended to our stimuli, because factors that have been argued to contribute to the discourse prominence associated with subjects are still preserved in the present stimuli. This set of factors include but not limit to topicality (e.g., Rohde & Kehler, 2014; Lam & Hwang, 2022), grammatical subjecthood and/or first-mention (e.g., Kaiser & Trueswell, 2008; Fukumura & van Gompel, 2015), and parallelism to the grammatical position of the upcoming referent (e.g., Stevenson et al., 1995). In our stimuli, the subject is the first-mentioned grammatical subject that is parallel to the position of the upcoming referent. Although the topicality of the subject is less clear in the active sentence, this discourse function is elicited by the passive construction (Davison, 1984; Gundel & Fretheim, 2004; Shibatani, 1985). Thus, despite the difference in sentence structure, the factors associated with subject prominence continue to favor the subject antecedent in the present stimuli.

If discourse prominence of an antecedent can be reduced to cognitive prominence of the antecedent in speakers' minds, as predicted by the strong version of the activation-based account, then the planning speed of a pronoun should reflect the discourse prominence of the antecedent it is referring to. Specifically, pronoun planning should be faster when it refers to a preceding subject than to a preceding non-subject, yielding a main effect of Antecedent Position (subject vs. non-subject). In addition, given the topicality effect on the choice of referential form, planning a pronoun referring to a passive subject antecedent should also be faster than planning one referring to an active subject antecedent. This should result in an interaction effect between Voice (active vs. passive) and Antecedent Position, with a larger effect size in the passive than active voice.

3.1. Methods

>Methods

3.1.1. Participants

Fifty-six self-reported monolingual English native speakers were recruited via Prolific Academic in exchange for USD2.5. Participants were filtered with the screener function of the Prolific platform to ensure that they only speak English, were raised only in English and have no language-related disorder. The same set of screeners was used for all experiments and informed consent was obtained for all participants in four experiments. The experiment lasted about 15 minutes. Eight participants were excluded for providing no audio data, being non-native speakers, misunderstanding the instructions, and using full expression violating Principle C. We excluded those who consistently produced responses that violated Principle C, because it is ambiguous whether these participants genuinely lack something like Principle C in their grammar, or just misunderstood the instructions and thus treated the two clauses as two separate sentences. This left 48 participants for analysis. We chose our sample size with reference to Zerkle et al. (2017), who examined referent name duration using a 2 × 2 design with 24 participants and 53 items. Because no effect-size estimate was available for the present manipulation, we adopted a more conservative target by doubling their participant sample and recruiting 48 participants.

3.1.2. Materials

We constructed 24 sets of experiment items akin to Figure 1, targeting responses in the frame shown in (4). Across conditions within each item, the visual stimuli differed only in the grammatical voice shown in the two screens ("informed" or "was informed by") and in the figure of the intended referent in the second screen.

The first screen displayed two gender-different characters alongside the verb "informed" or "was informed by." The two characters were always assigned a stereotypically male name and a stereotypically female name to ensure an unambiguous reference. The character on the left always corresponded to the subject of the first clause, and the order of gender was counterbalanced across items. An empty speech bubble, which indicates the existence of the second clause, was also included. The speech bubble always pointed to the informer. The second screen further depicted the informed event that involves either the subject or the non-subject character of the preceding clause. The informed event was always transitive, with the intended referent as the subject and the only character in the second clause. The figure of the intended antecedent, and the content of the event were provided in the speech bubble. In the end, participants were expected to produce sentences in the frame of "[Character 1] informed/was informed by [Character 2] that he/she [verb + direct object]".

The 24 experiment items were mixed with 16 fillers, for a total of 40 items. All fillers were in the same format as experimental items, except that the figure of the intended antecedent was at the object position of the second clause (e.g., Sam informed Mayi that Eva complained about heri). Like the experimental items, the filler items varied in the position of the pronoun's antecedent, but this position was fixed within each item and counterbalanced across items.

Stimuli were distributed across four lists according to a Latin Square design. Each list had a fixed pseudo-random order and was presented to all participants assigned to that list. No three experimental items were presented consecutively. Participants were randomly assigned to one of the four lists, so each of them was exposed to each item in only one condition, but encountered all conditions across different items.

3.1.4. Procedure

Experiments were run online using the platform Cognition.run (http://www.cognition.run/). Before the main experiment, participants were familiarized with the apparatus, two practice trials that include a reminder before the second screen for the timing to start the speech, and two practice trials that are in the same format as the experimental items. In the practice trials, the target sentence was given at the end of each trial to allow participants to compare their responses with the intended output. All these target sentences provided after the trials included the complementizer "that," although participants were not explicitly instructed to produce it.

On each trial, participants began with the first screen displaying two characters and the target verb form (e.g., "informed" or "was informed by") for 2500 ms without speaking. After a 1000 ms fixation cross, the second screen appeared, displaying additional details about the informed event for 6000ms. Participants were instructed to produce a complete sentence while their speech was recorded via microphone. Trials were separated by 1,000 ms fixation crosses. We chose this presentation rate because participants were not able to finish their speech at a faster presentation rate used in the pilot studies.

3.3. Discussion

Experiment 1 tested the effect of grammatical voice and grammatical function of the antecedent on antecedent activation by looking at their effect on the planning latency of the co-referring pronoun. Given the subject bias and the passive subject bias in pronoun use, which are supposed to reflect a higher level of discourse prominence associated with subject and passive subject antecedents, we expected that speakers would be faster at planning the pronoun referring to these antecedents, if discourse prominent antecedents are also cognitively more prominent in the memory. Contrary to the prediction, we found that speakers planned the co-referring pronouns faster when their antecedents were inside by-phrases than when the antecedents were the subject, but only in the passive conditions. Assuming the planning speed of the co-referring pronoun as an indicator of antecedent activation, this finding suggests that subject antecedents, or more generally topical antecedents, are not necessarily more highly activated in memory. In other words, entities that are prominent in the discourse are not always also easier to access in the memory of speakers. This provides evidence against the strong activation-based account.

An immediate question is why pronouns were produced faster when their antecedents were inside by-phrases than when they were the subject of passive sentences. One possibility is that some factors make the by-phrase argument the more prominent entity in the discourse compared to the subject, and thus more activated under the activation-based accounts. There are two potential factors that may promote its discourse prominence. First, it may be that the referents introduced as the object of the by-preposition in passives are more informative, because the passive by-phrase is typically optional, meaning that the explicit use of by-phrase promotes the informativity of the noun phrase inside it (see Levy, 2008 for optional elements being more informative). This additional unexpected information has been found to increases a referent's subsequent topicality. Brocher et al. (2018) found that brand-new referents were more likely than contextually inferable referents to be mentioned again and promoted to aboutness-topic position.

In particular, inferred and uniquely identifiable referents, which were maximally expected in their contexts, showed the lowest referential persistence and topic-shift potential. They suggest this is because unexpected referents are perceived as more noteworthy and therefore attract continued attention in subsequent discourse. This informativity-based account predicts that referents introduced by an optional phrase are more informative and probably more prominent in the discourse, and thus generally easier to retrieve. We examined this informativity-based account in Experiment 2.

Second, the object of the by-phrase in passives may be prominent in the discourse because the logophoricity gives the discourse participant a privileged status when it is the source of information, because the discourse is represented from their perspective (Sells, 1987). This perspective-based account predicts that pronouns co-referring to an antecedent introduced as the source of information (subjects in actives, prepositional objects in passives) should be planned faster than pronouns co-referring to an antecedent introduced as the perceiver of information (objects in actives, subjects in passives). Crucially, this account does not predict that the source thematic role in transfer-of-possession contexts is more activated. That is, "Gary" in "Gary gave a book to Anna" should not be more activated than the goal "Anna", consistent with previous findings that a goal character is more predictable and referred to by more pronouns (e.g., Arnold, 1998 Arnold, , 2001;; Rosa & Arnold, 2017). We did see evidence for an interaction effect consistent with this source-of-information advantage, though the pairwise comparison for active conditions revealed unclear evidence. Thus, we believe this explanation should be tested further.

Experiment 4 tested this perspective-based account.

7. General Discussion

Under the strong version of the activation-based account, more discourse prominent entities should be more highly activated in the speaker's memory, at least under the speaker oriented conception of prominence. Assuming that more activated items in memory are retrieved and hence produced faster, this account should predict that pronouns whose antecedents are more discourse prominent should be planned faster, all else being equal. The current results are not consistent with this prediction. Instead, pronouns referring to nonsubject antecedents in passives were planned faster than those referring to subject antecedents, which were assumed to be more discourse prominent. Experiments 2 and 4 further examined two properties of passive by-phrase arguments, i.e., optionality and source-of-information perspectival status, which might contribute to their discourse prominence, but neither was accompanied by the predicted planning advantage.

Thus, even if these properties made non-subjects more prominent, the results provide no evidence that greater discourse prominence leads to greater activation. This raises two questions: why were the by-phrase argument more activated, and how should we characterize the notion of discourse prominence, if not in memory terms?

There are two potential candidates to account for the non-subject advantage. The first one is recency. Proposals of memory architecture in language processing often emphasize the importance of recency in determining the activation of a linguistic item (e.g., Lewis & Vasishth, 2005; Lewis et al., 2006; Engelmann et al., 2019), such that a more recent item is more highly activated. In our stimuli, because by-phrase antecedents in passive sentences are both linearly and hierarchically closer to pronouns compared to subject antecedents, by-phrase antecedents may be retrieved faster when producing pronouns referring back to them. Another possible factor is the agentive properties associated with the by-phrase object. Both active subjects and passive objects of "informed" satisfy most of the proto-agent features proposed by Dowty (1991). It is possible that agency plays a special role in the human cognitive system, as others have suggested (e.g., New et al. 2007 a.o), and entities bearing the agent role are retrieved faster. Agency would also explain the interaction observed in Experiment 2. The by-phrase introduces the agent of the event, whereas the temporal before-phrase introduces no agent, so an advantage for the prepositional object is expected in by-passives but not before-passives, which is the pattern we obtained. However, planning speed in active sentences alone provided no clear evidence that agent (subject) antecedents were retrieved faster than patient (object) antecedents, which appears inconsistent with a straightforward agency-based prediction. This leads to a third possibility of a combined effect: Recency may play a small additive effect or an interactive effect with agency.

To evaluate these possibilities more directly, we analyze the aggregated data from four experiments and recoded the independent variables based on their agency and recency. All Kehler, 2002). Under this view, resolving referential ambiguity given a pronoun in comprehension is a part of the process of establishing a coherence relation between utterances using world knowledge. An analogous approach has recently been proposed for production. Arnold and Nozari (2017) proposed the discourse connectedness account, which suggests that referential form is a linguistic indicator of discourse connectedness. Under this view, speakers are more likely to use pronouns when the discourse is more strongly connected. In line with this account, they found that speakers used more pronouns when they took longer to consider the fit between the prior context and the upcoming discourse and when they used connectors such as "and" between sentences. In line with this idea, Arnold and Zerkle (2019; P.19-20) discussed that pronouns may serve not only to identify a referent but also to signal connectivity between the current utterance and the preceding discourse. On this view, pronoun use contributes to discourse coherence and the establishment of coherence relations. It is possible that a referent is prominent in the discourse because continuing the discourse with it helps maintain or promote the coherence, which also encourages pronoun use. This does not necessarily mean that it is more activated in the memory. A factor may affect the likelihood of how referring to a referent maintains discourse coherence, without necessarily affecting its activation; or it affects the two properties in different ways.

An immediate question this claim raises is how such a mechanism could be implemented within the processing model. We adopt the assumption that discourse prominence is encoded as a feature at the conceptual representation, as in both Schmitt et al. (1999) and Bader and Portele (2025)'s proposals, the two existing models concerning referential form production. Under this idea, discourse prominence can affect referential form choice through feature matching between the discourse-status feature and the function associated with a given pronoun lemma, which increases the pronoun lemma's own activation, but not affecting the referent's activation that our central claim concerns. This is consistent with the broader idea that referential form choice is constrained by form-function matching, which has been proposed to account for the distribution of multiple reduced expressions within a language (see Arnold & Zerkle, 2019, p. 19). Under this view, a matching feature increases the activation of the corresponding pronoun lemma. Yet, the increase in lemma activation is not expected to produce a corresponding planning-speed facilitation in our manipulation. As established in Section 2.1, Principle C minimizes competition of all other referential forms, so lemma selection proceeds at its fastest attainable rate, leaving little variation in planning time attributable to additional activation of the pronoun lemma. When multiple forms are available and if the activation of other competitor lemmas remains constant, we should expect a planning facilitation for more prominent referents in the discourse.

This account may explain why subjecthood and topichood facilitate pronoun use, but did not affect antecedent activation in Experiment 1: Discourse is more likely to be coherent when the speaker continues talking about the same topic, which is often encoded as the preceding subject (Reinhart, 1981). Under the current explanation, this could be because subjecthood and topichood are among the features that determine which entities are typically continued as topics in coherent discourse, and thus match the function associated with the pronoun lemma. However, being a subject or a topic does not make the conceptual or linguistic representation of the referent more activated, leading to the lack of planning advantage associated with pronouns referring to subject, especially passive subject antecedents found in Experiment 1. The coherence-based account also explains why informativity of the optional argument (tested in Experiment 2) and the source-of-information perspective associated with the by-phrase argument (tested in Experiment 4) did not have a clear effect on antecedent activation. Even if these factors contribute to discourse prominence, there is no guarantee that they also facilitate retrieval of conceptual and linguistic representations of the referent. Agency, by contrast, is not proposed to act through this discourse-status pathway. Rather than modulating referential form selection via feature matching, agency is hypothesized to directly affect the activation of the antecedent's own representation, consistent with the planning-speed advantage we in fact observed for agentive antecedents.

8. Conclusion

Despite being a central notion in discourse processing, it remains unclear whether discourse prominence can be conceptualized in terms of memory activation. The present study tested the straightforward prediction that entities that are prominent in the discourse, as indicated by a preference for pronoun use, should also be more highly activated and therefore retrieved more quickly during reference production. Four experiments were conducted to compare the planning speed of syntactically required pronouns across antecedents associated with different discourse statuses. The results suggest that discourse prominence does not necessarily correspond to greater activation. Instead, the one factor that did modulate planning speed, i.e., agency, is not itself a discourse-status property, indicating that the measure was sensitive to activation differences while those differences did not track discourse prominence. Thus, discourse prominence is unlikely to be expressible entirely in memory activation terms. As a tentative alternative, we suggest that discourse prominence may partly reflect how continued reference to an entity contributes to discourse coherence, a function that may guide pronoun use without necessarily facilitating antecedent retrieval.

Bayes-factor comparisons show very to extremely strong evidence (BF10 > 30) favoring the agency model over the recency, additive, and interaction models, indicating that agency provided the best-supported explanation of the latency pattern. Adding recency, either independently or conditionally on agency, did not improve the account. However, this does not necessarily argue against a role for recency in base activation. The two antecedents in our materials were relatively close to each other, which may have made the recency contrast too small to produce a detectable difference in activation.

The agency account is compatible with the null effects found across the experiments.

Agency is not a discourse-status property, as it is determined by the event structure of the verb rather than by how a referent has been treated in the preceding discourse. By constrast, all factors we manipulated in the four experiments are candidate sources of discourse prominence, i.e., subjecthood and topicality, informativity, and source-of-information status. While agency is unlikely to be the only factor that contributes to antecedent activation more generally, the support for the agency account contrasts with the consistent absence of effects from the discourse prominence manipulations. With other relevant factors held constant as in our task, this pattern suggests that agency is an important source of variation in antecedent activation, whereas discourse prominence plays a relatively limited role. This supports the idea that the factors driving variation in antecedent activation are at least partly distinct from those that determine discourse prominence.

A remaining question associated with the agency account is how it affects activation of conceptual and linguistic representation of the referent in the proposed processing model. We suggest that, like recency, agency may contribute to the base activation of these representations. This possibility is consistent with broader proposals that base activation can reflect factors beyond recency. Engelmann et al. (2019), for example, propose that prominence associated with subjecthood can contribute to base activation in addition to recency. If agency is an important source of the activation advantage associated with subjecthood in the present task, its effect can likewise be incorporated into our proposed model as an additional contributor to base activation.
