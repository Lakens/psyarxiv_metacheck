Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 3 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Full text

Abstract

People spend much of their lives in built environments, yet methods for evaluating how these environments are experienced remain limited. This research reports the first multi-study application of the NeuroDesign/NeuroArchitecture Index (NDIX), a multidimensional measure of environmental experience, and examines whether it is sensitive to meaningful differences between standard and environmentally enriched indoor spaces. Study 1 used a randomised repeated-measures crossover design in which 42 participants evaluated a standard quiet room and an enriched redesign of the same space. Study 2 (N = 40) evaluated standard and enriched innovation environments using complementary repeated-measures (n = 19) and independent-groups (n = 20 per group) comparisons. In both settings the instrument was administered in full in a single short session. In Study 1, NDIX Overall was higher in the enriched environment than in the standard environment (dz = 1.83), with higher scores across all six domains after false discovery rate correction and an equivalent effect in both presentation orders. Internal consistency was good for NDIX Overall (α = .87 and .89) but variable across domains (α = .40 to .77). Free-text descriptions of the most salient change centred on green plants, artwork and layout. Study 2 reproduced the pattern, with NDIX Overall higher in the enriched environment both within participants (dz = 2.50) and between independent groups (d = 1.90). The findings indicate that the NDIX is practical to administer and sensitive to meaningful environmental variation, while further psychometric evaluation is required across populations, environmental contexts, and more subtly differing settings.

1. Introduction

To understand how environments influence cognitive and emotional wellbeing, the concept of enriched environments offers valuable insights. Previous studies in humans and animal models demonstrate that exposure to complex, sensory-rich settings promotes neurogenesis and neural plasticity, particularly in areas such as the hippocampus and prefrontal cortex (Diamond, Krech, & Rosenzweig, 1964; Eriksson et al., 1998; Fuchs & Flügge, 2014; Kempermann, 2019; Kempermann, Kuhn, & Gage, 1997; Kühn et al., 2017; Diamond et al., 1972; Diamond, 2001; Spalding et al., 2013; van Praag, Kempermann, & Gage, 2000).

Similarly, theories of embedded and embodied cognition suggest that cognitive and emotional processes are fundamentally shaped by physical and sensory interactions with the environment (Gath-Morad et al., 2024; Zisch, Gage, & Spiers, 2014; Djebbara et al., 2019; Djebbara, Fich, & Gramann, 2021; Valentine et al., 2024). Environments that promote sensory and bodily engagement have been associated with enhanced effective connectivity and regulatory influence between the amygdala and prefrontal cortex, regions crucial for emotional regulation (Barbas & García-Cabezas, 2016; Barrett et al., 2007). In sum, biophilic and enriched environments may support emotional regulation, stress-reduction, interoceptive awareness, cognitive flexibility, and mental health. This body of work motivates the study of environmental quality, but it also makes plain how far the available evidence sits from the decisions designers actually make. Much of it derives from animal models, from tightly controlled laboratory exposures, or from neural measures obtained under conditions unlike ordinary occupancy. It establishes that environments matter to the brain; it does not establish how a given room, street, or building is experienced by the people in it, nor does it supply a way of recording that experience so that one setting can be compared with another. The present studies address the second of these problems rather than the first: they concern the measurement of environmental experience, and make no claim about underlying neural or physiological mechanisms.

1.1. NeuroArchitecture and NeuroDesign

The emerging interdisciplinary fields known as neuroarchitecture and neurodesign explore the intersection of how the brain reacts and responds to architecture and design, offering new perspectives for implementing solutions that can profoundly enhance built environments (Coburn, Vartanian, & Chatterjee, 2017; Eberhard, 2009; Guite, Clark, & Ackrill, 2006; Kirk, Skov, Hulme, Christensen, & Zeki, 2009; Mallgrave, 2010; Pallasmaa, 2024; Sternberg & Wilson, 2006; Sjövall & Spiers, 2024; Valentine et al., 2025; Vartanian et al., 2013; Zisch, Gage, & Spiers, 2014; Zisch et al., 2024). A key aim in these fields is to test real-world environments that account for a comprehensive and holistic approach including embodied cognition, complementing laboratory studies and focusing on how spaces can balance risk and resilience factors while supporting human, ecological, and economic sustainability. One dimension of neuroarchitecture and neurodesign is the recognition of how built environments impact mental health, which has led to a focus on biophilic design, grounded in theories that explore an intrinsic connection to nature (Sjövall & Spiers, 2024). The following theoretical frameworks, including the Biophilia Hypothesis (Kellert & Wilson, 1993), evolutionary perspectives, and established models of cognitive and emotional restoration, provide insight into how biophilic design can enhance wellbeing by addressing human psychological and physiological needs. Despite growing evidence that built environments influence human experience, there remains a need for multidimensional methods capable of detecting meaningful differences across controlled and real-world settings. Existing approaches capture parts of this. Building certification schemes such as WELL, LEED, and BREEAM assess provision and compliance, but record what a building contains rather than how it is experienced. Environmental audit instruments inventory physical features reliably and at scale, yet are similarly designed around presence rather than perception. Single-construct scales of perceived restorativeness, environmental preference, or satisfaction capture one dimension of experience well, but cannot indicate whether two settings differ in safety, sensory quality, emotional tone, cognitive ease, social affordance, or naturalness, nor which of these a design change has altered. What is missing is an instrument that represents environmental experience as a differentiated profile across theoretically motivated dimensions while remaining brief enough to administer in situ. The NDIX was developed to meet that requirement.

1.2. Aims and hypotheses

The primary aim was to examine whether the NDIX was sensitive to meaningful differences between standard and environmentally enriched indoor environments. Two hypotheses were tested: 1.

NDIX Overall scores would be higher for environmentally enriched environments than for standard comparison environments.

2. NDIX scores would be higher in the enriched environments in the domains most directly targeted by the enrichment (Sensory, Emotional, Cognitive and Naturalness), but not necessarily in the Social or Safety and Accessibility domains, which the modifications were not designed to change.

2. Methods

The present research comprised two studies evaluating whether the NeuroDesign and NeuroArchitecture Index (NDIX) was sensitive to meaningful differences between standard and environmentally enriched indoor environments. Study 1 used a repeated-measures design in which the same participants evaluated a standard quiet room and an enriched redesign of the same space. Study 2 combined repeated-measures and independent-groups comparisons of standard and enriched innovation environments. Across both studies, the primary outcome was the NDIX Overall score. Scores for the six NDIX domains were examined as secondary outcomes, and participants' free-text descriptions of the most salient environmental change were analysed alongside them. Across both studies, the environmental contrasts were primarily characterised by differences in biophilic design, alongside related sensory, aesthetic, and comfort features. These features are described to characterise the environments rather than as individually tested manipulations. The enriched environments were created within existing university spaces, redesigned by university students in line with the biophilic design literature outlined above and subject to practical constraints such as budget and the fixed structural shell of each room. Both studies also collected physiological and behavioural measures that address separate research questions and are reported elsewhere; the present article concerns the NDIX alone.

2.1.1. Study 1

The standard condition was a pre-existing quiet room in the Division of Psychology and Language Sciences, University College London (26 Bedford Way), designated as a space for quiet contemplation. 2.2. NeuroDesign and NeuroArchitecture Index

The NeuroDesign and NeuroArchitecture Index (NDIX) is an 88-item measure developed to assess perceived experience of the built environment. It was created in response to the limited availability of multidimensional methods for evaluating how architectural and environmental qualities are experienced (Sjövall et al., 2025). The conceptual framework was informed by interdisciplinary literature and expert judgement spanning neuroscience, psychology, physiology, architecture, design, and sensory processing (Chatterjee, 2014; Damasio, 1994; Evans, 2003; Jeffery & Zisch, 2022; Pallasmaa, 2024; Sjövall & Spiers, 2024; Spence, 2020; Vigliocco et al., 2024). The NDIX comprises six domains: Safety and Accessibility, Sensory, Emotional, Cognitive, Social, and Naturalness. Together, these domains are intended to capture complementary dimensions of environmental experience.

Safety and Accessibility includes 11 items and assesses perceived security, ease of navigation, autonomy, and inclusivity. Feeling safe is important for wellbeing and is closely linked to stress and comfort, while accessibility supports independence and participation across diverse populations (Spiers & Maguire, 2006 , 2007; Maslow, 1943; Molloy, Knight, & Woodfield, 2003).

Sensory includes 16 items and examines how the environment is experienced through visual, auditory, tactile, and other sensory qualities. Sensory experience is central to environmental perception and may influence wellbeing and cognitive functioning. Features such as natural elements and comfortable lighting have been associated with mood and stress-related outcomes (Pallasmaa, 2024; Tomchek & Dunn, 2007; Boubekri et al., 2014; Uvnäs Moberg et al., 2022).

Emotional includes 29 items and assesses the emotional tone and affective experience associated with the environment. Emotional processes play an important role in how people interpret and interact with their surroundings, and positively experienced environments may be associated with wellbeing and lower perceived stress and anxiety (Chatterjee & Vartanian, 2016; Damasio, 1996; Damasio & Damasio, 2024; Spence & Driver, 2004; Spence, 2011; Spence et al., 2014; Bratman et al., 2015).

Cognitive includes 14 items and examines perceived support for attention, concentration, creativity, curiosity, and problem-solving. Environmental experience has been linked to cognitive functioning, while spaces that foster curiosity and creativity may support cognitive flexibility (Clemenson, Deng, & Gage, 2015; Nilsson et al., 1999; Kempermann, Kuhn, & Gage, 1997; Kounios & Beeman, 2015).

Social includes eight items and assesses how the environment supports or constrains social interaction, belonging, and community. Social connection is important for psychological wellbeing, and the built environment can influence opportunities for interaction and participation (Gehl & Svarre, 2013; Gehl, 2011; Uvnäs Moberg et al., 2022).

Naturalness includes 10 items and evaluates the presence and experiential quality of natural elements, materials, forms, and connections to nature. Exposure to natural environments has been associated with restorative outcomes, including reduced stress and improved mood, while biophilic design incorporates natural materials, views, patterns, and forms that may contribute to psychological and physiological wellbeing (Hartig et al., 2014; Kellert & Calabrese, 2015; Ryan et al., 2014; Ulrich, 1983 Ulrich, , 1984;; Ulrich et al., 1991; Ulrich & Parsons, 1992; Zhong, Schröder, & Bekkering, 2022; Kellert, Heerwagen, & Mador, 2008).

Items were rated on a 5-point response scale. Negatively worded items were reverse-scored before analysis. Item responses were aggregated to produce scores for the six domains and an NDIX Overall score. Scores were expressed as percentages of the maximum possible score, with higher scores indicating a more positively experienced environment. NDIX Overall was treated as the primary outcome, while the six domain scores were examined as secondary outcomes. Internal consistency was assessed using Cronbach's alpha for NDIX Overall and each of the six domains, estimated separately within each participant group or environmental condition. Estimates were not pooled across conditions, because the same participants contributed observations to both.

2.3. Baseline wellbeing

Before the environmental assessment, participants completed the five-item World Health Organization Well-Being Index (WHO-5), a brief self-report measure of subjective psychological wellbeing over the preceding two weeks (Topp et al., 2015). The WHO-5 was collected as a baseline participant characteristic and as a screening measure; it does not index the environments under evaluation. Baseline scores were compared between condition-order groups to check group equivalence, and scores in the two environments were compared alongside the NDIX outcomes.

2.4. Room preference

Participants who evaluated both environments were additionally asked, after their second session, which of the two rooms they preferred. This single forced-choice item was collected as a global preference comparator against which the NDIX Overall difference could be considered, and is reported descriptively.

2.5. Qualitative responses

After completing the NDIX in each session, participants were asked to describe in free text the most salient change in the room since the previous session. Responses were coded using descriptive content analysis into environmental and experiential themes, with a response contributing to more than one theme where it named more than one feature. Theme frequencies were summarised descriptively. Responses were additionally classified by whether they framed the change as the presence of an added feature or as the absence of a feature. The qualitative data were collected to identify which aspects of the environmental contrast participants noticed spontaneously, and were not treated as an independent test of the study hypotheses.

2.6. Study 1: Quiet Room

>Study 1: Quiet Room

2.6.1. Participants

A total of 43 participants aged 18-55 years were recruited through the UCL SONA participant pool and social-media recruitment. One participant was excluded because both of their sessions were recorded under the same environmental condition, leaving no usable within-participant pair. This was a data-recording error and the exclusion was made on that basis alone, before any outcome data were examined. The final sample was N = 42, comprising 32 women and 10 men with a mean age of 25.0 years (SD = 9.40, range 18-55).

Educational background was secondary or further education for 24 participants, college or university for 8, and postgraduate for 10. All participants provided informed consent before participation. Participants were allocated to one of two condition-order groups, enriched-first (n = 21) or standard-first (n = 21), which did not differ in age, t(40) = 0.36, p = .722. The two order groups also did not differ on baseline WHO-5 Well-being Index scores, reported here on the raw 0-25 scale, enriched-first M = 9.86, SD = 3.97; standard-first M = 10.62, SD = 3.96, t(40) = 0.62, p = .538, d = 0.19.

2.6.2. Design

Study 1 included 42 participants and compared a standard quiet room with an enriched quiet room using a randomised repeated-measures crossover design. Each participant evaluated both environmental conditions. Environmental condition was therefore the within-participant independent variable, with two levels: standard and enriched. The primary dependent variable was NDIX Overall, and the six NDIX domain scores were examined as secondary dependent variables.

2.6.3. Procedure

Participants were randomly assigned to experience either the standard or enriched condition first, followed by the alternative condition after a washout period of 1-3 days. During each session, participants spent 3 minutes alone in the room before completing the NDIX in relation to that environment.

2.6.4. Statistical Analysis

Standard and enriched conditions were compared for NDIX Overall and the six NDIX domain scores. Distributional assumptions were examined using Shapiro-Wilk tests on the paired difference scores. Paired-samples t tests were used where the difference distribution was consistent with normality (p ≥ .05), and Wilcoxon signed-rank tests were used where it was not. Because seven outcomes were tested, p values were adjusted using the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate procedure. Standardised mean differences were reported as

Cohen's dz for paired observations, accompanied by 95% confidence intervals. To assess order effects and carryover in the crossover design, each outcome was additionally submitted to a 2 (condition: standard, enriched) × 2 (order: enriched-first, standard-first) mixed analysis of variance, with particular attention to the condition × order interaction.

Internal consistency was assessed using Cronbach's alpha for NDIX Overall and each of the six domains, computed after reverse-scoring the 19 negatively worded items. Because the same participants rated both environments, coefficients were estimated separately within each condition rather than pooled across them, and are reported alongside average inter-item correlations.

2.7. Study 2: Innovation Space

>Study 2: Innovation Space

2.7.1. Participants

A total of N = 40 participants were recruited through the UCL SONA participant pool and social-media recruitment, using the same recruitment approach as in Study 1. The sample comprised 28 women and 12 men. Participants were randomly allocated to Group A (n = 20) or Group B (n = 20), with each group comprising 14 women and six men.

Across the full sample, 15 participants had educational qualifications below bachelor's degree level, nine held a bachelor's degree, and 16 held a postgraduate qualification. All participants provided informed consent before participation.

2.7.2. Design

Study 2 compared a standard environment with an enriched innovation environment using both repeated-measures and independent-groups components. Group A attended two sessions, evaluating the standard environment in Session 1 and the enriched environment in Session 2; Group B attended a single session and evaluated the enriched environment only.

One Group A participant dropped out of the study before Session 2, so the repeated-measures analysis was based on the 19 participants with complete paired observations. That participant's Session 1 data were retained in the independent-groups comparison, which compared first-session assessments from Group A (n = 20) and Group B (n = 20). The primary dependent variable was NDIX Overall, and the six NDIX domain scores were examined as secondary dependent variables.

2.7.3. Procedure

Before the environmental assessment, participants completed a pre-experiment questionnaire including demographic information. During the first session, 20 participants completed the standard condition and 20 completed the enriched condition. In each condition, participants spent 5 min relaxing in the environment, completed a 10-min creativity task, and then completed the NDIX. Participants initially assigned to the standard condition subsequently completed a second session in the enriched condition following the same procedure.

Participants initially assigned to the enriched condition did not complete a second standardcondition session. NDIX Overall and domain scores were expressed as percentages of their respective maximum possible scores.

2.7.4. Statistical Analysis

Two complementary analytical approaches were used. First, paired-samples t tests compared the standard and enriched conditions among the 19 participants with complete observations in both conditions. Separate tests were conducted for NDIX Overall and each of the six NDIX domains: Safety and Accessibility, Sensory, Emotional, Cognitive, Social, and Naturalness.

Wilcoxon signed-rank tests were conducted as confirmatory non-parametric analyses.

Parametric tests were used as the primary analyses, with non-parametric tests retained as confirmatory throughout. Between-groups comparisons used Welch's t tests, which do not assume homogeneity of variance between the Session 1 groups.

Second, first-session scores were compared between Group A (standard; n = 20) and Group B (enriched; n = 20) using Welch's t tests, with Mann-Whitney U tests as confirmatory nonparametric analyses. Standardised mean differences were reported as Cohen's d; for paired comparisons, effect sizes were calculated as Cohen's dz . Internal consistency was assessed using Cronbach's alpha for NDIX Overall and each of the six domains, estimated separately within each environmental condition.

3.1. Study 1

These studies represent the first full empirical application of the NDIX, with Study 1 extending the pilot evaluation reported in Sjövall et al. (2025). In both the quiet-room and innovation-space settings, and in both the standard and enriched conditions, the instrument was administered in full within a single short session without practical difficulty.

Shapiro-Wilk tests indicated that the paired difference distributions departed from normality for NDIX Overall, Safety and Accessibility, Sensory and Cognitive (all p < .01) but not for Emotional, Social or Naturalness (all p ≥ .48); tests were selected accordingly. The dissociation is informative: the NDIX distinguished the two environments on every dimension while a general measure of subjective wellbeing did not, consistent with the NDIX indexing the experience of a space rather than a downstream change in the respondent's wellbeing after a brief exposure.

3.1.2. Room preference

Asked at the end of the second session which room they preferred, 95.2% of participants chose the enriched environment. Preference therefore tracked the direction of the NDIX Overall difference. Because the item is a single forced choice between two rooms rather than a graded rating of each, it indicates only that the enriched environment was preferred and cannot establish how much of the NDIX Overall difference a graded global preference measure would independently capture.

3.1.3. Internal consistency

Internal consistency estimates for NDIX Overall and the six domains are presented in Table 4, separately for each environmental condition. NDIX Overall showed good internal consistency in both the standard condition, α = .87, and the enriched condition, α = .89.

Across both studies, NDIX Overall was significantly higher in the enriched environments than in the standard environments, with large effect sizes. 4. Discussion

The present investigation examined whether the NeuroDesign and NeuroArchitecture Index (NDIX) was sensitive to theoretically and experientially meaningful differences between standard and environmentally enriched indoor environments. Across two studies conducted in different indoor settings, NDIX Overall consistently distinguished the enriched environments from their standard comparison environments. This pattern was observed in a repeatedmeasures quiet-room study and reproduced in an innovation setting using both withinparticipant and independent-groups comparisons. Together, the findings provide initial evidence that the NDIX is sensitive to substantial differences in perceived environmental experience across controlled indoor contexts.

Both hypotheses were largely supported. NDIX Overall was higher in the enriched environments in both studies, as were the four domains predicted to change: Sensory, Emotional, Cognitive and Naturalness. Naturalness showed the largest absolute difference in Study 1 and among the largest in Study 2, as expected given the explicit biophilic emphasis of the environmental modifications. Because the Naturalness domain indexes the perceived presence of natural elements, and the enrichment consisted substantially of adding such elements, this domain functions in part as a manipulation check and establishes that the intended environmental change was perceptually registered. The pronounced difference in this domain should therefore be read as confirmation that the manipulation was salient, rather than as an independent test of instrument sensitivity. Beyond the predicted domains, Social and Safety and Accessibility scores were also higher in the enriched environments in both studies, although the Social difference was the smallest effect in the Study 2 independentgroups comparison (d = 0.68). The environmental contrast was therefore reflected across all six dimensions of perceived experience, suggesting that a coherent redesign may also change how safe, accessible and socially inviting a space feels, even when these qualities are not targeted directly.

Two prerequisites for a usable instrument were addressed. The first is practical. An 88-item measure invites the objection that it is too long for field use, and the Introduction argued that what is needed is an instrument brief enough to administer in situ. These studies are the first full application of the NDIX, and in both indoor settings it was administered in full within a single short session without practical difficulty, suggesting that its length is not in itself a barrier to use in occupied environments. This is an encouraging first indication rather than a formal evaluation: no measure of respondent burden or acceptability was collected, and a systematic assessment of administration time, completion and user experience across settings remains to be carried out. The second prerequisite is discrimination. Across two different indoor settings, two different procedures and three analytic designs, NDIX Overall separated standard from enriched environments in the predicted direction and with large effects, and in Study 1 it did so equivalently whether participants met the enriched room first or second. The qualitative responses reinforce this reading: participants spontaneously named the specific features that had been introduced or removed, so the quantitative shift corresponds to environmental properties they actually perceived rather than to a diffuse impression of improvement. The prominence of Naturalness is theoretically coherent with the environmental contrast. Both enriched settings incorporated a strong biophilic component, and the particularly pronounced difference in this domain is consistent with literature linking contact with natural elements and environments to restorative, affective, and stress-related responses (Hartig et al., 2014; Ryan et al., 2014; Ulrich, 1983 Ulrich, , 1984;; Ulrich et al., 1991). At the same time, it is reasonable that the observed differences were not restricted to Naturalness. Biophilic and environmentally enriched settings are inherently multisensory and may alter visual, tactile, acoustic, spatial, and affective qualities simultaneously. Natural and enriched environments have been associated with sensory experience and comfort (Pallasmaa, 2024; Spence, 2020), emotional experience and affect (Bratman et al., 2015; Chatterjee & Vartanian, 2016), and attentional and cognitive processes related to restoration, curiosity, and engagement (Hartig et al., 2014; Kounios & Beeman, 2015). Built-environment characteristics may also shape perceptions of safety and accessibility and influence opportunities for social interaction, belonging, and participation (Evans, 2003; Gehl, 2011; Gehl & Svarre, 2013; Uvnäs Moberg et al., 2022). The higher scores across the other NDIX domains are therefore conceptually compatible with the integrated nature of the enrichment rather than being unexpected given the primarily biophilic manipulation.

The qualitative responses provide complementary context for this pattern. Asked what had changed, participants named a narrow and consistent set of features, dominated by green plants and followed by artwork, layout, other natural elements, books, scent and soft furnishings. These are the features the redesign actually introduced, and they map onto the domains that showed the largest quantitative differences. The framing asymmetry is also informative: the enriched room was described in terms of what had been added, whereas the standard room was described in terms of what was missing, in language such as bare, clinical and sterile. Participants therefore encoded the contrast in terms of concrete environmental features rather than global evaluation, which is consistent with the NDIX registering specific properties of the setting. These responses should not, however, be read as demonstrating that particular features caused the observed NDIX differences, since relationships between individual themes and domain scores were not formally tested. This pattern speaks to the multidimensional rationale underlying the NDIX. NDIX Overall provides a global summary of how an environment is experienced, whereas the six domains offer a differentiated profile across Safety and Accessibility, Sensory, Emotional, Cognitive, Social, and Naturalness.

Architectural experience is unlikely to be reducible to a single evaluative dimension and instead arises through interacting perceptual, affective, cognitive, social, and embodied processes (Chatterjee & Vartanian, 2016; Pallasmaa, 2024; Spence & Driver, 2004; Spence, 2020). In the present studies the domains moved together rather than selectively, which is consistent with an environmental manipulation that altered many properties of the setting at once but does not by itself demonstrate that the domains capture separable aspects of experience. Establishing that will require environments that differ on some domains and not others, and a concurrent measure of global environmental preference. Without such a comparator the present data cannot establish how much of the NDIX Overall difference a single-item rating would also capture, so the incremental value of the NDIX over simpler measures remains untested.

The consistency of the NDIX Overall findings is notable given that the studies differed in environmental setting and procedure. Rather than depending on a single room type or comparison, the overall score differentiated standard and enriched environments in both a quiet-room and an innovation-space context. Study 2 further reproduced the same result when the environmental contrast was examined within participants, and when independent groups were compared using first-session observations. Convergence across these two analyses, which draw on partly overlapping observations, strengthens confidence that the principal finding was not specific to a single analytical design. The repeated-measures evidence is particularly informative because each participant serves as their own comparison, thereby reducing the influence of stable between-person differences. The independent-groups comparison in Study 2 is informative for a different and equally important reason.

Participants in that comparison rated only one environment and therefore had no opportunity to infer the environmental contrast or the direction of the expected difference. That the effect persisted under these conditions (d = 1.90) indicates that the findings are not solely attributable to participants' awareness of having been shown two versions of the same space.

Internal consistency provides an additional perspective on the current performance of the NDIX. NDIX Overall showed good to high internal consistency in both studies and both conditions (α = .87 to .95). Domain-level estimates were more variable. In Study 1, Social (α = .40 and .47), Sensory (α = .63 and .42) and Emotional (α = .52 and .66) fell below conventional thresholds in at least one condition, and in Study 2 Sensory was low in the standard condition (α = .33). Emotional was considerably more consistent in Study 2 (α = .91 and .88) than in Study 1, a difference that should be examined at item level together with the weaker domains. The high overall coefficients should not be over-interpreted: Cronbach's alpha increases mechanically with the number of items, so a high value for an 88-item instrument is close to guaranteed and indicates only that the items are not mutually contradictory. Average inter-item correlations in Study 1 are more informative: they were low (.05 to .38), indicating that several domains combine partly distinct aspects of experience, which is useful guidance for item refinement. These findings have several implications for the further development of the NDIX. The principal contribution of the present studies is that the measure distinguished environments designed to differ in theoretically and experientially meaningful ways. The findings suggest that perceived environmental experience can be represented both as an overall evaluation and as a differentiated profile across theoretically relevant dimensions. This distinction may be useful for environmental research and practice because an overall score can indicate whether environments are experienced differently, whilst domain scores may help characterise the nature of those differences.

The findings also reinforce the importance of distinguishing perceived environmental experience from downstream outcomes such as mental health, cognition, or physiological regulation. The NDIX was designed to characterise how environmental qualities are experienced; it is not itself a measure of health or cognitive performance. Previous research indicates that environmental characteristics can be associated with affect, stress, attention, social experience, and wellbeing (Bratman et al., 2015; Evans, 2003; Hartig et al., 2014; Ulrich, 1984), but the present studies did not test whether differences in NDIX scores corresponded to such outcomes. The present work should therefore be regarded as an initial evaluation of sensitivity and feasibility rather than as a full psychometric validation.

Demonstrating that a measure differentiates theoretically meaningful environmental conditions is an important step, but represents only one component of validation. Further work is needed to examine factor structure, item performance, test-retest reliability, convergent and discriminant validity, predictive validity, responsiveness to longitudinal environmental change, and measurement invariance across populations and contexts. The variability observed in some domain-level reliability estimates further reinforces the need for continued item-level evaluation.

Several limitations should nevertheless be considered. First, the environments differed across multiple features, and this was deliberate. Studies that vary a single element, such as artwork (Nanda et al., 2012) or wall curvature (Strachan-Regan & Baumann, 2024), can isolate its contribution, but that was not the aim here. Instead, we examined a full redesign of the kind that might take place outside laboratory settings: in Study 1 the room was redesigned through co-creation with students, and in Study 2 using materials provided by a company, each representing the best that could be achieved within the available budget. The question was therefore whether a complete redesign produces a detectable difference in experience, as a first step before attributing effects to individual features. Biophilic design was central to the enrichment, but lighting, materials, colour, furniture, sensory qualities and spatial organisation also differed, so the findings concern environmental configurations as integrated wholes rather than the effects of individual design elements. Each redesign also added elements rather than removing them. This was part of the design strategy: none of the existing features failed the design criteria, and the original rooms, like many on campus, were sparsely furnished, so the redesigned rooms were enriched rather than reconfigured.

Relatedly, the contrasts were deliberately salient. This is appropriate for an initial test of sensitivity, and participants readily identified what had changed, but the data establish sensitivity to substantial environmental variation rather than to subtle or incremental change.

A measure intended for refurbishment decisions, comparative design assessment or postoccupancy evaluation will need to discriminate between environments that differ less conspicuously, and the effects reported here should not be taken as an estimate of what to expect from smaller interventions.

Second, NDIX scores are self-reported measures of environmental experience and may be influenced by expectations, familiarity, prior preferences, or awareness of environmental differences. The findings therefore demonstrate differences in perceived environmental experience rather than establishing differences in objective environmental quality. This concern applies with particular force to the within-participant comparisons, in which the same individuals evaluated two versions of the same space a short time apart and could reasonably infer both that a redesign had occurred and which version was intended to be the improvement. The order analyses in Study 1 provide direct evidence bearing on this. Ratings of the enriched room were almost invariant across presentation orders, whereas ratings of the standard room were substantially lower among participants who had already seen the enriched version. This asymmetry is the signature of a contrast effect rather than of a uniform expectancy bias, and it implies that within-participant estimates for the affected domains are inflated relative to a between-participants design. The interaction was not significant for NDIX Overall, and the enrichment effect held within both order groups, so the primary finding is not attributable to sequence. The independent-groups comparison in Study 2, in which each participant encountered only one environment, provides a further check.

Third, both studies involved relatively modest samples. This was due to term time constraints on testing by the teams involved. The modest sample size is particularly relevant for the domain-level reliability estimates, which are unstable at these sample sizes. Larger samples will be required to determine how stable these estimates are and whether the current domain structure performs consistently across different populations and environmental contexts.

Relatedly, neither study was large enough to permit a factor analysis of an instrument of this length, so the six-domain structure remains an a priori framework rather than an empirically established one.

Finally, the present studies did not include independent behavioural, physiological, cognitive, or clinical outcomes capable of establishing whether differences in NDIX scores correspond to differences in broader human functioning. The findings therefore establish sensitivity to perceived environmental differences, but not whether those differences translate into changes in health, cognition, behaviour, or physiological regulation.

Future work should extend the NDIX into naturally varying and occupied environments, including workplaces, healthcare settings, educational environments, homes, and public spaces. This will be important for determining whether the sensitivity observed under Future studies should additionally test environments that differ only modestly, rather than focusing exclusively on conspicuous environmental contrasts. This will be necessary to determine whether the NDIX is sufficiently sensitive to detect practically meaningful changes during real-world design and refurbishment processes. The potential decision-making value of the domain profiles should likewise be investigated by examining whether variation in particular domains corresponds to independently identifiable environmental characteristics and whether targeted modifications subsequently produce domain-specific changes. With appropriate validation, this could extend the NDIX from comparative evaluation towards a structured framework for identifying environmental strengths and priorities for intervention.

5. Conclusion

Across two studies, the NDIX was applied for the first time and proved straightforward to use in both indoor settings, being administered in full within a single short session in each environment. NDIX Overall consistently differentiated standard from environmentally enriched environments in the hypothesised direction, with large effects reproduced in a within-participant crossover, a within-participant comparison in a second setting, and an independent-groups comparison in which participants evaluated only one environment. In

Study 1 the effect was equivalent across presentation orders and survived correction for multiple comparisons. All six domains differed in the expected direction in both studies, and Naturalness showed the largest absolute difference in Study 1, consistent with the biophilic emphasis of the enrichment. Participants' free-text accounts of what had changed converged on the same features. The measure is therefore sensitive to meaningful environmental variation and to change within a single space that has been redesigned. In Study 1, NDIX Overall showed good internal consistency, while domain-level estimates were variable. The findings therefore provide initial evidence that NDIX Overall has discriminative sensitivity to theoretically and experientially meaningful environmental differences, while several domain subscales, notably Social, Sensory and Emotional in Study 1 and Sensory in the standard condition of Study 2, require further item-level evaluation. The next stage is to determine whether this sensitivity generalises to naturally varying real-world environments and whether NDIX scores correspond to independent behavioural, physiological, and neurobiological outcomes. With further validation, the NDIX may provide a structured bridge between subjective environmental experience and multimethod assessment of how built environments relate to human functioning.
