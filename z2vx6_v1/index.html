Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. Some differences come from the ground truth: it lacks p-values with a correction name (“p Bonf = .061”), and counts partial eta squared written with its subscript after it (“η 2 p = .05”), “p = .05”, and some table notes and figure legends (“† p < .10, *p < .05”), which this module leaves out, as non-significant p-values. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. Abstract

Since the release of ChatGPT, numerous articles emerged discussing the pros and cons of the implementing of generative AI tools in educational settings, and researchers predominantly investigated teachers' and students' use of those tools in terms of frequency, context of use, and perception. Little is known about students' processes and approaches when incorporating generative AI tools in their internet searches. Our main aim is to identify different use patterns when employing generative AI-tools for solving information problems that require internet searches. To this end, we investigate students' actual generative AI use while solving information problems that require internet searches. In our study, first-year university students were presented with generic as well as domain-specific information problems and were allowed to use the internet to write a concluding statement regarding the problem. Out of the total sample, 37 students used a generative AI-tool for task completion. Focusing on these students, we developed a rating scheme and analyzed the prompting pattern, the process of incorporating the generative AI-tool in the task completion, and whether statements from the generative AI-tool were checked against other information sources on the internet. We identified five different types of use patterns when incorporating generative AI-tools in in the process of solving information problems that require internet searches. Results further show that about half of the students heavily rely on the tool and barely verify its statements, while the other half is more cautious and aware of potential unreliable information.

[div-01]

The online information landscape is increasingly filled with open access Generative Artificial Intelligence (GAI)-tools, such as Google's Gemini, Microsoft's Co-Pilot, and OpenAI's ChatGPT. Since the release of ChatGPT 3.5, the impact of such tools on the educational system and student learning has been controversially discussed (Fütterer et al., 2023).

Numerous journal articles emerged, discussing the potentials and challenges of the implementation of GAI-tools in educational settings (e.g., Banihashem et al., 2024 , Chan & Hu, 2023; Farrokhnia et al., 2023; Kasneci et al., 2023; Kooli, 2023; Lim et al., 2023; Mills et al., 2023; Schön et al., 2023; Sok & Heng, 2023). A frequently mentioned benefit is the support AI tools can provide to students and teachers, such as providing summaries, explanations, arguments, exam material, example solutions, translations, and many more. The main weaknesses are the lack of trustworthiness and the hampering of students' skill development with excessive use.

Other educational researchers focused on the user experience and investigated teachers' and students' perceptions of those tools, the context of usage, and the frequency of GAI-tool usage (e.g., Abdaljaleel et al., 2024; Bitzenbauer, 2023; Bonsu et al., 2023; Lou, 2023; Köhler & Hartig, 2024; Ngo, 2023; Sallam et al., 2023; Strzelecki, 2023). However, little is known about the students' approaches when incorporating GAI-tools in problemsolving tasks that require internet searches. This topic is of particular interest since university students increasingly use information from the internet and GAI-tools for their studies (Castro & Tumibay, 2019). Therefore, the aim of the present study is to investigate whether students use information generated by the GAI-tool and how they incorporate it in their internet searches, to hence identify different types of information attainment patterns.

GAI-Tools in Higher Education

The implementation of GAI-tools in higher education have positive potential. For example, they can provide learning scaffolds or formative feedback for students (Banihashem et al., 2024). Bitzenbauer (2023) even proposes a procedure to implement GAI-tools as a chatbot in the classroom to support students' critical thinking when dealing with complex topics such as quantum physics. Kasneci et al. (2023) also suggest that GAI-tools can enhance students' critical thinking skills in the classroom. This notion is corroborated by Sedlbauer et al. (2024), who assigned undergraduate students the task of working on their seminar essays with the aid of ChatGPT, and found evidence for enhanced critical thinking.

In contrast, other researchers are skeptical regarding the enhancement of skills, arguing that the use of GAI-tools might hinder the development of problem-solving skills (Chan & Hu, 2023; Farrokhnia et al., 2023; Kasneci et al., 2023; Sok & Heng, 2023) or even lead to a general deterioration of cognitive skills (Macnamara et al., 2024). Recent experimental studies show that GAI-tools reduce both intrinsic and extraneous cognitive load during inquiry processes but also lead to diminished argumentative quality (Stadler et al., 2024). In this line, Stracke et al. (2024) and Ma et al. (2024) emphasize that students using GAI-tools tend to engage less with source evaluation and critical reasoning. Similarly, Abbas et al. (2024) also found that the use of ChatGPT dampens students' academic performance.

These findings support concerns that the cognitive ease of GAI-tools come at the cost of epistemic depth.

In terms of information search behavior, recent reviews (e.g., Jensen et al., 2024; Bano et al., 2024) underscore that most research to date focuses either on perceptions of AI or capabilities of AI tools, not on the actual learning processes-especially in authentic, openended, search-based academic tasks. Moreover, Bano et al. (2024) and Castillo-Martínez et al. (2025) argue that the design of prompts and the way users iteratively refine them represent a neglected, yet critical factor in learning outcomes. Prompting, in this context, is not a neutral interface command, but a central cognitive process: It encapsulates how users formulate epistemic goals, evaluate gaps in understanding, and guide AI reasoning. As Zhu et al. (2024) argue, prompts mediate between surface-level automation and deep, constructive engagement with content. Their conceptual framework identifies multiple layers of prompting-from basic queries to metacognitive strategies-but these have yet to be systematically observed in educational practice.

In summary, GAI-tools such as ChatGPT can support students during learning and problem solving, but they also pose several challenges to them, such as the application of prompting strategies and the critical reflection of the output of the GAI-tools. Integrating information from such tools into internet searches requires careful planning and implementation. Processes should be user-centered, and strategies should aim to build user confidence and habits. Through training, supporting resources and a gradual introduction, AI tools can be effectively integrated into search processes, ultimately leading to higher adoption and usage.

Against this backdrop, our study addresses two major research gaps: (1) How do university students utilize GAI-tools during open-ended academic search-based tasks and how consistent are they in their use patterns? (2) How much do they rely on the response from the GAI-tool? By combining information from log data and open-ended student responses, we aim to provide an empirical basis for a strategy-oriented instructional design in AI-mediated learning environments.

COR Framework

Critical Online Reasoning (COR) describes a set of skills that are necessary to find reliable internet sources, critically evaluate the information presented in those sources, and draw appropriate conclusions from the various encountered sources (Molerov et al., 2020; Nagel et al., 2022). COR comprises three interrelated facets, which are structured according to the typical process of online information problem-solving (Brand-Gruwel et al., 2009), namely online information acquisition (OIA), online information evaluation (CIE), and reasoning with evidence, argumentation and synthesis (REAS) (Molerov et al., 2020). OIA refers to the first steps of selecting the search engine, database, or platform and defining a search query. The second facet, CIE, encompasses the steps of search-result evaluation and the selection of internet sources from those that are displayed by the search engine. Once a website is selected, identifying website features or cues for differentiating between highquality and low-quality information is also part of the CIE facet. The REAS facet describes the ability to draw an evidence-based conclusion from the information that was gathered and write a convincing statement regarding the initially stated problem.

Clearly, the use of a GAI-tool may facilitate tasks that require online information problem solving. The steps of OIA (i.e., selecting a search engine and defining search queries) can be skipped entirely if the GAI-tool is employed. Instead, other decisions such as the choice for a GAI-tool and the wording of the prompts need to be made. Regarding the CIE facet, the response given by the AI tool could be cross-checked using other internet sources, but the AI tool itself could serve as a critical agent (e.g., by prompting the tool to find arguments refuting the response or to produce reliable sources). Lastly, the skill of writing an evidence-based conclusion from the information that was gathered (i.e., the REAS facet) is difficult to measure if the problem solving and writing of a concluding statement has been handed over to the AI tool entirely. However, the degree to which people rely on statements from GAI-tools varies widely, and they might differently deal with the risks of obtaining misleading information from GAI-tools.

Materials and Methods

The present study draws on data from the first time point of a longitudinal study conducted by the interdisciplinary research unit "CORE -Critical Online Reasoning in Higher Education" (Zlatkin-Troitschanskaia et al., 2023). The first data collection took place at three large German universities in the winter term of 2023.

Assessment of COR Skillset

To assess the three COR facets, problem-solving tasks that required a certain extent of research are presented to students. The students are allowed to use the open information space of the internet to write a concluding statement regarding the problem. They are also asked to cite their two most relevant sources, and to justify the selection of these sources. This means that for each task, three open-ended written responses were required. The students' skills in OIA, CIE, and REAS are assessed based on their responses and on their behavior while navigating the web, which is recorded in log data.

Since COR is needed not only for everyday information problems but also for studyspecific problems, generic (GEN) and domain specific (DOM) critical online information problems were constructed. The DOM-COR problems were restricted to the two study tracks economics and medicine, as they represent two fundamental types of academic programs with a heterogeneous student population. All tasks were embedded in a description of a realistic situation that might be encountered in real life. For example, one of the GEN-COR tasks revolved around the recyclability of Tetra Pak packaging; a DOM-COR task in medicine was to create a treatment plan for a patient. While the GEN-COR tasks contained only one context, each DOM-COR task scenario contained three reasoning contexts with specific problems, addressing fundamental theoretical skills, practical skills and transdisciplinary skills (for further information, see Zlatkin-Troitschanskaia et al., submitted). Note that although the GEN-and DOM-COR tasks were constructed very similarly, the order of the three open-ended written responses was slightly different in the Economics domain. The students were asked to conduct internet research and to cite the two most relevant sources prior to writing the concluding statement regarding the problem. Students were required to complete tasks using a Virtual Machine (VM) to ensure free access and data privacy while conducting internet searches. User and VM management were handled through Azure EntraID and Azure Labs, respectively. Students received a separate account consisting of a pseudorandom alphanumerical id and the domain to access the Azure Lab and authenticate within the VM. The VM was set up to decrease distractions and ensure a common experience across students. At the level of the operating system, such a setup consists of, for example, removing widgets, or disabling Copilot. The students were required to use Firefox at all times to conduct the assessment. Similarly, some of the setup of Firefox consisted of removing widgets and standardizing the homepage and redirections. We developed a Firefox plugin that facilitated user authentication with the backend, orchestrated the different task phases during the assessment, and collected various events triggered during task completion-such as button clicks, link clicks, copying text, and more. The plugin communicates with a custom backend implemented in Java with the help of the Quarkus framework via HTTP2. Some of the events triggered a screenshot, such as scrolling or page open events. This resulted in the collection of multimodal data, such as log and image data.

Therefore, the collected data was stored in both an Azure SQL database and Azure Storage.

Study Design and Sample

The N = 471 students participating in the study were first-semester students from four academic tracks, namely economics, medicine, sociology, and physics. Although sociology and physics were not one of the two main domains investigated in COR, students from sociology and physics were included to serve as a comparative sample for economics and medicine students, respectively. A complex incomplete booklet design was employed to minimize the workload per student. Every student received at least one DOM-and one GEN-COR task, with a maximum of three tasks in total. Each DOM-COR task contained only two out of the three possible context-specific problems (fundamental, practical, transdisciplinary).

The students could choose freely in the study period of approximately 5 months when to work on the tasks, with a maximum of three-hour online sessions. The students were allowed to interrupt their sessions and continue working on tasks at a later point in time. However, the students were instructed to work on independent tasks without any interruptions. In case the total session time was exceeded, they were given additional time. They received compensation of 50 Euro after completing the assessment.

Out of the entire sample, N = 37 students used a GAI-tool to complete the tasks (17 economics students, 12 medical students, five physics students, and three sociology students).

Since a student might be assigned two DOM-and one GEN-COR task and considering that the DOM-COR tasks consisted of two context-specific problems, a single student received up to five problems (2 DOM-COR tasks x 2 contexts + 1 GEN-COR task). So, although the sample consisted of only 37 students, the total number of problems that were solved with the aid of a GAI-tool was n = 115.

Scoring Scheme and Coding of ChatGPT Use

Since empirical studies investigating patterns or processes of GAI-tool-use while completing tasks that require internet searches are still rare, the construction of a standardized scoring scheme was a deductive-inductive procedure. This means that, tailored to the COR tasks, we first developed the categories, but adjusted them after looking at the data (i.e., we deleted categories when a behavior did not occur and added categories for behavior we had not anticipated). The coded aspects were: (1) prompting pattern, (2) integration of the GAItool, and (3) reliance on the GAI-tool. All aspects, sub-aspects, and the respective categories (i.e., the scoring scheme) are presented in Table 1. Based on log-data and the observed chat history, each task that was processed with the aid of a GAI-tool was evaluated on each aspect in Table 1. Note that depending on the number of categories, the maximum score of the aspects differs, running from 0 up to 4.

The GEN-CORE tasks and economics tasks were coded by two researchers who were involved in the development of the tasks and were thus very familiar with them. The medicine tasks were coded by two student research assistants. The coders had frequent meetings to discuss coding criteria (e.g., what constitutes the "beginning" and what the "middle" of the task) and to show each other examples in order to ensure a consistent coding.

Additional to the coded aspects, we collected the statements of students who admitted their GAI-tool use and categorized their justifications. This information provided insight into the amount of trust students placed in the GAI-tool.

Data Analysis

For identifying different kinds of use patterns, we used the scored data set and performed exploratory cluster analyses in R Statistical Software (v4.4.2; R Core Team, 2024).

Due to the different ordering of the open text fields in Economics, we combined only the GEN-COR and medicine tasks and conducted separate cluster analyses for the economics tasks. Note that although our n = 115 observations are nested within 37 students, we ignored possible dependencies as we do not assume that students apply a consistent pattern across the tasks. One reason is that the content of the tasks differs greatly and might appeal to a variety of interests. Further, the quality and accessibility of information students can find on the internet varies between the tasks. Also, the (perceived) quality of the GAI-tool response varies between the tasks, requiring more internet research for some tasks. Lastly, as time passes, students might be less motivated or run into time constraints, resulting in a change of their use pattern. We investigated these changes by analyzing how often students switched between clusters.

Using the R package cluster (v 2.1.6; Maechler et al., 2023), we first calculated the dissimilarity matrix using Gower's distance, which can handle different types of variables (i.e., continuous, ordinal, categorical). Regarding the clustering algorithm, we applied hierarchical clustering-with both agglomerative and divisive clustering-and partitioning around medoids (PAM), which is a nonhierarchical algorithm similar to K-means clustering.

We ran the algorithms multiple times for cluster sizes from two to ten. To assess the optimal number of clusters, we interpreted elbow plots (e.g., Ketchen & Shook, 1996) and silhouette plots (e.g., Kaufman & Rousseeuw, 1990).

Finally, we combined this exploratory approach with a bottom-up qualitative classification that was content driven. Using results from the cluster analyses, we defined different and unique use patterns such that each case could be assigned to one use pattern.

3. Cautious: these participants (partially) cross-check references claimed by the GAItool.

4. Creative prompting: these participants have the GAI-tool correct a statement, act as an agent, formulate a text based on internet sources, or have the GAI formulate a text they found on the internet.

Changes in Use Patterns

To detect changes in use pattern within the same person, we investigated how often a student switches between the five previously defined clusters. Out of the 25 students who used a GAI-tool for several tasks, only six stuck to the same use pattern. Most switch between two use patterns (14 students, of which 2 switched between C1 and C2, 3 switched between C1 and C3, 1 switched between C1 and C5, 1 switched between C2 and C3, 4 switched between C2 and C5, 1 switched between C3 and C5, and 2 switched between C4 and C5), and four employed three different use patterns. The switch in use patterns was unrelated to the domain (i.e., GEN-or DOM-COR) and the context-specific problem.

Limitations

This study was based on a relatively small group of students who chose to use

ChatGPT during the task. It is representative neither of the sample of the entire study nor on the entire population of students, especially since the overall sample only included first-year students from four subject areas. Therefore, the sample is selective, and the results may not apply to students from other academic backgrounds or levels of study. However, our aim in this study was to identify different use patterns without providing an exhaustive account.

Certainly, in a broader sample with a wider range of expertise and prior experience, more different behaviors will be observed.

In addition, students participating in this study received a small incentive for participating, which may have influenced their behavior. Some may have focused more on completing the task quickly than on finding the most accurate or reliable information. The limited time available for solving the tasks might also have led to faster, less reflective use patterns. Further, only eight of the 37 AI users openly stated and justified their use of the tool, which is a very small subset to support broader claims about users' trust in the GAI-tool.

Outlook

Future studies should include a larger and more diverse group of students to increase the generalizability of the findings. It would also be useful to control for sequence effects, such as the order in which tasks are presented, and to take into account students' prior knowledge and experience with GAI-tools. How the use pattern influences the quality of the response (i.e., the problem-solving skill) and the knowledge gain Since we did not assess the quality of the prompts or the responses generated by the GAI-tool, future research could explore how these factors relate to the use pattern and task success. It would also be interesting to find out whether students with high critical-thinking and problem-solving skills use different patterns than those scoring low on these dimensions.

This could aid in the debate on whether GAI-tool usage enhances or decreases these skills, when it is possibly the other way around or bi-directional.

In the next step, it will also be important to better understand why students choose a particular approach (i.e., use pattern) and how this choice develops over time. Think aloud protocols or questionnaires directly assessing the trustworthiness of the GAI-tool could aid in this endeavor. It would also be interesting to have an indicator on how certain students are that their written statement is correct, which would allow inferences on their trust in the GAItool.

Conclusion

This study provides one of the first detailed descriptions of how university students use GAI-tools such as ChatGPT during open-ended web-based research tasks. By identifying five different use patterns, ranging from superficial to reflective use, we show that students processes differ when integrating GAI-tools into solving web-based research tasks.

Summary of Findings

We established a rating scheme for evaluating aspects of prompting, the integration of the GAI-tool in the internet research, and the reliance on the GAI-tool. Based on the indicators we derived from the ratings, we were able to identify five main types of use patterns, namely unverified reliance with early GAI-tool use, unverified reliance with late GAI-tool use, cautious use, inventive prompters, and dedicated use. A satisfactory allocation of the cases to these five use patterns was possible, allowing a satisfactory description of the task-solving process. While about one half fell into one of the unverified reliance use patterns, the other half of the processed tasks involved a dedicated and cautious integration of the GAItool and creative prompting methods.
