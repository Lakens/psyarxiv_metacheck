What we found

We found 1 non-significant p value that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Evaluating Ratings from Large Language Models: A Simulation-Based Workflow

Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly used in psychological research to analyze and generate verbal data and stimulus materials. Examples include analyzing large quantities of qualitative data (e.g., Chew et al., 2023; Player et al., 2025; Rathje et al., 2024; Smirnov, 2025), automating item scale construction (e.g., Barends & de Vries, 2025; Fyffe et al., 2023; Hommel et al., 2022), generating ratings of words, expressions, and phrases regarding psychological constructs (e.g., Hommel, 2023; Martínez et al., 2024), or using LLMs to simulate and estimate human behavior and judgments (e.g., Loru et al., 2025; von der Heyde et al., 2025). In light of the numerous potential applications, it has been argued that LLMs have the capacity to facilitate various steps in the research process and to advance cognitive science (Feuerriegel et al., 2025; Wulff & Mata, 2025a).

The present paper focuses on the application of LLMs to analyze and rate text stimuli. For example, Martínez et al. (2024) used GPT-4o to generate ratings of valence (i.e., the pleasantness of a stimulus) and arousal (i.e., the intensity of emotional activation a stimulus elicits; Warriner et al., 2013) for multi-word expressions such as "hit rock bottom," "fall in love," or "ice cream cone." A defining feature of such tasks is that they involve a corpus of text stimuli (e.g., words, questionnaire items, sentences, social media posts, vignettes, etc.). The stimuli are assumed to differ with respect to relevant features and LLMs are used to rate the stimuli automatically with respect to psychological constructs (arousal, valence), linguistic properties (grammatical complexity, concreteness), or other quantitative or qualitative information (topic modeling, presence or absence of certain informational cues). Researchers using LLMs for such tasks are necessarily faced with the question of how to evaluate the quality of the generated ratings.

Challenges of Using LLMs in Research

LLMs offer many impressive capabilities but also come with notable limitations for research use. Both small and large models are known to be susceptible to hallucinations (Huang et al., 2025; Reddy et al., 2024) and they may generate synthetic response behavior that does not resemble that of human participants (Argyle et al., 2025; Hullman et al., 2026). Furthermore, LLMs may fall short in performing even seemingly simple tasks such as word counting (Markowitz, 2024). Despite their capacity to emulate plausible human responses in specific tasks, LLMs frequently do not succeed in replicating the full spectrum of human behavior. This limitation is exemplified by the substantial reduction in variability of responses observed in LLM outputs compared to humans (Abdurahman et al., 2024; Martínez et al., 2024). Such issues and shortcomings have the potential to result in errors when relying on LLM output for making statistical inferences and drawing substantive conclusions (Hullman et al., 2026). Accordingly, it is imperative not to interpret LLM output at face value, as it has the potential to be misleading or false.

Utilizing LLMs in research responsibly necessitates the implementation of rigorous quality control measures (e.g., Argyle et al., 2025; Knöchel et al., 2025; Nestler et al., 2026).

When aiming to use LLMs for a particular scientific task, researchers are faced with a multitude of different models and approaches. In light of the abundance of available LLMs, the selection of a particular setup often involves arbitrary choices, which introduces researcher degrees of freedom. Models differ in important aspects such as number of parameters, architecture, hyperparameter settings, and how and with which text corpora they have been trained (Argyle et al., 2025; Hussain et al., 2024). Emerging applications of LLMs often rely on a single, promising LLM (e.g., Barends and de Vries, 2025; von der Heyde et al., 2025). The selection of a particular model might be based on preliminary pilot testing or practical considerations such as accessibility and ease of use.

Output quality can be increased by systematic, iterative testing and may benefit from advanced methods such as fine-tuning (for a tutorial, see Conde et al., 2026). Reliance on a single model does not necessarily constitute a problem if its performance has been validated for the task at hand. For example, Martínez et al. (2024) used GPT-4o to obtain ratings of valence, arousal, and concreteness for thousands of single words and multi-word expressions. To validate their approach, they showed that the LLM ratings correlated highly both with existing human norms and with newly collected human ratings that were not part of the LLM training data. However, once LLM output is used to draw scientific conclusions, it is important to investigate whether the results are robust across different models. This is especially relevant given that new model variants with potential benefits (e.g., higher accuracy, increased efficiency, or reduced energy consumption) are continuously being developed.

A related issue is that LLM outcomes usually vary even when providing the same prompt repeatedly due to stochasticity inherent in model architecture (Demszky et al., 2023; Knöchel et al., 2025). However, computational demands might be too high to repeat the same prompt inputs multiple times to study variability and to attain stable estimates. As a remedy, one may enforce deterministic output by setting the temperature hyperparameter to zero. However, such an approach artificially ignores the uncertainty of the underlying, stochastic model as it merely picks the most likely response option irrespective of whether it is clearly or only slightly preferred to the second-best option (Abdurahman et al., 2024; Martínez et al., 2024). Even if temperature is set to zero, this does still not guarantee deterministic output for some LLMs (Abdurahman et al., 2025).

Research investigating this problem often identified settings of other parameters, such as the batch size, as the cause for issues with reproducibility (Coqueret et al., 2026; Yuan et al., 2025). Furthermore, minor variations in prompt design may result in divergent outputs, thereby casting doubt on the validity, robustness, and generalizability of LLM output (e.g., Baumann et al., 2025; Cummins, 2026; Feuerriegel et al., 2026; Sclar et al., 2024).

Researchers have also criticized the usage of commercial LLMs since it is contingent upon proprietary services that may not be available to all researchers equally and do not adhere to open science principles (Balluff et al., 2026; Conde et al., 2026; Guest & van Rooij, 2025; Hussain et al., 2024). As a remedy, researchers can use locally running, open LLMs for which the underlying model architecture and the parameter weights are known and available (Hussain et al., 2024), e.g., by using Ollama (Ollama, 2026). Further potential pitfalls of using LLMs have been reviewed by Argyle et al. (2025) and Wulff and Mata (2025a).

A Simulation-Based Workflow for Evaluating LLM Ratings

The overview of limitations above shows that results obtained with one particular (commercial) LLM may not be robust to modifications of model and prompt parameters.

In line with good scientific practice, we recommend that researchers use open-weight LLMs and report the employed procedures and results transparently to ensure reproducibility and replicability of the research process (Abdurahman et al., 2025; Feuerriegel et al., 2025; Hussain et al., 2024; Knöchel et al., 2025). More importantly, it is essential to study the robustness and generalizability of LLM output across the multiverse of possible decisions regarding model choice, configuration, and prompt design (Hussain et al., 2024; Steegen et al., 2016). Our evaluation framework thereby complements workflows focusing on method development via iterative improvement of LLM ratings (Conde et al., 2026).

Generalizability theory (Cronbach, 1972) provides a natural framework for analyzing ratings of different raters (e.g., LLM specifications) for a set of items (e.g., text stimuli) by partitioning the observed variance into systematic and error components. By distinguishing different sources of measurement error (termed facets), generalizability theory enables researchers to quantify the contributions of different factors. Besides reducing unexplained variability, this provides a better understanding whether measurements generalize across different raters, items, and/or contexts (Brennan, 2001).

Generalizability theory and variance-decomposition approaches have recently been applied for specific LLM tasks, such as automated essay scoring (Song et al., 2026) and user intent extraction (Kunievsky & Evans, 2026), as well as for AI benchmarks (Hardy et al., 2026) , and have been advocated more generally for establishing the reliability of AI-based scores (Speer et al., 2026).

Generalizability theory is particularly well-suited for studying the quality of LLM ratings in simulation studies, where output variability arises from multiple sources (e.g., prompt structure, model architecture, or stochasticity of the generation process). While empirical studies with human participants are often constrained by a relatively small number of measurements, research on LLM ratings enables the implementation of a fully crossed design with many facets to study the factors driving output variability. In a simulation, different models can be asked to rate all items in each combination of various conditions. Across repeated prompts, LLMs can be called in a fresh context-free of prior conversation history-to ensure stochastic independence between measurements and conditions. This high degree of methodological control enables a principled psychometric evaluation of LLM rating quality using generalizability theory.

From a pragmatic perspective, we recommend that researchers in psychology routinely employ a simulation-based workflow to evaluate the robustness of LLM-generated ratings for stimulus materials (e.g., words, sentences, test items, or vignettes), especially when there is no or little prior information regarding robustness of performance under different model and prompt parameters. When systematically varying model and prompt specifications in a factorial design, researchers can apply principles established for simulation studies in methodological research. Specifically, we adopt the ADEMP framework, which guides researchers in specifying the Aims, Data-generating mechanism, Estimands and targets, Methods, and Performance measures of a simulation study (Morris et al., 2019). We modified the ADEMP-PreReg template (Siepe et al., 2024), which provides a systematic structure for planning, analyzing, and reporting simulation studies that evaluate the performance of different statistical methods (Siepe et al., 2024). The template can also be used for preregistration, although the benefits and limitations of preregistering simulation studies remain debated (Siepe et al., 2026).

In the following, we develop a systematic simulation workflow to enable robust and transparent evaluation of LLM-generated ratings. We adopt generalizability theory and propose concrete procedural steps for evaluating the quality of LLM ratings of verbal materials. The modified ADEMP template for LLM simulation studies focuses on establishing a universally applicable, systematic workflow that can be utilized across diverse domains of psychological and social science research. We will refer to this type of evaluation study as LLM simulation. Note that other studies have employed similar terms (e.g., "LLM social simulations"; Anthis et al., 2025) to refer to research in which LLMs are employed to emulate hypothetical responses of human participants (so called "silicon samples", Cummins, 2026). In contrast, our use of the term LLM simulation refers to the systematic evaluation and variation of methodological design choices when eliciting LLM ratings.

A complementary line of work has proposed prompting guidelines for various research purposes (e.g., Conde et al., 2026; Fang et al., 2026; Giray, 2023; Ziems et al., 2024). For example, prompts should be clear, precise and contain definitions of the target constructs. Furthermore, prompt instructions may be accompanied by example cases illustrating the task, which is referred to as few-shot prompting, and prompts may assign roles to the LLM (e.g., "You are a statistics expert," Conde et al., 2026; Fang et al., 2026). However, none of these prompting approaches is a safeguard against perturbations in the prompts, as studies have repeatedly found that subtle changes in prompts can elicit major variations in LLM responses (Alahmari et al., 2025; Sclar et al., 2024).

Moreover, specific recommendations are necessarily limited in scope, since they may not transfer across research domains and tasks, across models, or across time, given the rapid development of new models. In contrast, our proposed framework and template are sufficiently general to evaluate a wide range of LLM pipelines for diverse research questions. The two approaches complement each other. While prompting guidelines suggest promising prompt designs, our evaluation workflow allows researchers to test whether these designs actually improve the quality of LLM ratings in a given application by including them as method factors.

Comparing Statistical and LLM Simulation Studies

In methodological simulation studies, researchers typically define a data-generating mechanism to simulate a large number of synthetic datasets. Next, different statistical methods are applied to each dataset. The employed methods are then evaluated using different performance measures that reflect how well each method achieves its goal (Morris et al., 2019). Using this approach, researchers aim to draw conclusions regarding properties of different methods and make recommendations for applied data analysis under a specified set of conditions (Siepe et al., 2024).

It is useful to adopt this general approach to study the multiverse of possibilities in LLM prompting. An LLM simulation study is characterized by systematically varying procedural details when generating ratings using different model specifications and prompt structures. The quality of the resulting item ratings is then evaluated using one or more performance measures. This allows researchers to quantify which design choices are critical for obtaining LLM-generated ratings in a specific application scenario. For example, researchers can quantify the consistency between various LLMs' ratings or assess whether the inter-rater reliability with human ratings depends on specific aspects of the prompting procedure. Despite many similarities, simulation studies evaluating statistical methods also differ from those evaluating LLM-generated ratings in important ways. In LLM simulations, it is particularly important to distinguish three types of variability that affect the obtained output. First, the variance of LLM ratings depends on the latent variability in the rated feature dimension across items. This type of variance constitutes construct variance, which is the target of generated ratings. To ensure a sufficient amount of construct variance, the items to be rated should be a representative sample from the target domain of interest. The definition of the target domain determines the generalizability of the observed findings to other items from this domain that have not been studied (Brennan, 2001). Second, systematically varying prompts and models enables researchers to estimate method variance in observed LLM ratings. 1 Third, LLM ratings are inherently stochastic and can vary even when using the same model and prompt for the same item repeatedly. This type of variability across iterations is particularly important for judging a model's degree of residual variance (i.e., stochastic error), which determines the stability of LLM responses to a particular task. Note that low variability of LLM output to repeated, identical prompting does not imply that the provided response is correct. Moreover, the variability of different models is only comparable if parameters that influence stochasticity (e.g., temperature, top-p) are set to 1 We adopt the terms construct variance and method variance as accessible labels for the more formal terms universe score variance and facet-related error variance components recognized in generalizability theory (Brennan, 2001; Cronbach, 1972). The term method variance is borrowed from the multitrait-multimethod tradition (Campbell & Fiske, 1959). The term residual variance is used as in generalizability theory and has to be distinguished from error variance (see the Performance Measures section of the case study).

Another important distinction between methodological simulation studies and LLM simulations concerns the data-generating mechanism. When employing LLMs, the data-generation mechanism refers to the process of mapping the provided input (i.e., a specific prompt version providing instructions and the target item to be rated) to the output (e.g., ratings about psychological variables) by running a certain model configuration. Many details of this process may be unknown or not clearly specified (e.g., the model architecture or parameter values). Many state-of-the-art models have billions of parameters (e.g., between four and 70 billion parameters for typical open source models), which interact in complex ways, making it particularly difficult to understand the inner processing of LLMs (Feuerriegel et al., 2025). In contrast to statistical simulation studies, LLM simulations do not aim at recovering some known, "true" parameters of the (underspecified) data-generating model. Instead, they aim at evaluating the quality of the obtained LLM output with respect to the task of interest (e.g., whether LLM ratings provide a sufficiently close approximation to a gold standard).

Data-Generating Mechanism

When using LLMs, the data-generating mechanism is the process of mapping the prompt input to observable output as generated by a certain model configuration.

However, the underlying process of how exactly the input is transformed, processed, and analyzed by an LLM is often unknown and difficult to grasp (Abdurahman et al., 2024), even if it can be formally specified with mathematical equations and parameter values.

What can, however, be specified when generating LLM ratings are analytical decisions at a more abstract level: (a) the selection or sampling of stimulus materials from the target domain and (b) the procedures employed to obtain LLM ratings regarding psychological features of interest. Whereas the former aspect is relevant for the generalizability to other, new items from the target domain, the latter matters for the robustness and generalizability across different LLM specifications. Accordingly, the Data-Generating Mechanism section contains information both about the type and selection of stimulus materials rated by the LLMs, and about the exact procedures of how LLM ratings will be elicited.

The stimulus materials used for LLM prompting (i.e., the corpus) can include statements, questions, vignettes, or items from a personality inventory. First, researchers must define the target domain of the psychological (or linguistic) construct they wish to investigate. Second, they need to decide on how to sample or construct stimuli that are representative for this domain (Cronbach, 1972). The definition of the corpus and the selection of items to be included directly shape the scope and generalizability of findings about LLM-extracted features. If the corpus consists only of statements assessing a particular psychological construct, the results of the LLM performance evaluation are informative only for comparable materials for the same construct. Ideally, one would draw a true random sample of items from a well-defined target domain (Brennan, 2001).

While this is practically difficult or even impossible, researchers should ensure that the verbal materials cover a sufficiently broad and heterogeneous subspace of the target domain to increase the generalizability of conclusions. What constitutes sufficiently diverse data as input depends on the research question.

Initial attempts at extracting features via LLM prompting will likely involve user-friendly, interactive chat interfaces. However, to render the generation of LLM ratings systematic, efficient, and reproducible, it should proceed in an automated and standardized manner. Accordingly, the Data-Generating Mechanism section should describe all procedural details that are identical across all simulation conditions. This includes the general design and structure of the LLM prompt input, while specific components of the prompt that vary across conditions (e.g., whether and which examples are given) should be reported in the Methods section.

One should also specify relevant details of the software employed to obtain LLM ratings, for instance, whether a remote, commercial service, a locally hosted, open-weights model, or an interactive chat interface will be used. To ensure independence between ratings, it is important to use a fresh context when eliciting LLM responses (Conde et al., 2026). It should be reported how the models are accessed (e.g., via an API, that is, an application programming interface), and whether and how a specific response format (e.g., a numeric Likert rating between 1 and 7) was ensured. For instance, it is possible to include specific instructions in the prompt to adhere to a certain response format or to use software features ensuring that the LLM output has a specific format, such as JSON (JavaScript Object Notation). Note that the choice for structured output against an open format itself constitutes a researcher degree of freedom and could be explicitly varied to assess to what extent LLM ratings depend on this methodological factor. We generally recommend using structured output for increasing standardization and software optimized for handling such output (e.g., the type_object() function family in the ellmer package in R; Wickham et al., 2025).

To ensure replicability of model calls, one should set random seeds, though this may not be possible for all models (Abdurahman et al., 2025). Reproducibility of LLM output also depends on hyperparameters such as temperature, top-k, and top-p that shape the distribution from which tokens are drawn. While top-k sampling uses only the k tokens with the highest probability, top-p sampling uses the smallest set of tokens with a cumulative probability of p (Coqueret et al., 2026). When prompting an LLM, even details such as the batch size can alter the obtained output. Full reproducibility is attainable only if a local model is run with fixed model and software versions, a fixed seed (or a temperature of 0), a controlled batch size, and identical hardware-conditions that are difficult to achieve in practice. Yet, it is important to minimize stochasticity where possible, because reproducibility in LLMs is not all-or-nothing. Fixing the seed, temperature and sampling parameters reduces unwanted variation and makes results more comparable. This is especially valuable when repeating prompts many times is computationally prohibitive (Coqueret et al., 2026; Yuan et al., 2025).

Estimands and Targets

The Estimands and Targets section describes the purpose of the ratings to be generated by clarifying the theoretical construct(s) being studied. In traditional, methodological simulation studies (Morris et al., 2019), the targets of interest are usually the unknown parameters of the data-generating mechanism, which are estimated with a statistical method. In contrast, the targets in LLM simulation studies are quantitative or qualitative characteristics of the text stimuli in terms of the psychological variables of interest. Correspondingly, the estimands of LLM-generated ratings are the unknown (numeric or discrete) values of the items on the variables to be rated.

Methods

This section provides information about the different models and prompt variants that will be analyzed in the LLM simulation (cf. Figure 1). The chosen method factors should be accompanied with a clear description and justification to ensure that readers can judge the scope of the LLM simulation (i.e., which range of model specifications has been investigated). The combination of all method factors varied in the LLM simulation determines the simulation design and the number of simulation conditions.

It should be stated which LLMs will be used. LLMs differ in numerous aspects such as the architecture (often referred to as model family), the number of parameters (i.e., model size), the company or institution developing a model, the languages in which an LLM was trained, and whether information about the model weights and training procedure is publicly available. If possible, justify why the employed models were selected. The reasoning for choosing specific models may concern availability, expected costs, openness, and/or computational feasibility (e.g., maximum model size that can be run locally). 2A fully factorial design encompassing all combinations of LLM families and model sizes is often infeasible, as not all models are available across different size variants. Thus, we recommend including all investigated LLM variants as a single factor in the analysis as opposed to handling certain aspects (e.g., model family and model size) as separate factors. Furthermore, report the values of all model hyperparameters that may affect the output (e.g., temperature, top-p, top-k, context length, seed, batch size), including those that were not changed from their defaults. Default values may change between software versions, differ between models, and be overridden by the software used to access a model.

For instance, in our case study, the documented default temperature of 0.8 in Ollama was replaced by a value of 1 because the models were accessed through an OpenAI-compatible endpoint (see Appendix B). This shows that it can be necessary to verify the effective settings rather than relying on the documentation. If hyperparameters are adjusted for each model, it is appropriate to treat all LLM variants as a single factor. Otherwise, if systematic variations of LLM settings (e.g., temperature, which controls the degree of stochasticity of the output) are of interest, these hyperparameters can be treated as separate method factors. For example, one may manipulate temperature and top-p in a factorial grid to study their relative impact on the variability and quality of LLM ratings.

The Methods section also describes the different prompt versions provided to the LLMs. Specify all components of the prompt input that vary across simulation conditions, such as different system prompts that may contain context about the purpose of the task, instructions for the LLM on which scale to return ratings, and whether examples are provided or not (zero-shot vs. few-shot prompting; Feuerriegel et al., 2025).

Each of these prompt components defines a method factor. In a fully factorial design, distinct prompt versions are obtained by combining all levels of the different prompt components. If some of the combinations do not result in meaningful prompt instructions, one may use an incomplete factorial design or treat the subset of meaningful combinations as a single factor. To ensure that the comparison of LLM rating quality across models is valid, the same prompt versions should be used for all LLM versions.

Performance Measures

The Performance Measures section provides information on how the consistency and agreement of LLM ratings will be evaluated and how the uncertainty of this evaluation will be quantified. The section also concerns the handling of missing values and the interpretation of performance measures, as well as the number of iterations in each simulation condition. To avoid ambiguity, one may also specify equations for how these measures will be calculated.

The choice of specific performance measures depends on the aims of the investigation. If the aim is to analyze the consistency in terms of the proportion of variance in LLM ratings due to different LLM model versions or prompt instructions, it is necessary to specify a suitable mixed-effects model for variance decomposition of the LLM simulation design. Generalizability theory provides a natural framework for such an analysis, which proceeds in two steps (Brennan, 2001). In the G study, a mixed model is used to estimate the variance components of the object of measurement (typically the stimuli), of all facets (e.g., model, prompt components), and of their interactions. In the D study, these components are combined into reliability coefficients for a specific measurement design, that is, for ratings averaged across a given number of conditions per facet. The generalizability coefficient Eρ 2 quantifies how consistently the relative ordering of the stimuli is reproduced across facet conditions, whereas the dependability coefficient Φ additionally takes differences in the absolute level of the ratings into account.

Because a D study can be computed for any number of conditions per facet, researchers can also project how reliability will change if different numbers of models, prompt variants, or iterations are used. When planning the analysis, researchers should specify the mixed model, which facets are treated as random or fixed, how the universe score is defined, which coefficient is of primary interest, and which D study designs will be evaluated. Importantly, high consistency across simulation conditions does not imply that the output is correct or valid. High estimates of consistency can also emerge if LLMs systematically give incorrect or biased responses. Therefore, the weak validation criterion is not sufficient by itself to judge the quality of LLM ratings. Still, the approach is useful as a first step to identify methodological factors that shape LLM response distributions when an external criterion is not (yet) available.

If an external criterion is available for comparison (e.g., human ratings), the inter-rater reliability between human ratings and LLM ratings may be computed as the Pearson or rank-order correlation of ratings across all stimulus materials (Goodwin, 2001).

The choice between measures of relative and absolute agreement should follow from the intended use of the LLM ratings. The distinction between relative and absolute agreement parallels the generalizability and dependability coefficients in generalizability theory, which concern the relative ordering and the absolute level of ratings, respectively (Brennan, 2001).

Measures of relative agreement (e.g., Pearson or rank-order correlations, or the consistency intraclass correlation ICC(C,1); McGraw and Wong, 1996) are sufficient when LLM ratings are used to order stimuli, to select stimuli covering the range of a construct, or as covariates or predictors in statistical models, since these uses are unaffected by linear transformations of the ratings. Measures of absolute agreement (e.g., mean bias, root mean square error across items, or the agreement intraclass correlation ICC(A,1)) are required whenever the generated values themselves are interpreted, for instance, when LLM ratings are combined with existing human norms, when stimuli are selected based on cutoff values (e.g., to select "neutral" items), or when LLMs are used to predict or forecast observable quantities such as voting behavior (von der Heyde et al., 2025).

Because high correlations do not preclude systematic shifts or a compressed range of LLM ratings (Abdurahman et al., 2024), researchers may additionally compare the distributions of LLM and human ratings (e.g., means and standard deviations).

For any measure, one should specify whether LLM ratings are averaged across prompt iterations before computing the mean across items. Furthermore, researchers may employ a rater non-inferiority criterion, demanding that the agreement between LLM and human ratings should be as high as the agreement between different human raters (Cummins et al., 2026). If a study targets specific comparisons between LLM configurations (e.g., whether a smaller local model agrees with human ratings as well as a commercial model), these comparisons can be tested statistically. Because both correlations share the same benchmark ratings, they are dependent, and tests for independent correlations are not appropriate. Suitable tests and confidence intervals for dependent correlations are implemented, for instance, in the R package cocor (Diedenhofen & Musch, 2015). Such targeted tests are most informative when the comparisons are specified in advance; in exploratory multiverse designs, the number of possible pairwise comparisons grows quickly, so that summarizing the overall pattern (e.g., by variance components or plots of all conditions) is often more useful than testing every pair. Researchers may be interested in computing logprobs of token predictions to obtain probability-weighted expected ratings that reflect the uncertainty associated with each token (e.g., Hussain et al., 2024; Martínez et al., 2024).

The estimation uncertainty of some performance measures (such as the correlation of LLM and human ratings) is bounded by the number n items of stimulus items included in the study. The sampling error due to having a finite number of items is relevant with respect to generalizing the results to other items from the target domain (Brennan, 2010).

To assess this type of uncertainty, we can average the standard error of an agreement measure across simulation iterations (e.g., the mean SE of the correlation coefficient).

Importantly, the averaged standard error depends on the number of items and does not automatically decrease even if more conditions or iterations are used.

A different source of uncertainty in LLM simulations is due to the stochasticity of repeated LLM ratings within the same condition (Knöchel et al., 2025). This uncertainty can be reported in the form of the Monte Carlo standard error (MCSE) and decreases for a larger number of iterations. When studying the quality of LLM-generated ratings, we consider the MCSE to be less relevant than the uncertainty due to studying only a limited number of items, since it depends on a property of the specific procedure (i.e., how many repeated LLM ratings were elicited) and not a property of the content (i.e., whether the rating methodology generalized across the item domain). LLM output can deviate from the allowed range of responses, resulting in invalid or missing values. If ratings are to be confined to numerical values in a bounded, predefined range (e.g., Likert ratings between 1 and 7), provide information on how other output (e.g., non-numeric text or values outside of the range) will be treated. One possibility is to handle these values as missing data and report their relative occurrence rate. To reduce such issues, researchers can use LLM software that ensures a certain output structure, for instance, by specifying a specific JSON template (for example with software packages such as ellmer in R; Wickham et al., 2025). If such methods are not used, it may be the case that the rating of interest is still included in the open text output and can potentially be retrieved by further text processing. If this is the case, describe the steps taken to extract relevant information from the open text format. As an alternative to treating invalid responses as missings, it is possible to impute iterations that did not return a valid rating, for instance, by implementing repeated prompting until a valid output rating is obtained (for different strategies of handling missingness in simulations, see Pawel et al., 2026). Furthermore, specify at which proportion of failed iterations a whole condition will be excluded from the analysis.

Finally, one may specify thresholds to reduce post-hoc flexibility in interpreting the results. For example, one could specify what will constitute 'acceptable' and 'unacceptable' levels of performance and what will count as 'relevant differences' in the performance of different LLMs. Still, any verbal labels should be interpreted cautiously, especially if they are based on arbitrary conventions.

Aims

The aims of the LLM simulation study are (1) to conduct a variance decomposition of LLM ratings of valence and arousal regarding multi-word expressions into construct variance (due to differences in the valence and arousal of the included items) and method variance (due to specific LLM and prompt variations) and ( 2) to assess the extent to which LLM ratings correspond to the perceived valence and arousal of multi-word expressions as rated by humans.

Data-Generating Mechanism

Our study uses two different sets of English multi-word expressions (each with n items = 96 items). Martínez et al. (2024) selected the stimuli based on their own GPT-4o ratings to cover the entire range of the respective construct. In Experiments 4 and 5, human raters rated the stimuli regarding valence and arousal, respectively. All analyses were conducted separately for the multi-word expressions of valence and arousal but with identical methodology reported in the Methods section. All LLM calls were independent by starting each LLM call with a fresh context (also see Conde et al., 2026).

We used open-weights LLMs which are run locally through the software Ollama (Ollama, 2026) and are accessed via the R package ellmer. To ensure that LLM ratings of valence and arousal only consist of numerical values, we used the type_object() and type_integer() functions to request output in JSON format with a predetermined response format of integers. Each prompt is composed according to the structure used by Martínez et al. (2024) in prompt 2 (valence) and prompt 3 (arousal) of their paper: (1)

An explanation of the task, (2) the range of responses allowed, (3) examples for multi-word expressions, (4) the target multi-word expression to be rated, and (5) a reminder for the range of responses allowed.

Estimands and Targets

Our target estimands are the valence and arousal of multi-word expressions (where the scale varies across conditions as detailed in the Methods section in the following). Valence and arousal are the two primary variables of dimensional models of affect (Russell, 1980). Valence refers to the pleasantness of a stimulus, ranging from very negative to very positive, whereas arousal refers to the intensity of the emotional activation elicited by a stimulus, ranging from calm to excited (Warriner et al., 2013).

Performance Measures

The present investigation used two performance measures: (1) Estimating the variance components in LLM-generated valence and arousal ratings associated with differences in the stimulus materials and differences in methods (secondary performance measure focusing on consistency), and (2) assessing the correlation between human and LLM ratings of valence and arousal (primary performance measure focusing on inter-rater reliability).

Regarding aim (1), we estimated the variance components of the following linear mixed model using the gtheory package (Moore, 2016) which uses the lmer() function, lmer(llm_rating ~(1 | stimulus * model * format * example),... )

The stimulus term accounts for differences in the target construct between the multi-word expressions (i.e., actual variability in valence or arousal), whereas the remaining terms (model, format, and example) account for methods effects.

Following generalizability theory, we first performed a G study, in which the variance components of all random effects (main effects and interactions) were estimated from the fitted model and expressed as percentages of their sum (Brennan, 2001). All variance components were estimated as random effects because each model, example type, and response format is drawn from a potentially infinite universe with the stimuli being the object of measurement. The realization of the chosen model, exact example type, and presentation of the response format are to some extent arbitrary and interchangeable and could differ across studies. Treating any of these facets as fixed would move interactions of the stimuli with these facets to the universe score variance and thereby increase the generalizability and dependability coefficients. The present analysis can therefore be regarded as conservative.

In the D study, the universe score of a stimulus is its expected rating across all admissible conditions of the facets (e.g., across all models and prompt variants that could have been used), and the variance of these scores across stimuli constitutes the universe score variance. We defined it as σ 2 (τ ) = σ 2 (stimulus) (i.e., as the true, unknown random-intercept variance of the stimuli), because we aimed to evaluate how well the obtained stimulus LLM ratings generalize across the investigated facets of the design (8 models, 4 example conditions, 2 response formats). The generalizability coefficient compares the universe score variance to the relative error variance σ 2 (δ), which comprises all components involving an interaction with the stimuli and thus affects the relative ordering of stimulus ratings.

In contrast, the dependability coefficient is based on the absolute error variance σ 2 (∆), which additionally includes facet main effects and their interactions, so that Φ ≤ Eρ 2 .

Our analysis focuses on Eρ 2 , as our study focuses on whether LLMs can produce stimulus ratings that show the same relative ordering (but not necessarily the exact same values) as those provided by humans.

The D study variance components were obtained by dividing the respective G study variance components by the product of the number of factor levels per involved facet (excluding the object of measurement -the stimuli). For example, the D study variance component for the interaction of the stimulus s, the model facet m, and the example facet e is given by σ 2 (sM E) = σ 2 (sme) , where σ 2 (sme) is the corresponding G study variance component and n ′ m and n ′ e are the number of models (= 8) and example conditions (= 4), respectively.

The capital letters M and E indicate that the estimated variance component is for stimulus mean scores across 8 models and 4 example type conditions. Because σ 2 (sM E) involves an interaction with the stimuli, it is part of σ 2 (δ), whereas components without such an interaction (e.g., σ 2 (M E)) enter only the absolute error variance σ 2 (∆).

These cutoffs follow common conventions for reliability coefficients, which were proposed mainly in the context of test-retest reliability (Groh, 2025; Matheson, 2019). The specific cutoffs and their verbal labels should therefore not be read as substantive quality categories, but rather serve to constrain post-hoc flexibility in interpreting the results. The continuous estimates and their uncertainty remain the primary basis for interpretation. We considered a certain method as performing better (or worse) than these thresholds on the employed corpus if the MCSE 95% CI was above (below) a threshold. We considered Method X as performing better than Method Y in a certain simulation condition on the employed corpus if the lower bound for the estimated inter-rater reliability of Method X ( IRR -1.96 • MCSE) was greater than the upper bound for the estimated inter-rater reliability of Method Y ( IRR + 1.96 • MCSE). Conversely, if the 95% CI based on the averaged standard errors of correlation coefficients between human-generated and LLM-generated ratings was above (below) one of the thresholds, we concluded that a certain method is generally better (worse) than this threshold for comparable stimulus material. As noted above, the MCSE-based CI only reflects the precision of the estimate in a condition, not whether performance generalizes to new stimuli.

Discussion

The present work proposes a systematic workflow for evaluating the quality of LLM-generated ratings for stimulus materials. We take a novel perspective by connecting LLM-based methodology for natural language processing with traditional methodological simulation studies. Our framework enhances the coherence across disparate fields that have used LLMs to elicit ratings and promotes the adoption of rigorous methodological standards that have been effective in related disciplines. To this end, we adapted the ADEMP framework originally proposed in the field of statistics for simulation studies (Morris et al., 2019) and the corresponding ADEMP-PreReg template (Siepe et al., 2024).

If only one or a few setups are tested, it remains unclear whether LLM performance generalizes to other materials and procedural variations, which raises the question of whether scientific knowledge has actually been generated (Argyle et al., 2025). Due to the fully crossed design of a multiverse study, its computational costs are much higher than those of a single LLM setup, especially for large stimulus sets such as the norms of concreteness, valence, and arousal generated by Martínez et al. (2024) for 126,397 single words and 63,680 multi-word expressions. These costs can be limited by applying an ADEMP validation study to a random subset of stimuli from the target corpus. Because the subset is a random sample, the findings generalize to the remaining stimuli, with the item-based standard error quantifying the uncertainty of this generalization. The best-performing configuration can then be used to rate the full corpus, for which obtaining human ratings would be infeasible (e.g., Beucler et al., 2026; Martínez et al., 2024). Such validated LLM ratings offer a fast and cheap alternative to human ratings and can be used to sample stimuli representatively from the whole range of a construct for subsequent studies with human participants, as done by Martínez et al. (2024). However, stimulus sets that cover all areas of the response distribution equally, as in the present case study, may increase construct (universe score) variance and thereby lead to an overestimation of the generalizability coefficient.

We illustrated the new ADEMP template by conceptually replicating and extending the validation study by Martínez et al. (2024). In line with their findings, we observed high correlations between human and LLM ratings of valence and arousal.

However, with smaller local models, rating quality depended on the chosen model and, to a lesser extent, on the response format and prompt examples, particularly in combination, as quantified by the variance components (Brennan, 2001). A limitation is that subjective human ratings served as the benchmark, which need not be correct and can be distorted by inattentive responding and other response biases (e.g., Stosic et al., 2024). Given the high reliability of the human ratings and the difficulty of measuring valence and arousal otherwise, however, there seems to be no better alternative benchmark. Despite the sensitivity of LLM-generated ratings to methodological choices, even modestly sized, locally run open-weight models with approximately 8 to 27 billion parameters produced valence ratings that correlated with human ratings comparably to GPT-4o, particularly when ratings were averaged over repeated queries under identical conditions.

Concerning limitations, LLM performance may vary even for related constructs:

For arousal, open-weight models reached only adequate performance (r ≈ .80; see Results), below that of GPT-4o (Martínez et al., 2024). This may partly reflect properties of the construct, since arousal ratings are generally less consistent across norming studies than valence ratings (Muraki & Pexman, 2025; Warriner et al., 2013).

Alternatively, the paper and data by Martínez et al. (2024) may have been part of the training data of some models (e.g., gpt-oss and gemma3, published in 2025), which could inflate performance estimates ("leakage"; Feuerriegel et al., 2025; Hullman et al., 2026), possibly more so for valence than for arousal. Ruling out this explanation requires replicating the present investigation with new stimulus materials and human ratings.

Finally, other procedural details besides model type, prompt examples, and response scale may affect LLM response quality; while we selected those deemed most relevant, future research is required to study other prompt design choices (Fang et al., 2026).

A fundamental caveat concerns the interpretation of LLMs' predictive performance. While LLM output may show high correlations of up to .95 with human ratings, such high predictive performance does not imply that LLMs have a causal understanding of cognitive processes in humans or that the underlying mechanisms are equivalent (Demszky et al., 2023). Loru et al. (2025) investigated this question and showed that LLMs produced output similar to that of human raters in a news classification task, but humans and LLMs still differed in the processes leading to their conclusions. Furthermore, high correlations between LLM and human ratings do not justify simply replacing human participants with LLMs. Rather, systematic LLM simulations varying procedural details, planned and possibly preregistered with the ADEMP template, allow researchers to investigate where discrepancies between LLM and human ratings may occur and which factors are relevant for minimizing this gap.

When using commercial LLMs as a research tool, the lack of information about model architecture as well as training data and procedures threatens scientific transparency and reproducibility. Additionally, many large models cannot be run locally and are accessible exclusively through APIs (Feuerriegel et al., 2025; Wulff & Mata, 2025a). In the present study, we employed open-weight models run locally using Ollama (Ollama, 2026), accessed via ellmer in R (Wickham et al., 2025), thereby enabling other researchers to easily replicate and extend our analyses. When accessed via programming languages such as Python, open models even allow researchers to access internal model information such as log probabilities of LLM predictions (which reflect the uncertainty associated with each response) or to apply fine-tuning to further increase the correlation with human ratings (Hussain et al., 2024; Martínez et al., 2024). However, open-weights models are not without limitations. For instance, even if the architecture and weights are openly available, the complete set of factors, design choices, and training data relevant to their development may not be known.

Another general issue is the substantial computational and environmental cost of LLMs (Abdurahman et al., 2024; Debelak et al., 2025; Demszky et al., 2023), which can be mitigated by smaller models that run locally on consumer-level hardware. Costs also increase with the context length (i.e., the number of input and output words a model can consider; Hussain et al., 2024), and long inputs may be more difficult for LLMs to process (Huang et al., 2025). In the present investigation, we used concise rating instructions and requested only integer ratings, thus keeping context length minimal.

Finally, LLM output may be biased related to demographic factors such as race or sex. This is especially critical when using LLMs for rating tasks, as LLMs may replicate existing societal biases present in their training data (Abdurahman et al., 2025; Debelak et al., 2025; Guest et al., 2025). By conducting an LLM simulation study using the present framework, researchers can investigate biases. On the one hand, biases not specific to particular items will manifest as changes in the absolute level of all LLM ratings, and thus in a larger main effect of model type on stimulus ratings (e.g., generally higher ratings for one model than another model). On the other hand, model biases specific to certain item properties will manifest as an interaction between model and stimulus type (e.g., systematically different ratings for items involving men vs. women). 8For instance, researchers may systematically vary certain terms and content related to specific demographic groups in the stimulus materials (e.g., whether an applicant is framed as male or female) to examine how specific aspects affect LLM ratings.

The proposed template can also be used for more specialized and advanced methods of eliciting LLM ratings. For instance, researchers may forgo LLM prompting altogether and instead extract psychological constructs from text data using embedding spaces provided by certain models. Recent studies have used embedding spaces to analyze semantic relationships between various psychological constructs and item texts (e.g., Herderich et al., 2024; Hommel and Arslan, 2025; Hommel et al., 2026; Wulff and Mata, 2025b). Similarly, fine-tuning can tailor LLMs to specific tasks (Conde et al., 2026; Hussain et al., 2024). Although a multiverse of design choices is not strictly needed for continually improving a fine-tuned model, the ADEMP approach can help evaluate how the choice of base model and hyperparameter settings affects the resulting fine-tuned models.

In conclusion, the present paper contributes to the robust use of language models as a research tool by developing a workflow for evaluating LLM-generated ratings. We adapted the ADEMP template for statistical simulation studies to facilitate the planning and reporting of LLM simulations. The framework draws on generalizability theory to disentangle the contributions of items, model and prompt choices as well as repeated iterations to the consistency of LLM ratings (Brennan, 2001; Cronbach, 1972), while using inter-rater reliability with human ratings as a gold standard for validity (Goodwin, 2001). Overall, our framework provides a psychometric perspective on evaluating LLM output across the multiverse of arbitrary model and prompt choices.
