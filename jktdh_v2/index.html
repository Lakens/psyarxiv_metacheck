Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. Abstract

Background. Existential distress and treatment-resistant depression are common and difficult to manage in patients with serious and life-limiting illnesses. Psilocybin-assisted therapy has shown early promise for these conditions, but access outside research settings remains narrow, inconsistent, expensive and often shaped by outdated eligibility rules. These include narrow lists of qualifying diagnoses and blanket age-based cut-offs that are not grounded in evidence specific to palliative populations. Regulators slowly adjust to the state-of-the-art knowledge generated by psilocybin-assisted therapy, and overall, often show poor understanding of palliative populations' specific needs.

Objective. To review current and emerging clinical evidence for psilocybin-assisted therapy in serious and life-limiting illness, highlighted with the examples of the EU "Horizon Europe" funded PsyPal project and a Johns Hopkins-led trial in amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), and to examine the regulatory, informational, and financial barriers that shape access for palliative populations.

Findings. Randomized trials in cancer-related existential distress report sustained reductions in depression and death anxiety. Furthermore, a majority of participants in the founding psilocybin studies rated their session among the most meaningful experiences of their life. Project PsyPal is currently the first randomized trial of psilocybin therapy in non-oncology palliative populations, enrolling patients with COPD, ALS, multiple sclerosis, and atypical parkinsonian disorders across four European sites with no upper age limit; a separate trial at Johns Hopkins is testing psilocybin for mood and hopelessness in ALS specifically. Real-world cohort data from Oregon's regulated program corroborate meaningful symptom improvement and low serious-adverse-event rates. Access, however, remains constrained by diagnostic and research-representation gaps, high outof-pocket cost, and inconsistent regulatory pathways, illustrated by a Canadian patient refused psilocybin for existential relief who was approved for medical assistance in dying within weeks. Two U.S. psilocybin new drug applications, for treatment-resistant and for major depression, were completed in November 2026, but neither addresses existential distress.

Clinical evidence relevant to palliative and serious-illness populations is accumulating rapidly. Eligibility frameworks for psilocybin-assisted therapy in palliative care should be built on individualized clinical assessment rather than systematic exclusions, paired with funding mechanisms that prevent access from becoming a function of wealth alone.

1. Introduction

Existential distress -the anguish that accompanies an awareness of approaching death, loss of meaning, or loss of self -is among the most difficult symptoms palliative care clinicians are asked to treat. Standard pharmacological approaches to depression and anxiety in this context are frequently slow to act, poorly tolerated in patients with advanced disease, or simply insufficient for suffering that is existential rather than purely biochemical in origin 1 . Against this gap, psilocybin-assisted therapy has re-emerged over the past two decades as a candidate intervention, supported by early but consistent signals in patients with cancer-related distress 2, 3.

Interest has grown quickly, but the evidence base specific to palliative and serious-illness populations outside oncology has, until recently, been almost nonexistent. Most of what is known about psilocybin's safety and effect comes from trials in physically healthy or psychiatric populations, not from patients living with COPD, ALS, multiple sclerosis, or atypical parkinsonian disorders, nor reliably from the oldest patients within any diagnosis, who are systematically underrepresented in psychedelic trials generally 4, 5 . This article reviews the evidence now closing that gap, with particular attention to two current trial programs -the European Union "Horizon Europe" funded PsyPal consortium and a Johns Hopkins-led trial in ALS -that are, between them, the first sustained effort to generate palliative-population-specific data for psilocybin therapy.

Evidence alone, however, does not determine access. This article therefore also examines the regulatory, informational, and financial barriers that shape whether a patient who might benefit from psilocybin-assisted therapy can actually obtain it, and argues that eligibility should rest on individualized clinical assessment -capacity, relevant risk factors, and informed consentrather than on systematic exclusions, including the age-based cut-offs still common in trial design and some regulatory frameworks, that are not well adjusted to the evidence reviewed here. Section 2 reviews the trial evidence and Section 3 examines access barriers relevant to palliative care systems. Section 4 sets out the ethical case for individualized eligibility. Section 5 reviews international regulatory approaches, and Section 6 translates these findings into policy and practice recommendations.

2. Emerging Trial Evidence in Serious and Life-Limiting Illness

Sources for this review were identified through targeted searches of PubMed, ClinicalTrials.gov, and medRxiv for psilocybin trials and cohort studies in palliative, oncology, and serious-illness populations published or registered through November 2026, supplemented by institutional and consortium communications for trials not yet reporting peer-reviewed results (PsyPal, the Johns Hopkins ALS trial). It is not a systematic review, and no formal risk-of-bias assessment was performed; evidence levels for each source are indicated in Table 1.

2.1 The founding evidence: cancer-related existential distress

The clearest existing evidence for psilocybin-assisted therapy in serious illness comes from oncology. Two randomized, placebo-controlled trials found that a single moderate-to-high dose of psilocybin, combined with psychological support, produced substantial reductions in depression and death-related anxiety in patients with life-threatening cancer, sustained at six months in roughly 60-80% of participants 2, 3 . A more recent, uncontrolled follow-up of a similar protocol found that about half of participants sustained a significant reduction in depressive symptoms at two years, the longest follow-up reported for this population to date 6 . In the founding Johns Hopkins study of psilocybin in healthy volunteers, 67% of participants rated their session as the single most meaningful experience of their life or among the top five, comparable in magnitude to the birth of a first child, with ratings holding at 14-month follow-up 7, 8 . Part of what makes the experience significant may be relational rather than purely symptomatic: a randomized, placebocontrolled trial in patients with major depression found that a single dose of psilocybin produced a measurable increase in emotional empathy still detectable two weeks later 9 , a dimension that may matter as much to a patient with a life-limiting illness and a lifetime of relationships behind them as any depression or anxiety score. This personal-meaning finding is consistent with the geriatric psychiatrist Robert Butler's description of the "life review," a naturally occurring process of looking back over one's life that contributes to serenity when it resolves well and to late-life depression when it stalls 10.

A November 2026 integrative systematic review of psilocybin-assisted therapy for cancer-related psychological and existential distress reached a consistent conclusion, describing the intervention as promising but still investigational 11 . These findings, while robust for their design, come from a single diagnostic population. Whether they generalize to the range of conditions palliative care actually manages -progressive neurological and respiratory disease, multi-morbidity, cognitive change -has, until recently, been untested.

2.2 PsyPal: the first trial in non-oncology palliative populations

The PsyPal consortium, funded under "Horizon Europe", the EU Framework Programme for Research and Innovation, is conducting the first randomized trial to examine psilocybin therapy specifically in non-oncology palliative populations 12 . Led by Prof. Robert Schoevers at the University Medical Center Groningen, this randomized, double-blind, low-dose-controlled Phase 2 trial is enrolling an estimated 108 participants with one of four progressive, life-limiting conditions across four European clinical sites: COPD at the University Medical Center Groningen in the Netherlands, atypical parkinsonian disorders at the Champalimaud Foundation in Portugal, multiple sclerosis at the National Institute of Mental Health in the Czech Republic, and ALS jointly at the University of Copenhagen and Bispebjerg Hospital in Denmark 13 . Participants attend three preparation sessions with psychotherapists (1-2 hours each), two supervised dosing sessions (6-8 hours each) given two weeks apart, and five integration sessions. The experimental group receives two escalating doses of psilocybin (15 mg, then 25 mg); the control group receives two low doses (1 mg). Follow-up assessments take place at 6 weeks, 3 months, and 6 months, with access to a digital care platform and peer support groups throughout follow-up, and control-group participants may receive one open-label 25 mg session after the primary endpoint. The trial sets no upper age limit for enrollment 12 . Its primary purpose is classified as supportive care; the primary endpoint is change in depression severity (MADRS) from baseline to 6 weeks after the second dosing session, and secondary outcomes include anxiety, demoralization, quality of life, and longer-term patient and family wellbeing, an endpoint the investigators have specifically noted is often overlooked in psychedelic trials 13 . Results are not yet published; the project began in January 2024 and the trial opened to enrollment in 2025; it is registered as NCT06782724. Its significance for palliative care extends beyond any single diagnosis: PsyPal is the first trial to test whether the effect sizes documented in cancer populations replicate across mechanistically distinct forms of terminal and progressive disease, and its lack of an upper age limit means it will, for the first time, generate prospective safety data for older patients within a structured research protocol rather than relying on the small, incidental numbers of older participants captured in prior psychedelic trials 4.

2.3 A dedicated ALS trial at Johns Hopkins

A second, independent program is testing psilocybin specifically in ALS. Investigators Drs. Ambereen Mehta and Albert Garcia-Romeu at Johns Hopkins University have initiated a clinical trial examining the effects of psilocybin on mood, hopelessness, and quality of life in patients with ALS 14 . ALS carries a uniformly poor prognosis and a high burden of hopelessness and existential distress with few effective pharmacological options, which the investigators cite as the rationale for the trial 14, 15 . Related pilot work using microdose psilocybin in palliative populations is also underway in Canada, led by palliative physician James Downar at Bruyère Health in Ottawa, testing whether low, non-hallucinogenic doses can reduce psychological distress in patients receiving palliative care without the intensity of a full-dose session 16 . An open-label Phase I/II study has since found microdose psilocybin safe and feasible, with signals of benefit, in patients with advanced, incurable illness 17 , and a multi-site, double-blind, placebo-controlled Phase 3 trial began recruiting in January 2026 18 . Downar has been explicit that the aim of this work is not to substitute for medical assistance in dying but to give patients additional time that is more bearable, a framing directly relevant to the tension examined in Section 3.3 below 16.

Taken together, PsyPal and the Johns Hopkins ALS trial represent the first coordinated effort to move psilocybin research beyond oncology and into the broader population of patients palliative care actually serves. Neither trial has yet reported results, and neither establishes psilocybin as an approved therapy for these indications; their significance for this review is that they are, for the first time, generating age-inclusive, diagnosis-diverse safety and efficacy data directly relevant to palliative practice, rather than requiring that practice extrapolate from oncology or from healthyvolunteer studies.

2.4 Real-world and safety data

Evidence from regulated non-research settings offers a complementary, if less controlled, picture. A prospective cohort of 2,363 adults using Oregon and Colorado's regulated psilocybin services found depression scores improved 49% and anxiety scores improved 51% at two weeks, with only 4% mild and 0.2% more serious adverse events recorded, although the authors are explicit that these are observational associations, not controlled trial results 19 (Figure 1). A separate, peerreviewed 346-person cohort through Oregon's program, followed to three months, found four serious adverse behavioral reactions (about 1% of participants), all in psychedelic-naive individuals with pre-existing symptoms of depression, anxiety and/or PTSD 20 . On cardiovascular safety specifically, a pooled analysis of 536 sessions found modest, transient blood-pressure elevation resolving within five hours, prompting the authors to recommend reconsidering some conservative cardiovascular exclusion thresholds 21 . Safety data specific to adults aged 65 and older remain limited -a systematic review identified only 19 participants of that age across 36 psilocybin trials, with no serious adverse events reported among those for whom safety data were available, though the sample is small 4 . A case report of transient improvement in speech, memory, and mobility following psilocybin in a patient with advanced Alzheimer's disease illustrates both a preliminary signal and the boundary of the autonomy argument developed below 22 . 3. Barriers to Access in Palliative Care Populations

Evidence is necessary but not sufficient for access. Three barriers recur across the jurisdictions and populations reviewed here, and each bears directly on whether a palliative patient who might benefit from psilocybin-assisted therapy can obtain it.

3.1 Representation and evidentiary circularity

Older adults and patients with the progressive, multi-morbid conditions typical of palliative care have historically been excluded from psychedelic trials, often without a stated rationale: a review of trials in four major medical journals found that 92.8% of studies with an upper age limit gave no explanation for the cut-off 5 , and only 1.4% of participants across 36 psilocybin trials were aged 65 or older 4 . This creates what might be called evidentiary circularity: the absence of data on a population is then used to justify continuing to exclude it, a pattern visible in regulatory decisions such as Australia's 2026 interim scheduling review, which cited limited supporting evidence for psilocybin use in existential distress at end of life 23 . PsyPal and the Hopkins ALS trial are notable precisely because they are structured to break this cycle rather than reproduce it.

3.2 Cost and the Current Regulatory Framework

Where legal access exists, it is often expensive. In Australia, a course of pharmaceutical-grade psilocybin therapy for treatment-resistant depression costs approximately AUD 20,000 24.

Oregon's regulated services cost approximately USD 1,500-3,000 per session, and Colorado's licensed healing centers charge a broadly comparable up to USD 3,500 per session 25, 26 . Reported program data from Oregon show pronounced income skew among clients: about 43% earn USD 95,376 or more annually, only about one in ten falls below a Medicaid-eligibility proxy income line, and two-thirds travel in from outside the state 27 . This cost is driven less by the strength of the underlying clinical evidence than by the current regulatory framework a product is filed under: establishing a substance as a registered medicine requires GMP production and a full marketingauthorization pathway, while the same effect delivered outside that framework, as with the Netherlands' food-law-based sale of psilocybin-containing truffles, can be substantially cheaper, illustrating that systematic exclusion by cost is a policy choice rather than a scientific necessity. Without parallel funding or subsidy mechanisms, removing systematic regulatory barriers to access risks simply replacing them with a wealth-based one, a concern directly relevant to palliative populations, who often face high care costs already. Even the furthest-advanced medicines-pathway program illustrates how slow that route remains even as it nears completion: Compass Pathways' COMP360 met its primary endpoint in both pivotal Phase 3 trials for treatment-resistant depression by early 2026 28 , and 52-week open-label data reported in September 2026 showed sustained benefit through one year and no new safety signals, with the U.S. new drug application completed on November 9, 2026, under a Commissioner's National Priority Voucher targeting a one-to-two-month review 29 , and commercial launch anticipated in the first half of 2027, subject to FDA approval 30 ; no jurisdiction had completed marketing authorization for it at the time of writing. However, if authorized, this therapy is expected to be expensive and, being indicated for treatment-resistant depression, unavailable to palliative patients who do not carry that diagnosis. The nonprofit Usona Institute completed a parallel application for psilocybin in major depressive disorder on November 2, 2026 31 ; if approved, it would widen labeled eligibility from treatment-resistant to major depression, but neither application addresses existential distress in serious illness, so palliative patients without a qualifying depressive diagnosis would remain outside both labels. Session staffing adds to any drug price: FDA's final guidance on psychedelic clinical investigations (July 2026) recommends two trained monitors for the full duration of each dosing session 32 . No official list price had been published at the time of writing, but a company executive indicated in September 2026 that annual cost was expected to approximate that of existing FDA-approved intranasal esketamine therapy (USD 50,000-65,000 per year), which, given the trial's multi-dose regimen, would imply a price per administration well above independent bottom-up cost estimates of roughly USD 3,000-15,000 per episode; this remains an informal signal rather than a confirmed price. 33

3.3 A case in point: reversible relief versus irreversible choice

The tension these barriers create is illustrated starkly by a Canadian patient with terminal lung disease who applied for legal psilocybin access under Health Canada's Special Access Program, backed by his own physicians 34 . After an eleven-month wait, the application was denied; weeks later, the same patient was approved for medical assistance in dying 35 . The juxtaposition -slow or refused access to a potentially reversible intervention against rapid approval for an irreversible one -raises a direct question for palliative systems about whether current thresholds are internally coherent. This sits within a broader pattern: a 2026 survey of 121 Canadian palliative care stakeholders found overall attitudes toward psilocybin-assisted therapy for existential distress were only 39% favorable, concealing a striking split in which 95% of physicians specifically reported favorable views; the barrier stakeholders identified as primary was a shortage of trained providers, not physician resistance, and 68% favored introducing the therapy earlier in a life-limiting illness rather than reserving it for the very end of life 1 . A related Canadian case, that of Thomas Hartle, the first Canadian granted legal psilocybin access in 2020 for cancer-related anxiety, is now the namesake of Bill C-286 ("Thomas's Bill"), introduced in the Canadian Parliament in June 2026 to reform the existing access pathway 36.

4. Ethical Considerations: Individualized Eligibility and Informed Consent

The case for individualized eligibility rests on a narrower claim than that psilocybin is proven beneficial for palliative populations: that a patient who understands the disclosed risks, uncertainties, and limitations of a supervised psilocybin experience should be able to accept or decline it. This should be subject to the same requirements of capacity, voluntariness, and clinical screening applied to other consequential decisions in serious illness care. This argument does not depend on waiting for definitive trial results, because the population being asked to wait is frequently the same population least represented in the trials that would produce that evidence, and because for patients with a terminal or rapidly progressive diagnosis, time itself is a scarce resource that systematic exclusion spends without their consent.

Systematic exclusion by age or diagnosis replaces individualized clinical judgment with a blunt rule. This is not a new tension in palliative care ethics, which already routinely balances patient autonomy against paternalistic caution in decisions with comparable stakes such as enrollment in early-phase trials. Age-based exclusion specifically is difficult to justify on the evidence reviewed in Section 2: it is not that older or multi-morbid patients have been shown to tolerate psilocybin poorly, but that they have rarely been studied, and the two are not the same finding. Where the evidence base does raise a genuine caution, it is cardiovascular and psychiatric risk assessed at the level of the individual patient, not chronological age or diagnostic category as such 21, 37.

Physician and public information gaps compound these barriers. Decades of restrictive scheduling have kept psilocybin outside most medical curricula, and a 2024 survey of U.S. healthcare professionals found generally low objective knowledge of its benefit-risk ratio and uses, with physicians reporting less openness than nurses 38 . For palliative care specifically, closing this gap is a precondition for the kind of individualized assessment this section argues for: clinicians cannot assess a treatment option they have not been trained to discuss. This concern about burden on services and providers is real, particularly given the workforce shortage identified in the Canadian survey above 1 , but it is an argument for investment in training and capacity, not for withholding the option from patients who might benefit.

5. Regulatory Approaches and Equity Considerations

Jurisdictions currently take markedly different approaches to eligibility. Oregon's regulated psilocybin services, established under Measure 109, require individualized preparation and screening by licensed facilitators rather than an upper-age rule 39 . The Netherlands permits psilocybin-containing truffles under food law, available to any adult without individualized clinical screening, an approach that lowers cost and removes age-based barriers entirely. Narrower medical-necessity pathways, including Canada's Special Access Program discussed above, Australia's authorized-prescriber framework, and comparable routes in Germany, Switzerland, and the Czech Republic, sit between these poles, generally permitting access case-by-case but at substantial cost and with variable waiting times. New Mexico's Medical Psilocybin Act (2025) is notable for listing end-of-life care among its qualifying conditions alongside treatment-resistant depression, PTSD, and substance use disorders, with first patients targeted by December 2026 40 ; it shows that a diagnosis-gated framework can recognize palliative need directly rather than requiring a qualifying psychiatric diagnosis. Belgium's proposed compassionate use program, submitted to the Federal Agency for Medicines and Health Products and supplied with GMPmanufactured botanical psilocybin, is by contrast limited to treatment-resistant depression 41 : a patient with existential distress in a life-limiting illness who does not meet those criteria would not qualify, illustrating the diagnosis-list problem described in this article.

5.1 Regulatory frameworks around the globe

The regulatory frameworks governing psilocybin access span the full range from unrestricted retail sale to full prohibition with extraterritorial enforcement, and this range matters for palliative populations because it, more than any single piece of clinical evidence, determines whether a patient can obtain psilocybin-assisted therapy at all (Table 2). Several Asian jurisdictions illustrate the restrictive end of this range and are worth naming alongside the European and North American frameworks discussed above. South Korea classifies psilocybin as a controlled psychotropic substance requiring regulatory approval even for trace-amount academic research and, unusually, asserts extraterritorial jurisdiction over its own citizens, so that a Korean national who used psilocybin lawfully in a permissive jurisdiction can still be prosecuted at home 42, 43, 44 . Japan closed a legal loophole in 2002 to ban psilocybin-containing mushrooms outright, with penalties comparable to those for cocaine, though a clinical trial for treatment-resistant depression has nonetheless been conducted at Keio University Hospital, Asia's first modern psilocybin trial (a 12patient, single-arm, open-label study 45 ) 46 . China's narcotics-control framework provides no medical or research exception for psilocybin 47 . Where no lawful route exists, palliative need does not disappear: a November 2026 report from Tokyo describes a family who, after reading about psilocybin-assisted therapy abroad, gave a mother with ALS an unidentified psilocybin "analogue" bought at a head shop, in a market where municipal test purchases have found products that did not match their labels 48 . The anecdote carries no evidential weight on efficacy, but it shows the safety cost of prohibition for palliative patients. These restrictive regulatory frameworks matter for palliative care specifically because they foreclose not only patient access but the clinical research needed to eventually inform it, reinforcing the evidentiary circularity described in Section 3.1.

Their existence suggests that the gap between documented palliative need and permitted access is not a fixed cultural preference but a policy choice subject to revision as evidence accumulates, the same conclusion this article reaches for the jurisdictions discussed above.

6. Implications for Palliative Care Policy and Practice

Four findings translate directly into recommendations for palliative care systems and policymakers.

Move from systematic to individualized eligibility. Where psilocybin-assisted therapy is legally available, eligibility criteria should assess cardiovascular status, psychiatric history, and decision-making capacity in each patient rather than applying blanket age or diagnostic exclusions not supported by the evidence reviewed in Section 2.

Set inclusion targets in future trials.

Funders and trial sponsors should require minimum representation of older adults and patients with the progressive, multi-morbid conditions typical of palliative populations, building on the precedent PsyPal and the Hopkins ALS trial have already set, so that age-specific and diagnosis-specific evidence continues to accumulate rather than stalling once these two programs report.

Pair any expansion of access with funding mechanisms. Jurisdictions considering broader psilocybin-assisted therapy access should build in public funding, subsidy, or insurance coverage from the outset, so that reduced systematic exclusion does not simply shift the barrier from age or diagnosis to ability to pay.

Invest in provider education.

Closing the documented gap in clinician knowledge of psilocybin's risks and uses is a precondition for the individualized clinical assessment this article recommends, and should be incorporated into palliative care training specifically, given the workforce shortage identified as the primary barrier in the Canadian stakeholder survey 1.

Practitioner points

Systematic age-or diagnosis-based exclusion from psilocybin-assisted therapy is not supported by the evidence currently available and should, where access is legally permitted, be replaced by individualized assessment of relevant physical, psychiatric, and capacity-related risk factors.

Clinicians should distinguish informed-consent capacity, which most palliative patients retain, from proof of medical benefit, which remains under active investigation in trials such as PsyPal and the Hopkins ALS study.

7. Conclusion

The evidence base for psilocybin-assisted therapy in serious and life-limiting illness is shifting quickly, from a foundation built almost entirely on oncology and healthy-volunteer studies toward trials, pioneered by the PsyPal project and the Johns Hopkins ALS program, designed specifically for the diagnostically diverse, often older population palliative care actually serves. Neither program has yet reported results, and this article does not argue that psilocybin can be treated as an established medicine for existential distress in serious illness. It argues for a narrower yet more immediate principle: where legal access already exists, eligibility should be determined by individualized clinical assessment of capacity, risk, and voluntariness (personal autonomy) rather than by systematic exclusions, including age-based cut-offs, that the evidence reviewed here does not support. The barriers documented in this review -evidentiary circularity, cost, regulatory fragmentation, and gaps in clinician training -are policy choices, not scientific necessities, and each is addressable independent of how the ongoing trials resolve. As PsyPal, the Hopkins ALS trial, and comparable programs report their results over the coming years, palliative care systems have an opportunity to build eligibility frameworks now that will not need to be rebuilt once that evidence arrives.
