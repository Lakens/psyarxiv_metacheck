Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

Full text

Abstract

Alexithymia is a dimensional trait including challenges labeling emotions. Alexithymia is elevated in autistic individuals relative to the general population and is suggested to be part of the broader autism phenotype. Alexithymia is also independently associated with emotion regulation challenges, which are more prevalent among autistic persons, and with two of the most common co-occurring conditions among autistic people: depression and anxiety. A better understanding of alexithymia, including its neural correlates, therefore promises to advance our understanding of risk profiles for mental health problems in autistic people.

We therefore sought to: 1) characterize alexithymia in autistic (n=54) and neurotypical (n=56) adolescents and young adults (15-25y) including group and sex differences; 2) examine group and sex differences in brain volume in eight regions of interest implicated in alexithymia; and 3) explore potential interactions between group and brain volume as predictors of alexithymia.

Autistic individuals self-reported significantly higher levels of alexithymia relative to neurotypical individuals, with autistic females reporting the highest levels and neurotypical males the lowest levels. There were no differences in group or sex based on brain volume.

Exploratory models predicting alexithymia found a significant interaction between group and right dorsal anterior cingulate cortex volume.

Introduction

Background

Alexithymia (derived from a compilation of the Greek morphemes a-'without', -lexis-'word', and -thymos 'feeling' to mean 'no words for feelings') (Sifneos, 1973) is a subclinical dimensional trait that includes difficulties mapping subjective experiences to words, as well as broader difficulties in understanding emotional experiences, such as distinguishing emotions from bodily sensations (Nemiah et al., 1976). Alexithymia is more common in autistic adults (~33-63% autistic adults) (Berthoz & Hill, 2005; Hill et al., 2004; Kinnaird et al., 2019; Milosavljevic et al., 2016; A. Samson et al., 2012) relative to adults in the general population (~7-17% of adults in the general population) (Franz et al., 2008; Joukamaa et al., 2003; Mason et al., 2005; Salminen et al., 1999). Alexithymia is also elevated in the first-degree biological relatives of autistic persons (Szatmari et al., 2008), motivating the proposal that alexithymia is part of the broader autism phenotype (Berthoz et al., 2013; Szatmari et al., 2008).

In autism, alexithymia is associated with behavioral and brain differences in processing others' emotions, suggesting that socio-emotional autistic traits may be attributable not to autism per se but to co-occurring alexithymia, termed the "alexithymia hypothesis of autism" (Bird & Cook, 2013). Consistent with this hypothesis, differences among autistic individuals in the crossmodal processing of emotions in others, including the production (Trevisan et al., 2016) and recognition (Cook et al., 2013; Ketelaars et al., 2016; Ola & Gullon-Scott, 2020) of emotional facial expressions, as well as the processing of emotion prosody (Heaton et al., 2012) is associated with alexithymia.

In addition to alexithymia being elevated among autistic individuals and associated with differences in their socio-emotional processing, alexithymia is also associated with psychiatric conditions that commonly co-occur with autism. In particular, alexithymia is associated with depression and anxiety (Lever & Geurts, 2016; Lugo-Marín et al., 2019), both of which occur at significantly elevated rates among autistic adults compared to general population estimates (Hedley et al., 2017; Hollocks et al., 2019; Hudson et al., 2019; Lugo-Marín et al., 2019). In autistic samples, higher levels of alexithymia are associated with increased depressive (Bloch et al., 2021; Morie et al., 2019) and anxious (Maisel et al., 2016) symptoms. Further, alexithymia is associated with emotion regulation challenges (Cai et al., 2018), which are common in autistic individuals (Conner et al., 2021; Mazefsky et al., 2014; Mazefsky & White, 2014; A. C. Samson et al., 2014). Emotion regulation challenges are themselves also associated with both depressive and anxious symptoms (Conner et al., 2023) and categorical depression and anxiety diagnoses (Charlton et al., 2020). Moreover, alexithymia may interfere with adaptive emotion regulation strategies in autistic persons (Mazefsky et al., 2014), such as cognitive reappraisal. Given that these strategies are an important component of current frontline psychotherapeutic approaches to depression and anxiety, such as cognitive behavioral therapy (Clark, 2022; Wang & Yin, 2023), greater understanding of how internalizing symptoms and alexithymia interrelate in autism may serve as a key step towards optimizing mental health care for autistic people.

In addition to associations of alexithymia with behavioral measures of emotion processing, both structural and functional neuroimaging studies of alexithymia in non-autistic populations implicate brain regions that are known to be involved in emotion processing. For instance, regions involved in bottom-up emotion processing, such as the amygdala (Goerlich et al., 2017; Goerlich-Dobre, Lamm, et al., 2015; Goerlich-Dobre, Votinov, et al., 2015; Ihme et al., 2013; Li et al., 2024; Xu et al., 2018), as well as regions implicated in more top-down processing of emotions, including the insula (Borsci et al., 2009; Goerlich-Dobre et al., 2014; Goerlich-Dobre, Votinov, et al., 2015; Hogeveen et al., 2016; Ihme et al., 2013; Xu et al., 2018; Zhang & Peng, 2023) and the anterior cingulate cortex [ACC] (Borsci et al., 2009; Goerlich-Dobre et al., 2014; Goerlich-Dobre, Votinov, et al., 2015; Grabe et al., 2014; Ihme et al., 2013; Paradiso et al., 2008; Sturm & Levenson, 2011; van der Velde et al., 2013 van der Velde et al., , 2014) ) are among those brain regions most commonly associated with alexithymia.

Among extant studies examining alexithymia and brain volume, evidence relatively consistently indicates that higher levels of alexithymia are associated with smaller volume in amygdala and insula (Goerlich-Dobre, Lamm, et al., 2015; Goerlich-Dobre, Votinov, et al., 2015; Ihme et al., 2013; Laricchiuta et al., 2015), though one study reported larger volume in the posterior insula for persons with high alexithymia compared to those with low alexithymia (Goerlich-Dobre et al., 2014). In contrast, studies are more mixed with respect to the anterior cingulate cortex, with some studies finding smaller volume in the ACC to be associated with higher alexithymia levels (Borsci et al., 2009; Paradiso et al., 2008) while other studies have found larger ACC volume is associated with higher alexithymia levels (Goerlich-Dobre et al., 2014; Ihme et al., 2013).

Notwithstanding the "alexithymia hypothesis of autism" and the proposal that alexithymia is part of the broader autism phenotype, most of our knowledge of brain-alexithymia associations are based on non-autistic populations. For instance, a recent systematic review and meta-analysis of 15 studies that compared alexithymia in autistic and neurotypical persons (Kinnaird et al., 2019) included a total of three studies directly examining brain-alexithymia associations, using task-based (Schneider et al., 2013; Silani et al., 2008) or resting-state (Hoffmann et al., 2016) fMRI.

Among the comparatively small number of studies that have examined brain and alexithymia in autistic individuals, findings are consistent with those in the broader literature, including associations of alexithymia with both bottom-up emotion processing regions such as the amygdala (Bird et al., 2010) as well as top-down emotion processing regions, including insula (Bernhardt et al., 2014; Bird et al., 2010; Butera et al., 2023) and ACC (Silani et al., 2008). All but one extant study including autistic persons has examined brain function: work by Bernhardt and colleagues (2014) is the only study to our knowledge that has examined brain structure and alexithymia associations. In this study, alexithymia and cortical thickness was examined in a small (n=16 per group) sample of autistic and non-autistic persons. No significant between-group differences in average cortical thickness, and no significant differences in cortical thickness based on alexithymia levels were identified (Bernhardt et al., 2014). Structural covariance analyses revealed that fronto-insular cortical thickness was modulated by level of alexithymia irrespective of group (i.e., autistic vs. non-autistic), where persons with higher levels of alexithymia had lower structural covariance of the left fronto-insular seed region (selected as the seed region based on its associations with empathy and interoception) with ipsilateral supramarginal, anterior temporo-parietal, and lateral occipital regions (Bernhardt et al., 2014).

Research objectives and hypotheses

Thus, given that alexithymia i) is substantially elevated in autistic individuals (and indeed may be a facet of the broader autism phenotype), ii) shows brain and behavioral associations with core autism traits in processing emotion in others, and iii) is associated with emotion regulation challenges and risk for depression and anxiety (all of which are elevated in autistic relative to non-autistic individuals), a better understanding of alexithymia, including its neural correlates, promises to advance understanding of one factor that may contribute to mental health disparities in autistic persons.

We therefore examined self-reported alexithymia, brain structure, and alexithymia-brain structure associations in a sample of autistic [AUT] and neurotypical [NT] youth and young adults. Our three aims were to 1) characterize alexithymia in AUT and NT youth and young adults and to examine potential group and sex differences in alexithymia; 2) examine potential group and sex differences in brain volume in four bilateral ROIs (eight regions in total) that have been implicated in associations with alexithymia in the literature (i.e., amygdala, insular cortex, ACC); and 3) conduct exploratory analyses examining potential interactions between group (AUT, NT) and gray matter volume as predictors of alexithymia.

Based on the literature indicating that autistic people demonstrate higher levels of alexithymia relative to NT individuals (Gerber et al., 2019) and literature indicating people assigned male sex report higher levels of alexithymia relative to people assigned female sex (Levant et al., 2009; Mendia et al., 2024), we predicted the following pattern of alexithymia, where AUT persons assigned male were hypothesized to have the highest levels and NT persons assigned female the lowest levels of alexithymia: AUT male > AUT female > NT male > NT female.

Because the ROIs were selected based on their associations with alexithymia in the literature, and because we predicted higher levels of alexithymia in AUT versus NT persons, we predicted that we would see between-group differences in volume in these regions (either increased or decreased volume, given the mixed findings reported in the literature on associations between brain volume and alexithymia in non-autistic individuals).

For the exploratory aim, we did not have specific predictions; rather, we were interested in exploring potential interactions between group (AUT, NT) and brain volume in the eight ROIs in predicting alexithymia levels.

Methods

Participants

Participants included in analyses here comprised a subsample of youth and their caregiver recruited from community and academic/clinical research centers for inclusion in AUT and NT groups as part of a longitudinal NIH-funded Autism Centers of Excellence [ACE] network (National Institutes of Health Data Archive Collection #2021). More specifically, those participants with usable/complete data on the measures of interest (see below) from both Time 1 and Time 2 were eligible for inclusion in analyses here. Participants completed neuroimaging and phenotypic assessment at Time 1 and behavioral assessment at Time 2, with an average of 5.7y between timepoints. There were three data collection sites involved in data that were collected at both timepoints: University of California, Los Angeles; University of Washington/Seattle Children's Research Institute; and Yale University. Additionally, a data analytic and coordination center was located at The University of Virginia.

Inclusionary criteria in both the AUT and NT groups included: usable structural magnetic resonance imaging [MRI] data at Time 1 (see section below entitled "MRI data acquisition and processing" and Supplemental Materials for details concerning structural MRI data quality assurance); aged 15y or older at Time 2; and completion (defined as missing <20% of item-level data) of the 20-item Toronto Alexithymia Scale (Bagby, Parker, et al., 1994; Bagby, Taylor, et al., 1994) at Time 2. For all participants, IQ was estimated at Time 1 via the Differential Ability Scales, Second edition [DAS-II] (Elliott, 2007). Study inclusionary criteria for both the AUT and NT groups further required at least one DAS-II metric to be ≥70 For participants in the AUT group, expert clinicians confirmed DSM-based diagnoses of an autism spectrum condition, informed by administration of the Autism Diagnostic Observation Schedule, Second Edition [ADOS-2] (module 3 or module 4, as deemed appropriate by the clinician conducting the assessment) (Lord et al., 2012) and the Autism Diagnostic Interview-Revised [ADI-R] (Rutter et al., 2003). For the purposes of the current study, to be included in the AUT group participants must have met diagnostic criteria on either the ADOS-2 or the ADI-R, and the clinician's impression must have been consistent with an autism spectrum diagnosis. For additional details concerning inclusionary and exclusionary criteria for the broader study, see Jack et al. (2021).

The final study sample included 56 AUT (Mean age=13.8y, SD=2.5; n=25 (44.6%) female sex assigned at birth) and 54 NT (Mean age=14.1y, SD=2.5; n=29 (53.7%) female sex assigned at birth) participants. Caregivers provided informed written consent, and youth provided written assent. Procedures were conducted in compliance with the sites' IRBs and the Declaration of Helsinki.

MRI data acquisition and processing

At Time 1, participants completed a T1-weighted structural MRI scan. Whole-brain T1weighted anatomical images were acquired using the Siemens MPRAGE (magnetizationprepared rapid gradient-echo) sequence. At the beginning of Time 1 data collection, all sites scanned on a Siemens 3 Tesla [T] Tim Trio magnet. Partway through data collection, two of the three sites upgraded to the Siemens 3T Prisma Fit. Data collection on a Tim Trio used a 12channel head-coil, and collection on a Prisma Fit used a 20-channel head-coil. As detailed below, scan site was included as a nuisance regressor in all MRI analyses, and data acquired pre-and post-upgrade were modeled as coming from different sites (see Table 1). Imaging parameters were as follows: TR=2530 ms; TETim Trio=3.31 ms/TEPrisma Fit=3.34 ms; flip angle=7°; FOV=256 mm; image matrix=256 mm 2 ; voxel size=1 mm 3 ; 176 slices; NEX=1.

Prior to MRI data processing, structural MRI scans were scrutinized for motion-related and other artifacts to determine the usability of each participant's scan. Details of this quality control procedure are provided in the Supplemental Materials.

Structural MRI data were processed in FreeSurfer v.7.0 using the default settings of the recon-all pipeline (Dale et al., 1999; Fischl et al., 1999 Fischl et al., , 2004)). FreeSurfer is a well-validated, open-source, automated software suite that is widely used, including in multisite neuroimaging studies (Hagler et al., 2019; Thompson et al., 2014). Briefly, using the anatomical MRI images as input, FreeSurfer performs a series of steps including registration of each individual's brain to a standard space, intensity normalization, skull-stripping, and image segmentation.

We obtained estimates of cortical and subcortical volumes using the Desikan-Killiany atlas (Desikan et al., 2006). Measurements were volume (in cubic millimeters [mm 3 ]) from eight regions of interest [ROIs] as well as estimated total intracranial volume [ICV]. ROIs were as follows: amygdala, insula, dorsal anterior cingulate cortex [dACC] (corresponding to the FreeSurfer Desikan-Killiany atlas label "caudal anterior cingulate"), and rostral anterior cingulate cortex [rACC]. Volume in each ROI was normalized by estimated total ICV prior to analyses.

Measures Demographics

Sociodemographic information was collected from caregivers of participants at Time 1. This information included sex assigned at birth (information regarding gender identity was not captured), age, ethno-racial identity, age at autism diagnosis, and maternal educational attainment.

Alexithymia: 20-item Toronto Alexithymia Scale

Alexithymia was assessed at Time 2 only via self-report on the 20-item Toronto Alexithymia Scale (Bagby, Parker, et al., 1994; Bagby, Taylor, et al., 1994).

Participants answered questions on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from1="Strongly Disagree" to5 ="Strongly Agree". The total TAS-20 score is generated by summing item responses after reverse scoring five items.

The TAS-20 is the most widely used measure of alexithymia globally (Bagby et al., 2020), with strong psychometric properties, including good-to-excellent test-retest reliability (Bagby et al., 2020). In its initial validation, the TAS-20 total score demonstrated good internal consistency reliability in undergraduate university student and psychiatric outpatient samples (Cronbach's α=0.80 and 0.83, respectively) (Bagby, Parker, et al., 1994). The TAS-20 has been used in autistic samples and has shown similarly good internal consistency reliability in these samples (Cronbach's α=0.85) (Lin et al., 2025). In general population adolescent samples, Cronbach's α for the total TAS-20 has been shown to vary with age, with internal consistency reliability below acceptable levels for younger adolescents (13-14y; α=.68), but acceptable reliability for those aged 15-16y (α=.75), and good reliability for those 17-18y (α=.82) and 19-21y (α=.82) (J. D. A. Parker et al., 2010). This finding regarding age-related differences in TAS-20 reliability informed the current study's rationale for administering the TAS-20 only to those individuals enrolled in the ACE study aged 15y and older. In the current study, internal consistency reliability was good for the total sample (Cronbach's α=0.82) and for the AUT and NT samples when considered separately (Cronbach's α=0.79 for both groups).

Autistic traits

For the purposes of sample characterization, total T-scores from caregiver-report at Time 1 on the Social Responsiveness Scale, Second Edition [SRS-2] (Constantino & Gruber, 2012) are presented as a continuous measure of autistic traits. Higher SRS-2 scores indicate greater caregiver-rated autistic traits.

Statistical approach

All statistical analyses were conducted in R v 4.5.2 (R Core Team, 2026).

Aim 1 statistical approach: Characterizing alexithymia and examining potential group (NT, AUT) and sex differences Given that checks revealed violations of assumptions for parametric testing (Box's Homogeneity of Covariance Matrices Test: χ²=136.04, p=.04; Shapiro-Wilk Multivariate Normality Test: W=0.91, p=.000003), we used a non-parametric multivariate analysis of covariance [MANCOVA] to examine potential group, sex, and group by sex differences in brain volume. Specifically, we used the adonis2 function from the vegan (Oksanen et al., 2025) package in R, with 10,000 permutations. Prior to analyses, scan site, where data acquired preand post-upgrade were considered to come from different sites (see Table 1), was regressed out of each ROI, which had been normalized by ICV.

The residualized and normalized values for the eight ROIs served as dependent variables.

Group (coded as NT=0; AUT=1), sex (coded as male=0; female=1), and the interaction of these, were independent variables of interest, while age at the time of MRI was included as a covariate of no interest. If the interaction term was not statistically significant, the term was dropped from the model and main effects were examined.

Aim 2 statistical approach: Examining group (NT, AUT) and sex differences in brain volume in ROIs Potential differences in self-reported alexithymia at Time 2 were examined using multiple linear regression implemented via the base R lm function. All variables were entered in a single step. The TAS-20 total score served as the dependent variable with group, sex, and their interaction as independent variables of interest. If the interaction term was not statistically significant, it was dropped from the model and main effects were examined. Age at TAS-20 completion was included as a covariate of no interest. All models were examined to confirm that parametric model assumptions were met (see additional details below concerning metrics used).

Aim 3 statistical approach: Exploratory analyses examining potential interactions between group (NT, AUT) and gray matter volume as predictors of alexithymia

To explore whether ROI volume and volume-by-group interactions might predict TAS-20 total scores, we fit eight multiple linear regression models, one for each of the eight ROIs. We elected to fit eight models and to eschew corrections for multiple comparisons due to the exploratory nature of these analyses: given that we are aware of no work that has examined brain volume and its associations with alexithymia in autistic individuals (either separately or in comparison with non-autistic individuals), we elected to prioritize discovery and thus the minimization of Type II (relative to Type I) error.

The TAS-20 total score served as the dependent variable. The residualized and normalized values (as described above) for each of the eight ROIs served as independent variables, along with group (NT, AUT), and their interaction. Independent variables were entered in a single step. Age at TAS-20 completion was included as a covariate of no interest.

Discussion

This study had three aims: to 1) characterize alexithymia in AUT and NT individuals, including possible group and sex differences in alexithymia; 2) examine differences in gray matter volume by group and sex within eight ROIs identified in the broader literature as linked to alexithymia and to emotion processing more broadly; and 3) to explore possible group, brain region and group by brain region interactions in predicting levels of alexithymia.

Characterization of alexithymia in the NT and AUT samples

Consistent with our predictions for Aim 1, AUT individuals self-reported significantly higher levels of alexithymia relative to NT individuals, after accounting for assigned sex and controlling for age at survey completion. There was no interaction of group and assigned sex, nor was there a significant main effect of sex in the regression model.

Group differences in alexithymia

In accordance with the regression model results, AUT individuals were >3 times more likely to meet the TAS-20 cutoff indicative of "high alexithymia". This finding is aligned with the literature, although the proportion of autistic persons classified as "high alexithymia" is lower than that reported in some studies (e.g., Williams & Gotham, 2021) and higher than that reported in others (Oakley et al., 2022). For instance, while we found that over a third of the autistic individuals met the TAS-20 cut-off, Williams and Gotham (2021) found that more than half (54.5%) of autistic adults aged 18 to 45 years were categorized as "high alexithymia", compared to 17.1% of a general population comparison sample of persons aged 18 to 60 years. In autistic individuals aged 12 to 30, a sample with an age range more similar to that in the current study, Oakley and colleagues (2022) found that autistic individuals self-reported significantly higher levels of alexithymia relative to same-aged non-autistic individuals, with 29.1% of autistic persons meeting the cutoff of ≥61 compared to 4.4 % of the non-autistic sample (Oakley et al., 2022).

Sex differences in alexithymia

Contrary to our prediction of higher alexithymia levels among male individuals, alexithymia levels did not statistically differ by sex; moreover, descriptively, the observed pattern of alexithymia levels was as follows: AUT female > AUT male > NT female > NT male. This finding is not consistent with existing literature in the general population, which has indicated that male persons report higher levels of alexithymia relative to female persons (Levant et al., 2009; Mendia et al., 2024). To better understand this unexpected pattern of results, additional analyses using the TAS-20 cutoff indicative of "high alexithymia" (total score of ≥61) were conducted within the AUT and NT groups separately. In the AUT sample, significantly more female than male individuals assigned met the cutoff of ≥61. Within the AUT group, it was more than twice as common for people assigned female sex at birth to be categorized as "high alexithymia" relative to those assigned male sex. As in the current study, Oakley et al. (2022) found a higher proportion of autistic persons assigned female sex at birth met the cutoff relative to persons assigned male sex at birth.

Associations between age and alexithymia

In these cross-sectional data, age was significantly inversely associated with TAS-20 total scores in linear regression models, and exploration of the data indicated this finding was driven by the AUT group. These results suggest a possible difference between the AUT and NT groups, wherein the association between older age and lower alexithymia scores is not as prominent in AUT as in NT.

Research indicates alexithymia decreases across adolescence (Gatta et al., 2014; Säkkinen et al., 2007; van der Cruijsen et al., 2019). Decreases in levels of alexithymia observed from early to late adolescence have been suggested to reflect development in emotional awareness and emotion regulation skills (Kekkonen et al., 2021), which would be consistent with evidence that emotion regulation strategy use changes with age, including the increased use of cognitive reappraisal (considered an "adaptive" emotion regulation strategy) with age (Nolen-Hoeksema & Aldao, 2011; Zimmermann & Iwanski, 2014). Alexithymia is associated with emotion dysregulation (Cai et al., 2018) and with the development and maintenance of disorders such as anxiety and depression in which emotion regulation challenges feature prominently (Aldao, 2016; Aldao et al., 2016; Fernandez et al., 2016; Lincoln et al., 2022). Emotion labeling, compromised in persons with higher levels of alexithymia, is itself a form of implicit emotion regulation (Torre & Lieberman, 2018; Zhu et al., 2026). It is interesting to speculate that agerelated development in emotional awareness and emotion regulation may differ for AUT relative to NT individuals and that these differences may contribute to increased vulnerability for anxiety and depression in AUT compared to NT individuals.

The pattern of age-alexithymia associations reported here differs from other age-related findings in the literature, where older age is associated with higher, not lower, levels of alexithymia (Mattila et al., 2006; Salminen et al., 1999). However, these studies have not included adolescents but rather were comprised of adults (including persons in middle and older adulthood). Additionally, and of relevance to the current findings, a meta-analysis of emotion regulation in persons aged 1 to 63y reported that the magnitude of difference in emotion dysregulation severity in autistic versus non-autistic persons was moderated by age, with greater differences between autistic and non-autistic persons with increasing age (McDonald et al., 2024). Given connections between alexithymia and emotion (dys)regulation (Cai et al., 2018; Mazefsky & White, 2014) this may suggest that with increasing age neurotypical, but not autistic persons, show improvements in emotion labeling and emotion regulation strategy use and decrements in emotion dysregulation.

Future studies implementing longitudinal designs should examine potential developmental effects of alexithymia in autistic relative to non-autistic individuals including earlier and later in the course of the lifespan to better understand potential similarities and differences in stability and change in alexithymia over time.

Brain volume and alexithymia

The second aim of the study was to examine differences in gray matter volume by group and sex (including potential group by sex interactions) within eight ROIs. The ROIs were selected based on the literature, which has identified associations between these regions and alexithymia. Contrary to our predictions, we found no significant group differences in volume for any of the eight ROIs. Specifically, after controlling for total intracranial volume, scan site, and scan age, no significant group differences were found for amygdala, insula, dorsal anterior cingulate cortex or rostral anterior cingulate cortex in either hemisphere.

Extant research examining volumetric differences (either using ROI-based or whole brain approaches) between autistic and non-autistic persons has produced mixed findings. For large scale investigations of brain morphometric differences in autistic versus non-autistic persons see Haar et al.,(2016) and van Rooij et al., (2017); and for a metanalysis see Del Casale et al., (2022). Potential explanations for the null findings in the current study relative to previous studies that have identified differences between autistic and non-autistic individuals include differences in study sample size and characteristics, including the proportion of persons assigned male sex at birth in the samples. There is thus a need to understand ways in which structural brain differences may link to phenotypic characteristics such as alexithymia, as opposed to simply examining group-level structural differences.

Brain volume and group interactions in alexithymia: Preliminary findings

The third aim of the study was to explore possible group by ROI interactions in predicting alexithymia. These exploratory analyses revealed a significant group by ROI interaction for a single ROI: the right dorsal anterior cingulate cortex [dACC]. Specifically, the association between (residualized and normalized) right dACC volume and self-reported alexithymia, after controlling for age at TAS-20 completion, varied as a function of group, where the association was positive for AUT individuals but negative for NT individuals.

The ACC, with connections to both limbic system structures and the prefrontal cortex, is implicated in the integration of bottom-up and top-down emotion processing (Stevens et al., 2011). Because of its position and connections within these larger brain systems associated with emotion and cognitive processing, the ACC is likely an important region in emotion regulation (Stevens et al., 2011). Consistent with such a role, larger dACC volume in women in the general population has been associated with cognitive reappraisal (but not expressive suppression) (Giuliani et al., 2011).

We emphasize that the examination of associations between alexithymia and the group and brain volume interaction term was exploratory. While brain volume was normalized by intracranial volume and confounding variables including scan site and age were controlled for, eight separate regression models were conducted (one per ROI); given that we sought to prioritize discovery (and thus minimize Type II error), corrections for multiple comparisons were not implemented. At the same time, the null findings for seven of the ROIs examined should not be interpreted as indicating that these regions are not important in understanding alexithymia in AUT individuals or in understanding differences in alexithymia between AUT and NT persons.

Rather, the findings reported here should be considered as a first step in interrogating potential relationships between brain volume and alexithymia in AUT versus NT individuals. Relatedly, the null findings here should not be interpreted to indicate that these regions should not be included as ROIs in future research examining brain structure. It may hold more promise, however, to examine these ROIs and their associations with alexithymia and differences between AUT and NT individuals in studies that examine brain function, including intrinsic functional connectivity, as brain function may provide insights into systems-level differences and thus may be more sensitive (relative to brain structure) to group differences and group-by-alexithymia interactions.

Limitations

The data examined in exploratory Aim 3 were collected at two time points. Having the ability to examine brain and behavioral data collected contemporaneously would have been desirable. However, one advantage of collecting behavioral data later than the MRI scan is that it afforded the opportunity to include younger participants in the study sample. The MRI scan was collected prior to the TAS-20, and there is evidence that the TAS-20 demonstrates poorer psychometric properties among individuals younger than 15y (J. D. A. Parker et al., 2010).

Brain and behavioral data reported here were both cross-sectional. Collecting data at multiple timepoints would have provided for the ability to examine change in brain structure and alexithymia, including potential associations between changes in brain structure and alexithymia, as well as within-person effects of age on alexithymia. Particularly given the age-related findings in the current study, and the broader literature on age associations with alexithymia, which hint at possible developmental differences in alexithymia from early adolescence to young adulthood, the ability to examine such developmental effects, including potential impacts on mental health outcomes and disparities in these outcomes for autistic relative to neurotypical persons, is an important avenue for future research.

Conclusion

In a sex-balanced sample of autistic adolescents and young adults, this study found that autistic individuals self-report significantly higher levels of alexithymia relative to neurotypical individuals, and that autistic persons assigned female sex at birth report significantly greater levels of alexithymia relative to autistic persons assigned male sex at birth. Follow-up analysis of age was suggestive of potential developmental differences in autistic persons relative to neurotypical persons. These group-based age differences warrant additional investigation, particularly including the collection of longitudinal alexithymia data.

Findings concerning interactions between brain volume and group (AUT vs. NT) in predicting alexithymia are exploratory. However, the findings concerning dACC, a region associated with affective processing and integration, suggest that future work examining connectivity between and among ROIs in the current study, for instance using resting-state functional MRI, may hold promise in elucidating associations between alexithymia and brainneurotype interactions. The 8-item general alexithymia factor score [GAFS-8] was developed by Williams and Gotham (2021) using item response theory to derive a subset of TAS-20 items that demonstrated psychometric robustness in autistic and non-autistic adults and that did not show differential item functioning. The GAFS-8, comprised of eight items from the TAS-20, does not include any reverse-scored items. Participants answer questions on the same 5-point Likert scale as the TAS-20, ranging from1="Strongly Disagree" to5 ="Strongly Agree". In the current study, internal consistency reliability for the GAFS-8 score (generated by summing item responses) was good for the full sample (Cronbach's α=0.86), as well as for the AUT (Cronbach's α=0.85) and NT (Cronbach's α=.84) samples separately.

In a posteriori statistical analyses for Aim 1 and exploratory Aim 3, we used GAFS-8 zscores, which are norm-referenced latent trait scores. We generated these z-scores from a calculator using R code obtained from the authors who proposed the GAFS-8 (personal communication, Z.J. Williams, February 14, 2026). This code is also available as a free online score calculator (Z. Williams, 2021). We note that the z-scores are based on a normative sample of individuals aged 18+. Although the current study sample includes individuals aged 15+, the raw GAFS-8 scores (which is a simple sum of item responses) and the z-scores were highly correlated in our sample (rs=0.88, p<.001); thus, we elected to use the z-scores in analyses.

Additional information concerning inclusionary/exclusionary criteria for current study autistic and neurotypical groups

To be included in the AUT group, individuals must have met the algorithm cut-off on the Autism Diagnostic Observation Schedule, Second Edition [ADOS-2] (Lord et al., 2012), or the Autism Diagnostic Interview-Revised [ADI-R] (Rutter, Le Couteur, et al., 2003), where for the latter, individuals were required to meet the measure's algorithm cut-off for each of the three domains (10 for Social Interaction; 8 for Communication; and 3 for Restricted, Repetitive Behaviors & Interests) (Lord et al., 1994).

Additional exclusionary criteria for the NT group included the following: diagnosed, referred or suspected autism spectrum disorder or suspicion by the assessing clinician at the site of autism; or total T-score on the Social Responsiveness Scale, Second Edition (Constantino & Gruber, 2012) >60; or a raw score of on the Social Communication Questionnaire, Lifetime version (Rutter, Bailey, et al., 2003) >11. Individuals recruited into the NT group were also excluded if they had a first-or second-degree biological relative with a diagnosis of an autism spectrum disorder.

In addition, individuals in the NT group were excluded if the caregiver reported a diagnosed, referred or suspected psychiatric, learning or other neurodevelopmental condition, or if the site's assessing clinician suspected a psychiatric condition, such as schizophrenia or bipolar disorder, that would limit engagement in daily activities.

Additional information concerning quality control procedures for MRI data

Prior to MRI data processing, structural MRI scans were rated by a team of trained research assistants. Specifically, scans were visually inspected (with raters blinded to AUT vs NT group status) for motion-related and other artifacts. Each scan was rated by at least two independent raters. To ensure standardization across raters, training included rating a set of 20 scans assigned to all raters. After training on these 20 scans, raters were assigned batches of unprocessed MRI scans. Each scan was rated by at least two independent raters. Scans were rated on a scale from 0 to 2 as follows:0="unusable scan,"1="usable but not high quality" and2 ="usable." "Unusable" designated those scans with severe motion and/or other artifacts that made them unsuitable for use in FreeSurfer. Scans rated as "usable but not high quality" were scans that showed, for example, some evidence of motion artifacts but not severe artifacts that would render them unusable for processing and analyses. Differences between ratings and scans rated as "1" (i.e., "usable but not high quality") were discussed during group meetings. In these cases, the final determination of whether to include or exclude a scan was made by one of the study authors with expertise in structural MRI analyses (G.L.W.).

Additional information concerning model assumptions and the use of bootstrap confidence intervals

All models were examined to ensure that model assumptions were met. Metrics examined included variance inflation factor [VIF] to probe for multicollinearity, and testing for outliers or influential cases by examining residuals. For any cases identified as having large residuals (greater or less than 2), Cook's distance (threshold of <1), leverage (threshold determined based on calculating average leverage (k+1)/n where k is the number of predictors and n the sample size and looking for values two to three times as large as this value), and covariance ratios (calculated as 1± [3(k+1)/n]) were scrutinized. No problematic multicollinearity was identified (all VIF values <2). For all eight models, covariance ratios for cases with larger residuals (which ranged between 1 to 6 cases) were slightly lower (or in one instance slightly larger) than the recommended values computed based on the number of predictors. However, inspection of Cook's distance and all other metrics were within recommended limits, moderating concern of the influence of these cases (Field et al., 2012).
