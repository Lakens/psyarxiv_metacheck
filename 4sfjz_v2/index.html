Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. Some differences come from the ground truth: it lacks p-values with a correction name (“p Bonf = .061”), and counts partial eta squared written with its subscript after it (“η 2 p = .05”), “p = .05”, and some table notes and figure legends (“† p < .10, *p < .05”), which this module leaves out, as non-significant p-values. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Introduction

Oppositional Defiant Disorder (ODD) and Conduct Disorder (CD) are disruptive behavior disorders that typically emerge in childhood and adolescence. ODD is characterized by a persistent pattern of anger, irritability, and defiant or vindictive behaviors, while CD involves behaviors that violate others' rights and societal norms. 1 These two disorders are among the most common behavioral conditions affecting youth, with estimated lifetime general population prevalence rates of 10% for ODD and 8% for CD. 2, 3 Rates are even higher in clinical settings, where disruptive behaviors are frequently a top problem driving referral and motivating continued engagement with treatment. 4 Though often grouped together as behavior/conduct problems, ODD and CD exhibit distinct risk factors, 5, 6 developmental pathways, 7, 8 and underlying brain activities, 9 underscoring the need to discuss them as separate conditions and identify youth at risk for one or both disorders. Without effective interventions, both disorders could lead to serious consequences, including diminished quality of life, 10 academic failures, 11, 12 and increased risk of developing mood and substance use disorders. 2, 11, 13 Accurate diagnosis of ODD and CD is crucial for matching evidence-based treatments like behavioral parent training 14 and psychotropic agents 15 to appropriate cases, while avoiding reliance on treatments with limited benefits, 16 thus improving outcomes for children with disruptive behaviors.

However, the diagnosis of ODD and CD faces significant diagnostic reliability challenges. While structured diagnostic interviews like the Schedule for Affective Disorders and Schizophrenia for School-Age Children-Present and Lifetime Version (K-SADS-PL) demonstrate strong reliability and establish an upper diagnostic bound (inter-rater kappa=.77 for ODD and .83 for CD), 17 practitioner diagnoses based on clinical evaluations often deviate substantially from these gold-standard diagnoses. For disruptive behavior disorders overall, clinician-generated diagnoses agreed with gold-standard diagnoses only 44% of the time, 18 with meta-analyses reporting mean kappa values of 0.43 for ODD and 0.34 for CD, 19 all indicating poor-to-fair reliability. Even when comparing diagnoses made by independent clinicians using usual clinical methods, agreement remains modest. DSM-5 field trials found kappa=0.40 for ODD and 0.46 for CD, 20 indicating only fair consistency between practitioners. When ODD and CD cannot be reliably assessed and identified by clinicians, there are real-world consequences: many children with ODD and CD symptoms may be misdiagnosed or go undiagnosed entirely, adding barriers to their access to medical attention and targeted interventions. More effective assessment tools are urgently needed to help clinicians accurately and reliably identify children with these disorders.

Clinical prediction models exist along a spectrum of complexity, from single-predictor tools to algorithms incorporating hundreds of features. Across this spectrum, Bayes' theorem provides a foundational inferential structure by describing how an initial probability estimate is updated with new evidence to yield a refined, evidence-informed probability of diagnosis. A probabilistic nomogram operationalizes this reasoning in a simple graphical format, enabling clinicians to visually trace how risk factors shift the diagnostic likelihood. Frequently used in evidence-based medicine 21 and evidence-based assessment, 22, 23 nomograms function as a transparent visual interface that requires minimal computational resources and statistical training.

Their accessibility and interpretability have supported calls for incorporating such tools into mental health fields to guide diagnoses, treatment planning, and progress monitoring. 24, 25 Machine learning encompasses a family of algorithms that are mathematically intricate and computationally intensive. Built around carefully selected algorithms and training procedures, machine learning can model complex, often non-linear patterns directly from large datasets, without requiring prior specification of which features are relevant or how they contribute to the outcome. Studies have demonstrated machine learning's encouraging performance in identifying individuals at risk for mental health conditions or differentiating patients from healthy controls across multiple psychiatric disorders, including bipolar spectrum disorder, 26 depression, 27 schizophrenia, 28 and others. 29 This capacity to detect patterns across high-dimensional data often comes at the cost of interpretability, with many algorithms functioning as "black boxes" relative to simpler graphical tools such as nomograms. The resulting models cannot be executed by a human and require software or computing infrastructure to score new cases and return a probability estimate. 30, 31 Despite the demonstrated utility of nomograms and machine learning models in certain psychiatric disorders, their effectiveness in predicting current ODD and CD diagnosis remains largely unexplored, and these methods' feasibility in clinical applications needs further investigations. More importantly, limited systematic attention has been paid to how these approaches perform across different clinical conditions and how their respective strengths position them as complementary tools. This gap is particularly concerning given the substantial clinical burden of ODD and CD and the documented challenges in their accurate diagnoses. The present study addresses this gap by leveraging two large clinical datasets of youth (n>500 each) to train both a parsimonious, clinician-executable nomogram and a set of machine learning algorithms for identifying children with ODD or CD. By evaluating accuracy, calibration, and generalizability across models, this study provides a systematic evaluation of each method's strengths and limitations and offers practical guidance for their clinical applications.

Methods

Participants

This study is based on the Stanley 32 and the ABACAB (Assessing Bipolar: A Community Academic Blend; NIH R01MH066647) 33 datasets. Participants were 5-to-18-year-old youth seeking outpatient mental health services in a Midwest city in the United States. The Stanley sample was recruited from an academic medical center, while the ABACAB sample was 80% community mental health center-recruited and 20% from the same academic center. We reorganized the two datasets into an academic sample (n=622) and a community sample (n=506) based on recruitment site, with the academic sample serving as the main training and testing data and the community sample as an external test. Table 1 presents characteristics of each sample. To quantify the sample differences, we used Cohen's d with 95% Confidence Interval (CI) for all variables, with effect sizes of dichotomous variables converted from Odds Ratio. 34, 35 Predictors Demographics: child age, sex, and race.

Diagnoses. Researchers interviewed the child and primary caregiver using the K-SADS-PL 17 to give DSM-IV 36 diagnoses. Diagnosis of mood disorders, bipolar spectrum disorders, ADHD, anxiety disorders, PTSD, and the total number of K-SADS diagnoses serve as predictors to account for child's comorbidity and clinical burden.

Behavior indicators. Primary caregivers completed the Child Behavior Checklist (CBCL) 37 . We treated each CBCL subscale T score as an individual predictor in machine learning models. We also included three aggregated CBCL T scores on externalizing, internalizing, and total problems to capture broad symptom dimensions. For probability nomograms alone, we used CBCL raw scores to avoid score conversion and enhance simplicity of clinical application.

Mood indicators. Clinicians administered the Young Mania Rating Scales (YMRS) 38 and Child Depression Rating Scales-Revised (CDRS-R) 39 to evaluate child' manic and depressive symptoms. Models

Probability Nomogram is a graphical tool that helps clinicians determine the probability of disease after a diagnostic test. The nomogram consists of three parallel vertical axes: probability estimate before testing (left axis), the strength of the diagnostic test's result, known as the likelihood ratio (middle axis), and the updated probability based on test result (right axis).

Clinicians locate the pre-test probability on the left axis and the likelihood ratio on the middle axis, draw a straight line through these points, and read the post-test probability on the right axis.

A single-predictor nomogram is mathematically equivalent to a logistic regression model with one binary predictor, while multiple predictors can be incorporated by drawing nomograms iteratively and updating the probability. Though family history is well-established as a quantifiable risk factor for mood disorder, aggressive behavior does not have similar quantifiable risk factors, 25 so we used a single-predictor nomogram as the baseline clinical tool.

Logistic Regression (LR)

estimates the probability of a diagnosis based on a combination of input variables. It handles all predictors simultaneously and combine them into a probability score between 0 and 1. A score closer to 1 indicates higher diagnosis likelihood, while a score closer to 0 indicates lower likelihood. This flexibility makes logistic regression more powerful than a nomogram when multiple evidences are available. Through estimated coefficients, individual predictors remain fully interpretable, though the resulting model is less visually intuitive than a nomogram.

Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) extended logistic

regression by adding a filtering step that guards against weak or redundant predictors.

Specifically, LASSO applies a mathematical penalty that progressively reduces the influence of less informative predictors, ultimately removing weak ones entirely. This built-in variable selection retains only strongest predictors, while preserving the interpretability of each predictor through its coefficient. In the current study, we also included all pairwise combinations of predictors as inputs, allowing LASSO to detect predictor interactions that enhance the model performance.

Support Vector Machine (SVM) is designed for classification. SVM works by plotting each case in a multidimensional space where each predictor defines one dimension, then identifying the boundary that separates the two diagnostic groups with the greatest possible margin. A tuning parameter, C, controls the trade-off between accuracy and generalizability, while kernel determines whether that boundary is drawn as a straight line or a curved one capable of capturing non-linear patterns. After a parameter search (Supplemental Table 1), we used the radial basis function (RBF) kernel for ODD classification and the linear kernel for CD classification.

Random Forest (RF) makes diagnostic decisions by combining the output of individual decision trees. 41 Each decision tree works like a structured flowchart, repeatedly splitting the data into smaller subgroups based on predictor values until a diagnostic classification is reached.

Because each tree is built on a different random sample of predictors and data, the trees collectively cover a wide range of patterns. The final prediction is determined by majority vote across all trees, making the overall model more stable and generalizable. Guided by literature 42 and parameter search (Supplemental Table 2), we grew 500 trees, with each tree having a random subset of 3-6 predictors at each split.

Procedure

We trained classification models for ODD and CD diagnoses separately. For probability nomograms, we identified the strongest predictor in the academic cohort and determined its optimal threshold that balances between sensitivity and specificity. We then cross-validated this nomogram in the community cohort. For machine learning models (LR, LASSO, SVM, RF), we trained each model on the academic sample for ODD/CD outcomes using the caret package in R version 4.6.0, 43 assessed model performance using stratified 10-fold cross-validation, a technique where data is split into 10 equal subsets, each used once for validation while the model trains on the other nine, with performance averaged across all iterations. Stratified sampling preserves the class prevalence of ODD and CD diagnoses within each fold, ensuring that performance estimates remain stable and representative across all cross-validation partitions. We then evaluated model generalizability on the community sample.

As a supplementary analysis, we trained models on the community sample and validated them on the academic sample, reversing the roles of the two settings, to examine whether model performance was influenced by clinical characteristics of the training sample. This represents an unconventional setup, as research data are typically collected in academic centers rather than community clinics, and the results should be interpreted with caution.

Metrics

Accuracy defines how well a model correctly distinguishes children who have a diagnosis from those who do not. Accuracy was evaluated with Area Under the Curve (AUC).

By plotting the true-positive rate against the false-positive rate, we get a Receiver Operating Characteristics (ROC) curve, and by calculating area under ROC curve, we get an AUC.

AUC=0.50 equals random chance, and AUC=1.00 equals perfect prediction. AUC values ≥0.70, ≥0.80, and ≥0.90 are interpreted as fair, good, and excellent discrimination, respectively. 44 Calibration refers to how closely a model's predicted probabilities match the actual probabilities of diagnosis in the data. We assessed model calibration with Spiegelhalter's z (Sz). 45 Positive Sz suggests the model under-predicts the event, and a negative Sz indicates the model over-predicts the event.

|Sz| ≤ 1.96 signals good calibration, 1.96<|Sz|<3.28 suggests moderate misfit, and |Sz|≥3.28 is significant miscalibration. Generalizability measures how well a model maintains its accuracy when applied to new patients. We assessed model generalizability by quantifying discrimination differences between model's internal and external performance. The accuracy change is represented as ΔAUC, calculated as AUC external -AUC internal , where smaller absolute values indicate stable cross-site performance, and larger absolute values indicate reduced generalizability to new settings.

Results

Sample Characteristics (Table 1)

The academic sample (n=622), being the training sample, was 39% female, averaged 11.49 years old (SD=3.3), and was 79% White. Participants averaged 2.2 K-SADS diagnoses (SD=1.3), while ODD and CD were present in 31% and 8%, respectively. For other diagnoses, mood disorders were most prevalent (68%), followed by ADHD (55%) and bipolar disorder (44%), with less common anxiety disorder (14%) and PTSD (2%). Participants showed elevated externalizing (M=66.6, SD=11.8) and internalizing (M=64.9, SD=11.5) symptoms on the CBCL, with mean scores of 10.3 (SD=7.9) on the PGBI-10M, 11.6 (SD=12.1) on the YMRS, and 35.3 (SD=16.4) on the CDRS-R.

The community sample (n=506), as the validation dataset, was 42% female, averaged 10.9 years old (SD=3.2), and was 90% Black. Participants averaged 2.7 K-SADS diagnoses (SD=1.4); ODD and CD were present in 39% and 13%, respectively. ADHD (66%) and mood disorders (41%) were most prevalent, followed by anxiety disorder (27%), bipolar (12%), and PTSD (10%). On the CBCL, participants showed elevated externalizing (M=70.3, SD=9.4) and internalizing (M=63.0, SD=10.5) symptoms, with mean scores of 7.5 (SD=6.4) on the PGBI-10M, 6.1 (SD=8.3) on the YMRS, and 30.2 (SD=13.3) on the CDRS-R.

The two samples differed substantially in racial composition, with the academic sample being predominantly White (d=2.26) and the community sample being predominantly Black.

Clinically, the academic sample demonstrated higher rates of mood disorders (d=0.60) and bipolar disorder (d=0.95), alongside greater manic (d=0.53) and depressive symptoms (d=0.34).

In comparison to the community sample, the academic sample had significantly lower rates of ODD (d=-0.18), CD (d=-0.31), anxiety disorder (d=-0.47), PTSD (d=-0.86), ADHD (d=-0.24), and lower total K-SADS diagnoses (d=-0.38) and CBCL externalizing symptom (d=-0.34).

Taken together, the community sample demonstrated an under-resourced population with heavier clinical burden and a mix of behavioral and mood disorders.

The sample differences are representative of the clinical landscape in the U.S.

Midwestern city where participants were recruited. Research has similarly found that patient characteristics differ systematically across clinic types, 46 though patterns may not mirror those observed in the present study. The marked differences across clinical settings underscore the importance of including populations that represent full spectrum of children seeking mental health services and systematically examining the validity of findings across diverse settings.

ODD Prediction with Machine Learning

Four machine learning algorithms trained on the academic sample demonstrated strong performance in predicting ODD diagnosis (see Figure 2A, B). The logistic regression model, built on 23 predictors, achieved good discrimination (AUC=.85), meaning it could correctly distinguish between ODD and non-ODD cases 85% of the time. However, it showed moderate miscalibration (Sz = 2.39), indicating a tendency to overpredict ODD risk. The LASSO model, drawing from 276 predictors including all two-way interactions, selected 21 predictors and balanced accuracy and calibration wonderfully (AUC=.86, Sz=-0.14). The near-zero Sz suggests that LASSO's probability estimates closely matched actual ODD diagnoses. The SVM model, with a radial basis function kernel (sigma=0.01, C=10), showed good accuracy (AUC=0.87) and almost-perfect calibration (Sz=-0.16). The Random Forest model, sampling six predictors at each split, demonstrated the highest discrimination of all models (AUC=.88), correctly classifying cases nearly 88% of the time, but it exhibited moderate miscalibration (Sz=2.38) and overpredicted ODD risk similarly to logistic regression. ODD Model Generalizability

When applied to the community sample, the nomogram maintained borderline fair discrimination (AUC=.67), whereas machine learning models showed substantial performance deterioration (Figure 2A, C). LR, LASSO, and SVM performed only marginally better than the one-predictor nomogram in terms of discrimination. Specifically, LR model demonstrated reduced predictive power (AUC=.71) and systematic overprediction (Sz=8.38), while LASSO and SVM similarly underperformed in external validation with sharp declines in both metrics (LASSO: AUC= .69, Sz=6.85; SVM: AUC=.70, Sz=7.70). Among all machine learning classifiers, RF model demonstrated the strongest generalizability for ODD prediction: discrimination showed the most modest decline (AUC=.75), while calibration improved (Sz=0.79).

CD Prediction with Probability Nomogram

The nomogram identified the CBCL Rule-Breaking Subscale as the strongest predictor of CD diagnosis and achieved good discrimination (AUC=.82). At the optimal threshold of 6.5 (raw score range: 0-34), the nomogram reached sensitivity=.85 and specificity=.65, indicating strong performance as a diagnostic prediction tool that captures most true CD cases while accepting some false positives. Based on the histograms (Figure 1B), non-CD cases centered around score 0-10 with a tight, peaked distribution, whereas CD cases showed a wider, flatter distribution spanning score 2-28. Substantial overlap between the two groups exists in the 5-10 range, explaining the moderate specificity, as many non-CD children exhibit some rule-breaking behaviors. However, scores above 15 were almost exclusively associated with CD diagnosis, suggesting higher severity levels are more diagnostically specific. Supplemental Figure 1B presents a T-score-based nomogram on CBCL Rule-Breaking (score range: 50-100), showing AUC=.810, sensitivity=.863, and specificity=.686 at a threshold of 68.5.

Machine learning models showed strong predictive power for CD diagnosis, though calibration varied by algorithm (see Figure 3A, B). The logistic regression model exhibited strong accuracy (AUC=.88) but severe miscalibration (Sz=3.50), presenting a risk of overprediction. In contrast, LASSO, SVM, and random forest models all achieved excellent discrimination with well-calibrated predictions (LASSO: AUC=.90, Sz=-0.20; SVM: AUC=.88, Sz=0.049; RF: AUC=.88, Sz=-0.86). CD Model Generalizability

When externally validated in the community sample, the nomogram built on CBCL rule breaking preserved good discriminatory power (AUC=.82), demonstrating robust performance across clinic settings. Machine learning models, by contrast, encountered notable generalizability challenges (see Figure 3A, C), dropping from near excellent to fair discrimination (LR AUC=.73, LASSO AUC=.76, SVM AUC=.76, RF AUC=.75). Despite the shared accuracy declines, distinctions emerged in calibration patterns. The logistic regression model showed increasingly severe miscalibration in the external test (Sz=5.86), while LASSO, SVM, and random forest models maintained reasonable calibration across samples (LASSO Sz=0.45, SVM Sz=-.94, RF Sz=-.45), with only mild underprediction tendencies.

Reversed Training: From Community to Academic

As shown in Figure 4, community-trained ODD ML models achieved internal AUCs of .70-.73, which modestly increased to .75-.78 upon external validation, while nomogram maintained AUC slightly below .700 at both sites. Community-trained CD models showed similarly stable performance, retaining AUCs around .820 internally and externally. However, these results should be interpreted cautiously and may not reflect a generalizable advantage of community-trained models. This pattern may be specific to behavior disorders in our sample, where ODD and CD prevalence rates were higher in the community clinic, a condition may not hold across other community samples. Indeed, prior research using the same datasets did not reveal a comparable generalizability pattern for bipolar disorders, 47 suggesting that the current finding is likely to be disorder-and sample-specific.

Discussion

This study evaluated the feasibility of using probability nomograms and machine learning to predict current diagnoses of child behavioral disorders. We assessed the accuracy, calibration, and generalizability of a nomogram and four machine learning algorithms, examining each method's advantages and limitations for identifying children with ODD or CD. Across both disorders, a consistent pattern emerged: the nomogram, built on a single well-chosen predictor, offered modest accuracy with strong cross-site stability, whereas machine learning models achieved superior accuracy in the training setting that attenuated when transported to an independent clinic.

For ODD prediction, the nomogram built on the CBCL Aggressive Behavior subscale consistently showed fair discrimination (AUC≈.70) across the academic and community samples. The four machine learning algorithms achieved good discrimination and clustered at AUC values of .85-.87 in the academic sample, but their performance deteriorated sharply to fair accuracy when applied to the community setting, with AUC dropping to .70-.75. For CD prediction, the nomogram built on the CBCL Rule-Breaking subscale achieved good accuracy (AUC≈.82) across both settings. The machine learning models approached the theoretical upper bound of K-SADS accuracy (AUC≈.87-.90) in the original sample. Yet this level of accuracy did not generalize to the community setting, with AUCs dropping to .73-.76. These cross-sample accuracy declines align with a broader concern in the clinical prediction model literature: models with strong internal validation can show considerable heterogeneity in discrimination when transported to a new setting, underscoring the frequently underestimated uncertainty in how well a model will generalize. 48 Several features of our data speak to why this deterioration was concentrated in the machine learning models.

As noted, the academic and community samples demonstrated different clinical presentations and demographic compositions, which could partially account for the accuracy deterioration of machine learning models. 49 Machine learning algorithms with larger feature sets captured important ODD and CD signals, which boosted their discriminatory power, but these signals may be specific to an academic setting and thus apply less well in a community mental health clinic, resulting in the generalizability challenge. Parsimonious nomograms, built on one clinically grounded feature, are less susceptible to overfitting and tend to demonstrate more stable performance across settings.

The predictor rankings in Supplemental Figure 3 illustrate a well-documented challenge associated with ML models regardless of interpretability level. Their feature selection is susceptible to multicollinearity, the high intercorrelations among predictors that destabilize coefficient estimates and obscure each variable's independent contribution to the outcome. For instance, CBCL-Aggression, perhaps the most face-valid behavioral indicator of ODD, did not appear among the top 10 predictors in logistic regression and ranked only eighth in LASSO importance. LASSO compounds this by incorporating interaction terms (e.g. child sex interacted with comorbid diagnosis), which could shift probability estimates but do not translate into actionable signals clinicians can apply to decision-making. This interpretive challenge extends further with SVM and random forest, where feature contributions cannot be directly read from model output and are approximated through post-hoc analyses. Clinical interpretability remains a limitation across machine learning models.

Given our findings, machine learning presents a valuable but context-dependent opportunity to improve the diagnosis of behavioral disorders. Successful implementation requires clinics to possess computational infrastructure and technical support, with the critical prerequisite that models have been validated in settings closely resembling the target clinical environment. For under-resourced clinics or those lacking validation data from comparable populations, probability nomograms offer an accessible, manually calculable tool that can enhance diagnostic accuracy. These two approaches are supplementary rather than competing: machine learning for well-resourced settings with validated local models, and nomograms as a robust solution that prioritizes consistency over maximum accuracy.

Four distinct machine learning algorithms demonstrated comparable accuracy and generalizability, indicating that model performance was primarily driven by sample and disorder characteristics rather than algorithm choice. Nevertheless, evaluating multiple models remained valuable, as algorithm-disorder combinations could yield meaningfully different results. For instance, the random forest model on ODD prediction exhibited the smallest accuracy drop (ΔAUC=-.12) and improvement in calibration (Sz=-2.38 to 0.79) during external validation, suggesting that ensemble structure may confer greater robustness to domain shift. Conversely, logistic regression models consistently showed moderate or severe overprediction of diagnoses across both disorders, raising concerns about their utility for actual deployment.

We acknowledge several limitations in the current study. First, our models were developed using diagnoses from K-SADS interviews administered by well-trained research interviewers, so the ODD and CD diagnoses had higher inter-rater reliability and diagnostic validity than typically seen in routine clinical practice. If nomograms and machine learning models were trained on billing diagnoses (diagnostic codes recorded primarily to satisfy insurance reimbursement requirements) or diagnoses from unstructured interviews, model performance could differ substantially. Therefore, our findings apply best to clinical diagnoses derived from structured interviews, and the finding validity with diagnoses from other sources remains to be tested. Second, this study focused exclusively on supervised machine learning algorithms with labeled outcomes. We did not explore unsupervised models or deep learning architectures (e.g. neural networks) which may offer unique insights into the prediction of ODD and CD. These alternative approaches could potentially reveal feature interactions not captured by our supervised methods and demonstrate different performance and generalizability patterns.

Third, our models were built entirely on clinical assessment data without biological or neuroimaging measures. Consequently, our approach focuses exclusively on symptom presentation and does not explore the neurobiological mechanisms underlying the disorders.

Integration of neurobiological markers into modeling could potentially enhance diagnostic accuracy and provide insights into the psychophysiological processes of behavior disorders.

Fourth, our external validation was conducted in a single independent sample, which precluded more systematic evaluation of generalizability, such as empirical Bayesian estimation of between-setting variance in discrimination. 48 Future work should evaluate individual predictors, such as sex differences in presentation, then develop parsimonious models with comparable accuracy. Identifying the most influential predictors would support machine learning models that are computationally efficient and more accessible in resource-limited settings, while inspection of predictor weights and significance would yield clinical insights into the development of behavior disorders grounded in statistical evidence. Researchers should also prioritize external validation across multiple settings and quantify model generalizability more rigorously, thus moving beyond single-site demonstrations toward evidence needed to support real-world clinical adoption.

In conclusion, this study evaluated probability nomograms and machine learning algorithms for predicting behavioral disorder diagnoses, demonstrating that each approach offers distinct strength. Nomograms provided modest but stable accuracy across settings, while machine learning achieved higher discrimination in the training environment with greater sensitivity to site shift. These findings support a complementary framework in which tool selection is guided by clinical context and available resources. Ultimately, effective diagnostic tools must balance predictive accuracy with considerations of generalizability and clinical implementation.
