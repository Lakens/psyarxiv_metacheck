Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 22 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. Abstract

Developmental language disorder is a highly common yet underdiagnosed neurodevelopmental disorder which impacts receptive and/or expressive language abilities and cannot be attributed to another etiology such as hearing loss or a lack of early exposure to language. There is very little research into the brain differences that characterize developmental language disorder, especially in adult populations In this study, we used diffusion MRI to determine whether adults with DLD show differences in white matter microstructure of the brain when compared to adults with typical language. Participants included 23 adults identified as meeting criteria for developmental language disorder, as well as a group of 26 adults with typical language. White matter microstructure was examined with fractional anisotropy via diffusion tensor imaging and precise biophysical models of intra-axonal signal fraction and orientation coherence via the Standard Model of diffusion imaging, using tractometry to evaluate microstructure differences along white matter tracts. Analysis showed reduced myelin integrity in DLD in the corpus callosum, but increased myelin integrity in the left superior longitudinal fasciculus. It is yet unclear whether differences exist in childhood and are retained into adulthood, are a result of different language environments during childhood, or are a result of behavioral and neural compensation.

Introduction

Developmental Language Disorder (DLD) is a neurodevelopmental disorder that affects expressive and/or receptive language skills, but cannot be attributed to other etiologies such as hearing loss, intellectual disability, neurological damage, or a lack of early exposure to language (Bishop et al., 2017). DLD is a prevalent disorder affecting approximately 7.6% of the population, yet it is under-identified and under-researched (Leonard, 2014; Norbury et al., 2016; Tomblin et al., 1997). Neuroimaging research in particular has been limited, with markedly few studies in adults with DLD (Abbott & Love, 2023) which has hindered progress toward a neurobiological account of DLD. Of interest to this study are differences in white matter microstructure in adults with DLD, as white matter carries the connections between the frontal and temporal regions that support language, and disruptions to these pathways could plausibly contribute to the language difficulties observed in DLD (Friederici, 2015).

White Matter Brain Differences in DLD

Diffusion-weighted imaging characterizes white matter microstructure by quantifying the directionality of water diffusion within a voxel. Fractional anisotropy (FA), the most commonly reported summary metric, reflects the degree to which diffusion is constrained along a primary axis: values approach 1 in highly organized, unidirectional tissue and approach 0 where diffusion is unconstrained, as in cerebrospinal fluid (Huisman, 2010). FA is generally thought of as a proxy measure of white matter integrity, but can index different aspects of white matter, including myelination of the white matter tract, axon diameter and density, and the amount or manner of crossing fibers (Budde et al., 2007; Friedrich et al., 2020).

Across the available diffusion literature in DLD, a consistent finding is altered diffusivity in language-related white matter tracts, though this work has been conducted almost entirely in children and remains sparse relative to that in other language-impaired populations (McGregor, 2020). Vydrova et al. (2015) found reduced FA bilaterally in the arcuate fasciculus (AF), inferior fronto-occipital fasciculus (IFOF), inferior longitudinal fasciculus (ILF), and uncinate fasciculus (UF) in children with DLD (ages 6 to 12) relative to typically developing peers. Verly et al. (2019) similarly found reduced FA in the left middle longitudinal fasciculus (MdLF) and ILF in children with DLD (ages 10 to 11), alongside increased FA in the right superior longitudinal fasciculus (SLF). More recently, Svaldi et al. (2026) applied fixel-based analysis, a diffusion approach that resolves crossing fibers with greater biological specificity than the tensor model, and found converging differences in the arcuate, uncinate, and superior longitudinal fasciculi, suggesting these tract-level alterations are not simply an artifact of DTI's methodological limitations. Differences in these same tracts also appear to be at least partly specific to DLD, as work comparing children with DLD to children with autism and language impairment has found dissociable diffusion profiles in the left SLF (Verhoeven et al., 2012) and left AF (Roberts et al., 2014).

Beyond canonical language tracts, at least one study has identified differences in more domain-general white matter. Kim et al. (2006) reported reduced FA in the genu of the corpus callosum in a group of young children with language impairment (ages 2.5 to 6.5 years; though it is unclear whether these children were eventually diagnosed with DLD). Myelination of the corpus callosum is thought to progress from more posterior to anterior regions, with the genu maturing later than other regions of the corpus callosum (Kochunov et al., 2012). Thus, reduced FA in this anterior region in DLD may be indicative of a delay in the maturational patterns observed in typically developing children. The corpus callosum has also been implicated in developmental dyslexia, where structural and microstructural differences in posterior callosal regions have been linked to reading and phonological processing abilities (Hasan et al., 2012; Vandermosten et al., 2012; von Plessen et al., 2002). While most models of dyslexia and DLD consider them distinct disorders with considerable co-occurrence, this literature raises the broader possibility that interhemispheric connectivity via the corpus callosum may be relevant to language and literacy development more generally.

Together, these findings point to a relationship between the language impairments that define DLD and altered white matter diffusivity. To date, however, only one study (Lee et al., 2020) has examined whether these diffusion differences persist into adulthood, despite behavioral evidence that the language impairment itself persists (Botting, 2020; Del Tufo & Earle, 2020; McGregor et al., 2020; Whitehouse et al., 2009). Lee et al. (2020) examined adolescents and adults with DLD (ages 14 to 27) and found reduced FA bilaterally in the SLF and IFOF and in the right UF relative to controls. FA also increased with age in the right SLF, left ILF, and right UF among typically developing participants but not among those with DLD, a divergence consistent with the typical trajectory of white matter FA increasing across the first three decades of life before peaking and declining in later adulthood (Kochunov et al., 2012).

Given the findings of reduced FA in both children and adults with DLD, it is possible that changes in mean FA either follow a delayed trajectory, peaking later in life, or remain stable from childhood to early adulthood, rather than increasing over time in DLD. The limited number of studies and heterogeneity of methodological approaches leave this question unresolved.

Current Study

The goal of the current study was to build on the findings from Lee et al. (2020) to further elucidate if and where adults with DLD show differences in white matter microstructure when compared to adults with typical language. Whereas Lee et al. used an atlas-based region of interest (ROI) approach to examine FA in predefined white matter tracts, the present study used automated fiber quantification (pyAFQ; Yeatman et al., 2012), a tractometry approach that segments individual white matter pathways directly from each participant's tractography data and characterizes microstructure at fine spatial resolution along the length of each tract. This approach provides greater anatomical specificity than atlas-based methods, enabling us to identify not only which tracts differ between groups but where along each tract those differences are most pronounced.

Based on Lee et al.'s finding of reduced FA in the SLF, IFOF, UF, and ILF in adults with DLD, we hypothesized that adults with DLD would show reduced FA along the same languagerelevant association tracts. We further hypothesized that reduced FA in these tracts would relate to poorer performance on standardized language assessments, consistent with a relationship between white matter integrity and language ability in this population. Given the lack of studies in adults with DLD generally, we employed a whole-brain analysis approach (corrected for multiple comparisons) rather than only assessing pre-specified tracts.

Additionally, because FA reflects a composite of multiple tissue properties, including axonal density, myelination, and fiber coherence, it does not identify which specific biological mechanisms underlie white matter differences. FA is also sensitive to acquisition parameters and scanner hardware, meaning its values are not fully comparable across studies or sites even when underlying tissue microstructure does not differ (Thieleking et al., 2021; Vollmar et al., 2010). To address these limitations, we also examined biologically specific metrics derived from the Standard Model of diffusion imaging (SMI; Coelho et al., 2022; Novikov et al., 2018), including intra-axonal signal fraction (f, an index of axonal density) and orientation coherence (p2, an index of axonal alignment). Because f and p2 are grounded in specific biophysical or physical models rather than a composite diffusion signal, they are more readily comparable across participants and studies than FA. Together, these metrics allow us to characterize not only whether white matter differs between groups, but what specific tissue properties drive those differences.

Methods Participants

Participants included 49 adults. Twenty-six adults met criteria for typical language development (mean age: 21.4  3.5; range 18-32 years) and 23 met criteria for DLD (mean age: 22.7  4.7; range 18-37; see Cohort Assignment below) (Table 1). Participant inclusion criteria for all participants included the following: they must have no history of neurological impairments (e.g., concussion or brain injury), no other reported neurodevelopmental disorders (e.g., ASD, ADHD, etc.) or genetic disorders (e.g., Down syndrome), normal hearing, no contraindications to MRI scanning, and they must have acquired English before age two.

Participants were recruited from the broader Seattle area. Eligibility was first established through a brief written screening questionnaire covering language, medical, and developmental history. All participants passed a hearing screening between 250 and 8000 Hz at 25 dB, and all participants reported normal or corrected-to-normal vision. As in our prior work with an overlapping cohort (Abbott et al., 2026), participants were required to have had a primary caregiver who spoke to them in English before age two; bilingual participants were retained in both groups provided this criterion was met. The final sample included 5 bilingual participants in the DLD group and 6 in the TD group. All participants gave written informed consent, and study procedures were approved by the University of Washington Institutional Review Board.

Participants received monetary compensation for their time.

Adult DLD Recruitment

There are currently no standard approaches for the recruitment of adults with DLD, thus we briefly outline the approach here. Recruitment was achieved mainly through two pathways: 1) physical flyers posted throughout the broader community (e.g., coffee shops, libraries, community centers) and at disability resource centers; and 2) online via university job boards and social media. Flyers targeting DLD started with broad language (e.g., "do you have difficulty with speech/language?") and were refined over time to address more specific difficulties adults with DLD may face (e.g., "do you have trouble with grammar?"). For typically developing controls, flyers and online postings provided generic details about the study. Interested participants would scan a QR code that linked to a screening form asking about speech and language history, services received, and a brief medical history to screen for other neurological impairments (e.g., stroke, ADHD, etc.). If participants were eligible for the study, they would be contacted for further screening via the Fidler protocol described below (Fidler et al., 2011). Importantly, participants who reported having diagnoses related to language (e.g., dyslexia, expressive/receptive language disorder) were never included in the TD group; however, on occasion participants in the DLD group would report having diagnoses such as dyslexia. These participants were included for further testing given that DLD is often under-/mis-diagnosed or undiagnosed entirely (McGregor, 2020).

Cohort Assignment

Group membership was assigned using the Fidler et al. (2011) protocol, adapted for online administration (Cler et al., 2024). This protocol combines performance on a 15-item written spelling test of irregularly spelled English words with the Modified Token Test, an auditory-comprehension task requiring participants to follow spoken directions of increasing complexity. The two scores are entered into a weighted equation (6.5727 -0.2184 × spelling -0.1298 × token), which Fidler et al. (2011) validated against a clinical history of language services with 80% sensitivity and 87% specificity. A resulting score below zero is consistent with typical language development, while a score above zero is consistent with DLD; recruitment prioritized participants scoring above 0.5 or below -0.5 to maximize group separation. One participant with DLD scored within the typical language development range but was retained in the sample as they had a childhood diagnosis from a speech-language pathologist that was consistent with DLD and they received accommodations for language impairment throughout their entire academic journey from primary school through graduate school. The MRI results are the same if we remove this participant. See Table 1 for demographic and assessment information and Figure 1 for the distribution of Fidler scores.

Behavioral Measures

Following the initial screening form and Fidler assessments, participants were asked to complete two in-person, behavioral testing sessions. Language ability was assessed using the Test of Integrated Language and Literacy Skills (TILLS; Nelson et al., 2016) which yields five composite scores (sound/word, sentence/discourse, oral, written, and identification core score).

No standardized language assessment currently spans our full adult age range with norms validated specifically for adults; the TILLS itself is norm-referenced only through age 18. Other candidate measures extend their norm-referencing further into adulthood but carry their own limitations: the Clinical Evaluation of Language Fundamentals, Fifth Edition (CELF-5; Wiig et al., 2013) norms adults up to 21;11 (years; months) by extrapolating from clinical samples of younger children, and the Comprehensive Assessment of Spoken Language, Second Edition (CASL-2 Carrow-Woolfolk, 2017) draws its adult norms from samples that include other clinical populations, such as autism (Choi-Tucci et al., 2024). Given these tradeoffs, we selected the TILLS as the more well-validated option and scored all participants against its 18-year-old norms regardless of chronological age, similar to (Abbott et al., 2026).

Nonverbal IQ was assessed using the Matrix Reasoning and Block Design subtests of the Wechsler Abbreviated Scale of Intelligence, Second Edition (WASI-II; Wechsler, 2018). Participants were required to score a T-score above 30 (equivalent to a standard score above 70) on at least one of the two subtests for inclusion in the study.

Across the behavioral subtests data were incomplete for a small subset of participants (as noted in Table 1). Due to various research administration errors (e.g., an incorrect time limit administered for Coding), four participants were missing individual subtest scores: one participant with DLD was not administered Matrix Reasoning but met nonverbal IQ inclusion criteria based on a Block Design T-score above 30; one participant with TD was not administered Block Design but likewise met nonverbal IQ criteria based on a Matrix Reasoning T-score above 30; one participant with DLD did not complete the Coding subtest; and one TD participant did not complete the coding subtest, the block design subtest or any of the TILLS assessment due to a scheduling conflict, but did complete the Matrix Reasoning subtest, so they were retained in the sample.

For the remaining behavioral data, two-tailed t-tests were conducted on age, Fidler scores, WASI-II, and WAIS-IV scores, to test whether groups differed significantly on these variables. Analyses were conducted using the statistical software R (R Core Team, 2023). Because the TILLS produces five composite scores, group differences across these measures were first assessed using a multivariate analysis of variance (MANOVA), with group (DLD vs. TD) as the between-subjects factor and the five composite scores as dependent variables. Followup univariate ANOVAs were conducted on each composite to determine which specific measures drove the group difference, calculating partial eta-squared (η²) as the effect size for each. Fisher's exact tests were used to compare groups on gender and college attended. All tests were evaluated at an alpha level of 0.05, and group means, p-values, and effect sizes are reported in Table 1.

MRI Data Acquisition and Preprocessing

Imaging data were acquired on a 3T Siemens Prisma scanner with a 64-channel head and neck coil at the UW Center for Human Neuroscience. Participants were fitted with earplugs and noise-canceling headphones (Optoacoustics), and foam padding was added around participants' heads to minimize head movement. High-resolution structural images were acquired using a T1weighted multi-echo magnetization-prepared rapid gradient echo sequence with navigator-based motion correction (ME-MPRAGEvNav; TR = 2,500 ms, TE = 1.81-7.18 ms, TI = 1,000 ms, flip angle = 8°, 0.8 mm³ isotropic voxels, GRAPPA acceleration factor = 2).

Diffusion-weighted data were acquired using the HCP-Lifespan acquisition protocol with a two-shell design (b ≈ 1,500 and b ≈ 3,000 s/mm²), comprising 47 diffusion directions at b ≈ 1,500 s/mm², 46 directions at b ≈ 3,000 s/mm², and 7 non-diffusion-weighted volumes (TR = 3,230 ms, TE = 89.20 ms, 1.5 mm³ isotropic voxels, 92 slices, multi-band acceleration factor = 4), collected with anterior-posterior phase encoding. A matched posterior-anterior acquisition was collected for susceptibility-induced distortion correction.

MRI Preprocessing

All diffusion preprocessing was conducted in FSL. Susceptibility-induced distortions were corrected using topup (Andersson et al., 2003), based on non-diffusion-weighted images acquired with opposing anterior-posterior and posterior-anterior phase-encoding directions. Topup was run using the default FSL (b02b0.cnf) configuration, and the corrected b=0 images were averaged to generate a high signal-to-noise reference image.

Eddy current-induced distortions and subject motion were corrected using eddy (Andersson & Sotiropoulos, 2016), incorporating the topup field estimates. Eddy was run with outlier replacement enabled (--repol; Andersson et al., 2016) and with slice-to-volume motion correction using a six-parameter model. Gradient directions were rotated accordingly, and the corrected diffusion-weighted images, b-values, and b-vectors were propagated to downstream analyses. Quality metrics were generated for each participant using eddy_quad and aggregated across the group using eddy_squad (Bastiani et al., 2019); no significant group differences in head motion were observed. DTI estimates were completed on the b ≈ 1500 data. The multishell data were used for SMI estimation (see below).

MRI Analysis

The remaining neuroimaging statistical analyses were conducted in Python (version 3.11) using the following libraries: pyAFQ (Kruper et al., 2021 (Kruper et al., , 2025) ) for tractometry, pandas and numpy for data organization (Harris et al., 2020), scipy for statistical tests (Virtanen et al., 2020), statsmodels for linear regression and FDR correction (Seabold & Perktold, 2010), and nibabel and trx-python for neuroimaging file handling (Markiewicz et al., 2026).

Tractometry (pyAFQ)

White matter microstructure was characterized using automated fiber quantification (pyAFQ; Kruper et al., 2021 Kruper et al., , 2025)), a tractometry approach that segments individual white matter pathways directly from each participant's whole-brain tractography and samples diffusion metrics at evenly spaced points along the length of each tract. Tractography was performed using probabilistic streamline tracking with 1,000,000 seeds per participant (random seeding), with FA-thresholded masks (threshold = 0.2) used for both seeding and stopping to restrict tractography to white matter. pyAFQ-derived brain masks, computed from b=0 volumes, were supplied as the seeding and stopping boundary. Tractography and segmentation were performed on a high-performance computing cluster to accommodate the computational demands of 1,000,000-seed tractography. pyAFQ automatically segmented streamlines into 40 named white matter tracts using waypoint regions of interest defined in MNI template space and warped to each participant's native diffusion space to determine tract membership. Fractional anisotropy (FA) was sampled at 100 evenly spaced nodes along each tract using a Gaussian-weighted average across streamlines at each node, which weights streamlines by their proximity to the tract core. All subsequent analyses were restricted to the middle 60% of nodes (nodes 20-79 of 100) to minimize partial volume effects near cortical gray matter terminations and crossing-fiber regions at tract endpoints (Yeatman et al., 2012). FA group differences were examined across all 40 tracts in a data-driven manner, with tracts showing significant group differences then examined with biologically specific metrics from SMI to characterize the nature of the white matter differences.

Standard Model of Diffusion Imaging

To characterize the biological basis of FA differences with greater specificity, the Standard Model of Diffusion Imaging (SMI; Novikov et al., 2018) was calculated from the multi-shell diffusion data to extract microstructural parameters that reflect distinct tissue properties. SMI fitting was performed with the Standard Model Imaging toolbox (https://github.com/NYU-DiffusionMRI/SMI.git) (Coelho et al., 2022b). For each subject, eddycorrected diffusion images, b-values, and eddy-rotated b-vectors were loaded together with a brain mask derived from the diffusion data. A two-compartment standard model (intra-axonal, extra-axonal) was fit voxelwise within the brain mask, assuming a single fixed echo time. No noise map was estimated; accordingly, no Rician noise-floor correction was applied during fitting. Machine-learning training bounds were left at the SMI toolbox's default values. The volume fraction (f), intra-axonal diffusivity (Da), extra-axonal parallel and perpendicular diffusivities (De,∥, De,⊥), and orientation dispersion (p2) were retained and saved as NIfTI volumes for each subject. For quality control, parameter maps were overlaid on a white-matter mask (FA > 0.2, derived from a b = 1500 s/mm² DTI fit) and visualized at a mid-axial slice. SMI parameter maps were then sampled along the pyAFQ tract profiles described above using the same Gaussian-weighted node-wise averaging approach applied to FA. For each participant, voxelwise SMI maps were interpolated at the streamline coordinates of each segmented tract and averaged across streamlines at each of the 100 evenly spaced nodes, yielding node-wise profiles of each SMI metric for all 40 tracts. Analyses were restricted to the middle 60% of nodes as described above. While all SMI parameters were computed, primary analyses focus on the intra-axonal signal fraction (f), an index of axonal density, and orientation coherence (p2), an index of axonal alignment within a tract, as the most interpretable indices of axonal density and organization (Liao et al., 2024; Novikov et al., 2019).

Statistical Analysis

Group Differences in FA

Prior to analysis, participants whose mean FA (across the middle 60% of nodes) fell more than 2 standard deviations above or below their group mean within a given tract were excluded from analyses of that tract. This exclusion was applied independently per tract, such that a participant could be excluded from one tract's analysis but retained in others, reflecting tractspecific data quality rather than global participant exclusion. False discovery rate (FDR) correction was applied within each tract (Benjamini & Hochberg, 1995) across the 60 middle nodes, and a tract was considered to show a significant group difference if at least one node survived FDR correction. Correction was not additionally applied across the 40 tracts given the limited prior literature on white matter in adults with DLD as correcting at that level would have been overly conservative and would have substantially increased the risk of Type II error, that is, failing to detect genuine group differences. We therefore prioritized sensitivity of our analyses.

To assess whether FA differences in significant tracts reflected white matter changes specific to those regions or a more global reduction in white matter integrity, a second set of node-wise models was fit including overall white matter FA as a covariate, computed for each participant as the mean FA across all 40 tracts and the middle 60% of nodes. A tract was considered to show a region-specific effect only if group differences survived FDR correction after controlling for overall white matter FA.

SMI Group Differences

For tracts that showed significant FA group differences, node-wise linear regression was additionally performed for each SMI metric (f, p2) using the same FDR correction approach applied within each tract across the 60 middle nodes. These analyses were hypothesis-driven follow-ups to the FA results rather than exploratory searches across all tracts and metrics; accordingly, a single level of FDR correction was applied within each tract rather than an additional correction across metric combinations. As with FA, models controlling for the overall white matter value of each metric (f and p2) were run to test whether effects were region-specific beyond a global reduction in that metric across white matter.

Whole Brain Volume and White Matter Geometry

Total brain volume and white matter geometry were compared between groups to determine whether global or white matter differences needed to be accounted for in subsequent analyses.

Total brain volume was estimated for each participant as the total voxel volume (in mm³) of the FSL-derived brain mask computed from the distortion-corrected b=0 diffusion volumes. White matter volume was estimated from tissue probability maps generated during pyAFQ preprocessing, by summing white matter probability values across all voxels and multiplying by voxel size in mm³. Group differences in brain volume and white matter volume were each assessed using an independent-samples t-test and a Mann-Whitney U test. Both parametric and non-parametric tests are reported for brain volume to assess robustness against the potential influence of extreme values.

Bundle Geometry.

Tract profiles average FA across the voxels a bundle occupies at each node, and FA is typically higher in the bundle core than at its periphery, where partial voluming with adjacent gray matter and cerebrospinal fluid and the presence of crossing fibers reduce anisotropy (Jones et al., 2013; Yeatman et al., 2012). If bundles reconstruct with systematically different shapes between groups, tract profiles could therefore sample different proportions of core and peripheral tissue, biasing FA independently of tissue microstructure. To assess this possibility, three geometric properties were computed for any tract showing a significant group difference in FA. Streamline count was the number of streamlines assigned to the bundle. Bundle volume was computed as the number of unique voxels traversed by the bundle's streamlines, multiplied by voxel volume. Bundle spread was computed by resampling each streamline to 100 nodes, deriving the bundle centroid as the mean position across streamlines at each node, and averaging the Euclidean distance of each streamline point from the corresponding centroid position. Streamline count and bundle volume index the extent of the reconstruction, whereas bundle spread indexes its shape and is therefore the measure most directly relevant to differential sampling of core versus peripheral tissue. Groups were compared on each measure using Welch's t-tests, with Hedges' g as the effect size (Hedges, 1981). These measures were computed on the full sample, as the question concerns tract reconstruction rather than the tract profiles themselves.

Correlations with Behavior

Associations between white matter microstructure and language ability were examined separately within each diagnostic group (TD and DLD) using Pearson correlations. Mean tract FA was correlated with two behavioral measures: the TILLS ID Core composite, which provides an overall index of language ability; and the TILLS Sentence-Discourse composite, which measures syntactic and discourse-level language processing and was selected for consistency with prior functional neuroimaging work from our group (Abbott et al., 2026).

Correlations were computed across five regions. For each of the four tracts showing a significant group difference in FA, mean FA was computed across only those nodes that differed significantly between groups. Because pyAFQ characterizes microstructure at each node along a tract, group differences can be localized to specific tract segments; restricting the correlations to the nodes driving the between-group difference therefore provides a more sensitive test of whether microstructure in those segments relates to language ability within group. The fifth region was the whole corpus callosum, computed as each participant's mean FA across all eight callosal subdivisions over the middle 60% of each tract profile.

The per-tract outlier exclusions described above were carried forward to these analyses. The same 2 SD exclusion criterion was applied to the whole corpus callosum composite (n = 1 DLD, 1 TD). False discovery rate correction (Benjamini & Hochberg, 1995) was applied across the ten resulting comparisons (two measures × five regions) within each group.

Results White Matter FA Group Differences

Overall white matter FA did not differ significantly between groups (TD: M = 0.5622, SD = 0.0182; DLD: M = 0.5548, SD = 0.0220; t = 1.285, p = 0.205), indicating no global reduction in white matter integrity in adults with DLD.

Node-wise FA group differences were examined across all 40 tracts after per-tract outlier exclusion (>2 SD from group mean). Four tracts showed at least one FDR-corrected significant node: occipital corpus callosum (CC), posterior parietal CC, temporal CC, and left superior longitudinal fasciculus I (SLF-I; Figure 2). FA was lower in adults with DLD than in TD adults across three of the four significant tracts, all of which were callosal; the left superior longitudinal fasciculus I showed higher FA in the DLD group (Table 2). A fifth tract, the superior parietal CC, showed nominally significant differences prior to outlier exclusion (5 nodes, DLD < TD) but did not survive after removing two participants whose mean FA fell more than 2 standard deviations below their group mean (0 nodes, p = 0.084), indicating that this finding was driven by extreme values rather than a reliable group difference. To determine whether effects in the four significant tracts were region-specific or reflected a more global reduction in white matter FA, a second set of models was fit controlling for each participant's overall white matter FA. Three tracts remained significant after this control (Table 2): occipital CC, posterior parietal CC, and the left SLF-I. The left SLF-I showed a nearcomplete retention of significant nodes (24 to 29), supporting the region-specificity of its effect. The temporal CC did not survive the covariate model (0 significant nodes), indicating that its FA difference was not specific to that tract beyond a global white matter effect.

SMI Group Differences

To characterize the biological basis of FA differences in the four significant tracts, group differences in SMI metrics (f, intra-axonal signal fraction; p2, orientation coherence) were examined using the same node-wise regression approach and per-tract outlier exclusions as the primary FA analyses. Significant group differences were observed only in the occipital CC (Figure 3). Adults with DLD showed lower intra-axonal signal fraction (f) than TD adults across 11 nodes of the occipital CC (DLD: M = 0.5889; TD: M = 0.6095; min FDR p = 0.006), suggesting reduced axonal density in this tract. Orientation coherence (p2) was also lower in adults with DLD across 58 of the 60 middle nodes of the occipital CC (DLD: M = 0.4793; TD: M = 0.5277; min FDR p = 0.001), indicating substantially reduced axonal alignment throughout nearly the entire length of the tract. No significant group differences were observed in f orp² in the left superior longitudinal fasciculus I, posterior parietal CC, or temporal CC.

Brain Volume and White Matter Geometry

Total brain volume and white matter volume were examined prior to microstructural analyses to determine whether global or tract-level size differences required statistical control in subsequent models. Total brain volume did not differ significantly between groups. Although an independent-samples t-test yielded a significant result (TD: M = 1,348,049 mm³, SD = 134,556; DLD: M = 1,277,640 mm³, SD = 146,382; t = 2.114, p = 0.040), this difference was not robust to the presence of a single high-volume outlier in the TD group (t = 1.857, p = 0.070) and removing all participants whose total brain volume fell more than 2 standard deviations above or below their group mean rendered the difference non-significant (t = 1.649, p = 0.106). A nonparametric Mann-Whitney U test, which is less sensitive to extreme values, confirmed no significant group difference (U = 373, p = 0.141). White matter volume, estimated from tissue probability maps, did not differ between groups by either test (TD: M = 499,040 mm³, SD = 42,785; DLD: M = 488,196 mm³, SD = 44,834; t = 0.866, p = 0.391; Mann-Whitney U = 330, p = 0.541).

At the tract level, bundle volume and streamline count were lower in the DLD group for the occipital CC (volume: 41.6 vs 51.6 cm³, g = -0.73, p = 0.020; streamline count: g = -0.64, p = 0.032), and streamline count was lower for the posterior parietal CC (g = -0.71, p = 0.013). Neither measure differed for the temporal CC or the left SLF-I (all p ≥ 0.10). Bundle spread did not differ between groups in any tract (occipital CC g = -0.32; posterior parietal CC g = +0.30; temporal CC g = -0.05; left SLF-I g = +0.13; all p ≥ 0.26). Because streamline count and reconstructed volume depend on the local orientation distribution function, they are not independent of the microstructural measures of interest; bundle spread therefore provides the more interpretable index of whether tract profiles sampled systematically different tissue between groups. The absence of spread differences indicates that tract profiles sampled comparable proportions of core and peripheral bundle tissue in both groups, supporting the interpretation that FA group differences reported below reflect the diffusion properties of the tissue traversed rather than differential sampling of the bundle interior.

Correlations with Behavior

No correlation survived FDR correction in either group. Two reached uncorrected significance, both in the DLD group: left SLF-I FA was positively associated with ID Core (r = 0.427, p = 0.047, FDR-corrected p = 0.237), and temporal CC FA was negatively associated with Sentence/Discourse (r = -0.503, p = 0.017, FDR-corrected p = 0.170). No correlation approached significance in the TD group (all p ≥ 0.281). Full results are given in Table 3.

Whole corpus callosum FA was positively associated with both measures in adults with DLD, though neither reached significance (ID Core r = 0.314, p = 0.155; Sentence/Discourse r = 0.295, p = 0.182), and showed no association in TD adults (both p ≥ 0.620). Discussion This study examined white matter microstructure in adults with DLD using a tractometry approach with pyAFQ that characterizes diffusion metrics at fine spatial resolution along 40 named white matter tracts. Rather than finding patterns of reduced FA in language-related tracts predicted by prior work in children and adolescents with DLD, we found that the most robust group differences were concentrated in the corpus callosum, specifically the occipital CC and posterior parietal CC. We also found an unexpected finding of increased FA in the left superior longitudinal fasciculus I. A biologically specific follow-up analysis using SMI metrics revealed that the callosal differences in adults with DLD reflect reduced axonal density and alignment rather than myelination differences. Together, these findings suggest that the corpus callosum may play a more central role in DLD than the existing literature has recognized.

Corpus Callosum in Language Development and DLD

The corpus callosum is divided anatomically from anterior to posterior into the rostrum, genu, body, isthmus, and splenium (Witelson, 1989), with the anterior portion (the rostrum and genu) connecting prefrontal and orbital cortices, the body connecting motor, premotor, and somatosensory cortices, and the posterior portion (the isthmus and splenium) connecting temporal, parietal, and occipital cortices (Dougherty et al., 2007; Fabri & Polonara, 2013; Hofer & Frahm, 2006; Knyazeva, 2013). It undergoes a protracted developmental trajectory, with myelination progressing broadly from posterior to anterior regions and continuing well into the third decade of life (Kochunov et al., 2012). Although the posterior third matures earliest, its role in language development is increasingly recognized as foundational. Friederici et al. (2007) demonstrated that lesions specifically in the posterior corpus callosum disrupt the interhemispheric coordination of prosodic and syntactic information during speech comprehension, while anterior lesions leave this process intact. Swanson et al. (2017) found that the rate of FA development in the splenium from 6 to 24 months predicted word production at 24 months in typically developing infants, a relationship that did not emerge for the arcuate, uncinate, or inferior longitudinal fasciculi, suggesting that the splenium plays a foundational role in early language acquisition that precedes the specialization of the perisylvian network. More broadly, Bartha-Doering et al. (2020) found that larger posterior corpus callosum volumes in school-age children were associated with increased interhemispheric language network connectivity and better performance across language domains, and Hinkley et al. (2016) demonstrated that the corpus callosum actively drives the left-lateralization of language, with individuals with callosal agenesis showing markedly more bilateral language activations than controls.

Situating the present findings within this literature requires care, because the corpus callosum is parcellated differently across studies. The gross anatomical divisions described above are the most common framework in the literature, but the pyAFQ parcellation used in the present study instead subdivides the corpus callosum based on cortical projection zones, yielding eight subdivisions that do not map precisely onto traditional divisions but offer greater anatomical specificity. Approximate correspondences can be drawn: the occipital CC subdivision overlaps primarily with the posterior splenium, carrying fibers connecting primary and secondary visual cortices; the posterior parietal CC overlaps with the posterior body, isthmus, and anterior splenium, carrying fibers connecting posterior parietal cortices; and the temporal CC overlaps with the posterior body, isthmus, and anterior splenium, carrying fibers connecting temporal cortices involved in auditory and language processing. Where the present findings are discussed in relation to the broader callosal literature, these approximate correspondences are used to situate projection-zone-based subdivisions within the more familiar gross anatomical framework. Critically, all three callosal subdivisions showing significant FA differences in the present study fall within the posterior third of the corpus callosum, whose relevance to language and literacy development has been well-documented (Hasan et al., 2012; Vandermosten et al., 2012; von Plessen et al., 2002).

Despite this relevance to language development, the corpus callosum has received relatively little attention in the DLD literature, which may partly reflect early macrostructural studies finding no differences. Preis et al. (2000) found no significant differences in callosal morphometry in children with DLD, and Herbert et al. (2004) similarly found no corpus callosum volume differences in children with DLD, despite reporting a broader pattern of white matter enlargement. These null findings led Lee et al. (2020) to treat the corpus callosum as a control tract in their adolescent and adult DLD population under the assumption that it was unaffected. However, Lee et al.'s corpus callosum measures were derived from the JHU white matter tractography atlas applied in standard space, a probabilistic mask approach that averages FA across a broad spatial region without accounting for individual variation in tract anatomy. This method may be less sensitive to spatially localized microstructural differences than the individual, subject-space tractography and node-wise profiling used in the present study. Thus, their null finding in corpus callosum FA differences may reflect methodological sensitivity rather than a true absence of microstructural differences.

Where posterior callosal differences have been documented most consistently is in developmental dyslexia, a disorder that is diagnostically distinct from DLD but frequently cooccurs with it and has phenotypic overlap (McGregor et al., 2025). In their comprehensive DTI review of the dyslexia literature, Vandermosten et al. (2012) identified the posterior corpus callosum as a site of white matter differences, with lower FA in this region generally associated with poorer decoding ability. Hasan et al. (2012) further demonstrated that microstructural properties of the posterior corpus callosum correlated with word reading and comprehension scores in children with dyslexia. Overall, these findings raise the possibility that posterior callosal white matter differences may represent a shared neurobiological substrate underlying the frequent co-occurrence of the two disorders, one that is not specific to reading per se but reflects a more general disruption to the interhemispheric connectivity that supports language and literacy development across domains.

This shared-substrate interpretation has a competing explanation, however. Carreiras et al. (2009) found that adults who learned to read later in life had greater white matter volume in the splenium of the corpus callosum than matched illiterate adults, indicating that literacy experience itself contributes to posterior callosal structure. Reduced reading experience, whether it arises from comorbid dyslexia, from the additional demands that language difficulties place on reading, or simply from reading less across development and adulthood could therefore produce the posterior callosal differences observed here. Consistent with this concern, adults with DLD in the present sample scored significantly lower than TD adults on the TILLS written language composite.

Though comorbid dyslexia was not formally assessed, the present data allow a partial test of this alternative. In unplanned exploratory analyses, we examined whether reading-related subtests were associated with FA in the regions showing group differences. Within the DLD group, no reading measure was associated with callosal FA in any subdivision (all r ≤ 0.32, all p > 0.15). The only association of note was between left SLF-I FA and the TILLS nonword reading subtest (r = 0.512, p = 0.015), but it did not survive correction for multiple comparisons. The absence of callosal reading relationships within the DLD group is not what would be expected if reading skill was the primary driver of the callosal findings. Distinguishing a shared neurobiological substrate from the accumulated structural consequence of differing literacy experience will require direct measures of reading exposure alongside longitudinal data spanning the period in which posterior callosal pathways mature.

Corpus Callosum in Adults with DLD

The most robust finding in this study was reduced FA in the occipital CC in adults with DLD, accompanied by reduced axonal density (f) and orientation coherence (p2) using SMI metrics of microstructure. Reduced axonal density and coherence may impair the speed and fidelity of interhemispheric signal transmission through the posterior corpus callosum, raising the possibility that these differences reflect disrupted interhemispheric transfer of orthographic and linguistic information.

The behavioral data, however, provide little direct support for this account. Neither planned TILLS composite correlation with occipital CC FA approached significance in the DLD group (ID Core r = -.043; Sentence/Discourse r = .129), and as stated above, exploratory analyses found no association between occipital CC FA and any reading measure. Whole corpus callosum FA showed positive but non-significant associations with both composites (r = .314 and .295). The occipital CC difference is thus the most robust group-level finding in this study while showing no behavioral correlate with the assessments used here.

The dissociation between a reliable group difference with no within-group relationship to performance is itself informative, and points toward a second interpretation. If the occipital CC difference reflects an atypical developmental trajectory rather than a structure currently constraining language performance, it would not necessarily track current ability. Kim et al. (2006) found reduced FA in the genu, an anterior corpus callosum region, in preschool children with language impairment, interpreted as evidence of delayed callosal maturation. In the present adult sample, the pattern was reversed: differences emerged in posterior callosal regions, while the genu did not differ between groups. The differing maturational windows of the two regions offer one way to reconcile these observations. Posterior callosal pathways myelinate early, so differences established during early development have a relatively short window in which to resolve. Anterior regions continue myelinating into the third decade, affording a far longer period over which an initially atypical trajectory might converge toward typical values. On this account, the genu differences Kim et al. observed in preschoolers may represent an early marker of atypical development that normalizes across an extended maturational window, while posterior differences established early persist into adulthood.

This interpretation remains speculative. It rests on comparison across studies with different samples, ages, and methods rather than on longitudinal data. It also need not compete with the experience-dependent account outlined above: an early-established difference in posterior callosal structure could plausibly be compounded by reduced literacy experience during the school-age years, when these pathways are still consolidating. Longitudinal data spanning early childhood through adulthood would be required to distinguish differences that are present early and persist from those that accumulate with experience.

The one callosal region where microstructure did track language performance was the temporal CC, where FA was negatively associated with Sentence-Discourse scores in the DLD group (r = -0.503, p = 0.017, uncorrected). This association did not survive FDR correction and its direction is counterintuitive, but the whole-callosum average ran positive for the same measure, indicating that the temporal effect runs counter to the callosum as a whole rather than reflecting a global callosal relationship.

Posterior Parietal and Temporal Corpus Callosum Findings

Two additional callosal subdivisions, the posterior parietal CC and temporal CC also showed significant FA differences, though with more limited evidence for region-specificity and functional significance. The posterior parietal CC, which survived both outlier removal and the white matter FA covariate model, carries fibers connecting parietal cortices involved in visuospatial integration, attention, and phonological processing. Phonological processing deficits are among the most consistently documented features of DLD across the lifespan, encompassing difficulties with nonword repetition (Pham et al., 2026; Schwob et al., 2021), novel word-form learning (Bahar et al., 2026; Gordon & McGregor, 2014; McGregor et al., 2017), and the rapid processing of phonological information (Kail, 1994; Miller et al., 2001). The present data, however, offer little evidence of a functional relationship. Neither planned composite correlated with posterior parietal CC FA in either group (DLD: ID Core r = -0.036, Sentence-Discourse r = -0.165; TD: r = -0.235 and 0.064), and in exploratory analyses Sound-Word showed no association in the DLD group (r = -0.015, p = 0.949). The one phonological measure to approach significance was nonword repetition, negatively associated with posterior parietal CC FA after controlling for overall white matter FA (partial r = -0.457, p = 0.043, uncorrected), a direction opposite to what a straightforward support-for-phonology account would predict.

This absence may reflect limitations of these behavioral measures rather than a true absence of functional relevance. The Sound-Word composite indexes accuracy on phonological awareness tasks but is untimed, and posterior parietal callosal pathways may be more relevant to the speed and efficiency of phonological and orthographic processing than to accuracy. A measure targeting decoding or phonological recoding, which we did not include, might also have been more sensitive given established links between posterior callosal microstructure and reading ability in typical development and dyslexia (Dougherty et al., 2007; Vandermosten et al., 2012). Future work examining timed phonological and reading measures alongside posterior callosal microstructure in adults with DLD would help clarify the functional significance of this finding.

The temporal CC showed significant FA differences between groups but did not survive control for overall white matter FA, suggesting the group difference may reflect a more global pattern rather than a tract-specific effect. It was nonetheless the only callosal region in which microstructure tracked language performance: temporal CC FA was negatively associated with Sentence-Discourse scores in adults with DLD (r = -0.503, p = 0.017, uncorrected).

This direction converges with Dougherty et al. (2007), who found FA in the temporal callosal pathway negatively correlated with phonological awareness in typically developing children, interpreted as reflecting developmental pruning whereby skilled readers retain fewer but larger, more efficiently conducting axons. This suggests that FA in the temporal callosal pathway may follow a non-linear developmental trajectory, where lower FA early in development reflects immaturity, but a modest reduction in FA later reflects efficient pruning. In this context, the lower FA observed in adults with DLD may reflect a tract that failed to reach full maturational peak, resulting in lower FA for a qualitatively different reason. This interpretation is speculative given the fragility of the group difference and its failure to survive covariate control thus it should be further evaluated.

Contributions of Tract Geometry to Callosal FA Differences

One alternative explanation for the concentration of significant findings in the corpus callosum is that these tracts comprise a larger volume of white matter than the association tracts examined in prior DLD research. Averaging FA across more voxels at each node yields more stable per-participant estimates, which could in principle afford greater sensitivity to small group differences. The present findings argue against this account. The left SLF-I is roughly one third the reconstructed volume of the occipital CC, yet showed a comparably robust group difference, one that strengthened rather than weakened after controlling for overall white matter FA. If bundle size governed detectability, the smallest tract examined would not be among the clearest findings.

Bundle geometry is similarly unsupportive. FA is computed voxel-wise and is not scaled by tract volume, so geometry can bias tract profiles only by changing which tissue is sampled, and bundle spread, which indexes this directly, did not differ between groups in any tract. Consistent with this, Preis et al. (2000) measured callosal morphometry directly in children with DLD and found no volumetric differences, suggesting that gross callosal size differences are not an established feature of the disorder. However, because averaging across more voxels yields less noisy per-participant estimates, some advantage in detecting differences within larger bundles cannot be fully excluded.

Left Superior Longitudinal Fasciculus I

The finding of increased FA in the left SLF-I in adults with DLD stands in contrast to the pattern of reduced FA in children and adolescents with DLD reported across multiple prior studies, including reduced FA in the left SLF in children with DLD (Verly et al., 2019) and in adolescents and adults with DLD (Lee et al., 2020). Verly et al. (2019) notably reported both reduced FA in the left middle longitudinal fasciculus and increased FA in the right superior longitudinal fasciculus in children with DLD. The direction of our left SLF I finding (higher FA in DLD than TD) parallels the right SLF finding in Verly et al., raising the possibility that increased FA in SLF subcomponents may reflect a compensatory or reorganized pattern in DLD rather than straightforward white matter degradation. Increased FA can reflect increased axonal coherence, reduced crossing fiber complexity, or in some cases, atypical developmental pruning that results in a less diverse but more coherent fiber architecture. In the absence of SMI evidence of group differences in f orp² in the left SLF I, we cannot readily distinguish between these possibilities, but the absence of axonal density or alignment differences suggests the FA increase is not simply a reflection of more or better-aligned axons.

The contrast with Lee et al. (2020) is also methodologically informative. Lee et al. used an atlas-based ROI approach applied to a predefined SLF mask, which averages FA across the full extent of the tract and all its subdivisions. Our tractometry approach segments the SLF into its anatomically distinct subcomponents, SLF I, II, and III, and characterizes FA at fine spatial resolution along the tract's length. It is plausible that the SLF subcomponents show different, even opposing, patterns of group differences, and that atlas-based averaging obscures this heterogeneity.

The SLF I is the most dorsal of the three SLF branches, connecting the superior parietal lobule and precuneus to superior frontal and dorsal premotor cortex, and is associated with visuospatial processing and the regulation of motor behavior (Makris et al., 2005). The group difference was evident across much of the central tract and reached significance over nodes 44-67 of the 100-node profile. Unlike the callosal findings, it ran in the opposite direction, with higher FA in the DLD group. The SLF I result therefore complements rather than extends the callosal pattern. What the two share is not a common anatomical locus but a common functional neighborhood: both implicate posterior systems that support language indirectly, rather than the perisylvian pathways typically implicated in children with DLD. The parietal cortex sits at the intersection of dorsal attentional (Corbetta & Shulman, 2002), visuospatial (Whitlock, 2017), and language networks (Aflalo et al., 2020; Gurd et al., 2002; Segal & Petrides, 2012), and reduced interhemispheric posterior connectivity alongside elevated intrahemispheric dorsal connectivity is potentially consistent with a system in which early differences in one set of pathways are accompanied by differences in another serving overlapping cognitive demands.

A further possibility is that the SLF I finding is specific to our recruitment practices and thus cohort. Adults with DLD who complete secondary education, respond to research advertisements, and tolerate an MRI session are likely not representative of all adults with DLD. If elevated SLF I FA indexes successful linguistic compensation, then a sample enriched for successful compensators is precisely the sample in which it would be most visible, and the positive within-group association between SLF I FA and overall language ability is consistent with that interpretation. Accordingly, the SLF I difference would mark compensatory success in a subgroup rather than a feature of DLD in adulthood generally (see similar findings in autism spectrum disorder; Eigsti et al., 2016). Distinguishing these accounts requires samples spanning a wider range of language and educational outcomes and measures of real-world attainment against which SLF I microstructure can be evaluated directly.

Biological Specificity: Axonal Differences

A key contribution of this study is the characterization of FA differences using biologically specific metrics from SMI. The finding that group differences in the occipital CC were accompanied by reduced f and p2 indicates that the white matter differences in adults with DLD reflect reduced axonal density and alignment. This is a substantively different conclusion from what FA alone would imply, since FA is sensitive to myelination, axonal organization, and crossing fiber geometry, and reduced FA is often interpreted loosely as reflecting "reduced myelin integrity."

A Developmental Lens on DLD in Adulthood

A recurring challenge in interpreting white matter findings in adults with DLD is that the literature has been built almost entirely on studies of children, with Lee et al. (2020) representing the only prior study to examine adults directly. The behavioral profile of DLD in adulthood adds additional interpretive complexity: adults with DLD show persistent language impairments, but these impairments are often more subtle in standardized testing than in childhood, with many adults performing within the average range on individual subtests while still experiencing significant functional difficulty (Kalandadze et al., 2026; Orrego et al., 2023). Our sample reflects this pattern. Only 35% of our DLD participants met TILLS criteria for a language or literacy disorder, despite all meeting the Fidler protocol criteria for DLD, consistent with Choi-Tucci et al. (2024) and Abbott et al. (2026).

One framework for integrating these observations is that the language-related association tracts, like the dorsal frontotemporal pathways most commonly implicated in DLD in children, may undergo a degree of normalization or compensatory reorganization by adulthood, while interhemispheric connectivity via the corpus callosum may not fully normalize. Adults with DLD frequently report that they can complete language tasks successfully but must expend considerably more effort and time to do so (Hirn, 2023; Kalandadze et al., 2026; Orrego et al., 2023). This is consistent with our own behavioral observations during TILLS administration. DLD participants were near ceiling on comprehension accuracy but took substantially longer to complete tasks. Reduced callosal integrity may limit the efficiency of interhemispheric information transfer, necessitating greater cognitive effort to achieve the same outcome. This account is also consistent with recent findings from Abbott et al. (2026) in which the authors found that adults with DLD recruited additional, possibly compensatory brain regions during language processing.

Limitations and Future Directions

Several limitations of this study warrant acknowledgment. First, this is a cross-sectional study of adults, which limits causal inference about whether the callosal differences observed here reflect a failure of normalization from childhood, a compensatory response, or a developmental trajectory distinct from what is observed in children. Longitudinal neuroimaging studies tracking individuals with DLD from childhood through adulthood are needed to address these questions directly.

Second, our sample, while relatively large compared to prior DLD neuroimaging studies, remains modest in size, and the per-tract outlier exclusions further reduce power in individual tract analyses. Third, adults with DLD who finish school, answer a research ad, and are willing to sit through an MRI are likely to be those whose early language problems became manageable, not those whose problems stayed disabling (Stothard et al., 1998). That cuts in opposite directions for our two sets of results: it should make compensatory differences easier to find and deficit-related differences harder. The SLF I result in particular may say more about adults who have compensated well than about adults with DLD in general.

Finally, the corpus callosum has historically received less attention in the DLD DTI literature than frontotemporal association tracts, and many prior studies using atlas-based approaches may have lacked the spatial resolution or anatomical specificity to detect differences in specific callosal subdivisions. The present findings motivate a more systematic examination of the corpus callosum in future DLD neuroimaging work, including fixel-based analyses that can more precisely characterize axonal organization in regions of crossing fibers.

Conclusion

This study provides evidence that white matter microstructure in adults with DLD differs most robustly in the corpus callosum, particularly the occipital CC and posterior parietal CC, rather than in the frontotemporal language association tracts that have been the focus of prior work in children. Biologically specific metrics from SMI indicate that these callosal differences may reflect reduced axonal density and alignment. An unexpected finding of increased FA in the left superior longitudinal fasciculus I, in contrast to prior reports of reduced SLF FA in younger populations, suggests that white matter organization in language tracts may shift across development in ways that a cross-sectional snapshot cannot fully resolve. Together, these findings support a developmental account in which the neurobiological signature of DLD in adulthood is shaped by a combination of persistent differences and potentially compensatory reorganization, with the corpus callosum emerging as a structure whose role in DLD across the lifespan merits substantially more investigation.
