Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (e.g., “decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

The APA manual states: Report exact p values (e.g., p = .031) to two or three decimal places. However, report p values less than .001 as p < .001. However, 2 decimals is too imprecise for many use-cases (e.g., a p value meta-analysis), so report p values with three digits.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). 1. Introduction

>Introduction

1.1. Social Background and the Need for Measures of AI Attitudes

In recent years, artificial intelligence (AI), including generative AI, has been increasingly adopted across diverse domains such as education, healthcare, public administration, finance, the military, and creative work (Ministry of Internal Affairs and Communications [MIC], 2025). As AI's societal influence grows, attitudes toward AI have become increasingly important for understanding its responsible development, deployment, and social acceptance. Advancing such research requires brief instruments that can reliably measure general attitudes toward AI.

In Japan, demographic challenges such as an aging population and labor shortages are accelerating AI use in healthcare, nursing care, manufacturing, and public administration. Alongside the government's promotion of AI adoption (MIC, 2025) and expanding corporate use, AI is increasingly introduced not only through individual choice but also in organizational, institutional, and educational settings. A Japanese-language measure of AI attitudes is therefore useful for domestic research and applied evaluation, including human-computer interaction (HCI) research that characterizes users' general attitudes toward AI when evaluating AI-enabled systems. Linking Japanese data to international comparative research, however, also requires evidence that the measure functions comparably across languages and populations.

1.2. Theoretical Background: General AI Attitudes and Technology Acceptance Models

The Technology Acceptance Model (TAM; Davis, 1989), a representative framework in technology acceptance research, proposes that perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use determine usage intention, which predicts actual use. TAM has been incorporated into broader frameworks, notably the Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology (UTAUT; Venkatesh et al., 2003), which added contextual factors such as social influence and facilitating conditions. UTAUT also notes that usage intention accounts less well for behavior when use is mandated; as AI is increasingly introduced independently of individual choice, models centered on usage intention alone may not fully capture attitudes toward AI.

To capture attitudes toward AI, we must measure not only positive aspects, such as evaluations of its usefulness, but also negative ones, such as concerns. TAM constructs do not directly capture this broader attitudinal structure in which positive and negative evaluations coexist. The General Attitudes toward Artificial Intelligence Scale (GAAIS; Schepman & Rodway, 2020 , 2023) was developed to measure these complex attitudes. Positive attitudes encompass interest in AI, positive expectations about AI, and perceptions of its social and personal usefulness, whereas negative attitudes encompass concerns about its danger, sinister nature, uncontrollability, and associated discomfort. The GAAIS has been translated and validated across many languages and cultures, including Spanish (Arees et al., 2025), Chinese (Huang et al., 2025), Hungarian (Rózsa et al., 2025), Korean (Seo & Ahn, 2022), Turkish (Kaya et al., 2024), and Japanese (Yamaguchi et al., 2025), with two independently validated Italian versions (Cicero et al., 2025; Sacco et al., 2025) and a large-scale national survey in Bulgaria (Markova & Yordanova, 2025). Single-item measurement has also been discussed (Montag & Ali, 2025). Schepman and Rodway (2026) subsequently developed the Short GAAIS-10 as a shortened and refined version of the scale. Rather than simply shortening the 20-item version, they used a multistage item-purification process combining CFA, Rasch analysis, and semantic judgements, with measurement efficiency and item fit supported across multiple independent samples. They recommend the Short GAAIS-10 as a practical option for multi-scale surveys.

1.3. Related Constructs for Validation

The original GAAIS studies found moderate associations with the Technology Readiness Index (TRI), a measure of general technology readiness, suggesting links between general AI attitudes and broader technology acceptance tendencies (Schepman & Rodway, 2020 , 2026). However, associations with TAM constructs that are particularly relevant to AI use, such as perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use, remain insufficiently examined. Examining these central TAM constructs, together with AI usage intention as a downstream outcome, provides validity evidence based on relations with other variables and clarifies the extent to which the Short GAAIS-10 captures constructs related to, but distinguishable from, established technology acceptance measures. An incremental validity analysis can further test whether the Short GAAIS-10 explains variance in AI usage intention beyond perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use, that is, whether general AI attitudes add information beyond core TAM constructs.

Negative and emotional evaluations provide another basis for validation. Indicators based on the Negative Attitudes towards Artificial Intelligence Scale (NAAIS; Koga et al., 2022) assess anxiety and concern about the adverse effects of AI, rejection of AI with emotions, and emotional affinity with AI. AI use experience and intention, together with comfort with hypothetical AI applications, can further clarify how general AI attitudes relate to experience, future use orientation, and evaluations of specific use cases.

A further individual-difference factor concerns how compelled people feel to use AI. Existing models do not explicitly measure this subjective sense of compulsion, which may vary across individuals even within the same setting (cf. Brown et al., 2002) and may relate to the personal impact dimension of attitudes toward AI (Borwein et al., 2024). We therefore included perceived compulsory use of AI, defined as the degree to which individuals feel that AI use is required by circumstances, organizations, institutions, or rules rather than chosen freely; this construct corresponds to the compulsory aspect of voluntariness in TAM2 (Venkatesh & Davis, 2000). Individuals with higher perceived compulsory use may report greater concern about AI-related risks. Because this indicator is newly constructed and not yet validated, findings involving it should be regarded as preliminary.

1.4. Theoretical Positioning of the Present Study

Two considerations motivate this study: the need to validate a brief measure of general attitudes toward AI in Japan and the need to establish whether such a measure can support comparisons across countries.

We position general attitudes toward AI as related to, but not reducible to, technology acceptance, and examine this distinction by assessing the Short GAAIS-10 in relation to core TAM constructs (perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use), AI usage intention, negative and emotional evaluations of AI, AI use experiences, and comfort with specific AI applications. We further examine perceived compulsory use of AI, a subjective sense of external pressure that is relevant to increasingly non-voluntary AI use contexts but not explicitly captured by existing technology acceptance models. Together, these analyses are intended to clarify the measurement scope of general attitudes toward AI and their relationship to established technology acceptance constructs and externally driven AI use.

From a psychometric perspective, we also treat the Short GAAIS-10 as an independent measurement unit whose validity should be evaluated at both the factor and item levels. Applying the same Rasch rating scale model as the original study provides item-level evidence and a basis for future comparisons across linguistic and cultural contexts.

For cross-national comparisons of attitudes toward AI to be meaningful, it is necessary to examine whether the scale functions comparably across populations. To address this question, we use publicly available data from the original UK validation sample to examine measurement invariance with the Japanese sample. Where sufficient invariance is supported, latent mean differences can also be examined while accounting for item-level measurement differences.

1.5. Positioning Relative to the Existing Japanese GAAIS-J

A Japanese version of the GAAIS (the GAAIS-J; Yamaguchi et al., 2025) has already been developed, with evidence for its factor structure based on a large sample (N = 3,689) and high internal consistency. Because it includes all 10 items in the Short GAAIS-10, a Japanese equivalent could in principle be assembled by extracting the corresponding items. Nevertheless, there are three reasons to translate and validate the Short GAAIS-10 independently.

First, responses to items embedded in the GAAIS-J do not establish the psychometric properties of the Short GAAIS-10 as a standalone scale because item order, instructions, and response context differ. As Kruyen et al. (2013) note, a shortened scale should be validated in an independent sample using the shortened version itself, rather than through post hoc extraction from parent-scale data.

Second, the Short GAAIS-10 is an independent measurement unit developed through CFA, Rasch analysis, and semantic judgements (Schepman & Rodway, 2026), rather than a simple shortening of the 20-item scale. Establishing whether this refined 10-item structure functions similarly in a Japanese-speaking context is a distinct psychometric task.

Third, reducing respondent burden and survey costs is important in multivariable research in which AI attitudes are assessed alongside other psychological variables. A 10-item scale facilitates inclusion in multi-scale batteries and may reduce measurement error associated with participant fatigue. This was a primary motivation for developing the Short GAAIS-10, which Schepman and Rodway (2026) recommend for multi-scale surveys.

The GAAIS-J is a pioneering contribution in the Japanese-speaking context, and the present study complements, rather than replaces, it. Whereas the GAAIS-J established structural evidence primarily through CFA and examined validity in relation to variables such as trust in AI and frequency of AI use, the present study adds item-level evidence based on item response theory (IRT) and the Rasch model, examines associations with a broader set of theoretically relevant external variables, including incremental validity beyond core TAM constructs, and tests measurement invariance against the original UK sample.

1.6. Study Objectives

The present study had two objectives: (1) to validate the Short GAAIS-10 (Schepman & Rodway, 2026) in Japan and (2) to explore its measurement comparability with the original UK sample.

For the first objective, we translated the Short GAAIS-10 into Japanese (GAAIS-10-J) and examined its psychometric properties from four perspectives: structural evidence, item-level evidence, reliability and temporal stability, and associations with related constructs. CFA was used as the primary test of the original two-factor structure, with EFA as a supplementary examination, and item fit was evaluated using Rasch analysis. Internal consistency and test-retest reliability were then assessed. Associations were examined with perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use, indicators based on the NAAIS (Koga et al., 2022), frequency of generative AI use, perceived compulsory use of AI, AI usage intention, and comfort with hypothetical AI applications. A post hoc exploratory hierarchical regression further tested whether the GAAIS-10-J subscales explained variance in AI usage intention beyond perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use.

For the second objective, motivated by the need to establish whether the scale can support crossnational comparisons of attitudes toward AI, we conducted a post hoc exploratory comparison with the original UK Sample 4 (Schepman & Rodway, 2026). We examined measurement invariance across the Japanese and UK samples and, where sufficient invariance was supported, explored latent mean differences between the two samples.

2. Method

>Method

2.1. Research Design

This online survey study translated and validated the GAAIS-10-J, a Japanese version of the Short GAAIS-10 (Schepman & Rodway, 2026), for use with Japanese adults. The main survey (T1) examined factor structure, item characteristics, internal consistency, and correlations with related measures; a retest conducted approximately one month later (T2) examined the temporal stability of the GAAIS-10-J's subscale scores.

2.2. Participants and Procedure

Participants were recruited through the crowdsourcing service Lancers, one of the largest crowdsourcing services in Japan. Because experience with AI and attitudes toward it may differ by age and sex, we recruited 50 participants for each combination of sex and age band (20s, 30s, 40s, 50s, and 60s), with a target of up to 500 participants. As preregistered, the recruitment period was set to one week. Of the 472 valid responses obtained, 468 participants who answered the attention-check items correctly were included in the T1 analyses in accordance with the preregistered exclusion criterion. The T1 sample comprised 79 participants in their 20s (46 women), 98 in their 30s (50 women), 99 in their 40s (49 women), 100 in their 50s (50 women), and 92 in their 60s (42 women).

The T2 survey was conducted approximately one month after T1. Among the participants included in the T1 analyses, the 429 who also participated in T2 and whose T1 and T2 data could be matched were included in the test-retest reliability analyses. The 39 participants who did not take part in T2 were included in analyses using the T1 data only and were excluded from the test-retest reliability analyses. Reasons for nonparticipation at T2 were not assessed. Participants' identifying information disclosed at the time of the survey was used solely to match the T1 and T2 data and was not included in the analysis dataset.

2.3. Ethical Considerations

This study was conducted with the approval of the Ergonomics Experiment Committee of the National Institute of Advanced Industrial Science and Technology (reference: Hito2026-1579).

Before the survey began, participants were informed of the purpose of the study, the voluntary nature of their responses, that their data would be analyzed in a form that could not identify individuals, and that they could withdraw at any time; only those who consented took part.

2.4. Preregistration

The main analysis plan was preregistered on the Open Science Framework (OSF) on April 10, 2026, before the survey began (https://osf.io/473n2/overview?view_only=a5b825311d0540b296ef8877d1be502f). The T1 survey began on April 16, 2026. The preregistration plan included the factor structure, item characteristics, internal consistency, test-retest reliability based on correlations between the two administrations, and correlations with the planned external variables. The hierarchical regression assessing incremental validity for AI usage intention and the additional test-retest analysesintraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs), paired t tests of mean differences, a comparison of T2 participants and non-participants, and a sensitivity analysis excluding Neg19-were added after data collection, as were supplementary analyses of specific-item influences, the construction of NAAIS-based subscale indicators, and the confirmation of the perceived compulsory use indicator.

All of these analyses, as well as the UK-Japan measurement-invariance and latent-mean analyses (also conducted after data collection), were treated as exploratory or supplementary and interpreted separately from the confirmatory analyses.

2.5. Translation Procedure of the GAAIS-10-J

The GAAIS-10-J was translated with permission from the original authors. First, one AI researcher and one psychology researcher translated the 10 Short GAAIS-10 items (Schepman & Rodway, 2026) into Japanese. Next, three professional translators proficient in Japanese and English were engaged; two independently produced back-translations, and the third compared and integrated the two back-translations. The researchers then compared the integrated back-translation with the original and examined differences in meaning and expression. The final back-translation was reviewed by the original authors, and their comments were incorporated to ensure conceptual and semantic equivalence.

2.6. Measures

The full item wordings of the GAAIS-10-J, participant instructions, and scoring details are provided in Supplementary Information 1. The item wordings for the other measures are provided in Supplementary Tables S1a and S1b.

2.6.1. GAAIS-10-J

The GAAIS-10-J consists of five Positive and five Negative items rated on a 5-point scale (1 = strongly disagree,5 = strongly agree). Subscale (Positive or Negative) scores were calculated as the mean of the items belonging to each subscale. The item codes (e.g., Pos7, Neg19) correspond to the numbering of the original 20-item GAAIS (see the note to Table 1 for details). To examine the original factor structure and item fit, the Negative items were not reverse-scored in the EFA, CFA, and Rasch analyses, and the original item direction was retained. For the correlation, multiple regression, and test-retest reliability analyses, the Negative items were reverse-scored to facilitate interpretation of associations with external variables.

2.6.2. Perceived Usefulness and Perceived Ease of Use

Perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use regarding AI were each assessed with three items developed for this study with reference to TAM (Davis, 1989). Perceived usefulness assessed the extent to which respondents believed that AI contributes to the efficiency, outcomes, and benefits of their tasks and activities, whereas perceived ease of use assessed comprehensibility, ease of use, and low effort. Both were rated on a 5-point scale (1 = strongly disagree,5 = strongly agree), and each subscale score was calculated as the item mean.

2.6.3. NAAIS

As external variables concerning negative attitudes toward AI and emotional responses to AI, we constructed indicators based on the NAAIS (Koga et al., 2022). The response format was a 5-point scale (1 = strongly disagree,5 = strongly agree). We used the eight items that showed relatively clear factor loadings in Koga et al. (2022). Based on the items' content, we computed scores for anxiety about and concerns over the adverse effects of AI devices (NAAIS-1), rejection of AI with emotions (NAAIS-2), and emotional affinity with AI (NAAIS-3), and used these indicators to examine how the GAAIS-10-J relates to negative and emotional evaluations of AI.

>Frequency of Generative AI Use

2.6.5. AI Usage Intention

AI usage intention was assessed with three items adapted for the present study; two were based on the usage-intention items of the TAM2 (Venkatesh & Davis, 2000), reworded for application to AI in general, and the third was a usage-intention item whose prior use in a GAAIS context has been reported (Cicero et al., 2025). The items assessed whether respondents intended to use AI when available, thought they would use AI, and wanted to start or continue using AI. To maintain consistency with the other scales, responses were given on a 5-point scale (1 = strongly disagree,5 = strongly agree), and the mean of the three items was used as the AI usage intention score.

2.6.6. Perceived Compulsory Use of AI

Perceived compulsory use of AI was operationalized as the degree to which individuals feel that AI use is required by circumstances, organizations, institutions, or rules rather than chosen freely.

With reference to voluntariness in TAM2 (Venkatesh & Davis, 2000), we developed five items for the present study to assess perceived compulsory use of AI in general. These preregistered items were rated on a 5-point scale (1 = strongly disagree,5 = strongly agree), and their mean was used in the analyses. This variable does not measure mandatory use within a specific organization, but rather the general tendency to feel that AI use is externally required.

2.6.7. Comfort with AI Applications

To measure subjective comfort with specific AI use cases, we translated the AI application items from the original study (Schepman & Rodway, 2026) into Japanese. Participants were asked: "How comfortable would you feel about AI being used for the following purposes?

Please answer in terms of how acceptable it feels to you, rather than whether you think it is ethically desirable."

The response format was a 5-point scale (1 = very uncomfortable,5 = very comfortable). We did not re-estimate the cluster structure of the AI application items ourselves; instead, following the original study, we calculated the means of items belonging to each cluster. These scores do not directly measure actual use behavior or institutional acceptance but were treated as indicators of subjective comfort with hypothetical AI use cases.

2.7. Statistical Analysis for the Validation of the GAAIS-10-J

The analyses addressed four aspects of the GAAIS-10-J: factor structure, item-level functioning, reliability and temporal stability, and associations with related constructs. Analyses not included in the preregistration (Section 2.4) were treated as exploratory or supplementary.

Statistical analyses were conducted using HAD v18.020 (Shimizu, 2016) and the snowIRT module of Jamovi v2.6 (Seol, 2022). The primary test of factor structure was a CFA of the a priori twofactor (Positive, Negative) structure of the original Short GAAIS-10 (Schepman & Rodway, 2026), using maximum likelihood estimation. Each item loaded only on its corresponding factor, and the covariance between factors was freely estimated. Following the preregistration, model fit was evaluated using CFI, TLI, RMSEA, and SRMR (CFI ≥ .90, TLI ≥ .90, RMSEA ≤ .08, SRMR ≤ .08), with GFI, AIC, and BIC additionally reported; standardized CFA loadings below .40 were considered problematic. A supplementary EFA using maximum likelihood estimation and promax rotation (power = 4) was conducted on the same sample, with the number of factors evaluated from the scree plot (Cattell, 1966), interpretability, and the original two-factor structure. Because the EFA and CFA used the same sample, the CFA was interpreted as a preliminary confirmation, rather than an independent cross-validation. In the preregistered primary CFA, Likert-type items were treated as approximately continuous; the exploratory cross-cultural analyses in Section 2.8 additionally treated the items as ordered categorical variables using WLSMV.

Item-level characteristics were examined using a polytomous Rasch analysis following the original study. Using Jamovi's snowIRT module, we estimated a rating scale model (RSM) with marginal maximum likelihood (MML) estimation separately for the Positive and Negative subscales and examined infit and outfit MNSQ and person separation reliability (PSR). Infit and outfit MNSQ values of 0.70-1.30 were considered acceptable (Smith et al., 2008). We also examined threshold ordering as a check of category functioning. Item location (difficulty) parameters were estimated as part of the Rasch analysis and are reported in Supplementary Table S3.

Internal consistency was evaluated using Cronbach's alpha and McDonald's omega. Cronbach's alpha for each variable is reported in Table 2, and omega coefficients for the GAAIS-10-J are reported in the text. As a supplementary assessment of the discriminability of the Positive and Negative subscales, we calculated the heterotrait-monotrait ratio (HTMT) from inter-item correlations by standardizing heterotrait correlations between Positive and Negative items by within-subscale item correlations (Henseler et al., 2015), using absolute values for betweensubscale inter-item correlations.

Associations with external variables were examined using Pearson product-moment correlations.

Preregistered directional expectations-a positive association of the Positive subscale with perceived usefulness, perceived ease of use, and AI usage intention, and a negative association of reverse-scored Negative scores with NAAIS scores-were treated as confirmatory; the remaining correlations were used descriptively to characterize the scale. Because the correlations were not For perceived compulsory use of AI, which was measured with newly constructed items, we examined the internal consistency and the common component of the items in a supplementary manner before using the five-item mean score. We calculated Cronbach's alpha and conducted a principal component analysis to determine whether all items loaded positively on the first principal component. Because the indicator has not been validated as an independent scale and no directional hypothesis was preregistered, its associations were interpreted as exploratory.

Test-retest reliability was examined among the 429 participants whose T1 and T2 data could be matched. After evaluating internal consistency at T2, we calculated Pearson correlations and single-measure, consistency-type ICCs between T1 and T2 subscale scores. Mean differences between T1 and T2 were examined using paired t tests, and Welch's t tests compared T1 Positive and reverse-scored Negative scores between T2 participants and non-participants. As an additional sensitivity analysis, we examined test-retest reliability for the Negative-4 score excluding Neg19.

Additional details are provided in the Supplementary Analysis Procedure.

2.8. Exploratory Cross-Cultural Psychometric Comparison

For the exploratory cross-cultural psychometric comparison, the present Japanese sample (N = 468) was compared with publicly available data from Sample 4 of the original Short GAAIS-10 validation study (N = 500 UK adults; Schepman & Rodway, 2026), the representative UK sample used in the final validation of the Short GAAIS-10. Age and sex composition were compared between samples using Welch's t test and a chi-square test, with Cohen's d and Cramer's V used to describe the magnitude of sample differences.

Measurement invariance was examined using ordinal multi-group CFA in R 4.5.2 (R Core Team, 2025) with lavaan 0.7-2 (Rosseel, 2012) and semTools 0.5-7 (Jorgensen et al., 2025). The 10 GAAIS items were treated as ordered categorical variables and estimated using WLSMV with theta parameterization. Identification followed the approach for ordered categorical indicators described by Wu and Estabrook (2016). Configural, threshold, metric (equal thresholds and loadings), and scalar (equal thresholds, loadings, and intercepts) models were tested sequentially.

Scaled chi-square difference tests were supplemented by changes in approximate fit indices; changes of no more than .010 in CFI and .015 in RMSEA were treated as approximate evidence that additional equality constraints did not materially worsen model fit (Chen, 2007).

Because full scalar invariance was not supported, partial scalar invariance was explored post hoc by freeing item intercept constraints one at a time. The largest improvements were obtained for Pos11 and Neg10, and a final partial scalar model freed these two intercepts simultaneously. Latent factor means were then estimated under this partial scalar model with the UK sample as the reference group. Negative items retained their original, non-reversed direction in all invariance and latent-mean analyses, so higher latent Negative scores indicate greater concern or more negative attitudes toward AI. All cross-cultural analyses were exploratory.

3. Results

>Results >Japanese Validation Results

3.1.1. Factor Structure

To examine whether the two-factor (Positive, Negative) structure specified a priori by the original Short GAAIS-10 (Schepman & Rodway, 2026) held in the Japanese data, we first conducted a CFA as the primary test of the factor structure (HAD v18.020; Shimizu, 2016). The model chisquare was significant, χ²(34) = 193.69, p < .001. The fit indices were CFI = .918, TLI = .892, GFI = .917, SRMR = .076, and RMSEA = .100, 90% CI [.087, .114] (AIC = 235.690, BIC = 322.808).

Not all four preregistered fit criteria were met: CFI and SRMR satisfied the preregistered criteria (CFI ≥ .90, SRMR ≤ .08; Hu & Bentler, 1999), and GFI, reported as an additional index, also exceeded .90, whereas TLI (.892) fell marginally below the preregistered threshold of .90 and RMSEA (.100) exceeded the threshold of .08. All standardized factor loadings, however, exceeded the preregistered criterion of .40 (Positive subscale: .491-.800; Negative subscale: .538-.771; all ps < .001), including Neg19 (.538). The CFA therefore provided partial support for the original two-factor structure.

As a supplementary examination of the factor structure, we conducted an EFA (HAD v18.020; maximum likelihood estimation, promax rotation, power = 4). On the basis of the pattern of eigenvalues and the original study (Schepman & Rodway, 2026), a two-factor solution was adopted (factor contributions: Factor1 = 3.358, Factor2 = 3.142; CFI = .969, RMSEA = .071).

The five Positive items loaded primarily on the first factor, and the five Negative items loaded primarily on the second factor (Table 1). Because the Negative items were not reverse-scored in the EFA, the inter-factor correlation was r = -.46. Neg19 ("People like me will suffer if Artificial

Intelligence is used more and more") showed a relatively weak pattern coefficient on the Negative factor (λ = .392), together with a communality (h² = .276) that was low relative to the other nine items (range: .374-.692).

3.1.2. Item Functioning

To examine item characteristics, we conducted a polytomous Rasch analysis using the same software (Jamovi v2.6 with the snowIRT module; Seol, 2022) and the same model (RSM, MML estimation) as the original study (Schepman & Rodway, 2026; Table 1). The threshold parameters shared by all items in the rating scale model showed the ordering τ₁ < τ₂ < τ₃ < τ₄, indicating that, at the subscale level, the five response categories functioned in the expected order. Although the original study noted a possible threshold reversal for Neg15 (Schepman & Rodway, 2026), the subscale-level threshold ordering was maintained in the present rating scale model; moreover, the item fit for Neg15 was acceptable (infit = 0.939, outfit = 0.911).

Item location parameters were also inspected as preregistered and are reported in Supplementary Table S3. Within each subscale, item locations were moderately dispersed, and no item showed an extreme location; thus, the elevated infit and outfit for Neg19 appear to reflect response pattern misfit, rather than an unusually high or low endorsement location within the Negative subscale.

In the EFA and Rasch analyses above, Neg19 showed relatively weak item characteristics compared with the other Negative items. We therefore fitted a supplementary nine-item CFA model excluding Neg19. The resulting fit indices were CFI = .921, TLI = .891, GFI = .922, SRMR = .077, and RMSEA = .109. Compared with the ten-item model (CFI = .918, TLI = .892, GFI = .917, SRMR = .076, RMSEA = .100), the CFI and GFI were slightly higher, the TLI was essentially unchanged, the SRMR was comparable, and the RMSEA was slightly higher. Thus, excluding Neg19 did not yield a clear improvement in model fit.

3.1.3. Internal Consistency and Associations with External Variables

Descriptive statistics, internal consistency coefficients, and inter-variable correlations are presented in Table 2. Both the Positive (α = .84, ω = .85) and Negative (α = .82, ω = .83) subscales of the GAAIS-10-J showed adequate internal consistency. In the analyses that follow, composite scores were computed after reverse-scoring the Negative items; accordingly, higher reverse-scored

Negative scores indicate lower concern about the problems and risks of AI.

The correlation between the Positive and reverse-scored Negative scores was r = .45 (p < .001), indicating a moderate positive association. In addition, the HTMT was .543, a value that provides supplementary support for discriminant validity (Henseler et al., 2015). These values suggest that, although the Positive and reverse-scored Negative scores are related, they are not completely overlapping subscales. They were also broadly consistent with the values in Sample 4 of the original study (r = .49, HTMT = .547; Schepman & Rodway, 2026).

As shown in Table 2, the Positive subscale showed significant positive correlations with TAMbased perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use. It was also positively correlated with NAAIS-3 and negatively correlated with NAAIS-1 and NAAIS-2. The reverse-scored Negative score showed associations in the same direction, although the magnitudes tended to be smaller than those for the Positive subscale.

The frequency of generative AI use showed a moderate-to-strong positive correlation with the Positive score on the GAAIS-10-J (r = .62, p < .001). It also showed strong positive associations with TAM-based perceived usefulness (r = .64, p < .001) and AI usage intention (r = .67, p < .001).

In contrast, its association with the reverse-scored Negative score was relatively weak (r = .23, p < .001). These results indicate that individuals who used generative AI more frequently tended to show more positive evaluations of AI, greater perceived usefulness, and a stronger intention to use it in the future, whereas the association with lower concern about AI-related risks was limited.

Because perceived compulsory use of AI was measured with newly constructed items, we examined its internal consistency and the loadings of the items on a common component before computing a composite score. Internal consistency was acceptable (α = .72), and a supplementary principal component analysis showed that all five items had positive loadings on the first principal component (loadings = .542-.779). However, because some items also loaded on the second principal component, and because no directional hypothesis was preregistered for it, its associations were interpreted on an exploratory basis. In the correlational analyses, perceived compulsory use was not significantly associated with the Positive score (r = -.05), whereas it showed a weak negative correlation with the reverse-scored Negative score (r = -.21, p < .001). It was also not significantly associated with AI usage intention (r = -.05) or the frequency of generative AI use (r = .00). Age was significantly and negatively correlated only with the Positive subscale (r = -.22, p < .001); its association with the reverse-scored Negative score was not significant (r = -.06).

To examine incremental validity beyond core TAM constructs, we conducted an exploratory hierarchical regression predicting AI usage intention (Table 3). Perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use entered at Step 1 explained 62.8% of the variance (R² = .628). Adding the Positive and reverse-scored Negative GAAIS-10-J scores at Step 2 increased explained variance to 69.7% (ΔR² = .070, ΔF(2, 463) = 53.191, p < .001). In the final model, perceived usefulness (β = .394, 95% CI [.304, .484], p < .001) and the Positive score (β = .457, 95% CI [.366, .548], p < .001) showed significant unique associations with AI usage intention, whereas perceived ease of use (β = -.002, p = .932) and the reverse-scored Negative score (β = .047, p = .107) did not. VIFs ranged from 1.234 to 3.282, indicating no severe multicollinearity.

We next examined associations between the GAAIS-10-J and comfort with hypothetical AI applications (Table 4). Following the cluster classification of the original study (Schepman & Rodway, 2026), composite scores were computed as the item means of each cluster. The Positive subscale showed significant positive correlations with all seven clusters (r = .21-.52, all ps < .001).

The reverse-scored Negative score also showed significant positive correlations with all seven clusters, but its correlation with the "malice and intrusion" cluster (r = .10, p < .05) was weaker than those with the other six clusters (r = .15-.32). Thus, associations with comfort with hypothetical AI applications were consistently stronger for the Positive subscale than for the reverse-scored Negative score.

3.1.4. Test-Retest Reliability

Test-retest reliability was examined in the 429 participants with matched T1 and T2 data (Table 5). The T1 and T2 composite scores were computed using the same scoring rules. Internal consistency at the retest was adequate for both subscales (Positive: α = .846, ω = .858; Negative: α = .834, ω = .836). The correlations between the T1 and T2 subscale scores were r = .848, 95%

CI [.819, .872] for the Positive subscale and r = .811, 95% CI [.775, .841] for the reverse-scored Negative score. The single-measure, consistency-type ICCs were .847, 95% CI [.825, .866] for the Positive subscale and .810, 95% CI [.782, .833] for the reverse-scored Negative score, indicating adequate temporal stability for both.

In paired t tests, the difference in means for the Positive subscale was not significant (T1: M = 3.61, SD = 0.73; T2: M = 3.59, SD = 0.71; t(428) = 1.218, p = .224, d = 0.033). For the reversescored Negative score, the T2 mean was significantly lower than the T1 mean (T1: M = 3.50, SD = 0.73; T2: M = 3.40, SD = 0.76; t(428) = 4.667, p < .001, d = 0.139), but the effect size was small.

Comparing the T1 scores of participants and non-participants in the retest survey revealed no significant difference for either the Positive or Negative subscale (Positive: Welch's t(50.80) = -0.853, p = .398; Negative: Welch's t(44.54) = 0.497, p = .621). In addition, as a supplementary analysis informed by the item characteristics of Neg19, we examined the test-retest reliability of the Negative-4 score (excluding Neg19); the T1-T2 correlation was r = .792, 95% CI [.753, .824], and the single-measure, consistency-type ICC was .792, 95% CI [.761, .817].

3.2. Exploratory Cross-Cultural Psychometric Comparison

The Japanese (N = 468) and UK (N = 500) samples were broadly comparable in age and sex composition (Supplementary Results). To examine the extent to which the Short GAAIS-10 showed comparable measurement properties across the two samples and whether latent mean comparisons were feasible, we tested measurement invariance using ordinal multi-group confirmatory factor analysis (CFA; Table 6). Detailed results are provided in the Supplementary Results.

Separate ordinal CFAs showed acceptable CFI and SRMR values but elevated RMSEA values in both the UK (RMSEA = .100) and Japanese (RMSEA = .141) samples. The configural invariance model also showed an elevated RMSEA (.123; Supplementary Results). Therefore, measurement invariance was evaluated primarily based on relative changes in approximate fit indices. Imposing equality constraints on response thresholds across the two samples produced only small changes in model fit (ΔCFI = -.004, ΔRMSEA = -.010). Adding equality constraints on factor loadings did not worsen model fit (ΔCFI = +.005, ΔRMSEA = -.010). Thus, threshold and metric invariance were approximately supported, suggesting that response thresholds and the relationships between items and their underlying factors were broadly comparable across the two samples. In contrast, additionally constraining item intercepts to equality substantially worsened model fit (ΔCFI = -.060, ΔRMSEA = +.046), indicating that full scalar invariance was not supported. Therefore, direct comparisons of observed subscale means were considered inappropriate.

To examine whether latent mean comparisons were possible under more limited conditions, we explored partial scalar invariance. Freeing the intercept constraints for two items, Pos11 (positive impacts of AI on well-being) and Neg10 (AI as dangerous), yielded a model that met the criteria for approximate changes in fit relative to the metric invariance model (ΔCFI = -.010, ΔRMSEA = +.007). However, the partial scalar invariance model still showed an elevated RMSEA (.109; Supplementary Results). Under this partial scalar invariance model, with the UK sample as the reference group, the Japanese sample showed a higher latent Positive mean (estimate = 0.200, p = .002, 95% CI [0.074, 0.326]) and a lower latent Negative mean (estimate = -1.045, p < .001, 95% CI [-1.193, -0.896]). Because the Negative items were not reverse-scored, the lower latent Negative mean indicates lower concern about AI.

Overall, the Short GAAIS-10 showed broadly comparable measurement properties across the Japanese and UK samples, although item-level differences were suggested for Pos11 and Neg10, limiting direct comparisons of observed scores. In the exploratory latent mean comparison accounting for these differences, the Japanese sample had a higher latent Positive mean and a lower latent Negative mean than the UK sample.

4. Discussion

The present study addressed two complementary questions: how the Short GAAIS-10 functions as a standalone measure in Japan and to what extent its measurement properties are comparable with those of the original UK sample.

4.1. Validation of the GAAIS-10-J 4.1.1. Interpretation of the Two-Subscale Structure

The present study translated the Short GAAIS-10 (Schepman & Rodway, 2026) into Japanese as the GAAIS-10-J and found partial support for the original two-factor structure, together with good internal consistency and adequate one-month test-retest reliability. In the CFA, CFI, SRMR, and all standardized factor loadings met the preregistered criteria, whereas TLI and RMSEA did not.

The 90% confidence interval for RMSEA [.087, .114] lay entirely above the preregistered threshold of .08, indicating that the model did not meet this criterion for approximate fit; independent cross-validation is therefore needed (Section 4.3).

The results also support interpreting Positive and Negative attitudes as related but distinguishable aspects of general attitudes toward AI. Positive evaluations of AI are related to, but not identical with, low concern about AI-related risks, and the reverse-scored Negative score should be interpreted as an indicator of lower concern, rather than as a direct measure of ambivalence.

Accordingly, the two subscale scores should not simply be combined into a single total score. This interpretation is consistent with the recommendation of Schepman and Rodway (2026) to interpret the Positive and Negative subscales separately.

4.1.2. Item Functioning and Reliability

Neg19 ("People like me will suffer if Artificial Intelligence is used more and more") showed relatively weak item characteristics. Its standardized CFA loading (.538) exceeded the preregistered .40 criterion, whereas its EFA pattern coefficient was below .40 and its Rasch infit and outfit statistics exceeded the acceptable range. Unlike the other Negative items, which primarily concern general dangers, uncontrollability, or discomfort associated with AI, Neg19 explicitly refers to personal impact. This distinction may partly explain its different response pattern. A similar weakness has been reported in the original studies (Schepman & Rodway, 2020 , 2026) and in the existing Japanese GAAIS-J (Yamaguchi et al., 2025), suggesting that the issue is not unique to the present translation or sample, although cultural or linguistic explanations cannot be established from the present data alone. Importantly, excluding Neg19 did not clearly improve CFA fit, the pattern of associations with external variables, or test-retest reliability. We therefore retained the original 10-item structure to preserve comparability with the Short GAAIS-10, while noting the item-level caveat.

Although the reverse-scored Negative score decreased slightly at retest, the reason for this change remains unclear because this study did not assess changes in AI-related experiences or exposure during the interval. Nevertheless, rank-order individual differences in both subscales were stable over one month.

4.1.3. Associations With Related Constructs

The associations with external variables further clarify what the GAAIS-10-J measures. The Positive subscale was positively associated with TAM-based perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use and showed theoretically coherent associations with NAAIS indicators. These findings indicate overlap with established technology acceptance constructs; however, the GAAIS-10-J does not directly measure usefulness, ease of use, or acceptance of a specific AI system, but instead provides general evaluations encompassing interest, positive expectations about AI, and perceived benefits.

The exploratory hierarchical regression showed that the GAAIS-10-J subscales increased explained variance in AI usage intention by 7.0 percentage points beyond perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use. The Positive score retained a substantial unique association with usage intention after the TAM variables were controlled, whereas the reverse-scored Negative score did not. This result provides criterion-specific evidence that general positive AI attitudes are not fully reducible to perceived usefulness and ease of use. At the same time, the high zero-order correlation between the Positive score and perceived usefulness (r = .82) and their VIFs in the final model indicate substantial overlap, so the finding should not be taken as evidence that the constructs are wholly independent.

Perceived compulsory use of AI was largely unrelated to the Positive score and AI usage intention but showed a weak negative association with the reverse-scored Negative score. This suggests that feeling pressured to use AI may be associated with greater concern about AI-related risks without necessarily reducing positive evaluations of AI. However, because perceived compulsory use was assessed using newly constructed items and not preregistered as a directional hypothesis, these findings should be treated as preliminary.

The GAAIS-10-J subscales were also associated with comfort across all seven hypothetical AI application clusters, generally more strongly for the Positive subscale, while the reverse-scored Negative score showed a particularly weak association with the "malice and intrusion" cluster.

This further distinguishes general AI attitudes from evaluations of specific applications: individuals who generally evaluate AI positively or report relatively low concern may still be uncomfortable with harmful, intrusive, or ethically sensitive uses. Thus, the GAAIS-10-J should not be used as a proxy for acceptance of specific AI technologies or use cases. Because the original cluster classification was not re-estimated in the Japanese sample, these findings do not establish the validity of that classification.

4.2. Cross-Cultural Psychometric Comparison with the Original UK Sample

The present UK-Japan comparison extends the validation beyond separate comparisons of model fit and reliability by directly examining measurement invariance. The approximate support for threshold and metric invariance suggests that the Short GAAIS-10 has broadly comparable measurement properties across the two samples, providing a basis for cautious comparisons of structural relationships, such as associations between latent factors and other variables. However, the lack of full scalar invariance suggests that response patterns for some items may differ between the Japanese and UK samples even among individuals with similar underlying attitudes toward AI.

These findings highlight the importance of testing measurement invariance and accounting for item-level differences, rather than directly comparing observed subscale scores, when using the Short GAAIS-10 in cross-cultural research. Taken together with the within-sample results, these findings illustrate that evidence of reliability and validity within a sample does not, by itself, establish the comparability of observed scale scores across samples.

The exploratory comparison based on the partial scalar invariance model indicated that the Japanese sample had higher Positive and lower Negative latent factor means than the UK sample.

However, these differences cannot be attributed directly to national or cultural factors. Although the two samples were broadly comparable in age and sex composition, differences in sampling methods, survey timing, and AI use experiences were not controlled. Furthermore, the lack of full scalar invariance and the limitations in absolute model fit warrant caution, particularly when interpreting the magnitude of the latent mean differences. Thus, these findings should be interpreted as differences between the two samples rather than as direct evidence of national or cultural differences.

Item-level measurement noninvariance also warrants attention. The items that most strongly affected the fit of the scalar invariance model were Pos11 (positive impacts of AI on well-being) and Neg10 (AI as dangerous), rather than Neg19, which showed relatively poor item fit within the Japanese sample. This suggests that item fit within a single sample and measurement equivalence across samples may reflect different psychometric properties. Future studies using samples recruited during the same period and with harmonized measures of AI exposure and use should examine whether these item-level differences can be replicated. It is also important to investigate whether these differences arise from translation, response styles, generational differences, or broader cultural contexts.

4.3. Strengths and Limitations

A strength of the present study is that the GAAIS-10-J was evaluated at multiple levels, including factor structure, Rasch item functioning, internal consistency, temporal stability, and associations with a broad range of related constructs. The sample also included adults from their 20s through their 60s, and the overall psychometric pattern was broadly comparable with that reported for the original UK samples. The exploratory measurement-invariance analysis further extends the validation by directly testing comparability with the original UK Sample 4 rather than relying only on separate-study benchmarks.

Several limitations should be noted. First, the findings were based primarily on cross-sectional self-report data and do not establish causal relationships between AI attitudes and actual AI use behavior. Longitudinal studies and studies using objective usage indicators would provide stronger evidence regarding predictive validity. Second, the CFA and EFA were conducted in the same sample, and the TLI and RMSEA did not meet the preregistered thresholds; independent crossvalidation is needed. Third, the perceived compulsory use indicator was newly constructed, and some items showed secondary loadings, so its validity as an independent measure requires further investigation. Fourth, the cross-cultural comparison was exploratory and based on independently collected samples. Although age and sex composition were broadly comparable, survey timing, sampling context, and generative AI use could not be fully harmonized across the two samples. In addition, absolute fit in the ordinal CFA models was not uniformly strong, particularly for RMSEA; therefore, support for metric and partial scalar invariance rests primarily on relative changes in approximate fit indices rather than uniformly good absolute fit. Fifth, the Japanese sample was recruited online through a crowdsourcing platform and may not represent the general Japanese adult population, particularly in terms of AI exposure and use; the generalizability of the findings and measurement invariance should therefore be examined in more diverse samples.

Finally, the GAAIS-10-J measures general attitudes toward AI and should not be assumed to capture domain-specific risk perceptions or acceptance of AI in contexts such as medicine, education, hiring, or creative work. Because attitudes toward AI may shift as AI technologies and public discourse change (Fast & Horvitz, 2017), continued validation will be important. The GAAIS-10-J complements, rather than replaces, the existing Japanese GAAIS-J (Yamaguchi et al., 2025): the latter provides a validated Japanese version of the full GAAIS, whereas the GAAIS-10-J offers a shorter option for multi-scale surveys, longitudinal research, and cross-cultural comparisons.

5. Conclusion

The GAAIS-10-J provides a brief and reliable measure of two related but distinguishable aspects of general attitudes toward AI: positive evaluation and concern about AI-related risks, which should be scored and interpreted separately. The scale showed adequate internal consistency, temporal stability, and theoretically coherent associations with related constructs. Exploratory analyses further indicated that the GAAIS-10-J explained additional variance in AI usage intention beyond core TAM variables, and that threshold and metric invariance with the original UK sample were approximately supported. However, because full scalar invariance was not supported, crossnational comparisons of Short GAAIS-10 scores should account for item-level differences. The GAAIS-10-J is a practical tool for research on general attitudes toward AI in Japanese-speaking populations, including HCI studies of AI-enabled systems, and, with appropriate attention to measurement invariance, for future cross-cultural comparisons.
