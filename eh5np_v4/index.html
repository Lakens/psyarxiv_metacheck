How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How Marginal Significance works

List all sentences that describe an effect as ‘marginally significant’.

The marginal module searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The list of terms is a subset of those listed in a blog post by Matthew Hankins. The module returns all sentences that match terms describing ‘marginally significant’ results.

Some of the terms identified might not be problematic in some contexts, and there are ways to describe ‘marginal significance’ that are not detected by the module.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download files from additional data repositories reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Purpose: The development of reading fluency relies on phonological skills and prosodic competence, but evidence about the underlying mechanisms and concurrent relationships supporting this link remain scarce. Previous research has relied on subjective rating scales or adult-based acoustic comparisons, mostly in stress-timed languages. This study investigated whether objective acoustic markers of oral reading prosody serve as a potential link in the relationship between cognitive skills (phonological awareness [PA] and rapid automatized naming [RAN]) and reading fluency in a syllable-timed language.

Method: The sample consisted of 56 typically developing, Hungarian-speaking primary school children (Mage = 8.96 years, 55.36% boys) who completed standardized cognitive assessments. Prosodic competence was measured by comparing acoustic features from an oral reading task against each child's own prosodic imitation baseline, using dynamic time warping to align melodic and rhythmic contours. Data were analyzed using path analysis.

Results: Temporal features, especially tempo stability, emerged as the strongest predictor of reading fluency, whereas melodic and rhythmic markers added no unique variance. Path analysis revealed that RAN predicted reading fluency directly, while PA's contribution was entirely indirect: stronger PA reduced pausal intrusions, which maintained temporal stability and, through it, reading fluency.

Conclusion: PA appears to be linked to reading fluency indirectly through sequential, temporal prosodic execution. In the context of Hungarian (a syllable-timed language), sentence-level temporal mechanisms may carry the primary explanatory weight, highlighting that reading development models should account for the specific rhythmic properties of the reader's native language.

Introduction

Reading fluency is the ability to read text quickly, accurately and with proper expression (Miller & Schwanenflugel, 2008; Benjamin & Schwanenflugel, 2010; Sarris & Dimakos, 2015), integrating lower-level decoding and higher-level comprehension processes (Duong et al., 2011). The present study investigates whether acoustic dimensions of reading prosody account for the shared variance in the link between cognitive precursors and reading fluency in Hungarian primary school children.

Traditional Predictors of Reading Fluency

Phonological awareness (PA), the ability to identify, manipulate, and analyze the sound structure of spoken words (syllables, onset-rime units, phonemes), is strongly associated with reading development (Calet et al., 2015; Holliman et al., 2017; Wade-Woolley & Heggie, 2016). Traditional reading models treat segmental PA as the primary linguistic foundation of reading (Ziegler & Goswami, 2005; Critten et al., 2021), yet its contribution appears limited both developmentally, as it eventually no longer accounts for individual differences in reading ability (Griffith, 1991), and cross-linguistically, as it explains less variance in shallow orthographies than in deeper ones such as English (Schmidt et al., 2023). Rapid automatized naming (RAN), the speed of retrieving and articulating familiar symbols, predicts reading fluency across orthographies of varying consistency (Norton & Wolf, 2012) and is thought to tap domain-general serial processing and articulation mechanisms, independently of prosodic measures in shallow orthographies (Sarris & Dimakos, 2015).

The Role of Prosody in Reading

Even silent reading is accompanied by an "inner voice": according to the Implicit Prosody Hypothesis (Fodor, 2002), readers automatically generate an abstract prosodic structure that guides syntactic parsing (Breen, 2015; Chung & Bidelman, 2022; Tong et al., 2023) and engages voice-selective auditory areas even in silence (Kreiner & Eviatar, 2014; Yao, Belin, & Scheepers, 2011). Because implicit prosody must first be scaffolded through audible, oral reading, oral reading prosody offers a window onto the processes that later support silent phrasing.

At the foundation of this scaffolding is suprasegmental phonology: the ability to identify and manipulate stress, timing, and intonation across syllables (Holliman et al., 2010; Calet et al., 2015; Holliman et al., 2017). This view suggests that the PA-reading relationship is more complex than segmental accounts alone capture (Schmidt et al., 2023). Sensitivity to prosody has been labeled inconsistently, as prosodic awareness (Chan & Wade-Woolley, 2018), prosodic sensitivity (Schmidt et al., 2023; Tong et al., 2023), stress sensitivity (Wood, 2006) or speech rhythm sensitivity (Holliman et al., 2010; Wade-Woolley et al., 2022).

Following Wade-Woolley et al. (2022), we use the umbrella term prosodic competence: individual differences in the ability to reflect on and process speech prosody, manifesting through variations in pitch, duration, and amplitude (Beckman, 1996).

Prosody operates at word, phrase, and discourse levels (Wade-Woolley et al., 2022).

Word-level prosodic competence predicts word reading accuracy, whereas phrase-level competence predicts decoding and comprehension (Whalley & Hansen, 2006 , as cited in Holliman et al., 2010). At the sentence level, intonational phrase boundaries (IPBs), signaled by pitch change, lengthening, or pausing (Selkirk, 2011), help listeners and readers segment utterances; sentences are processed faster when intonation aligns with syntactic boundaries (Kjelgaard & Speer, 1999), and IPBs are used to resolve syntactic ambiguity in both speech and silent reading (Chung & Bidelman, 2022).

While prosody is inherent to skilled reading (Ashby, 2016), sensitivity to suprasegmental features emerges well before formal reading instruction (Johnson & Jusczyk, 2001; Mandel et al., 1994). Automaticity and prosodic text reading are expected to develop from the end of first grade through third grade (Miller & Schwanenflugel, 2008), with prosodic competence contributing from word-level sensitivity in early reading to phrase-level execution as reading becomes more automated (Calet et al., 2015). Several studies have found that prosodic competence contributes to reading independently of segmental PA (Calet et al., 2015; Wade-Woolley & Heggie, 2016; Holliman et al., 2017; Obergfell et al., 2021; Schmidt et al., 2023).

Theoretical Frameworks

Integrating prosody into reading models remains a challenge, and some researchers question whether it is distinct from PA at all (Wade-Woolley & Heggie, 2016). Dual-route accounts place prosodic sensitivity in the lexical route, via stored stress patterns that distinguish grammatical categories and meanings (Coltheart, 2005; Holliman et al., 2017; Wade-Woolley & Heggie, 2016), while others see it as a foundational process underlying PA (Wood, 2006). The Reading Systems Framework (RSF; Wade-Woolley et al., 2022) treats prosodic competence as a core phonological component that informs the orthographic system, the lexicon, and higher-level comprehension.

The developmental mechanisms were specified by Wood, Wade-Woolley, and Holliman (2009), who proposed that prosodic competence influences word reading and spelling both directly and indirectly, via vocabulary growth, phonological awareness and morphological awareness, with rhythm sensitivity supporting the segmentation of continuous speech into word units (Calet et al., 2015; Holliman et al., 2017; Critten et al., 2021).

Empirical findings are mixed. In a longitudinal sample of pre-reading four-to five-year-olds, Critten et al. (2021) found that prosodic competence predicted later reading and spelling only indirectly, through other literacy skills; notably, sensitivity to word-level stress is detectable from 6 months of age (Ragó et al., 2014). By contrast, Holliman et al. (2017) reported a direct contribution even after vocabulary, segmental PA, and morphological awareness were controlled. The inconsistency may reflect developmental timing, but also how prosodic competence is operationalized: most studies rely on behavioral tasks (see Wade-Woolley et al., 2022) rather than acoustic measurement of children's own oral reading prosody.

Reading Fluency and its Connection to Prosodic Competence

Prosody is closely tied to fluency development: as reading rate approaches that of natural speech, oral reading shows more adult-like intonation and rhythm (Ashby, 2016; Schwanenflugel & Benjamin, 2017). Children with stronger decoding read faster and with better-formed prosodic contours (Ashby, 2016) and increasingly produce prosodic distinctions that match the meaning of the text (Schwanenflugel & Benjamin, 2016), whereas dysfluent readers often read with flattened or inappropriate intonation (Kuhn, Schwanenflugel & Meisinger, 2010). Prosodic reading, characterized by pauses, stress, intonation, and phrasing (Calet et al., 2019), is itself a defining feature of fluency (Schwanenflugel et al., 2004), yet its developmental trajectory alongside other reading skills remains underexplored (Miller & Schwanenflugel, 2008). Because fluency and comprehension are bidirectionally linked (Morrison & Wilcox, 2020), prosody also relates to comprehension: children who produce more adult-like contours and sentence-type-appropriate pitch changes show stronger comprehension, whereas pause structure makes no independent contribution once intonation is accounted for (Miller & Schwanenflugel, 2008), suggesting that prosody's contribution to comprehension operates mainly through pitch-based cues.

The Methodological Gap

Reading with expression is a core component of fluency (Dowhower, 1991; Miller & Schwanenflugel, 2006 , 2008; Schwanenflugel et al., 2004) and is commonly rated by humans, using holistic scales such as the NAEP fluency scale or analytic ones such as the Multidimensional Fluency Scale (MDFS; Zutell & Rasinski, 1991). Such ratings depend on rater judgment and require trained raters or multiple rating occasions for acceptable reliability, and the more recent Comprehensive Oral Reading Fluency Scale (CORFS) has seen too limited use for its findings to be compared across studies (Morrison & Wilcox, 2020).

Spectrographic assessment allows acoustic features to be measured directly and has shown that different features play distinct developmental roles: pausing relates to early decoding speed, whereas intonation, captured through F0 contours, accounts for variance in fluency once decoding is automatized (Miller & Schwanenflugel, 2008; Schwanenflugel et al., 2004). Tracking children from Grade 1 to Grade 3, Miller and Schwanenflugel (2008) found that pausal intrusions decreased while intonation became more adult-like; pausing at Grades 1 and 2 did not predict later fluency once word reading was controlled, whereas intonation did. Chung and Bidelman (2022) further showed that oral reading prosody is multidimensional, with distinct acoustic markers (e.g., pause intrusions, pitch matching) differentially predicting reading rate, accuracy, and comprehension. Extracting such measures from children's speech is nonetheless challenging: because it is slower and more variable than adult speech, comparison methods and pause thresholds calibrated on adults may not transfer reliably (Schwanenflugel et al., 2004).

Linguistic and Rhythmic Context

The prosody-comprehension relationship depends on linguistic context: orthographic depth does not appear to moderate it, but the rhythmic class of the reader's first language does (Tong et al., 2023; Wolters et al., 2022). In syllable-timed languages stress prominences occur less frequently, so stress processing may carry far less weight for reading than in stress-timed languages such as English (Calet et al., 2015). Hungarian occupies a distinctive position: it is syllable-timed with predictable, fixed initial stress, and neither pitch nor stress is lexically contrastive. As recent acoustic research demonstrates, stress contrasts are notably weaker in Hungarian than in stress-timed languages. The cognitive weight of prosodic processing is therefore expected to shift away from the word level; sentence-level prosody, specifically temporal execution, rhythmic phrasing, and pausing, should emerge as the primary suprasegmental mechanism supporting syntactic parsing and reading fluency.

Present Study

Previous acoustic approaches have mostly correlated a child's prosodic contour with an adult model of the same sentence (Duong et al., 2011), which may not capture the natural variability in children's speech rate. The present study instead benchmarks each child's oral reading against their own imitation of the same sentence, produced in a separate Prosodic Imitation Task (see Method), with the two aligned using dynamic time warping (DTW).

Reading prosody is characterized by pitch (F0), duration, pausing and loudness (Schwanenflugel et al., 2004 (Schwanenflugel et al., , 2015)); pausing has been linked to decoding difficulty, and pitch to comprehension (Miller & Schwanenflugel, 2008).

We extract four acoustic markers. Melody Error captures the correspondence between a child's reading pitch contour and their own imitation baseline (Chung & Bidelman, 2022; Miller & Schwanenflugel, 2008). Rhythm Error aligns intensity contours (amplitude envelopes) against the same child-specific baseline (Goswami, 2011). Tempo Stability draws on evidence that duration marks syntactic boundaries through preboundary lengthening, facilitating parsing and fluent reading (Wagner & Watson, 2010). Finally, Pausal Intrusions, pauses above a minimum duration occurring within rather than between syntactic units (Chung & Bidelman, 2022), decrease in frequency and length as fluency develops (Schwanenflugel & Benjamin, 2017) and predict both reading rate and comprehension (Chung & Bidelman, 2022; Miller & Schwanenflugel, 2008). All participants were native Hungarian speakers with no reported history of hearing impairment, learning disabilities, speech-language disorders, or neurological conditions. Tasks and Measures

Cognitive and Reading Fluency Measures

We utilized the Hungarian adaptation of the Dyslexia Differential Diagnosis Maastricht Test battery (3DM-H; Tóth et al., 2014). The complete diagnostic battery contains 11 tests designed to assess cognitive processes essential for reading and spelling. For the purposes of the present study, only three subtests were included in the current analyses due to their direct relevance to the study's focus, namely Reading Fluency, Phonological Awareness (PA) and Rapid Automatized Naming (RAN).

Reading Fluency.

The Reading Fluency subtest measures the speed and accuracy of word decoding. Participants were presented with lists of frequent words, rare words, and pseudowords across three blocks. Within each block, items appeared on the screen in three vertical columns, with 15 items displayed simultaneously. Children were instructed to read the words aloud column-by-column as quickly and accurately as possible. The examiner manually advanced the screen as soon as the child finished reading the visible words, allowing a new set of 15 items to appear. The task continued until the child completed all 75 items in the block or the 30-second time limit elapsed. Age-standardized fluency scores including both speed and accuracy measures generated by the 3DM-H software were used as the outcome measure.

Phonological Awareness (PA).

Phonological Awareness was assessed using the Phoneme Deletion subtest. In this task, participants heard pseudowords through headphones and were asked to repeat the item while omitting a specific target phoneme (e.g., saying the pseudoword "hilm" without the "m" sound).

The task comprised 27 test items divided into three blocks of increasing complexity, evaluating the deletion of initial, final, and internal consonants. The examiner recorded response accuracy, and the software logged reaction times to compute the age-standardized PA scores used in the analyses.

Rapid Automatized Naming (RAN).

The Rapid Automatized Naming subtest evaluated the speed of visual-verbal processing and lexical retrieval. Participants completed distinct conditions consisting of separate blocks for letters, numbers, and pictures of familiar, high-frequency objects (e.g., fish, chair, dog). Within each block, 15 stimuli from the same category were displayed simultaneously, organized into three vertical columns on the screen.

Children were required to name the items aloud, column-by-column, as rapidly as possible.

The examiner recorded the total completion time required to name all items within each category. Age-standardized speed scores computed by the 3DM-H software served as the final RAN outcome measure.

Prosodic Tasks and Stimuli

To evaluate prosodic execution, we designed a custom Prosodic Reading Task (PRT) and Prosodic Imitation Task (PIT).

Stimulus Properties.

All stimuli comprised everyday, age-appropriate utterances familiar to children. Sentence length was controlled, ranging from 1 to 7 words (M = 3.85) and from 2 to 15 syllables (M = 7.65).

Although the two tasks were designed to be symmetrical with a total pool of 30 sentences, omissions during data collection resulted in one missing item per task. Consequently, each task contained 29 sentences, 28 of which were identical across both conditions to ensure direct acoustic comparability. Specifically, one sentence was unique to the PIT ("Kik azok?" ['Who are they?']), and one was unique to the PRT ("Miért nem volt itt tegnap?" ['Why was he/she not here yesterday?']).

Sentences were systematically varied across syntactic structures: Declaratives (n=10), Questions (n=9), Imperatives (n=5), and Exclamations (n=5). These were cross-matched with three Hungarian target intonation patterns: Sustained (n=10; e.g., "Bárcsak lenne testvérem!"

['If only I had a sibling!']), Descending (n=9; e.g., "A hétvégén moziban voltunk." ['We went to the movies this weekend.']), and Falling (n=9-10; e.g., "Indulj már a tízóraiért!" ['Go get your snack!']). For the PIT, the reference audio was pre-recorded by two female, trained speech-language therapists using clear, expressive intonation.

Prosodic Reading Task (PRT).

The PRT evaluated how children applied prosodic features when decoding text, without the aid of an auditory model. The 29 sentences were presented visually on a computer screen one by one, centered in an 88-point Calibri font on a solid grey background. Participants were instructed to read the sentences aloud at a natural pace; a new sentence appeared immediately after they finished reading the current one.

Prosodic Imitation Task (PIT). The PIT measured the child's maximum articulatory capacity, providing a child-specific benchmark for the objective acoustic analysis. In this condition, auditory stimuli were presented via headphones, while the corresponding text was simultaneously displayed on the screen using the same visual parameters as the PRT.

Participants were instructed to repeat each sentence aloud after the auditory playback.

Apparatus and Procedure

To prevent auditory priming or memory trace effects from influencing the children's natural reading prosody, task administration followed a fixed sequential order for all participants: the PRT was always administered prior to the PIT. During the testing sessions, visual stimuli were presented in full-screen mode on a 15-inch laptop using Microsoft PowerPoint.

Instructions for the PRT were presented on-screen and read by the children, whereas the examiner explicitly read the on-screen instructions aloud prior to the PIT. Before commencing the 29 experimental trials, each task began with a practice phase consisting of three identical sentences: "Miért?" ['Why?'], "Mi az ebéd ma?" ['What is for lunch today?'], and "Anyukám bolti eladó, apukám villamosvezető." ['My mom is a shop assistant, my dad is a tram driver.']. These practice items were designed to familiarize the children with the procedure and covered different target intonation contours (falling, descending, and sustained, respectively). Audio recordings from the practice phase were excluded from all subsequent acoustic analyses. Continuous audio recording was maintained throughout the entire administration of both tasks using the Windows Voice Recorder application. The children's vocal responses were captured using a headset microphone.

Acoustic Pre-processing and Segmentation

All automated acoustic pre-processing and feature extraction were performed using Python (Version 3.13.5; Python Software Foundation, 2025). The raw, continuous audio recordings of both the reading and imitation tasks were segmented into sentence-level audio files using an automated pipeline. First, the recordings were transcribed with timestamps using the OpenAI Whisper neural network model (large-v2; Radford et al., 2023). To accurately isolate the target sentences, the generated transcriptions were aligned with the predetermined reference text using fuzzy string matching (via the rapidfuzz Python library).

Sentences were extracted from the continuous audio based on the matched timestamps, applying a padding of 0.25 seconds before and 0.70 to 1.0 seconds after the utterances. For the imitation task, where the recordings contained both the adult model's prompt and the child's repetition, the audio files were split at the midpoint, retaining only the child's response for further analysis. To ensure the reliability of the segmentation, a subset of the extracted audio files was manually audited to confirm boundary accuracy. All segmented wav files were converted to mono and resampled to a uniform 16 kHz sampling rate (librosa v. 0.10.0; McFee et al., 2015).

Acoustic Feature Extraction and Prosodic Metrics

Acoustic features were extracted using a custom signal processing pipeline built in Python, utilizing the parselmouth library (Jadoul et al., 2018) to directly access Praat's speech analysis algorithms (Boersma & Weenink, 2021) with a standardized time step of 10 ms.

To quantify prosodic competence, each child's reading performance was mapped against their own Imitation Baseline for each sentence. Four scores were computed:

Tempo Stability (Duration Ratio).

The quotient of the total reading duration divided by the total imitation duration (DurationReading /DurationImitation ). Values greater than 1.0 indicate temporal slowing and reduced processing speed during reading compared to their imitation baseline.

Pausal Intrusions.

The frequency of syntactically inappropriate pauses. While studies have conventionally defined pauses as acoustic silences falling within a 100 to 3,000 ms interval (Benjamin & Schwanenflugel, 2010; Chung & Bidelman, 2022; Miller & Schwanenflugel, 2008), this lower temporal threshold (100 ms) is highly susceptible to capturing natural phonetic closures, particularly given the slower articulation rates typical of pediatric populations. We optimized the temporal threshold to 150 ms. Furthermore, an automated relative energy threshold was implemented: a pausal intrusion was acoustically identified only if the root-mean-square (RMS) energy fell below 4% of the segment's maximum energy for a continuous duration of ≥ 150 ms. The final metric was calculated by subtracting the number of baseline imitation pauses from the reading pauses.

Dynamic Time Warping (DTW) Metrics.

Because reading and imitation naturally unfold at different temporal rates, standard linear comparisons (e.g., point-by-point correlations) of acoustic contours are mathematically unfeasible. Therefore, we applied the Dynamic Time Warping (DTW) algorithm (Senin, 2008), which non-linearly aligns two time-series sequences to find the optimal match, minimizing the distance between them. The alignment was implemented via the librosa Python library.

Melody Error (F0 DTW Cost).

Breakdowns in sentence-level intonation representing the alignment distance (DTW cost) between the fundamental frequency (F0 ) contours of reading and its corresponding imitation baseline. F0 contours were resampled to a fixed length of 50 data points, in order to capture structural contour shape. To ensure the comparison reflects the true melodic shape rather than absolute pitch differences, the contours were z-score standardized prior to analysis. DTW non-linearly warps the reading contour along the time axis to find the optimal alignment path. The final cost was normalized by the alignment path length. Higher scores indicate greater deviation from the target intonation.

Rhythm Error (Amplitude DTW Cost).

Deviations in stress and syllabic rhythm.

Continuous intensity contours (dB) were extracted, resampled to 50 data points, and z-score standardized. It is calculated as the path-normalized DTW distance between the standardized amplitude envelopes (intensity contours) of the two recordings. Higher scores indicate greater deviation from the target rhythmic stress pattern.

Data Analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using the R statistical environment (Version 4.5.1; R Core Team, 2025). Prior to statistical analysis, comprehensive data screening was performed to verify parametric assumptions. The distributions of all continuous variables were assessed for univariate normality by examining skewness and kurtosis values, with acceptable ranges defined as |skewness| < 2.0 and |kurtosis| < 7.0 (George & Mallery, 2010).

Results

Preliminary Data Screening

The distribution of all variables was found to be suitable for parametric testing.

Specifically, all variables showed acceptable normality, with skewness values ranging from -0.27 to 1.04 and kurtosis values ranging from -0.60 to 2.18. Descriptive statistics for all study variables are presented in Table 1.

Pearson Correlation

Prior to conducting the correlation analyses, the assumption of normality was assessed. While univariate Shapiro-Wilk tests indicated minor deviations from normality for two of the temporal prosodic metrics (Tempo Stability and Pausal Intrusions; ps < .01), a multivariate Shapiro-Wilk test confirmed that the assumption of multivariate normality for the overall dataset was not significantly violated (W = 0.971, p = .065).

Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated to examine the relationships between cognitive measures and prosodic competence scores (Table 2). To control for the false discovery rate (FDR) associated with multiple comparisons, all p-values were adjusted using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure. The adjusted p-values demonstrated that the significant relationships were highly robust. Reading Fluency showed the strongest associations with the temporal prosodic markers, with high negative correlations for Tempo Stability (r = -.73, p < .001) and Pausal Intrusions (r = -.64, p < .001). Furthermore, Reading Fluency was significantly associated with both Melody Error (r = -.52, p < .001) and Rhythm Error (r = -.47, p = .001).

Phonological Awareness (PA) correlated significantly with Tempo Stability (r = -.54, p < .001), Rhythm Error (r = -.49, p < .001), Pausal Intrusions (r = -.49, p < .001), and Melody Error (r = -.35, p = .010). Rapid Automatized Naming (RAN) showed negative correlations with all four prosodic competence scores (rs = -.30 to -.42, all ps < .05), indicating that slower naming speed was associated with greater prosodic disruption during reading.

Among the prosodic measures, Tempo Stability and Pausal Intrusions were strongly correlated (r = .84, p < .001), suggesting that children who read more slowly also produced more inappropriate pauses. Rhythm Error showed strong associations with both Tempo Stability (r = .65, p < .001) and Pausal Intrusions (r = .61, p < .001). Melody Error was moderately correlated with the temporal measures (r = .44 and r = .35, respectively) but showed only a marginal association with Rhythm Error (r = .26, p = .051).

Linear regressions

To evaluate the independent predictive power of each prosodic marker on reading fluency, four simple linear regressions were conducted, with assumption checks performed prior to interpretation. Residual normality was confirmed for Pausal Intrusions and Melody Error (Shapiro-Wilk ps > .05), while minor deviations were detected for Tempo Stability (W = 0.951, p = .029) and Rhythm Error (W = 0.946, p = .020). Homoscedasticity was satisfied for most predictors, with a minor violation observed for Pausal Intrusions (BP = 5.21, p = .022). Independence of residuals was confirmed across all models (Durbin-Watson values: 1.94-2.24).

Given the robustness of linear regression to minor normality violations with the present sample size, all models were retained for interpretation.

All four prosodic markers emerged as significant negative predictors of reading fluency. The temporal features demonstrated the highest predictive value: Tempo Stability alone accounted for 52.3% of the variance in reading fluency (R² = .523, B = -12.17, p < .001), and Pausal Intrusions explained 40.6% (R² = .406, B = -6.08, p < .001). The DTW-based acoustic features also yielded significant effects: Melody Error predicted 32.3% of the variance (R² = .323, B = -2.97, p < .001), and Rhythm Error accounted for 21.8% (R² = .218, B = -4.20, p < .001) (Figure 1).

Path analysis

To investigate the directional and mediational relationships between foundational cognitive skills (PA, RAN), acoustic-prosodic markers, and reading fluency, a path analysis was conducted with full information maximum likelihood (FIML) estimation. Given the relatively small sample size for structural equation modeling, the path analysis was conducted as an exploratory approach to investigate the potential structural relationships between cognitive skills, prosodic markers, and reading fluency.

Model Fit.

The path model demonstrated an excellent fit to the data. The chi-square test was non-significant, indicating that the model did not significantly deviate from the observed data structure (χ²(7) = 7.97, p = .335). Furthermore, all indices indicated excellent model fit (CFI = .995, TLI = .985, RMSEA = .050, SRMR = .054). Given the small sample size, a post-hoc power and sensitivity analysis was conducted using the semPower package (Moshagen & Bader, 2024) to evaluate the model's capacity to detect misspecifications.

Assuming a standard acceptable misfit (RMSEA = .08) and an alpha level of .05, the statistical power to reject a misspecified model with 7 degrees of freedom was low (1 -β = .16). Further sensitivity analysis indicated that with N = 56, the current model only achieves adequate power (80%) to reliably detect large effect sizes (RMSEA ≥ .193). Consequently, while the descriptive fit indices suggest an excellent model fit, the non-significant chi-square statistic should be interpreted with caution due to limited statistical power, which reinforces the exploratory nature of investigating these structural relationships.

Discussion

The present study set out to examine (1) the extent to which objective acoustic markers of reading prosody predict reading fluency beyond the contribution of phonological awareness (PA) and rapid automatized naming (RAN), (2) whether PA and RAN relate to reading fluency directly or indirectly through children's acoustic prosodic execution, and (3) which specific acoustic dimensions of prosody carry the greatest explanatory weight.

Acoustic markers of reading prosody, specifically temporal features, were found to predict reading fluency beyond the contribution of cognitive markers. Tempo stability emerged as the strongest direct predictor, whereas melodic and rhythmic markers did not account for any additional unique variance once temporal features were controlled for. PA and RAN related to reading fluency through distinct routes. While RAN maintained a direct relationship with reading fluency, the contribution of PA was fully indirect, explained by children's acoustic prosodic execution. Finally, among the acoustic dimensions, temporal markers carried the greatest explanatory weight within this mediating pathway. Stronger PA reduced pausal intrusions, which in turn limited subsequent shifts in rhythmic execution.

These downstream effects supported tempo stability, which consequently predicted reading fluency.

Our finding that PA influences reading fluency entirely through an indirect, mediated pathway aligns with the developmental model proposed by Critten et al. (2021), although our cross-sectional data reflect concurrent relationships rather than developmental change over time. By demonstrating that PA serves as a fundamental skill that operates through children's prosodic execution, our results differ from frameworks that report a direct contribution of prosodic competence to reading (e.g. Holliman et al., 2010 Holliman et al., , 2017)). This inconsistency could originate from how prosodic competence has been operationalized in previous studies (cf. Critten et al., 2021). Research relying on behavioral tasks, such as stress-judgment or rhythm-detection, has frequently reported direct effects. In contrast, our objective measurement approach uncovers a sequential relationship instead. Specifically, phonological skills appear to provide the segmental foundation required to reduce pausal intrusions, which in turn allows for temporal execution during reading.

Moreover, the predominance of temporal markers in our model diverges from much of the largely English-language literature, which may reflect the distinct rhythmic and prosodic character of Hungarian language. Hungarian is a syllable-timed language with fixed stress that carries no lexically contrastive function -unlike English, where stress alone can shift meaning (e.g., record vs. reCORD; Wade-Woolley & Heggie, 2016), or Mandarin, where pitch does (Chung & Bidelman, 2022). Because stress prominences in syllable-timed languages occur less frequently than in stress-timed languages, stress-based processing was expected to carry little weight for reading in Hungarian (Calet et al., 2015). Our finding that temporal features predicted reading fluency most strongly is consistent with this prediction.

Within this Hungarian sample, prosodic execution in reading appeared to be driven mainly by sentence-level mechanisms, such as temporal execution, rhythmic phrasing, and pausing, rather than by pitch-or stress-based aspects. This pattern may also help explain why melody error, the dimension most directly tied to pitch, contributed no significant unique variance in the current path analysis.

Previously, measuring reading prosody has relied mostly on subjective, human-rated scales (e.g. MFS; Zutell & Rasinski, 1991). While these scales provide valuable estimates of oral reading, they do not offer direct measurements of acoustic features (Schwanenflugel et al., 2004) and are limited by the subjectivity of human judgment (Morrison & Wilcox, 2020).

To overcome these limitations, researchers have turned to spectrographic analysis to quantify reading prosody objectively (Chung & Bidelman, 2022; Miller & Schwanenflugel, 2008; Schwanenflugel et al., 2004). The present study introduces a methodological advancement to better capture the realities of pediatric speech. Previous spectrographic approaches have often relied on adult template models, correlating a child's prosodic contour against an averaged adult standard (Duong et al., 2011); however, because children's speech rate is slower and more variable than that of adults, such comparisons can introduce developmental confounds.

The present methodology addressed this by benchmarking each child's reading performance against their own prosodic imitation, produced during a separate task, rather than against an external adult standard. This individualized approach was applied selectively: dynamic time warping was used to align the melody and rhythm measures against each child's own imitation baseline, whereas tempo stability and pausal intrusions were derived directly from the reading performance itself, without requiring this alignment.

Limitations and Future Research

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the size of the current sample limits the statistical power available for detecting smaller effects, particularly within the path model, and constrains the generalizability of the pathway structure. As confirmed by sensitivity analysis, a sample size of 56 children only provides adequate statistical power to reliably detect large structural misspecifications (RMSEA ≥ .193). Consequently, the path model may lack the precision needed to capture smaller, yet theoretically meaningful pathways among the multiple cognitive and acoustic predictors. Therefore,conclusions regarding the unique contribution of each prosodic marker and the specific indirect role of phonological awareness should be considered exploratory and require validation in adequately powered future cohorts.

Second, the sample consisted of a single, narrow age range (8-11 years) of typically developing children. Because prosodic competence and reading fluency both follow prolonged developmental trajectories, the mediating role of pausal intrusions and temporal stability observed here may not generalize to younger, pre-literate children or to older, more fluent readers, nor to children with reading difficulties.

Third, the cross-sectional design limits causal interpretation of our results. Because all data were collected at the same time point, the temporal ordering required to establish a mediation chain cannot be confirmed. While our path model tests a directional sequence based on previous frameworks (e.g., Wood et al., 2009), the findings represent concurrent structural relationships rather than developmental mechanisms. Future longitudinal studies are needed to establish the temporal progression of these skills. Relatedly, because participants were already literate, their orthographic knowledge may have influenced performance on the phonological awareness measures themselves, rather than these measures reflecting a pre-literate skill (Castles & Coltheart, 2004 , as cited in Critten et al., 2021). This raises a directionality concern that extends to the prosodic measures as well: because prosodic competence was assessed through oral reading rather than through spontaneous speech, it cannot be definitively established whether prosodic execution shapes reading fluency, or whether more fluent, automatized reading itself enables more accurate prosodic production during the task.

Finally, the ecological validity of using oral reading as an indicator for children's broader prosodic competence must be taken with careful consideration, as reading prosody is not identical to spontaneous speech. Spontaneous speech typically exhibits greater pitch variability and more frequent end-of-sentence pitch rises to mark minor syntactic boundaries (Esser & Polomski, 1988 , as cited in Schwanenflugel et al., 2015), alongside more frequent pausing and less consistent stress marking (Howell & Kadi-Hanifi, 1991 , as cited in Schwanenflugel et al., 2015). Children's oral reading may therefore reflect a narrower range of their overall prosodic competence than the present measures suggest.

Despite these limitations, the study has several methodological and theoretical strengths. Most importantly, by benchmarking each child's reading against their own prosodic imitation rather than an adult baseline, the study achieved a level of measurement sensitivity that allowed individual-level acoustic differences to emerge as meaningful predictors of reading fluency. Furthermore, the focus on Hungarian language provided a theoretically In conclusion, the present study demonstrates that objective, individually matched acoustic measures of reading prosody can reveal structure in the relationship between foundational cognitive skills and reading fluency that behavioral and group-level measures may obscure. In Hungarian primary school children, phonological awareness influenced reading fluency entirely through prosodic execution, with temporal stability as the strongest predictor. Overall, these findings highlight the importance of acoustically grounded, language-specific approaches to understanding how prosody supports the development of reading fluency.
