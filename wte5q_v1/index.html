Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Marginal Significance works

List all sentences that describe an effect as ‘marginally significant’.

The marginal module searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The list of terms is a subset of those listed in a blog post by Matthew Hankins. The module returns all sentences that match terms describing ‘marginally significant’ results.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. Many tasks require expertise spanning multiple skill sets and are too complex for any single individual to address alone, making collective expertise essential (Kozlowski & Bell, 2003). Teamwork has therefore become a central mode of organizing work, as it allows diverse skills to be pooled within a collaborative climate. Team performance, however, is not merely the sum of individual capabilities. It depends on the quality of the interactions and dynamics that develop within the team. Research has shown, for example, that individual job performance is shaped by the quality of team dynamics and interpersonal interactions (van de Brake et al., 2018).

The contribution of teamwork to complex cognitive and problem-solving challenges is well documented (for a review, see Schmutz et al., 2019). Teams, as dynamic and interactive units, can generate diverse perspectives, stimulate creative thinking, and enhance problemsolving strategies (Hagemann & Kluge, 2017). These benefits extend well beyond the business realm, and team dynamics have been studied in sports (Filho et al., 2015), e-sports (Tang, 2018), and education (Heutte et al., 2021). Within this literature, a growing body of work indicates that the experience of team flow is associated with enhanced team performance (Aubé et al., 2014; Sawyer, 2006 Sawyer, , 2007;; van den Hout et al., 2018). The concept of team flow originates in Csikszentmihalyi's (1990) research on flow in individuals, defined as a gratifying state of deep involvement and absorption that people report when they face a challenging activity and perceive their abilities as adequate to cope with it (European Flow Researchers Network [EFRN], 2014). One of the components of flow described by Csikszentmihalyi is the loss of self-consciousness, whereby individuals become so absorbed in the activity that they stop attending to their own needs (Csikszentmihalyi & Nakamura, 2018). This concept was later extended to group dynamics (e.g., Burow, 2015; Sawyer, 2006 Sawyer, , 2007;; van den Hout et al., 2018). Team flow is thought to emerge when individuals who work together toward a shared objective collectively experience this state of flow. It is characterized by intense concentration, a diminished sense of self-awareness, and a shared feeling of progress and satisfaction. In this collective form, the loss of self-consciousness takes on a particular meaning, as members become absorbed in the shared activity to the point of losing focus on their individual needs (Csikszentmihalyi & Nakamura, 2018). Sawyer (2007) similarly describes a blending of egos, in which individual identities merge into a collective team identity.

Development and Prior Validation of the Team Flow Monitor

Building on this conceptualization, van den Hout et al. (2019) developed the Team Flow Monitor (TFM) to operationalize team flow through item generation and refinement, exploratory and confirmatory factor analyses, and assessments of reliability and validity. The TFM comprises two sets of items. The first assesses seven prerequisites of team flow (e.g., collective ambition, common goal, open communication) and the second assesses four core characteristics of the team flow experience, namely (a) sense of unity, (b) sense of joint progress, (c) mutual trust, and (d) holistic focus. In the original validation conducted with 110 work teams, team flow was best represented as a second-order model comprising two higherorder factors, the prerequisites and the characteristics, with good fit and internal consistency reliabilities above .70 (van den Hout et al., 2019). The present research focuses on the 14-item characteristics subscale, which captures the subjective experience of team flow itself rather than its antecedents.

One of the main characteristics of team flow is the sense of unity. This dimension refers to a deep feeling of belonging and shared purpose, in which team members perceive themselves as a cohesive unit rather than as a collection of individuals. This unity emerges from the alignment of personal goals with the collective ambition. Research has shown that when team members are dedicated to achieving their shared goals, this fosters a sense of unity and contributes to team flow (van Oortmerssen et al., 2022).

The second dimension is the sense of joint progress, a shared feeling of moving forward together toward common goals. It is facilitated by the integration of diverse skills and by open communication, which allow members to understand their individual contributions and to collaborate effectively toward both common and personal goals. Sports teams, for example, use a variety of communication strategies such as code words, hand signals, and specific phrases to coordinate action, provide instant feedback, and maintain motivation, all of which contribute to a sense of shared momentum toward the objective (Orr & Malone, 2010; Ryan et al., 2024).

A third dimension is mutual trust. Mutual trust allows members to feel safe, to take risks without fear of failure, and to rely on each other's capabilities. Open communication provides the necessary feedback and fosters a sense of control and autonomy within the team, which, as Sawyer (2007) suggests, empowers individuals to act on their ideas without seeking constant permission. Trust essentially involves a willingness to be vulnerable, that is, to let go and rely on others (Mayer et al., 1995; Nienaber et al., 2015). Vulnerability reflects the acceptance of one's dependence on the actions and decisions of others (van den Hout et al., 2018). Trust thus creates a safe environment in which everyone feels supported and confident in their contributions, conditions that align with the needs for relatedness and competence described in self-determination theory (Deci & Ryan, 2000) and that foster collective efficacy and, ultimately, team flow (Salanova et al., 2014).

The fourth dimension is holistic focus, a state of shared concentration and engagement in which the team operates with a collective consciousness. Shared beliefs and emotions bind members together (Wortmann, 2016) and, in a manner similar to Snow's (2010) notion of total concentration on the shared activity, this focus arises from a mutual commitment to the 6 collective ambition combined with focused effort on the individual tasks that contribute to the overall objective (Katzenbach & Smith, 1992). According to van den Hout et al. (2018), the presence and strength of these four characteristics determine the emergence and intensity of team flow.

Team flow shares conceptual similarities with work engagement, as both involve deep absorption and sustained motivation during activity (EFRN, 2014). Team flow is nonetheless inherently collective and situational, arising from dynamic interactions between team members, whereas engagement is typically conceptualized at the individual level. Team flow is also assumed to improve collective performance and efficiency, as the shared optimal experience reinforces coordination and collective effort (Aubé et al., 2014; Salanova et al., 2014). By contrast, grit, defined as perseverance and passion for long-term goals (Duckworth et al., 2007), is a relatively stable individual disposition that operates over the long term, independently of the collective and momentary dynamics that characterize team flow. These conceptual distinctions guided our hypotheses regarding convergent and discriminant validity.

The Present Research

Beyond factor structure and internal consistency, establishing construct validity requires examining how the adapted French TFM relates to theoretically connected and unrelated constructs, in line with established psychometric standards (American Educational Research Association et al., 2014). The present research therefore aimed to provide initial evidence of the factorial, convergent, and discriminant validity of the French TFM characteristics subscale across two studies. In Study 1, we translated the scale, examined its factor structure and reliability, and tested its associations with work engagement, self-rated task performance, and grit. Because work engagement and self-rated task performance share an experiential core with team flow but operate at the individual level, we expected the French TFM to correlate positively, though only moderately, with both. We expected little or no association with grit, a stable individual disposition (Cocić et al., 2025; Duckworth et al., 2007) that is conceptually distinct from the collective, situational state of team flow, a pattern that would support discriminant validity (Rönkkö & Cho, 2022). In Study 2, we examined whether the scale relates to group-level outcomes, as a valid measure of a collective state should, by testing its associations with perceived team performance and satisfaction with team performance in intact student teams, while expecting these associations to be substantially stronger than the association with perceived individual performance.

Study 1 Method

Participants

A total of 225 participants aged 18 to 58 years took part in Study 1 (Mage = 27.83, SDage = 9.58). Among them, 180 (80%) identified as women, 29 (13%) as men, and 16 (7%) preferred not to answer or did not identify with these two options.

Participants were recruited through psychology courses at French universities and through higher education mailing lists.

Sample size was determined a priori in two ways. First, prior recommendations suggest at least 10 participants per item for factor analysis (Costello & Osborne, 2005), which corresponds to 140 participants for a 14-item scale. Second, a power analysis for the confirmatory factor analysis was conducted using the RMSEA-based approach of MacCallum et al. (1996), which is adapted to tests of model fit rather than relying on a conventional effect size drawn from previous studies. With RMSEA0 = .05, RMSEAa = .08, df = 76, α = .05, and power set at .80, the minimum required sample size was 159 participants. The final sample of 225 participants exceeded both requirements.

Procedure

Data were collected during the 2017-2018 academic year using Qualtrics, an online survey platform. Before accessing the questionnaire, participants read an information page describing the study and gave their explicit consent by clicking a confirmation button. Only participants who consented could proceed. Participation was voluntary and unpaid, and all data were collected anonymously, so that no participant could be identified. Demographic information (gender, age) was collected at the end of the survey. The study consisted exclusively of anonymous self-report questionnaires on non-sensitive topics and involved no intervention and no health-related data. In accordance with French regulations on research involving human participants (Loi Jardé, Law No. 2012 -300 and Decree No. 2016 -1537), such non-interventional questionnaire research in the humanities and social sciences does not fall within the scope of the mandatory review by a Comité de Protection des Personnes, and no formal ethics approval was therefore required. Both studies were nonetheless conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and with the French Code of Ethics for Psychologists.

Measures

Team Flow Monitor. Participants completed a French adaptation of the 14-item characteristics subscale of the Team Flow Monitor (van den Hout et al., 2019). This subscale measures the four core characteristics of team flow, namely sense of unity (3 items; e.g., "We feel as one with the team"), sense of joint progress (4 items; e.g., "We progress collectively"), mutual trust (4 items; e.g., "We trust each other to collectively complete our task"), and holistic focus (3 items; e.g., "Everyone is completely focused on the shared task"). Items are worded with the team as referent, consistent with a referent-shift consensus model of teamlevel constructs (Chan, 1998). Scores therefore reflect each member's perception of the team's experience, and whether these perceptions are sufficiently shared within teams to justify aggregation is a distinct question that is discussed in the General Discussion. Participants rated each item on a 7-point scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree).

Internal consistency in the present sample is reported in the Results section. The scale demonstrated strong internal consistency (α = 0.96; ω = 0.96).

Regarding the translation procedure, the original English version of the scale was translated into French following Vallerand's (1989) procedure for cross-cultural adaptation of psychological instruments. One bilingual co-author (i.e., the second author) translated the original English version into French. Next, the third co-author, blind to the original items, then back-translated the French translation into English. Discrepancies between original and back-translated versions were reviewed and discussed by the third co-author until full semantic and conceptual equivalence was reached. This iterative process ensured that the final French version preserved the scale's original meaning while being culturally fit to Frenchspeaking contexts. The scale was then administered to the main sample.

Work Engagement. Work engagement in studies was assessed with the Utrecht Work Engagement Scale -Student Form (UWES-SF; Gusy et al., 2019). The scale comprises nine items rated on a 7-point frequency scale (0 = never;6= always). It measures two facets of engagement in academic activities, namely, vigor (e.g., "I feel enthusiastic about my studies"), dedication (e.g., "I feel enthusiastic about my studies"), and absorption (e.g., "I am immersed in my studies"). Items were averaged into a total score, with higher values indicating greater engagement. In the present sample, internal consistency was strong (α = .90, ω = .90).

Task Performance. Self-rated task performance was assessed with Task Performance (TP) subscale of the Individual Work Performance Scale (Koopmans et al., 2012). The scale comprises 7 items on a six-point Likert Scale and assesses the task-oriented aspects of performance, including absolute performance regarding work quality and quantity (e.g., "how do you rate the quantity of your work during the last three months"), relative performance compared to last year (e.g., compared to last year, I judge the quantity of my work during the last three months as…"), and ability to achieve goals (e.g., "I worked towards the end result of my work"). We used task performance as a measure of criterion validity. The scale displayed good internal consistency (α = 0.80; ω = 0.80).

Grit.

Grit was assessed with the 12-item Grit Scale (Grit-O; Duckworth et al., 2007), using the French-Grit-O scale as a validated (Elimari et al., 2026), translated tool which measures perseverance of effort and consistency of interests (e.g., "I finish whatever I begin"). Items are rated on a 5-point scale ranging from 1 (not like me at all) to 5 (very much like me), and higher mean scores reflect higher grit. Internal consistency in the present sample was acceptable (α = .76; ω = 76).

Data Analysis

Descriptive statistics, item-rest correlations, skewness, and kurtosis were first examined for each item. The dimensionality of the French TFM was then investigated in two steps. Because the factor structure of a translated instrument may differ from that of the original, we first conducted an exploratory factor analysis (EFA) with maximum likelihood extraction and oblimin rotation, after verifying sampling adequacy with the Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) index and Bartlett's test of sphericity. The number of factors was examined with the Empirical Kaiser Criterion (EKC; Braeken & van Assen, 2017), parallel analysis (Horn, 1965), and the minimum average partial (MAP) test (Velicer, 1976). Because these criteria can retain too few factors when factors are strongly correlated (Auerswald & Moshagen, 2019), as expected for the facets of team flow, the structure to retain was then determined by comparing five confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) models of the 14 items: a one-factor model; the two-factor model suggested by the EFA; a three-factor model in which unity and joint progress, the two most strongly correlated facets, were merged; the original model with four correlated factors; and the original model with four factors loading on a second-order team flow factor (van den Hout et al., 2019). Because the items had seven response categories and their distributions did not depart markedly from normality (skewness from -0.95 to -0.23 and kurtosis from -0.82 to 0.76; Online Resource 1), the CFA models were estimated with maximum likelihood (ML), treating the items as continuous (Rhemtulla et al., 2012).

Model fit was evaluated with the chi-square test, the comparative fit index (CFI), the Tucker-Lewis index (TLI), the standardized root mean square residual (SRMR), and the root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA), using conventional cut-offs for good fit (CFI and TLI ≥ .95, SRMR ≤ .08, RMSEA ≤ .06; Hu & Bentler, 1999), with RMSEA values up to .08 considered indicative of acceptable fit (MacCallum et al., 1996). Nested models were compared with chi-square difference tests, and all models were compared with the Akaike (AIC) and Bayesian (BIC) information criteria, lower values indicating a better balance between fit and parsimony. The best-fitting model was defined as the model that met these fit criteria and had the lowest AIC and BIC; among nested models, the more parsimonious model was preferred when it did not fit significantly worse. The distinctiveness of the facets was examined through their latent correlations and 95% confidence intervals (Rönkkö & Cho, 2022). Reliability was estimated with Cronbach's alpha and McDonald's omega for the total scale and for each facet. Convergent validity was examined through Pearson correlations between team flow, work engagement, and task performance, and discriminant validity was assessed with the disattenuated correlation between team flow and grit, following the guideline of Rönkkö and Cho (2022). All analyses were conducted with jamovi (Version 2.6;

The jamovi project, 2024) which includes the R packages lavaan (Rosseel, 2012) and psych (Revelle, 2023).

Convergent Validity

Descriptive statistics and correlations for the study variables are presented in Table 3.

To assess convergent validity, we examined the correlations between team flow and two theoretically related constructs, task performance and work engagement. Although both correlations were significant, their magnitude was small to moderate, which suggests that these measures capture related but distinct aspects of the work experience, as expected for constructs assessed at different levels of analysis.

Discriminant Validity

We followed the updated guideline of Rönkkö and Cho (2022) for assessing discriminant validity, according to which two measures can be regarded as measuring distinct constructs when the absolute value of their correlation, after correction for measurement error, is sufficiently low. The disattenuated correlation was computed as the observed correlation divided by the square root of the product of the two reliability coefficients. The observed correlation between the Grit Scale and the French TFM was -.13 (p > .05), and the reliabilities were .96 for the TFM and .76 for the Grit Scale. The resulting absolute disattenuated correlation, |-.13| / √(.96 × .76) = .15, is far below the thresholds at which discriminant validity becomes questionable (Rönkkö & Cho, 2022), which supports the conclusion that the French TFM does not measure the same construct as grit.

Discussion

Study 1 aimed to validate a French translation of the characteristics subscale of the Team Flow Monitor (van den Hout et al., 2019). The French adaptation showed excellent internal consistency and initial evidence of construct validity. It correlated positively with self-rated task performance and work engagement, and its disattenuated correlation with grit was close to zero, which supports its distinctiveness from a stable, trait-like individual disposition involving perseverance and passion for long-term goals (Duckworth et al., 2007).

Unlike team flow, which is a collective and situational state, grit operates at the individual level and independently of moment-to-moment group dynamics.

Regarding the scale's dimensionality, the comparison of confirmatory models identified the original structure of four facets loading on a second-order team flow factor as the best-fitting model (van den Hout et al., 2019). The one-and two-factor models, including the two-factor solution suggested by the exploratory analysis, fit significantly worse, whereas the second-order model fit as well as the model with four correlated factors and had the lowest information criteria. The facets were nonetheless strongly correlated, and unity and joint progress were hardly distinguishable, since merging them did not significantly reduce fit.

From a theoretical standpoint, this psychometric convergence is consistent with multilevel organizational frameworks. As Kozlowski and Klein (2000) note, composition models of emergence assume that lower-level psychological characteristics can coalesce and lose variability through shared experiences and interactions. When team dynamics are tightly synchronized, facets such as joint progress may become difficult to separate from the overall sense of unity. This interpretation is consistent with evidence that high social interdependence within groups intensifies the collective flow experience (Walker, 2010). In practice, these results support the use of the total score as the main indicator of team flow, with facet scores interpreted with caution. We return to what this structure might mean, and whether it holds beyond student teams, in the General Discussion.

While Study 1 supported the reliability and the second-order four-factor structure of the French TFM, its convergent validity evidence remained limited to individual-level measures. The moderate associations observed with engagement and task performance are what one would expect between related but distinct constructs assessed at different levels, and they should not be read as a failure of the scale to capture team flow. Team flow is nonetheless an intrinsically collective phenomenon, and establishing its association with the team's perceived success is essential for grounding the scale's construct validity. Study 1, which focused primarily on factor structure and internal consistency, did not include such a measure. Study 2 was designed to address this gap by examining the convergent validity of the French TFM through its correlations with perceived group performance in intact student teams. Because a change in the referent alters the conceptual meaning of a construct (Chan, 1998), we further expected team flow scores to be much less related to perceived individual performance than to perceived team performance, which would provide additional discriminant validity evidence.

Study 2 Method

Participants

Ninety-three students from a French university, all enrolled in a course that required them to complete a collaborative assignment in teams, completed the survey. One participant did not give consent on the first page of the questionnaire and was excluded, leaving a final sample of 92 participants. The sample consisted of 80 women (87.0%), 6 men (6.5%), and 6 participants (6.5%) who identified as non-binary or preferred not to respond. Participants were between 18 and 37 years old (Mage = 19.86, SDage = 3.20). Regarding academic level, 64 (69.6%) were first-year undergraduate students and 26 (28.3%) were master's students. This information was missing for two participants (2.2%). Team membership was recorded through free-text fields (tutorial group number and project topic) that did not allow teams to be identified unambiguously, so that the number of teams and their size could not be established.

With 92 participants, the study had 80% power to detect correlations of approximately .29 or larger (α = .05, two-tailed; Cohen, 1988).

Measures

Participants completed the French TFM described in Study 1. In line with the secondorder four-factor structure retained in Study 1, a total team flow score was computed as the mean of the 14 items, and four facet scores were computed as the mean of the corresponding items, namely sense of unity (Items 1-3), sense of joint progress (Items 4-7), mutual trust (Items 8-11), and holistic focus (Items 12-14). Internal consistency in this sample was excellent for the total scale (α = .97, ω = .97) and good to excellent for the four facets (sense of unity: α = .91, ω = .91; sense of joint progress: α = .89, ω = .89; mutual trust: α = .95, ω = .96; holistic focus: α = .94, ω = .94). In addition, several individual-level and team-level indicators were developed for this study to assess convergent and discriminant validity. Each of these constructs was assessed with a single item rated on a numerical scale from 0 to 100.

Single-item measures have been shown to be valid for relatively narrow and unambiguous constructs (Cheung & Lucas, 2014), and they were preferred here to limit participant burden after the collaborative assignment.

Individual-Level Measures.

Five items assessed participants' perceptions of their own performance and contribution. Perceived individual performance was rated from 0 (worst possible individual performance) to 100 (best possible performance). Satisfaction with personal contribution was rated from 0 (total dissatisfaction) to 100 (total satisfaction).

Perceived relative contribution asked participants to compare their contribution with the average contribution of the other team members, from 0 (contributed much less than the team average) to 100 (contributed much more than the team average). Finally, two items assessed the extent to which participants felt that their individual performance depended on the team (0 = not at all dependent on the team,100 = completely dependent on the team) and on themselves (0 = not at all dependent on myself,100 = completely dependent on myself).

Team-Level Measures. Two items assessed perceptions of the team as a whole.

Perceived overall team performance was rated from 0 (worst possible performance) to 100 (best possible performance), and satisfaction with team performance from 0 (not at all satisfied) to 100 (totally satisfied).

Procedure

Data were collected through an online Qualtrics survey. Participants were recruited during their university courses and completed the questionnaire after finishing a collaborative group assignment. As in Study 1, participants gave their explicit consent on the first page of the survey, participation was voluntary and anonymous, and demographic information (gender, age, academic level) was collected at the end of the survey. The same regulatory framework as in Study 1 applied, and no formal ethics approval was required for this noninterventional questionnaire study.

Data Analysis

Prior to the main analyses, the distributions of all variables were examined. Skewness and kurtosis values fell within the range of -2 to +2 (George & Mallery, 2010), which was considered adequate for parametric testing. Pearson correlations were therefore computed between team flow (overall mean and the four facet scores) and the individual-level and teamlevel perceptions. This approach is consistent with standard psychometric procedures for examining construct validity through patterns of convergent and discriminant correlations (American Educational Research Association et al., 2014). Because the individual-level and team-level perceptions were assessed with single items, their reliability could not be estimated and disattenuated correlations were not computed in this study. To test whether team flow was more strongly related to team-level than to individual-level perceptions, dependent correlations sharing the team flow score were compared with the z test of Meng et al. (1992).

The same comparisons were run for each facet, and the chi-square test of heterogeneity among correlated correlations (Meng et al., 1992) was used to examine whether the four facets differed in their association with a given criterion. Partial correlations were used to examine whether the association between team flow and an individual-level evaluation persisted once the corresponding team-level evaluation was controlled, and conversely.

Because some criteria were negatively skewed, Spearman rank correlations were computed as a robustness check. Item responses were exported with a 0 to 6 coding and were rescaled to the 1 to 7 metric of Study 1 for descriptive purposes, which leaves correlations unchanged.

Participants were nested within teams and the analyses were conducted at the individual level.

Because team membership could not be identified, the possible inflation of Type I error due to non-independence was examined with a sensitivity analysis based on the design effect (Kish, 1965). The significance of each correlation was recomputed for effective sample sizes corresponding to design effects ranging from 1.5 to 4, which covers the values expected for teams of three to six members with intraclass correlations up to .60. Finally, although the sample was small for this purpose, the five measurement models of Study 1 were re-estimated with maximum likelihood as a supplementary check of whether the ordering of the models replicated in an independent sample. Because the maximum likelihood chi-square and the fit indices derived from it are biased toward misfit in small samples (Herzog & Boomsma, 2009), this check focused on the comparison between models rather than on absolute fit.

Convergent Validity With Team-Level Measures

Consistent with expectations, team flow scores were strongly and positively related to the team-level perceptions. The overall team flow score correlated with perceived overall team performance (r = .68, 95% CI [.56, .78], p < .001) and with satisfaction with team performance (r = .72, 95% CI [.61, .81], p < .001). The same pattern held for each of the four facets (Table 4). Perceived team performance correlated with sense of unity (r = .59), sense of joint progress (r = .66), mutual trust (r = .65), and holistic focus (r = .61), and these four correlations did not differ significantly from one another, χ²(3) = 2.45, p = .485. Satisfaction with team performance also correlated with each facet (all ps < .001), with more heterogeneous values, χ²(3) = 10.69, p = .013. The correlations were descriptively higher for sense of joint progress (r = .71) and mutual trust (r = .72) than for sense of unity (r = .62) and holistic focus (r = .59). Given the strong overlap among facets and the number of criteria examined, this difference should be interpreted with caution. Spearman rank correlations led to the same conclusions (for the total score, ρ = .61 with perceived team performance and ρ = .65 with satisfaction with team performance; for the facets, ρs from .52 to .65).

Higher levels of team flow, on each of its four facets, were thus associated with more positive evaluations of the team's overall performance and with greater satisfaction with that performance, which supports the convergent validity of the scale with group-level constructs.

These correlations reflect associations between constructs and do not imply causal relationships.

Discriminant Validity With Individual-Level Measures

Team flow scores were also related to some individual-level perceptions, but to a much lesser extent. The overall team flow score correlated weakly with perceived individual performance (r = .28, 95% CI [.08, .46], p = .006) and with satisfaction with personal contribution (r = .22, 95% CI [.01, .40], p = .037). The four facets showed the same pattern (Table 4), with correlations from .23 to .30 with perceived individual performance and from .16 to .27 with satisfaction with personal contribution, the latter being non-significant for mutual trust and holistic focus. Comparisons of dependent correlations confirmed that team flow was significantly more strongly related to perceived team performance than to perceived individual performance (z = 4.26, p < .001), and more strongly related to satisfaction with team performance than to satisfaction with personal contribution (z = 4.66, p < .001). Both differences were also significant for each of the four facets (zs from 2.95 to 4.31 for performance and from 3.12 to 5.06 for satisfaction, all ps ≤ .003). Moreover, once perceived team performance was controlled, team flow was no longer related to perceived individual performance (partial r = .01, p = .897), whereas its association with perceived team performance remained strong when perceived individual performance was controlled (partial r = .65, p < .001). The same held for satisfaction, with a partial correlation of .15 (p = .160) with satisfaction with personal contribution and of .71 (p < .001) with satisfaction with team performance. Team flow was, in addition, negatively related to perceived relative contribution (r = -.40, p < .001) and to the perception that one's performance depended on oneself (r = -.41, p < .001), and positively related to the perception that one's performance depended on the team (r = .34, p < .001). The four facets did not differ significantly in their associations with any of the individual-level perceptions, χ²(3) ≤ 4.03, ps ≥ .258.

This pattern supports the discriminant validity of the French TFM with respect to individual-level performance constructs, in the sense that the scale is far more closely tied to evaluations of the team than to evaluations of oneself. The small positive associations with perceived individual performance and with satisfaction with personal contribution are not unexpected, since members of a well-functioning team may also feel that they performed well, and their magnitude remains well below the values at which two measures would be suspected of capturing the same construct (Rönkkö & Cho, 2022). Because the individuallevel and team-level measures were single items, disattenuated correlations could not be computed. As above, these correlations reflect associations between constructs rather than evidence of causal relationships.

Sensitivity to Non-Independence

The sensitivity analysis indicated that the associations between team flow and the team-level perceptions were robust to non-independence. They remained significant at p < .001 for design effects up to 4, and would only lose significance for a design effect above 10, that is, an effective sample size below 10 participants. The associations of the four facets with the team-level perceptions were similarly robust, as all of them remained significant for design effects up to 8, and at p < .001 for design effects up to 3. The negative associations with perceived relative contribution and with dependence on oneself remained significant for design effects up to about 3.5, and the association with dependence on the team up to about 2.5. By contrast, the small associations with perceived individual performance and with satisfaction with personal contribution lost significance for design effects of about 2 and 1.1, respectively. Whatever the actual degree of clustering, the pattern of strong team-level and weak individual-level associations therefore holds.

Supplementary Check of the Factor Structure

The ordering of the five measurement models replicated in this independent sample.

The one-factor model (χ²(77) = 314.67, CFI = .839, RMSEA = .183), the two-factor model (χ²(76) = 241.77, CFI = .888, RMSEA = .154), and the three-factor model in which unity and joint progress were merged (χ²(74) = 171.20, CFI = .934, RMSEA = .119) all fit significantly worse than the model with four correlated factors (χ²(71) = 158.81, CFI = .940, TLI = .924, SRMR = .040, RMSEA = .116), with Δχ²(6) = 155.86, p < .001, Δχ²(5) = 82.96, p < .001, and Δχ²(3) = 12.39, p = .006, respectively. As in Study 1, adding a second-order team flow factor did not reduce fit (Δχ²(2) = 2.37, p = .305), and the second-order four-factor model (χ²(73) = 161.19, p < .001, CFI = .940, TLI = .925, SRMR = .041, RMSEA = .115, 90% CI [.091, .139]) again had the lowest AIC and BIC of the five models. Standardized loadings ranged from .69 to .96 for the items and from .88 to .96 for the facets on the second-order factor, and the latent correlations among facets ranged from .82 to .93. Unlike in Study 1, merging unity and joint progress significantly reduced fit, which lends some support to the distinction between these two facets (latent r = .93). Absolute fit was good according to SRMR but fell short of conventional cut-offs for CFI, TLI, and RMSEA, as can be expected when a model with 32 free parameters is estimated on 92 participants (Herzog & Boomsma, 2009). These results should therefore be read as a replication of the relative ordering of the models, which supports the use of four facet scores in the present study, rather than as a cross-validation of the measurement model.

Discussion

Team flow is a collective, situational state that results from interactions among team members and is distinct from individual assessments of one's own performance or contribution (van den Hout et al., 2018). A valid measure of team flow should therefore be closely related to group-level outcomes and much less to individual self-assessment. Building on this distinction, Study 2 addressed the main limitation of Study 1 by examining whether scores on the French TFM correlated with perceptions of the team's overall performance and with satisfaction with that performance (convergent validity), and whether these associations were substantially stronger than the association with perceived individual performance (discriminant validity).

The results provide clear support for these expectations. Team flow scores were strongly and positively associated with perceived team performance and with satisfaction with team performance, and these associations were significantly stronger than those observed with the corresponding individual-level perceptions. These associations are consistent with theoretical predictions regarding a group state that is expected to relate to collective outcomes while remaining conceptually distinct from them (Salanova et al., 2014; van den Hout et al., 2018), and with previous work showing that collective flow relates to positive team outcomes such as collective efficacy and team performance (Aubé et al., 2014; Salanova et al., 2014).

All four facets contributed to this pattern. The associations of sense of joint progress and mutual trust with satisfaction with team performance were descriptively the strongest, which is in line with the importance of shared momentum and trust in positive collective experiences (Salanova et al., 2014; van den Hout et al., 2018), although the strong overlap among facets calls for caution when interpreting such differences. These findings offer the first evidence linking the French TFM to group-level evaluations within collaborative tasks, and they provide the convergent validity evidence that Study 1 lacked.

Team flow was also modestly related to perceived individual performance, but this association was significantly weaker than the association with perceived team performance, for the total score as for each facet. A small overlap of this kind is consistent with the multilevel nature of team flow. Members of a team that functions well may also evaluate their own performance more favorably, without the two constructs being confounded (Chan, 1998).

In line with this interpretation, the overlap disappeared once perceived team performance was controlled, which suggests that it reflects the spillover of a favorable evaluation of the team onto the evaluation of oneself. More telling is the pattern observed for the self-referenced perceptions. Team flow was negatively related to the perception of having contributed more than the other members and to the perception that one's performance depended on oneself, and positively related to the perception that it depended on the team.

These associations are theoretically meaningful. A strong sense of unity and trust may be accompanied by the feeling that one's own work serves the collective, which reduces the salience of comparing one's contribution with that of the others and shifts the perceived locus of performance from the self to the team. This pattern corresponds to a fluid team dynamic in which individual comparison becomes less relevant, consistent with the diminished self-focus and the heightened interdependence that characterize team flow (Csikszentmihalyi & Nakamura, 2018; van den Hout et al., 2018).

General Discussion

This research aimed to validate a French version of the characteristics subscale of the Team Flow Monitor (van den Hout et al., 2019) in order to provide a psychometrically sound tool for assessing the subjective experience of team flow in Francophone contexts. Across two studies, we investigated the factor structure, reliability, and construct validity of the scale in French-speaking samples composed of university students engaged in collaborative tasks.

In line with the original instrument (van den Hout et al., 2019), the comparison of confirmatory models identified four facets (sense of unity, sense of joint progress, mutual trust, and holistic focus) loading on a second-order team flow factor as the best-fitting structure, although the facets were strongly correlated and unity and joint progress were hardly distinguishable. In Study 1, the scale showed excellent reliability, correlated moderately with work engagement and self-rated task performance, and was clearly distinct from grit. Study 2 then examined the French TFM in student teams performing collaborative coursework. Team flow scores, both overall and for each of the four facets, were strongly and positively correlated with perceived team performance and satisfaction with team performance, which provides the convergent validity evidence with group-level outcomes that a measure of a collective state requires. Study 2 also reinforced the discriminant validity of the scale by showing that team flow was far less related to perceived individual performance than to perceived team performance, suggesting that the scale captures members' perception of a collective experience, distinct from individual self-assessment, and a sensitivity analysis showed that this pattern could not be explained by the non-independence of observations.

These results contribute to the literature on team flow in several ways. First, the French adaptation of the TFM extends the cross-cultural scope of its measurement and contributes to the study of collective flow beyond its original Dutch and English contexts (Gana et al., 2021; Vallerand, 1989). To our knowledge, this is the first validated French adaptation of the TFM, and it addresses a gap for Francophone researchers studying team dynamics in professional and educational settings.

Second, although the original four-facet structure was retained, the strong overlap among facets raises theoretical questions regarding the cross-cultural stability of the team flow construct. Rather than reflecting a substantive cross-cultural variation in how team flow is subjectively experienced, this overlap may be better explained by psychometric and theoretical mechanisms of construct emergence. The four facets shared a large amount of variance in our data (latent correlations from .81 to .96), and unity and joint progress could hardly be distinguished. This is consistent with multilevel models of emergence, in which lower-level characteristics converge and lose variability through shared experience and interaction (Kozlowski & Klein, 2000), and in which stronger interdependence within a group intensifies the collective experience (Walker, 2010). When team dynamics are tightly coupled, facets such as joint progress may merge into a broader sense of unity. Cultural or linguistic factors remain plausible alternative explanations, and the strong overlap among facets could also reflect collinearity specific to this sample or to the student teams studied here.

Establishing the source of this overlap will require tests of measurement invariance across languages and team types, and replication in other samples, including professional teams, in which more differentiated roles may make the original facets more distinguishable (Gana et al., 2021).

Third, the strong link established in Study 2 between French TFM scores and positive group-level perceptions supports the conceptualization of team flow as a beneficial state for team functioning (Sawyer, 2006 (Sawyer, , 2007;; van den Hout et al., 2018). The clear asymmetry between the associations of team flow with team-level and with individual-level evaluations, together with its negative associations with self-referenced perceptions of contribution and performance, further clarifies the collective nature of the construct measured by the TFM, in line with the shared absorption and diminished self-focus inherent in flow theory (Csikszentmihalyi & Nakamura, 2018).

Practical Implications

This research provides the Francophone scientific and practitioner communities with a validated tool to measure the characteristics of team flow. Research on group dynamics and team flow has been conducted in organizational, performance, and educational settings (Csikszentmihalyi, 1990; Heutte et al., 2021; Sawyer, 2007), which highlights the relevance of these concepts beyond the workplace. Collaborative learning and group projects are increasingly prevalent in higher education, and group dynamics have long been recognized as a determinant of academic success (Johnson & Johnson, 1989; Slavin, 1983).

Previous work in Francophone contexts has captured individual optimal experience with instruments such as the EduFlow scale (Heutte et al., 2021), but no validated instrument was available to measure the shared psychological state of interacting groups. The originality of the French TFM lies in its capacity to shift the evaluative lens from individual psychological states to the collective experience of the team. Concretely, the instrument offers educators a metric of collective engagement that can be used to develop and evaluate pedagogical approaches such as problem-based learning or highly interdependent collaborative projects, rather than relying solely on distal outcomes such as final grades. In higher education and organizational settings alike, the French TFM may also serve as a diagnostic tool for monitoring team functioning over time. Its total score provides a global indicator of team flow, and its facet scores, interpreted with caution given their strong intercorrelations, may help researchers and practitioners examine whether difficulties in a team relate more to a lack of mutual trust or to a weak sense of unity, and thereby orient interventions accordingly. These applications remain to be evaluated empirically, and the cross-sectional design of the present studies does not allow claims about the effects of such interventions.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the results. First, both studies relied on university student samples that were predominantly young and female and that worked on course-related collaborative tasks. This limits the generalizability of the findings, particularly the factor structure, to professional work teams and to other demographic groups. The gender imbalance also means that the absence of a gender effect on the TFM dimensions in Study 1 should be read as preliminary rather than as evidence of measurement equivalence across gender, which future studies with more balanced samples should test formally. Second, the exploratory and confirmatory factor analyses of Study 1 were conducted on the same sample, and Study 2 was too small to provide more than a supplementary check, in which the ordering of the models replicated but absolute fit remained below conventional standards. The retained structure therefore awaits cross-validation in an independent sample, ideally with tests of measurement invariance against the original Dutch and English versions. Third, the observations in Study 2 were not independent, as participants were nested within teams, whereas correlations were computed at the individual level.

Because team membership could not be identified from the information collected, neither the number of teams nor within-team agreement could be established. The sensitivity analysis indicates that the main convergent findings cannot be explained by non-independence, but the aggregation properties of the French TFM, such as ICC(1) and within-team agreement indices, remain to be documented. The present findings should therefore be read as evidence about members' perceptions of team flow rather than about team-level scores. Future work should record team membership explicitly, model the team structure with multilevel approaches, and examine within-team agreement to determine whether team flow scores can be aggregated at the team level. Fourth, all measures were self-reported and collected at a single time point, so that common method variance may have inflated some associations (Podsakoff et al., 2003), and several validity criteria in Study 2 were single-item measures whose reliability could not be estimated. Finally, the design of both studies was correlational, and the associations between team flow and group-level outcomes cannot be interpreted causally. Future research should validate the French TFM in diverse organizational settings and across different types of teams and tasks in the Francophone world, examine its temporal stability, and test its sensitivity to interventions designed to foster team flow.

This research adapted and validated a French version of the characteristics subscale of the Team Flow Monitor. The original structure of four facets loading on a second-order team flow factor provided the best fit, and the scale demonstrated excellent reliability and strong evidence of construct validity, notably through its strong association with perceived team performance and satisfaction, its much weaker association with perceived individual performance, and its distinction from grit. This work thus provides a psychometrically sound instrument that opens promising avenues for researchers and practitioners in French-speaking contexts, while further validation in professional settings remains an important next step.
