How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Abstract

This study examined whether a 20-minute, sustained time-on-task protocol to induce cognitive fatigue affects x-ray baggage screening performance and perceived cognitive demand. Thirty-six untrained participants (15 female) completed two laboratory sessions involving watching a nature documentary (control) or an incongruent, cuedriven Stroop task (experimental). Participants rated cognitive fatigue, motivation, and boredom before and after each manipulation, then completed a 96-image, twodimensional, x-ray screening task. Performance was assessed using total correct responses and signal-detection metrics (e.g., 𝑑′ ), and response times. Post-task, individuals rated the perceived demands of the task through an adapted task load index.

Cognitive fatigue increased from pre-post experimental manipulation and was higher after the experimental than control condition (𝑝 = .004) . Motivation did not differ between conditions, although boredom was higher post-manipulation in the experimental versus control condition (𝑝 < .001) . Participants' x-ray screening performance was similar between conditions (𝑝 ′ 𝑠 = .076 -.684). Perceived cognitive demand was higher after the experimental condition compared to the control (means 65.5 versus 59.1 a.u.). Our targeted investigation of time-on-task effects on x-ray screening performance indicate novices can maintain their threat detection accuracy when cognitively fatigued by a prolonged time-on-task manipulation. However, time-ontask-related fatigue is linked to increased cognitive resource demands to sustain the same level of x-ray screening performance. To continue the momentum of this emerging field, we advocate for a combination of laboratory assessments, analyses of real-world data, and quasi-experimental studies to inform the aviation security sector of time-on-Draft Version #1 -24/11/2026, submitted for peer review 3 task and related fatigue effects on x-ray screening performance and how these findings 47 can shape policy and training practices.

Introduction

The screening of passenger's baggage items in the aviation sector is a critical security measure to prevent dangerous items making their way onto flights (Meuter and Lacherez 2016). Despite many recent technological innovations (Cordova 2022), the crucial decision about whether a bag is marked as safe or requires further inspection remains with the human screener (Kruger and Suchan 2015). As such, studies have emphasised the importance of understanding how individual capabilities impact job performance, especially work environments with a direct impact on public safety (Warnes 2024). One factor is the effect of cognitive fatigue, a natural phenomenon which is characterised by a depletion of resource(s) induced by prolonged engagement in a task or an inability to access these resources (Aaronson et al. 1999; Mangin and Pageaux 2025).

Airport security staff rotate across several roles within the airport cabin baggage security checkpoint. These roles involve assisting with tray loading, body searches, and most notably, screening of 2D and 3D imagery of the cabin baggage items. Over the course of a single 8-12-hour shift, security officers complete several rotations of screening x-ray imagery of baggage. Many national/regional guidelines limit staff to 20 minutes of x-ray baggage screening (e.g., European Union Commission 2015) due to the arduous cognitive demands related to continuous visual inspection of low-prevalence threats amongst ever-changing imagery and a constant stream of bags (Arcurio et al. 2018).

During prolonged task engagement, sustained engagement of cognitive resources (effort) accrues cognitive fatigue (Pessiglione et al. 2025) which negatively impacts visual search performance (Godwin et al. 2025) if a compensatory increase in resources is not invested (Mangin and Pageaux 2026). Moreover, prolonged time-on-task can stimulate other cognitive changes such as decreases in task motivation (Kok 2022), boredom (Bieleke et al. 2021), and stress (Thomas et al. 2014), all of which have been associated with poorer cognitive performance (e.g., Demirkol and Nalla 2018). To counteract the negative effects of prolonged, time-on-task, interventions (e.g., rest breaks) which help dissipate cognitive fatigue and/or increase motivation have consistently restored visual search performance (Gilsoul et al. 2021; Waldfogle et al. 2021).

Previous studies focusing on x-ray screening performance changes over prolonged timeon-task have been observational or retrospectively analysed real-world data (e.g., Buser et al. 2023; Grady et al. 2022). Expectedly, most studies indicate that with prolonged time-on-task, x-ray screening performance measures like hit rates (correct identification of threats) decrease (Buser et al. 2023; Godwin et al. 2025; Grady et al. 2022). Moreover, when task demands are higher (e.g., higher number of trials per minute) and therefore require more cognitive effort, x-ray screening performance declines faster with time-ontask. However, declines in visual search performance with time-on-task are less likely amongst alert and conscientious individuals (Grady et al. 2022) suggesting other cognitive states (e.g., motivation) can temper fatigue-related effects. Furthermore, if an individual is given autonomy to control their time-on-task, individuals can maintain their ability to detect threats for longer (Buser et al. 2020; Chavaillaz et al. 2019b).

Together, these results indicate that time-on-task typically reduces x-ray screening performance due to the cognitive fatigue and motivational changes incurred with sustained task engagement (Mangin and Pageaux 2025). However, current studies have not be able to fully control the task demands such as the trial rates (i.e., number of trials per minute). Moreover, these studies have speculated on a cognitive fatigue mechanism which affects x-ray screening performance but no study has measured cognitive fatigue, motivation, or other cognitive state changes with prolonged time-on-task. While the aviation security sector may have an idea of the effects of time-on-task on x-ray screening performance to inform policy like mandated shift times, there is likely more to be understood. Specifically, if time-on-task does cause fatigue and this impacts x-ray screening performance, how severe is its impact, how do individuals adapt to fatigue, and what can training and policy do to minimise any negative effects.

Consequently, we conducted a targeted experimental study which sought to address this gap. Specifically, we simulated prolonged time-on-task using a validated task (incongruent, cue-driven Stroop task) that is widely used in experimental psychology to induce cognitive fatigue (e.g., Mangin et al. 2021). Moreover, we controlled the task duration to replicate a typical 20-minute shift of screening x-ray baggage, and we matched the trial rate (12 trials.min -1 ) to previous study findings where time-on-task effects are expected to be most prevalent (Buser et al. 2023). Comparatively, our control condition involved prolonged task engagement but without a substantial resource demand (watching a nature documentary) to incur additional cognitive fatigue (Mangin et al. 2021). Therefore, this study aims to assess the effects of time-on-task through a controlled cognitive fatigue protocol on x-ray screening performance. Our primary research hypothesis is that the experimental (fatiguing) condition will cause worse x-ray baggage screening performance (threat detection accuracy) than a control condition.

Our second hypothesis is that the experimental condition will cause the x-ray baggage screening task to be perceived as more cognitively demanding than the control.

Methods

This study was pre-registered prior to any data collection at https://osf.io/d6fmk/. All data and supplementary materials relating to the project can be found at https://osf.io/4y3hr/overview.

Participants

An a-priori calculation (Supplementary Material 1) performed in G*Power software (version 3.1, G*Power, Germany) with the "as in SPSS" option selected determined a required sample of 36 participants for a one-tailed, matched pairs sample comparison with a moderate effect size (𝑑𝑧 = 0.5), alpha level of 0.05, and a moderate-high power (𝛽= 0.90). Thirty-six participants were recruited for this study (15 female, mean ± SD age: 28.4 ± 5.8 years, age range: 18-46 years). Participants were untrained with no x-ray screening experience. Evidence suggests that while novices and experts may differ in how they visually inspect x-ray imagery (Swann et al. 2024), similar effects between these populations have existed for other targeted interventions when presented with grayscale x-ray imagery of baggage containing simple threats (e.g., firearms, sharps) and nonthreats (e.g., Chavaillaz et al. 2019a). All participants were free from any psychiatric and/or neurological diseases, had normal or corrected-to-normal vision, and regular colour vision. Participants abstained from food (2 hours), caffeine (4 hours), vigorous exercise (24 hours), and alcohol (24 hours) before each visit. The study was approved by the institutional ethics committee (Ref ID: 12200416). Data collection took place between May-October 2026. Participants provided written informed consent in person at the first visit after receiving an information sheet at least 48 hours prior. Participants did not receive any remuneration for their participation and retained the right to withdraw Draft Version #1 -24/11/2026, submitted for peer review 8 from the study up until the end of November 2026 without giving reason. Design

This study employed a repeated measures, pseudorandomised, and counterbalanced design. Participants attended two 45-minute visits in the same laboratory with the similar ambient conditions. Visits were separated by a minimum of 24 hours and scheduled within ±1 hour of each other to control for circadian effects confounds on cognitive fatigue and x-ray screening performance. Sessions were separated by a minimum of 24 hours. The control (no added cognitive fatigue) and experimental (cognitively fatiguing protocol) conditions and x-ray screening tasks were pseudorandomised and counterbalanced across all participants (Supplementary Material 2).

Protocol

Participants were first given an overview of the protocols and then completed a prescreening form to confirm abstinence from food, caffeine, vigorous exercise, and alcohol before each session, and a consent form. Participants completed a three single-item visual analogue scales (VAS) relating to task motivation, boredom, and cognitive fatigue (Supplementary Materials 3) using an online survey software (Qualtrics, USA) before and after the control or experimental manipulation (Figure 1a). Following the manipulation, participants then completed a computerised two-dimensional x-ray screening task (see X-Ray screening task). Immediately after this task, participants completed an adapted six-item visual-search task load index (Supplementary Material 4) via the online survey platform.

Control Manipulation

Participants watched a 20-minute segment of the "From Deserts to Grasslands" nature documentary (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=XmtXC_n6X6Q) (Figure 1b). Such documentaries have maintained motivation, avoided boredom (mean 10 a.u. increase), and avoided cognitive fatigue in previous studies (e.g., Mangin et al. 2021). Participants were seated 70 cm from a high-definition 55 x 35 cm monitor with headphones in a darkened room whilst watching the documentary. The researcher was seated out of view and did not interact with the participant during this time.

Experimental Manipulation

Incongruent Stroop tasks have been shown as effective measures to induce cognitive fatigue (Mangin et al. 2021) with additional cue measures further preventing any cheating or disengagement from the task. Participants completed a 20-minute incongruent, cue-Draft Version #1 -24/11/2026, submitted for peer review 10 driven Stroop test using the open-source Stroop App (Beghin et al. 2026). Participants observed a '+' icon for 500 ms, followed by a circle or square cue surrounding the '+' icon for 1000 ms. This icon was quickly followed by the word 'RED', 'BLUE', 'GREEN', or 'YELLOW' which could be written in a red, blue, green, or yellow colour ink and was shown for a maximum of 3500ms. The protocol was programmed so that 75% of the trials were incongruent meaning the ink colour was different to the word written on screen.

Therefore 25% of the trials were congruent where the ink colour and word were the same.

If participants were cued with a circle shape, they were required to press the key corresponding to the ink colour. If participants were cued with a square shape, they were required to press the key corresponding to the word. Immediately, after the key response, the next trial began but adapted the duration of the fixation cross to ensure each trial lasted precisely 5000ms (12 trials.min -1 ). An overview of the cue-driven incongruent Stroop task is shown in Figure 1c. In total, participants completed 240 trials lasting 20 minutes. A 20-minute protocol was chosen to reflect mandated shift times within aviation security, and thus, simulate time-on-task effects in a controlled experiment.

Participants were instructed "to respond as accurately and as quickly as they could". No feedback was given during or after the manipulation by the researcher. The number of correct responses and response times (ms) for trials with correct responses were extracted.

X-Ray Screening Task

A two-dimensional x-ray screening task was developed in version 2.4. of PsychoPy (Peirce et al. 2022). Using a sample of pre-scanned baggage items from Smiths Detection's (https://www.smithsdetection.com) image library, a total of 198 images were used (provided on https://osf.io/4y3hr/overview). Six were used as a practice trial with three images containing a firearm, sharp, or an explosive, and the other three containing no threatening items. Of the remaining 192 items, there was an equal share (25%) containing either a firearm, sharp, explosive, or no threat. Firearms comprised either an assembled firearm, or an individual component such as a magazine, barrel, or ammunition. Sharps comprised either a butter knife, bread knife, penknife, switchblade, cleaver, secateurs, or large scissors (nail scissors did not count as a threat). Explosives comprised either grenades or components of improvised explosives such as organic materials with wiring connecting a detonator/timer, switch, and battery. If a threat was present, only one threat item was present in the image. All images were in grayscale and presented in a mixture of overhead and side-on orientations.

The image bank was split into two batches of 96 images, and each batch labelled as either 'XRAY1' or 'XRAY2'. The XRAY1 and XRAY2 tests were then pseudorandomised and counterbalanced across the study cohort with one test (XRAY1 or XRAY2) completed post-manipulation per visit (Supplementary Material 2). After the researcher described the task, participants completed a six-trial familiarisation (one image of each threat type and three images of non-threats). The protocol mirrored the main protocol in which a '+' icon was shown for 1000 ms, followed by a two-dimensional image. Participants had 20 seconds to respond by pressing a key with a firearm, a sharp, an explosive, or a bag with a tick (no threat) (Figure 1d). As soon as they pressed the key, the next trial began until all six familiarisations were complete. When familiarised, participants could ask any questions or request more practice (no participants requested more practice). Once the familiarisation was finished, participants completed the main 96 trial x-ray screening task.

Measures

Task Motivation

Participants were asked to rate "what is your current motivation to carry out the x-ray screening task?" on a 0-100 VAS with 0 denoting "no motivation", 50 denoting "moderate motivation", and 100 denoting "maximal motivation".

Boredom

Participants were asked to rate "what is your current level of boredom?" on a 0-100 VAS with 0 denoting "no boredom", 50 denoting "moderate boredom", and 100 denoting "maximal boredom".

Cognitive Fatigue

Participants were asked to rate "what is your current level of cognitive fatigue?" on a 0-100 VAS with 0 denoting "no fatigue at all", 50 denoting "moderate fatigue", and 100 denoting "maximal fatigue".

X-Ray Screening Performance

The number of correct responses (i.e., pressing the correct key), and response time (ms) were obtained for every trial. The total number of correct responses were calculated for each participant. Response times for incorrect responses were removed, and a mean value was calculated for response times during correct responses for each participant.

Additional signal detection metrics which establish how well a screener distinguishes threats from non-threats were calculated. The hits (correct threats detected), misses (missed threats), false alarms (incorrect non-threats flagged), correct rejections (correct non-threat identified), were recorded for each participant. A hit rate (equation 1a) and Draft Version #1 -24/11/2026, submitted for peer review 13 false alarm rate (equation 1b) were calculated. Hit and false alarm rates were then converted into 𝑧-scores using cumulative probability constants (equation 1c). Finally, the 𝑑' value was calculated (equation 1d). Calculated 𝑑' values were interpreted as0 = no ability to distinguish threat from no-threat, 0.8-1.5 = weak discrimination, 1.5-2.49 = good discrimination, and ≥2.5 = excellent discrimination.

A criterion value (𝑐) was also calculated to interpret each participant's liberal (i.e. flagging more threats) or conservative (i.e., flagging more non-threats) decision-making process. Criterion values (𝑐) was calculated using steps a-c of equation 1 but with a different last step (equation 2a). Criterion values were interpreted as <0 "liberal", i.e., more non-threat responses to items that may have had threats, >0 "conservative, i.e., more threat responses to items that may have had no threats, or0 = neutral decision.

Adapted Visual Search Task Load Index Items were adapted from an existing task load index measure (Wilson et al. 2011).

Participants were asked to rate their perceived cognitive demand ("how cognitively demanding was the task?"), physical demand ("how physically demanding was the task"), temporal demand ("how hurried or rushed was the pace of the task?"), task complexity ("how difficult was the task to complete?"), distractions ("how distracting was the environment you completed the task in?"), and performance evaluation ("how successful do you think you were at accomplishing what you were asked to do?").

Participants moved a dial on a 0-100 VAS with 0 denoting "not at all", 50 denoting "moderate", and 100 denoting "extreme(ly)".

Data Analysis

Data Processing

Self-report VAS, cue-driven incongruent Stroop test, and x-ray screening performance data were extracted into Excel (Microsoft, USA). In a column denoting the condition, a second member of the research team randomly labelled the conditions with a '1' or '2' to blind the first author from the conditions during subsequent analysis.

For the selfreport VAS responses, a change (∆) in task motivation, boredom, and cognitive fatigue pre-to post-manipulation was also calculated for each participant. Individual and group mean responses were extracted from the six items of the task load index. An additional linear mixed model regression was performed in Jamovi (version 2.3.28.0, Australia). Model one included x-ray screening performance measures of total correct responses and 𝑑' as the dependent variable and the condition as a factor. In model two, the changes in cognitive fatigue, motivation, boredom ratings from pre-to postmanipulation were added as a covariate to judge the degree at which these perceptions impacted x-ray screening performance. Another version of model two included cognitive demand as the covariate. Finally, a third model looked at the interaction between condition and psychological variables from model two to determine if changes in a psychological parameter affected x-ray screening performance differently between conditions. Participants were added a random factor for all models.

Discussion

The aim of the present study was to assess the effects of time-on-task through a controlled cognitive fatigue protocol on x-ray screening performance. Contrary to our first hypothesis, x-ray screening performance was similar between control and experimental conditions. Consistent with our second hypothesis, participants reported higher cognitive demand to complete the x-ray screening task after the experimentally fatiguing manipulation versus the non-fatiguing control. Together, these results suggest that 20 minutes of sustained time-on-task does not impact x-ray screening performance in a novice sample but it does make sustaining a given level of performance more cognitively demanding.

Our confirmatory analyses show that after the experimental manipulations, participants reported an increase in cognitive fatigue compared to before the manipulation. They also reported higher cognitive fatigue in the experimental versus the control condition after the manipulation. Meanwhile, reported task motivation was similar between conditions post-manipulation. These results confirm the main purpose of our experimental manipulation to increase cognitive fatigue with prolonged time-on-task was successful, and participants were equally motivated towards the x-ray screening task after each condition's manipulation.

Our study is one of the first investigations to assess whether cognitive fatigue induced through a controlled time-on-task experimental manipulation affected x-ray screening performance in a novice sample. We observed no clear decrement in x-ray screening performance due to prolonged time-on-task-induced fatigue. However, participants reported that when cognitively fatigued, the task required was more cognitively demanding to sustain a similar level of x-ray screening performance to the control. These findings are broadly consistent with other large-scale studies which index trivial-small effects when investigating pre-test cognitive fatigue manipulations (e.g., Hagger et al.

2016).

The motivational intensity theory (Brehm and Self 1989) posits that task performance is contingent on individuals applying resources (effort) which match the demands of the task (Richter et al. 2016). With increasing time-on-task, cognitive fatigue accrues, thus, increasing task demands (Pessiglione et al. 2025) and reducing operational performance across numerous contexts involving visual search (Hanna et al. 2018; Stec et al. 2018).

Therefore, it is logical that participants would need to invest more resources to sustain performance when cognitively fatigued compared to conditions with less cognitive fatigue (Mangin and Pageaux 2025). This could then be reflected in the perceived cognitive demand of the task (Mangin and Pageaux 2026). Yet it was interesting that our exploratory regression found a negative relationship between perceived cognitive demand and x-ray screening performance measures. This potentially indicates the timeon-task fatigue manipulation in this study caused a growing inability to access cognitive resources to sustain task performance amongst a novice sample (Aaronson et al. 1999) rather than a "depletion" of resources (Mangin and Pageaux 2026).

However, the motivational intensity theory does not fully account that the novice population of this study, on average, incorrectly answered 48 (~50%) of the trials, most of which were missed threats. Alternatively, guided search theory (Wolfe 2014) proposes that attention is directed across a visual scene according to a set of internal representations/templates of target features learnt through experience and training (Wolfe 2021; Wolfe et al. 2010). Fatigue could plausibly lower the quitting threshold (as measured by the response time of the non-threats correctly identified), predicting faster target-absent responses and more misses (Wolfe 2020 (Wolfe , 2021)), but data showed no difference in response times between conditions, which is weak evidence against this shift. Participants' limited experience and training with x-ray screening could imply inaccurate/non-existent templates of certain threats (Swann et al. 2020; Wolfe 2021).

While increased perceived cognitive demand and a potentially higher cognitive effort investment may have helped preserve x-ray screening performance relative to the control condition (Richter et al. 2016), increased effort in the face of increased task demands due to time-on-task effects like fatigue does not necessarily compensate for the underlying limitations a novice sample has to detect threats (Swann et al. 2020).

Next, this study has illustrated that after a sustained 20-minute protocol which matches a typical shift length in aviation security, performance on a subsequent x-ray task was unaffected. These findings indicate individuals can potentially work longer than 20minutes without notable declines in threat detection accuracy which jeopardise passenger safety (Buser et al. 2020 (Buser et al. , 2023;; Chavaillaz et al. 2019b). Furthermore, if performance can be sustained with a small increase in perceived cognitive demand, and studies illustrate that effort demands for a given task are reduced through training (Audriffen et al. 2022; Ghafari Goushe et al. 2025), this provides a novel approach for training providers of x-ray baggage screeners. Specifically, future training could monitor effort demands for two reasons. First, trainers could use effort reports to understand how training reduces cognitive resources demands for essential components of an x-ray baggage screening task that allow individuals to maintain threat detection accuracy for longer. Second, trainee's reports of effort could also be used as an indicator of when the Draft Version #1 -24/11/2026, submitted for peer review 25 task is becoming too difficult (Shenhav et al. 2017) and the likelihood of missed threats might be increasing. Individuals who are fatigued are expected to report higher cognitive demand and perceived effort investment (Mangin and Pageaux 2025, 2026). All of which are collectively linked to a decreases motivation and a tendency to avoid further effort investment (Inzlicht et al. 2018; Müller and Apps 2019). Therefore, cognitive demand and effort monitoring could be a useful safeguard in training settings to understand when the task is becoming too demanding to sustain performance and learning.

Limitations and Future Research Directions

As part of our method, we captured ratings of motivation and boredom which represent distinct concepts to cognitive fatigue (Mangin and Pageaux 2025). We acknowledged in our results that while motivation was similar between conditions post-manipulation, the experimental manipulation caused higher boredom than the control. This could have impacted whether differences in x-ray screening performance between conditions were fully attributable to cognitive fatigue effects we targeted with our prolonged time-on-task manipulation or whether effects were potentially confounded by boredom (Horowitz et al. 2003). However, our exploratory linear regression analyses help diffuse this notion as changes in boredom reports did not impact x-ray screening performance measures.

Nevertheless, we have highlighted some of the previous research which posit other personal attributes and human factors which have been shown to impact x-ray screening performance in both laboratory (e.g., Godwin et al. 2025) and applied settings (e.g., Grady et al. 2022). Therefore, we advocate for future research to build upon this study's findings through a-priori assessment of time-on-task effects on x-ray screening performance alongside cognitive fatigue and additional personal/human factors measures to understand which factors moderate these effects.

Next, we also acknowledge that our study involved a volunteer sample of novices without training or certification in x-ray baggage screening in a relatively artificial laboratory setting. We have discussed the impacts this may have had on the visual search task we used (Wolfe 2014 (Wolfe , 2020 (Wolfe , 2021) ) and that effort demands could change with training (Audriffen et al. 2022). Moreover, while laboratories provide a controlled setting to assess a basic scientific question, the consequences of incorrect responses do not translate to the consequences of missed threats in real-world settings (Meuter and Lacherez 2016; Swann et al. 2020). That is, screeners that potentially miss a threat could be endangering many lives (Klenka 2019). In contexts with more responsibility, the effects of a similar experimental manipulation would likely alter screeners' motivation, boredom, and cognitive fatigue responses and their moderating effects on task performance (Massar et al. 2010; Pessiglione et al. 2025). Nevertheless, we observed a similar trend to "real-world" data (e.g., Buser et al. 2023), whereby the identification of threats is similar at a standard throughput rate after a fatiguing condition (i.e., late phases of time-on-task or after a cognitive fatigue manipulation). Only through a combination of laboratory assessments, retrospective analyses of real-world data, and quasi-experimental studies will research provide the aviation security sector with a comprehensive understanding of time-on-task and related fatigue effects on x-ray screening performance (Godwin et al. 2025). We believe this study is an important contribution to this area by providing a direct assessment of fatigue effects on x-ray screening performance with controlled experimental parameters (e.g., time-on-task and trial rates) which were lacking in previous studies which analysed real-world data Draft Version #1 -24/11/2026, submitted for peer review 27 retrospectively (e.g., Buser et al. 2023; Grady et al. 2022). An interesting next step would be to apply a similar experimental design to a cohort of trained and certified screeners to compare whether time-on-task and cognitive fatigue related effects are consistent between sub-populations. Additional experimental manipulations of time-on-task and trial rate parameters would also be interesting. Alongside these future research direction, further analyses of real-world data with a focus on time-on-task and fatigue parameters will help to consolidate our knowledge of these effects and provide informed arguments about policy relating to x-ray baggage screeners shift lengths, shift scheduling, and potential safeguard measures to avoid errors.

Lastly, x-ray baggage screening is a visual search and decision task. We have indicated that without training, novice individuals may have visually inspected the correct part of the image but lack the schema to appropriately decide what they are viewing is a threat/non-threat (Wolfe 2014; Swann et al. 2020 Swann et al. , 2024)). Conclusion

Our study indicates that x-ray screening performance was unaffected by a 20-minute experimental manipulation of cognitive fatigue through sustained time-on-task in a novice sample. While performance was not significantly affected, it appears to increase the perceived cognitive demand to maintain the same performance level. Accordingly, we infer that individuals could maintain their threat detection accuracy over a 20-minute shifts, this may only be possible if the individual has the requisite skills (e.g., developed templates of threats) and cognitive resources to compensate for the increased demands that time-on-task imposes. Nevertheless, we also acknowledge that more direct comparisons of varied time-on-task manipulations (e.g., duration, trial rates) with targeted fatigue and effort measures would be a critical step to inform training, policy, and practice further. We believe our study is one that helps contribute to this emerging field in which a combination of laboratory assessments, retrospective analyses of realworld data, and quasi-experimental studies will better inform the aviation security sector of time-on-task and related fatigue effects on x-ray screening performance.
