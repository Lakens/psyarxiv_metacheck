Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. Some differences come from the ground truth: it lacks p-values with a correction name (“p Bonf = .061”), and counts partial eta squared written with its subscript after it (“η 2 p = .05”), “p = .05”, and some table notes and figure legends (“† p < .10, *p < .05”), which this module leaves out, as non-significant p-values. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Full text

Abstract

Individuals with schizophrenia-spectrum disorders commonly exhibit debilitating negative symptoms, including avolition and anhedonia, which represent a significant barrier to engagement in goal-directed behaviors, such as physical exercise. Although exercise can significantly alleviate these symptoms, a clinical paradox arises as the symptoms themselves impede regular physical activity. This narrative review addresses this paradox by proposing an integrated neurocognitive framework centered on the proximal exercise experience, specifically exercise-related pleasure and perceived exertion. We argue that negative symptoms alter the cost-benefit analysis of exercise due to core deficits in anticipatory pleasure, effort valuation, and reinforcement learning. We propose that structured exercise acts as a corrective learning context capable of updating maladaptive internal models through prediction errors, the discrepancies between anticipated and actual experiences of effort and pleasure. We also highlight the importance of learning from both positive prediction errors (discovering exercise is less effortful or more pleasurable than expected) and carefully supported negative prediction errors, which can re-associate physical effort with mastery, competence, and control rather than threat. By focusing on these experiential and computational mechanisms, this framework provides a theoretical foundation for targeted clinical interventions designed to leverage real-time affect and post-session reflection to break the self-reinforcing cycle of inactivity and promote sustained symptom improvement.

1. Introduction

Schizophrenia is a complex and chronic mental disorder characterized by positive, negative, disorganized, and cognitive symptoms (Owen et al., 2016). Among these dimensions, negative symptoms are particularly disabling because they affect motivation, pleasure, emotional expression, social engagement, and goal-directed behavior (Chang et al., 2020; Fulford et al., 2018; Giuliani et al., 2021; Glenthøj et al., 2020). Experiential negative symptoms, such as avolition, anhedonia, and asociality, are especially relevant for daily functioning because they directly impair the capacity to initiate and sustain meaningful activities. Despite their clinical importance, negative symptoms remain difficult to treat and show limited response to available antipsychotic medications (Correll & Schooler, 2020; Li et al., 2016). This has led to growing interest in complementary interventions capable of targeting motivational, affective, and functional impairments in schizophrenia-spectrum disorders.

Against this background, physical exercise (i.e., planned, structured, and repetitive physical activity intended to improve or maintain physical fitness; Caspersen et al., 1985) has received increasing attention as a complementary intervention for schizophrenia-spectrum disorders. Reviews and meta-analyses suggest that exercise may improve several clinically relevant outcomes in schizophrenia-spectrum disorders, including psychiatric symptoms, cognition, physical health, functioning, and quality of life (Dauwan et al., 2016; Gallardo-Gómez et al., 2023; Girdler et al., 2019; Kim et al., 2023; Sabe et al., 2020). Of particular relevance to the present review, exercise has also been associated with reductions in negative symptoms (Sabe et al., 2020). This raises a clinically important paradox: the very symptoms that exercise may help alleviate may also make exercise difficult to initiate, sustain, and repeat. This paradox is especially important because individuals with schizophrenia exhibit a generally lower level of physical activity (i.e., any bodily movement produced by skeletal muscles that results in energy expenditure; Caspersen et al., 1985) than the general population (Ohi et al., 2019; Stubbs et al., 2016), and reduced physical activity appears particularly pronounced among those with more severe negative symptoms (Gorczynski et al., 2018).

Negative symptoms may therefore contribute to a self-reinforcing cycle in which reduced motivation, diminished reward anticipation, and altered effort valuation decrease exercise engagement, thereby limiting opportunities to experience the affective, cognitive, social, and physiological benefits of exercise.

Breaking this cycle requires moving beyond the question of whether exercise works, toward the question of how exercise is experienced. Physical exercise is not only a physiological stimulus, but also an affective and effortful experience. From the perspective of negative symptoms, two subjective dimensions appear especially important: exercise-related pleasure and perceived exertion.

Here, exercise-related pleasure refers specifically to the hedonic valence of the exercise experience, that is, the degree to which a given exercise session is anticipated, experienced, or recalled as pleasant or unpleasant. This use follows the exercise-affect literature, in which pleasure-displeasure is treated as a core affective response and is commonly assessed through the Feeling Scale (Ekkekakis, 2003; Ekkekakis et al., 2011 Ekkekakis et al., , 2020;; Hardy & Rejeski, 1989), rather than as synonymous with broader constructs such as enjoyment, intrinsic motivation, or affective attitudes (Kimiecik & Harris, 1996; Rhodes & Kates, 2015; Stevens et al., 2020; Teixeira et al., 2022). Perceived exertion refers to the subjective experience of how intense the effort or strain feels during physical activity; see also (Haile et al., 2015; Robertson & Noble, 1997) Halperin & Emanuel, 2020) for conceptual discussion regarding its definition. Although "effort" and "exertion" are sometimes used as synonyms, and their definitions vary across disciplines (Halperin & Vigotsky, 2024), we distinguish them in this review: "perceived exertion" denotes the subjective experience just described, whereas "effort" (or "effort cost") denotes the mobilization of physical and cognitive resources required by the activity, as valued in cost-benefit models of motivated behavior.

Crucially, pleasure and effort dimensions are closely linked in the broader exercise psychology literature, as formalized by the Dual-Mode Theory of exercise affect (Ekkekakis, 2003). This relationship is intensity-dependent: affective responses tend to remain positive and relatively homogeneous at lower intensities, become markedly more variable across individuals at moderate intensities (between the first and second metabolic thresholds, corresponding to an RPE of about 4 to 5 on the 10-point category-ratio scale; Bishop et al., 2025), and shift more consistently toward displeasure at higher intensities that exceed these thresholds. In the present review, "higher perceived exertion" therefore refers primarily to intensities approaching or exceeding the upper threshold, where increases in perceived exertion are most reliably associated with less pleasant affective responses.

The present narrative review develops the "breaking the cycle" argument in four steps.

First, we establish that physical exercise can reduce negative symptoms in schizophreniaspectrum disorders. Second, we examine evidence that negative symptoms interfere with physical activity and exercise engagement. Third, we focus on the proximal exercise experience by reviewing studies that have examined subjective pleasure and perceived exertion experienced during exercise in schizophrenia-spectrum disorders. Finally, we propose a learning-based framework, informed by the reward, effort, and prediction-error alterations summarized in Box 1, in which physical exercise may help rewire negative symptoms by updating maladaptive internal models related to effort and pleasure experiences.

According to the proposed framework, individuals with schizophrenia-spectrum disorders may anticipate exercise as effortful and hard control. However, when exercise is experienced as more manageable or more rewarding than expected, it may generate expectancy violations or prediction errors that support the updating of internal models.

Concurrently, the physiological adaptations induced by physical exertion drive a potent neurometabolic and neuroprotective cascade, that may contribute to restore frontostriatal network sensitivity to catalyze this computational re-learning process. Repeated exercise experiences that violate negative expectations may gradually change how effort is anticipated, experienced, remembered, and valued. The objective of this review is therefore to articulate an integrated neurocognitive framework linking negative symptoms, exercise engagement, perceived exertion, pleasure, and prediction-error-based learning, with the aim of generating testable hypotheses for future research and intervention development.

2. Effects of physical exercise on negative symptoms

At a broad level, the literature suggests that exercise can produce beneficial effects across several clinically relevant domains in individuals with schizophrenia. Studies assessing immediate responses to exercise bouts have shown that physical exercise can reduce state anxiety and psychological distress while increasing positive well-being in people with schizophrenia (Heggelund et al., 2014; Korman et al., 2026; Vancampfort et al., 2011).

Exercise also shows benefits on cognitive and functional outcomes. Meta-analytic evidence suggests that aerobic exercise may improve global cognition, attention, processing speed, and functional outcomes in people with schizophrenia-spectrum disorders (Girdler et al., 2019; Korman et al., 2023; Lak et al., 2024; Vila-Barrios et al., 2023). These effects are relevant because cognitive dysfunction and negative symptoms are closely intertwined in daily functioning, particularly for goal-directed behavior, social participation, and occupational engagement.

Regular moderate-to-vigorous aerobic exercise has also been associated with reductions in body weight, waist circumference, fasting blood glucose, triglycerides, and systolic blood pressure (Bredin et al., 2013; Schmitt et al., 2018; Tumiel et al., 2019; Vancampfort et al., 2019). These somatic benefits may also contribute to downregulating the chronic low-grade systemic inflammation frequently observed in schizophrenia (Furman et al., 2019; Khandaker et al., 2015). This is clinically relevant since inflammatory signaling is known to alter frontostriatal circuits, reducing ventral striatal responses to reward and lowering dopamine availability, which directly impairs motivation and motor activity (Felger & Treadway, 2017; Goldsmith & Rapaport, 2020). Systematic reviews of exercise interventions suggest that regular exercise could improve lipid metabolism and lower inflammation in this population (Gómez-Rubio & Trapero, 2019), although further studies are needed to confirm this pattern. In a recent experimental clinical trial conducted on patients with major depressive disorder (Miller et al., 2025), targeting inflammation led to a reduction in motivational deficits, reflected by an increased willingness to select high-effort options during effort-based decision-making tasks. This later finding highlights another promising avenue for future research in schizophrenia.

Of particular interest for the present paper is whether physical exercise can specifically reduce negative symptoms. Systematic reviews and meta-analyses reveal that exercise interventions have been associated with improvements in general psychopathology, with some evidence for reductions in positive symptoms, such as hallucinations and delusions, and more consistent evidence for reductions in negative symptoms, including motivational and expressive dimensions such as avolition, asociality, anhedonia, alogia, and blunted affect (Gallardo-Gómez et al., 2023; Girdler et al., 2019; Kim et al., 2023; Korman et al., 2023; Mittal et al., 2017; Sabe et al., 2020; Veerman et al., 2017). The magnitude of these effects, quantified as standardized mean differences (SMD), varies across meta-analyses. Sabe et al. (2020), focusing specifically on randomized controlled trials of physical exercise for negative symptoms, reported a small effect size (SMD = -0.20, 95% CI -0.37 to -0.03), mainly driven by aerobic interventions (SMD = -0.31, 95% CI -0.54 to -0.09), whereas non-aerobic interventions showed less consistent benefits. By contrast, Kim et al. (2023), which included a broader range of exercise modalities, including aerobic, multicomponent, and neuromotor forms of exercise, reported a medium effect size on negative symptoms (SMD = -0.51, 95% CI -0.72 to -0.31), as well as a smaller effect size on positive symptoms. Similarly, Gallardo-Gómez et al. (2023), using a multilevel meta-analytic approach across randomized controlled trials, found a medium-to-large effect size on negative symptoms (SMD = 0.65, 95% CI 0.53 to 0.78, with positive values indicating improvement), but no significant effect on positive symptoms (SMD = -0.05). These differences in effect size likely reflect several sources of heterogeneity, including the type of exercise intervention, intervention dose and number of sessions, clinical setting, comparison condition, methodological quality, and whether studies included participants with primary or predominant negative symptoms.

Importantly, Sabe et al. (2020) suggested that improvements in negative symptoms may not be fully reducible to improvements in depressive symptoms, as a subgroup analysis limited to studies excluding participants with baseline depression still showed a moderate reduction in negative symptoms. This point is important but should be interpreted cautiously: the authors also emphasized that none of the included trials specifically targeted primary or predominant negative symptoms. Thus, the current evidence does not yet establish whether exercise acts directly on core negative symptoms, or whether its effects are partly mediated by changes in mood, physical health, social engagement, or other secondary mechanisms. Further reinforcing the idea that exercise may alter the broader clinical organization of schizophrenia, Laurendeau et al. (2026) showed, using a pooled network analysis, that negative symptoms were among the most central nodes of psychopathology at baseline, and that a structured exercise intervention reorganized the overall symptom network toward a less dense and less connected architecture. Together, these findings suggest that exercise may not simply reduce isolated symptoms, but may influence broader motivational and experiential processes that are central to negative symptoms, including effort valuation, hedonic experience, and behavioral initiation.

3. Negative symptoms and engagement in physical activity

The evidence reviewed in the previous section suggests that structured physical exercise may reduce negative symptoms in schizophrenia-spectrum disorders. However, the clinical relevance of this finding depends on a central paradox: the very symptoms that exercise may help improve are also likely to reduce the willingness to engage in exercise programs, as well as everyday physical activity (i.e., everyday movement, such as walking, transportation, domestic activities, or leisure activities).

A first line of evidence comes from cross-sectional research on motivation toward physical activity. Vancampfort et al. (2015) showed that, among inpatients with schizophrenia, higher levels of negative symptoms were associated with lower autonomous motivation to engage in physical activity. In this sense, negative symptoms are not only correlated with lower activity levels, but also with a reduced capacity to experience physical activity as personally meaningful, rewarding, or self-endorsed.

Longitudinal evidence points in a broadly similar direction. In a systematic review of prospective studies in people with severe mental illness, Hassan et al. (2019) highlighted that higher negative symptom scores predicted poorer physical activity-related outcomes, including lower cardiorespiratory fitness and lower physical activity levels. This review included studies involving people with schizophrenia, schizoaffective disorder, psychosis, and broader severe mental illness samples. In particular, Storch Jakobsen et al. (2018) reported that negative symptoms predicted reduced cardiorespiratory fitness in people with schizophrenia spectrum disorders, whereas (Nyboe et al. (2016) reported that negative symptoms predicted lower physical activity over time in people with first-episode schizophrenia. Importantly, Hassan et al. also emphasized the scarcity and methodological heterogeneity of prospective studies, meaning that the temporal and potentially bidirectional relationship between negative symptoms and physical activity remains insufficiently established.

Evidence focusing on aerobic fitness provides a more nuanced picture. Aerobic fitness refers to the physiological capacity to sustain aerobic effort, often indexed by cardiorespiratory fitness or estimated maximal oxygen uptake (Caspersen et al., 1985; Ross et al., 2016). Maurus et al. (2022), in a large cross-sectional study examining aerobic fitness, negative symptoms, cognitive deficits, and brain structure in individuals with schizophrenia, found that aerobic fitness was related to markers of brain structure, particularly hippocampal and parahippocampal volumes, but reported only inconclusive evidence for an association with negative symptoms. This pattern contrasts with recent digital phenotyping evidence, which provides a more direct window into everyday activity and mobility. Yang et al. (2025), using wrist wearables and smartphones in 99 recently discharged individuals with schizophrenia spectrum disorders, found that negative symptoms were among the clinical dimensions most strongly associated with passive digital markers. These markers included physical activity, GPS-derived distance travelled, sleep-related measures, phone use, and social behavior. The authors reported a negative association between physical activity, measured through step count, and negative symptom severity, especially the Brief Negative Symptom Scale's subdomains of avolition, blunted affect, and alogia. They also found that distance travelled, messages sent, and touchscreen tapping speed yielded strong signals associated with negative symptoms, particularly experiential deficits, notably avolition and anhedonia. This latter pattern of findings suggests that digital markers, especially those related to physical activity and mobility, are particularly informative for documenting how negative symptoms are associated with reduced movement in everyday contexts. Yet these objective markers leave open a key experiential question: how is physical activity felt when it is actually performed? They indicate whether individuals move, but not whether bodily effort feels costly, tolerable, rewarding, or pleasurable. This distinction is particularly relevant in schizophrenia, because avolition and anhedonia are thought to involve impairments in reward anticipation, effort valuation, and the willingness to mobilize effort for future benefits (see Box 1 for a summary of fundamental alterations in reward, pleasure, volition, and effort-based decision-making). Applied to physical activity and exercise, these impairments may be especially important because movement often requires individuals to tolerate bodily effort before potential benefits are experienced. Accordingly, the next section focuses on two proximal experiential variables that may help explain how negative symptoms interfere with physical activity engagement: exercise-related pleasure and perceived exertion.

Box 1. Fundamental alterations in reward, pleasure, effort, and related neurobiology in schizophrenia

Negative symptoms in schizophrenia, especially avolition and anhedonia, are closely linked to alterations in how rewards are anticipated, experienced, learned from, and weighed against effort costs (Abram et al., 2022; Correll & Schooler, 2020; Strauss & Cohen, 2017). These processes provide the psychological and neurocognitive background for understanding why beginning and then maintaining a physical exercise routine is so difficult in this population. For each process, behavioral and neural findings are presented together, followed by its implication for physical exercise.

(i) Anticipatory and consummatory pleasure

At the subjective level, individuals with schizophrenia typically report relatively preserved consummatory (in-the-moment) pleasure but reduced anticipatory pleasure, that is, the pleasure expected before an event (Cohen et al., 2010; Gard et al., 2007; Kring & Barch, 2014; Moran et al., 2022). This subjective picture is complemented, rather than contradicted, by neuroimaging: metaanalyses show reduced activation of the striatum and cingulate cortex (regions that are commonly triggered by expected reward; (Bartra et al., 2013; Knutson et al., 2001) during reward anticipation, whereas activation during reward consumption is more variable across studies (Wang et al., 2023; Zeng et al., 2022).

Implication for physical exercise: an individual with schizophrenia may experience relief, calmness, accomplishment, or pleasure after an exercise session, yet still fail to anticipate these positive outcomes before the next one. This may help explain why a positive exercise experience does not automatically translate into spontaneous future engagement.

(ii) Effort valuation and the anticipation of effort

Motivated behavior depends not only on reward value but also on the effort required to obtain it (Treadway et al., 2009; Walton et al., 2018). Individuals with schizophrenia are less willing to choose high-effort options for larger rewards, particularly when motivational negative symptoms are more severe (Barch et al., 2014; Blouzard et al., 2023; Culbreth et al., 2018; Fervaha et al., 2015; Gold et al., 2013), and this partly reflects heightened sensitivity to effort cost and an altered perception of effort (Culbreth et al., 2025; Décombe et al., 2020). A recent transdiagnostic multilevel meta-analysis confirms a moderate effort-based decision-making deficit in schizophrenia-spectrum disorders (g = -0.40), with the severity of negative and positive symptoms among its significant moderators (Pillny et al., 2026). These behavioral deficits are accompanied by weaker task-related activation (measured with functional MRI) of the ventral striatum, anterior cingulate, and dorsolateral prefrontal cortex, a core brain network for weighing effort against reward (Bartra et al., 2013; Prévost et al., 2010), when individuals perform effort-based decision tasks (Saleh et al., 2021). At the neurochemical level, this circuitry depends on a balance between the main excitatory neurotransmitter, glutamate, and the main inhibitory one, GABA: a glutamatergic-GABAergic imbalance in the prefrontal cortex disrupts its top-down control of subcortical dopamine circuits (dopamine being the neuromodulator that signals reward and mobilises effort; Howes et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2021). Causal animal evidence confirms this link: reducing cortical and hippocampal GABA is sufficient to produce effort-based decisionmaking deficits and avolition-like behaviour distinct from anhedonia, alongside attenuated effortevoked dopamine release in the anterior cingulate cortex (Kolata et al., 2018).

Implication for physical exercise: exercise may be rejected not because it lacks potential value, but because its anticipated effort cost is too salient relative to its expected reward. Even low-or moderate-intensity exercise may then be experienced as difficult to initiate when the expected benefit is weak, delayed, or uncertain.

(iii) Reward and reinforcement learning

Reinforcement learning, the process of adjusting behavior based on the outcomes of past actions (Niv, 2009; Rangel et al., 2008), is altered in schizophrenia, particularly the ability to acquire and use positive reward associations to guide future behavior (Dowd & Barch, 2012; Strauss, Frank, et al., 2011; Waltz et al., 2007). Deficits are most apparent when behavior requires integrating past outcomes, maintaining value representations (the brain's internal estimates of how rewarding or worthwhile an option is; (Bartra et al., 2013), and using them to guide later decisions (Culbreth et al., 2021; Gold et al., 2013). These deficits are closely tied to negative symptoms, particularly avolition and anhedonia (Moran et al., 2019; Strauss, Wilbur, et al., 2011; Waltz & Gold, 2015).

Implication for physical exercise: even when exercise produces a positive affective response, this experience may not be automatically encoded as a reason to repeat the behaviour. Interventions may therefore need to make positive outcomes explicit, repeated, and memorable, rather than assuming that pleasant experiences will spontaneously guide future engagement.

(iv) Reward prediction errors and expectation updating

Reward prediction errors, the discrepancies between expected and actual outcomes, signal when internal models should be updated. They are coded by dopamine activity in frontostriatal circuits (the loops linking the prefrontal cortex and the striatum that support reward valuation and goal-directed action; (Haber & Knutson, 2010), including the ventral striatum, midbrain, and orbitofrontal cortex (D'Astolfo & Rief, 2017; Papalini et al., 2020; Schultz, 2016; Steinberg et al., 2013). In schizophrenia, neural responses in these dopamine target regions during reward anticipation are attenuated, and positive prediction errors are used less effectively to guide future behaviour (Arrondo et al., 2015; Dowd & Barch, 2012; Leroy et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2023).

Implication for physical exercise: if an individual expects exercise to be exhausting, unpleasant, or unrewarding but the session is experienced as manageable, calming, or rewarding, this discrepancy can create a clinically useful prediction error. Because prediction-error processing may be altered, such expectancy-violating experiences may need to be explicitly identified, discussed, and repeated across sessions.

(v) Modulating factors: delay discounting and prospection

Several additional processes modulate this reward-effort balance. Many benefits of exercise are delayed (fitness, metabolic and cardiovascular health), yet individuals with schizophrenia show steeper delay discounting, that is, they devalue delayed rewards more rapidly than immediate ones, a tendency itself associated with negative symptoms (Bartolomeo et al., 2020; Brown et al., 2018; Heerey et al., 2011; Horan et al., 2017). Using past experience to imagine future rewards also depends on autobiographical memory and future simulation (the capacity to mentally pre-experience an event), both of which can be impaired in schizophrenia (D'Argembeau et al., 2008; Raffard et al., 2010; Schacter et al., 2007).

Implication for physical exercise: interventions should not rely primarily on delayed health outcomes to motivate engagement. They should instead make immediate or near-term benefits salient (calm, a sense of achievement, or connection with others), and may benefit from post-session reflection that helps individuals register the gap between what they expected and what actually happened.

4. Experienced pleasure and perceived exertion during exercise in schizophrenia

As acknowledged in Section 2., several studies have shown that single bouts of physical exercise can reduce state anxiety and psychological stress while increasing positive wellbeing in people with schizophrenia or psychotic disorders (Heggelund et al., 2014; Vancampfort et al., 2011). However, these studies assessed psychological states before and after exercise, but not during the exercise bout itself. Only a small but relevant body of research has examined how people with schizophrenia or schizophrenia-spectrum disorders experience exercise at the subjective level. This subset of studies is important as, based on the Dual-Mode Theory (Ekkekakis, 2003), affective responses during exercise are shaped by the balance between cognitive-affective factors and interoceptive cues of bodily strain. Inmoment (or retrospective) ratings of pleasure and effort may thus provide fine-grained insight into the characterization of the exercise experience, as the activity is often experienced at different levels of pleasantness and effortfulness while it is being performed (Brevers et al., 2024; Ekkekakis et al., 2011; Hardy & Rejeski, 1989).

A study by Duncan et al. (2016) assessed core subjective pleasure before, during, and after a 10-min bout of moderate-intensity walking (defined as 64 to 76% of the calculated maximum heart rate) in outpatients with schizophrenia, using the Feeling Scale to assess pleasure-displeasure and the Felt Arousal Scale (a single-item measure of perceived activation, independent of hedonic valence; (Svebak & Murgatroyd, 1985) to assess activation. Pleasure increased after exercise and remained elevated 10 min later, but did not significantly improve during exercise itself. Perceived exertion, assessed with the Borg RPE scale, increased from baseline to the in-task assessment during exercise while remaining stable during passive sitting. Duncan et al. thus provide a particularly informative pattern: moderate-intensity walking was experienced as more effortful during the task, whereas the increase in pleasure emerged mainly after task completion.

However, several limitations temper Duncan et al. (2016) findings. First, there was no healthy control group: the comparison was a passive sitting condition completed by the same participants, so the study cannot indicate whether individuals with schizophrenia differ from healthy controls in their affective response to exercise. Second, pleasure ratings did not significantly differ between the exercise and passive sitting conditions, suggesting that the post-task increase in pleasure was not clearly specific to exercise. Crucially, this increase concerned pleasure experienced at and shortly after completion rather than pleasure experienced during the task itself, which did not improve. Such post-completion increases in pleasure are a well-documented phenomenon (the feel-good effect or post-exercise affective rebound; Ekkekakis et al., 2011) and need not reflect the hedonic quality of the activity as it is performed: they can follow even effortful bouts, where they may index relief at the cessation of effort, and here pleasure also rose after passive sitting, which points to non-specific factors.

The post-completion rise therefore says little about whether the exercise itself was experienced as pleasant while being performed. Third, felt arousal did not significantly change from baseline and did not differ from sitting, contrary to the authors' hypothesis: of the two affective dimensions assessed (pleasure and activation), only pleasure shifted, and only over time rather than specifically with exercise, leaving the affective response only partially characterized. Finally, most participants were overweight or obese (75%), raising the possibility that the observed pattern, namely increased perceived exertion during walking and improved pleasure mainly after completion, partly reflects obesity-related physical constraints rather than schizophrenia-specific affective dysfunction.

In this respect, Pitois et al. (2025) is informative, although only indirectly related to schizophrenia. This study examined adults with severe obesity, with or without mental disorders, during two consecutive 6-min walking exercises. The psychiatric subgroup was mainly composed of individuals with mood disorders, with only one participant classified as having a psychotic disorder and no specific indication of schizophrenia. Pitois et al. found that pleasure declined over time across the walking exercises in both groups, without a significant group by time interaction. This suggests that reduced pleasure during repeated walking may be linked to obesity-related physical constraints rather than to mental disorder per se.

Accordingly, the role of BMI and metabolic burden should be considered when interpreting affective responses to walking-based exercise in schizophrenia-spectrum disorders. Finally, and most importantly for the present framework, the study did not examine the association between (changes in) perceived exertion and pleasure. It therefore remains unclear whether higher perceived exertion during exercise modulated pleasure across the exercise session. Décombe et al. (2020) further extended the line of evidence on physical effort in schizophrenia by employing a walking task in which physical demand was manipulated by increasing the distance to be covered. The authors found that perceived exertion and perceived difficulty increased with task demand in both groups, indicating that individuals with schizophrenia were sensitive to changes in physical demand. However, participants with schizophrenia reported higher perceived exertion and difficulty overall than healthy controls. This suggests that even relatively low-effortful forms of physical exercise may carry a higher subjective effort cost in schizophrenia. Notably, perceived exertion was positively associated with BMI in the schizophrenia group, suggesting that bodily or metabolic factors may contribute to the subjective cost of physical effort. Korman et al. (2026) recently examined the immediate (acute) psychological responses to single bouts of resistance training and aerobic interval training in people with psychotic disorders receiving psychiatric rehabilitation, most of whom had schizophrenia or schizoaffective disorder. Both exercise modalities produced immediate increases in positive well-being and reductions in psychological distress. Importantly, in models adjusted for BMI, fatigue followed a modality-specific pattern: it increased after resistance training, whereas it decreased after aerobic exercise. This finding suggests that the psychological benefits of exercise may coexist with different subjective exertional costs depending on the type of activity performed. In schizophrenia-spectrum disorders, where fatigue, reduced energy, amotivation, and difficulties with behavioral initiation are major barriers to physical activity, such immediate fatigue responses may influence whether an exercise bout is experienced as tolerable, aversive, or worth repeating.

Taken together, this literature suggests that people with schizophrenia-spectrum disorders can experience acute psychological benefits from exercise, but it also highlights a major gap: few studies have examined how perceived exertion, fatigue, and pleasure interact during the exercise bout itself. A complementary framework, developed in the context of post-stroke rehabilitation, proposes that fatigue and pain reduce available resources and thereby increase perceived effort, which in turn feeds back onto fatigue and pain in a selfsustaining loop that can undermine engagement and adherence (Charonitis et al., 2026).

Although this model has not been tested in schizophrenia-spectrum disorders, it converges with the present emphasis on perceived exertion and fatigue as determinants of exercise engagement. This gap is important because future physical activity engagement may depend not only on post-exercise well-being, but also on the experienced and remembered balance between effort and pleasure. Understanding this balance may be particularly relevant for negative symptoms, where anticipatory pleasure, effort valuation, and behavioral initiation are often disrupted. Future studies should therefore assess pleasure, perceived exertion, fatigue, BMI, fitness level, and symptom dimensions within the same design, in order to clarify when exercise is experienced as rewarding, costly, or difficult to repeat.

Beyond measuring this balance, the central question is how it can be shifted: the next section develops a learning-based framework in which physical exercise acts as an experiential context where repeated discrepancies between expected and experienced effort and pleasure progressively update the maladaptive internal models that sustain negative symptoms.

5. Rewiring negative symptoms through physical exercise

The previous sections suggest a central clinical paradox. Structured physical exercise may reduce negative symptoms in schizophrenia-spectrum disorders, yet negative symptoms themselves may undermine the capacity to take up exercise and persist with it over repeated sessions. This paradox provides the rationale for a learning-based framework in which exercise is not viewed only as a physiological intervention, but also as an experiential context in which maladaptive expectations about effort, pleasure, reward, and control can be updated.

This section builds on the fundamental alterations summarized in Box 1, including impairments in reward anticipation, hedonic experience, effort valuation, reinforcement learning, and prediction error processing. For each mechanism, Box 1 also highlights potential implications for physical exercise, which are developed further in the intervention framework below. These alterations may contribute to the maintenance of physical exercise avoidance by making exercise appear costly, unrewarding, or not worth initiating. However, they also identify potential intervention targets. If physical exercise is repeatedly experienced as less effortful or more rewarding than expected, it may gradually update internal models of exercise and weaken the self-reinforcing cycle between negative symptoms and exercise avoidance. As illustrated in Figure 1, this framework conceptualizes physical exercise as a corrective learning context in which repeated discrepancies between expected and experienced effort and pleasure may progressively recalibrate future exercise expectations and support renewed engagement.

5.1 Physical exercise as a corrective learning experience

Physical effort is often perceived as costly, and individuals generally tend to minimize unnecessary effort (Cheval & Boisgontier, 2021; Inzlicht et al., 2018). From a learning perspective, physical exercise may also become associated with aversive bodily states, such as fatigue, breathlessness, or discomfort. In classical conditioning terms, exercise-related cues, such as the idea of going for a walk, entering a fitness room, wearing sport clothes, or anticipating bodily exertion, may function as conditioned stimuli if they have been repeatedly paired with aversive unconditioned stimuli such as exhaustion or physical discomfort. Over time, these cues may elicit conditioned responses, such as negative affect, anticipatory fatigue, or avoidance (Craske et al., 2014; Rescorla & Wagner, 1972). In the absence of sufficiently positive or corrective experiences, the association between exercise and aversive bodily states may remain unchanged. This learning account is particularly relevant for schizophrenia-spectrum disorders. As described in Box 1, negative symptoms are associated with reduced reward anticipation, altered effort valuation, impairments in reinforcement learning, and difficulties translating expected reward into action. As a result, individuals may assign greater weight to the immediate costs of exercise than to its delayed or uncertain benefits. The anticipated effort may be vivid and concrete, whereas the expected rewards, such as pleasure, accomplishment, or long-term health benefits, may be weakly represented. This imbalance may bias individuals toward avoidance before the activity is even initiated.

Within the present framework, physical exercise becomes clinically relevant not only because it can improve fitness or reduce symptoms, but because it can create opportunities for corrective learning. When exercise is experienced as less effortful, less aversive, more controllable, or more rewarding than expected, the discrepancy between expectation and experience can generate an expectancy violation or prediction error. Such discrepancies are central to associative learning because they signal that existing predictions no longer fit the experienced outcome and should therefore be updated (D'Astolfo & Rief, 2017; Rescorla & Wagner, 1972; Schultz et al., 1997). In anxiety disorders, exposure-based interventions rely on a similar principle: feared outcomes are challenged when threat cues are not followed by the expected aversive consequence, thereby promoting inhibitory learning and expectation change (Craske et al., 2014; Papalini et al., 2020; Rief et al., 2022).

Here, we propose that a comparable logic may apply to physical exercise in schizophrenia-spectrum disorders. If an individual expects a walk, a bike ride, or a strength session to feel exhausting and beyond their control, but the activity turns out to be tolerable, manageable, or even pleasant, this experience may challenge the prior expectation and support the formation of a new internal model of exercise.

5.2. Prediction errors during physical exercise: from pacing regulation to subjective learning

Physical exercise regulation involves a continuous adjustment between bodily feedback, perceived exertion, and expectations about what remains to be done (Smits et al., 2014; Tucker, 2009; Ulmer, 1996). During exercise, individuals do not simply respond to current bodily sensations; they also estimate whether the ongoing level of effort is sustainable relative to the expected duration, distance, or endpoint of the task. Teleo-anticipatory and pacing models propose that performance is regulated through this dynamic comparison between current physiological and perceptual states, anticipated task demands, and the remaining work to be completed (Noakes, 2012; St Clair Gibson et al., 2006; Tucker, 2009; Ulmer, 1996).

Perceived exertion is central to this regulatory process: rather than being a passive readout of peripheral fatigue, it functions as an integrative signal that helps individuals decide whether to maintain pace, slow down, accelerate, stop, or tolerate discomfort.

Thus, because exercise regulation depends on the comparison between ongoing bodily feedback and expected task demands, discrepancies may emerge between expected and experienced bodily states. If the activity feels easier than expected, the person may continue, increase intensity, or experience the situation as more manageable than anticipated. This within-exercise regulation is therefore already based on discrepancies between expected and experienced bodily states. In schizophrenia-spectrum disorders, such discrepancies may be clinically important because they do not only guide moment-to-moment pacing. They may also shape how exercise is encoded, remembered, and valued.

A central aim of exercise-based interventions may therefore be to help individuals identify and learn from these prediction errors. In the context of physical exercise, prediction errors may concern perceived exertion, fatigue, pleasure, or post-exercise well-being. For example, a person may expect a short walk to be exhausting but find it manageable. Another person may expect an exercise session to be unpleasant, but experience relief or accomplishment after completion. Conversely, a person may underestimate the difficulty of an activity and experience it as more effortful than anticipated. These mismatches matter because they provide information about whether exercise is really as costly or unpleasant as expected.

Positive prediction errors, such as experiencing less effort than expected, are especially relevant because they may directly enhance the rewarding value of exercise. This mechanism is consistent with recent studies (Brevers et al., 2024; Öz et al., 2026) showing that when exertion during running was lower than expected, participants reported greater pleasure. In other words, exercise was not only pleasurable because it was objectively easy, but because it was easier than anticipated. Applied to schizophrenia-spectrum disorders, this suggests that exercise interventions should not necessarily aim to reduce objective effort per se. Rather, they should be shaped around the dynamic relation between expected and experienced effort, so that individuals have opportunities to discover that exercise can be less costly or more rewarding than predicted.

5.3. Learning from both positive and negative prediction errors

Although positive prediction errors, where an exercise is experienced as less effortful than anticipated, are associated with increased pleasure, the intervention target should not be restricted to positive prediction errors only. First, negative prediction errors, where an exercise is experienced as more effortful than anticipated, are an inherent part of physical exercise. A session will sometimes feel more difficult, more tiring, or less pleasant than anticipated. Such experiences may reduce pleasure and reinforce avoidance if they are only interpreted as confirmation that exercise is aversive, uncontrollable, or not worth repeating. In this context, a more relevant clinical target is to help individuals become aware of the gap between anticipation and experience, and to use this gap as a source of learning. This is crucial because expectation violations do not automatically lead to belief updating. Individuals may discount, ignore, or reinterpret expectancy-violating experiences in ways that preserve prior beliefs, a process sometimes described as cognitive immunization (Kube et al., 2017). For example, after completing a walk that was easier than expected, a person may conclude that "it was only manageable today because the weather was good" or "it does not mean I could do it again." For this reason, exercise interventions should not only generate prediction errors, but also help individuals notice, interpret, and encode them.

One way to support this process is to make predictions explicit. Before an exercise bout, individuals can be asked to predict how effortful (or unpleasant, tiring, controllable, or rewarding) the activity will be. After the bout, they can compare these predictions with their actual experience. Explicit prediction may increase the salience of prediction errors and support memory updating (Brod et al., 2022; Rief et al., 2022). This strategy may be particularly useful in schizophrenia-spectrum disorders, where prediction error signaling and reinforcement learning are often altered (Arrondo et al., 2015; Leroy et al., 2020; Maia & Frank, 2017; Wang et al., 2023).

When appropriately supported, negative prediction errors may become opportunities for learning. If an activity is more difficult than expected, the therapeutic focus can shift from "this was too hard" to "this was difficult, but I managed it." In this case, the relevant update is not that exercise is easier than expected, but that difficulty can be tolerated, regulated, and overcome. This type of learning may contribute to perceived competence, mastery, and selfefficacy, which are important determinants of exercise engagement (Bandura, 1997; Hutchinson et al., 2008; McAuley et al., 2003). This dynamic is central to the present framework. The goal is to help individuals build a more flexible internal model of physical exercise: sometimes effort is lower than expected, sometimes effort is higher than expected, but in both cases the experience can provide useful information. Positive prediction errors may help re-associate exercise with pleasure, relief, and accomplishment. Negative prediction errors, when carefully supported, may help reassociate effort with mastery, competence, and control rather than threat or failure. This learning process may also be central for modifying the meaning of effort itself.

Effort is typically avoided prospectively because it discounts reward value, but exerted effort can retrospectively increase the value of outcomes once they have been obtained (Inzlicht et al., 2018; Yi et al., 2020; Zhang & Zheng, 2022). In healthy participants, higher physical effort has been associated with stronger neural responses to reward outcomes, suggesting that effort can enhance reward valuation after completion (Palidis & Gribble, 2020; Umemoto et al., 2023). This idea is consistent with learned industriousness theory, which proposes that effort can acquire secondary reinforcing properties when it is repeatedly paired with positive outcomes (Eisenberger, 1992). Applied to physical exercise, repeated experiences in which effort is followed by relief, accomplishment, pleasure, or social reinforcement may gradually reduce the aversive meaning of exertion and help re-associate effort with reward. Although these mechanisms remain to be tested directly in schizophrenia-spectrum disorders, they suggest that exercise interventions should not necessarily reduce effort costs but rather help individuals reinterpret effort as a signal of accomplishment, progress, and reward.

5.4. Designing exercise experiences that consolidate new internal models

The practical implication of this framework is that exercise interventions should be designed to maximize tolerable expectancy violations. The therapeutic target is therefore a calibrated mismatch between expected and experienced exercise, in which the person can discover that physical exercise is more manageable, rewarding, or controllable than anticipated. This corrective learning requires repetition. Specifically, across repeated sessions, individuals can gradually build a more adaptive internal model of physical exercise: effort is not always overwhelming, discomfort can be tolerated, pleasure can emerge unexpectedly, and completion can be rewarding. Active engagement is central to this process. Continuing a walk that turns out to be less effortful than expected, completing a session despite initial doubt, or repeating the activity on subsequent days may all help consolidate a new representation of exercise. This is consistent with evidence showing that behavioral adaptation following expectation violations recruits neural circuits involved in prediction error, reward processing, and avoidance learning, including the striatum and insula (D'Astolfo & Rief, 2017). and make them more available during future decisions to exercise. This is important because future exercise engagement may depend not only on the actual affective experience during exercise, but also on how that experience is remembered and integrated into later expectations. Here, the affect linked to a session can be considered at successive time points: as experienced moment to moment during the task, as recalled immediately afterwards, and as remembered over longer intervals. This recalled affective response to a behavior, which predicts its future performance, is termed remembered affect in the Affect and Health Behavior Framework (Williams & Evans, 2014 ; for a recent application in physical activity, see Stevens et al., 2020). The post-session reflection described above operates on the immediate, retrospective recall and may help consolidate a more durable, positively toned memory of exercise that remains available when future opportunities arise. How a session ends is particularly relevant: because pleasure tends to rebound once effort decreases, ending a bout at a lower intensity or with easier exercises, so that pleasure rises toward the end, increases post-exercise and remembered pleasure (assessed from fifteen minutes to seven days later) in nonclinical samples (Zenko et al., 2016). Structuring the final minutes of a session in this way may be a simple way to make exercise more positively remembered, and therefore more likely to be repeated, in schizophrenia-spectrum disorders. This focus on immediate experience is also important because many benefits of physical exercise, such as weight loss, improved physical strength, better metabolic health, or increased fitness, emerge only after repeated engagement. These delayed outcomes may be too abstract or too distant to motivate behavior when the immediate cost of effort is highly salient. Early intervention should therefore not rely primarily on long-term health outcomes.

Instead, each session should be structured to produce near-term reinforcement, such as pleasure, relief, or a sense of accomplishment.

One concrete way to achieve this is to let individuals regulate the intensity of exercise according to how it feels, rather than imposing a fixed prescribed intensity: people can either self-select their own intensity (self-paced or preferred-intensity exercise) or adjust it to maintain a positive value on the pleasure-displeasure dimension introduced earlier (affectregulated exercise, typically guided by the Feeling Scale). Anchored in pleasure rather than in external performance targets, such approaches keep the session within a pleasant or at least tolerable range and have been associated with better adherence and mental-health outcomes (Callaghan et al., 2011; Carter et al., 2012; Parfitt et al., 2012). Beyond intensity regulation, other features of the activity also shape preferences and engagement in people with severe mental illness, including whether it is supervised (by a trainer or a health professional) or selfdirected, performed in a group or alone, and the type of activity, such as walking or musclestrengthening (Tew et al., 2023). Tailoring these features to individual preferences may further support a positive exercise experience. More broadly, the experiential and rewardbased strategies outlined here can be expressed as specific, reportable intervention components: in the terms of the Behaviour Change Technique Taxonomy (Michie et al., 2013), these include non-specific reward and self-reward for completing a session, alongside self-monitoring of behaviour and feedback on behavior to support the post-session reflection described above.

Over time, sustained physical exercise may also improve the valuation of delayed outcomes, as suggested by evidence from healthy volunteers and animal models showing that regular exercise can reduce preference for immediate rewards and improve delay-related decision-making (Sofis et al., 2017; Strickland et al., 2016). Given that steeper delay discounting has been associated with negative symptoms in schizophrenia-spectrum disorders, this may represent another pathway through which exercise could influence motivational dysfunction (Bartolomeo et al., 2020; Brown et al., 2018; Heerey et al., 2011; Horan et al., 2017).

6. Concluding discussion

The framework proposed here conceptualizes physical exercise as a learning context potentially capable of updating maladaptive internal models of effort and pleasure (see Figure 1 for a graphical summary). In schizophrenia-spectrum disorders, this imbalance is particularly pronounced: anticipated effort is especially salient and immediate, whereas reward is even more uncertain, delayed, or weakly represented than in the general population, reflecting the documented alterations in effort valuation and reward anticipation summarized in Box 1.

Exercise interventions may help reverse this imbalance when they repeatedly generate experiences that are easier, or more rewarding than expected: such discrepancies between expectation and experience constitute prediction errors that, encoded by dopaminergic signaling in frontostriatal reward circuits (see also Box 1), can progressively recalibrate maladaptive internal models.

At the experiential level, when dysfunctional expectations, such as anticipating that exercise will be exhausting or unrewarding, are disconfirmed by a lower physical cost than anticipated, a salient positive prediction error is generated. Reinforced by other immediate rewarding aspects of exercise, such as improved mood, increased energy, or positive social interaction, these expectancy violations may gradually weaken the association between exercise and aversive experience and support renewed engagement.

Overall, exercise may contribute to the reduction of negative symptoms through several complementary mechanisms. At the level of learning, it can generate positive prediction errors while also helping individuals draw useful information from negative ones.

At the level of valuation, it may increase the reward value of effort, lower perceived exertion, and improve how delayed rewards are weighed. And at the experiential level, it may amplify immediate positive affect and strengthen a sense of mastery and self-efficacy. These mechanisms are intended as a heuristic framework rather than a definitive account, and they generate concrete, testable hypotheses: future trials should examine whether positive prediction errors of perceived exertion predict exercise-related pleasure, whether pleasure and fatigue during exercise predict adherence, whether changes in effort valuation mediate improvements in negative symptoms, and whether these effects are moderated by BMI, baseline fatigue, reward-learning impairments, or the severity of avolition and anhedonia.

These proposals should be read with two qualifications in mind. First, this review deliberately adopts a particular perspective: it approaches negative symptoms and exercise through a learning-based, neurocognitive lens centred on the proximal experience of effort and pleasure. This focus is complementary to, rather than a substitute for, other frameworks, including socio-cognitive accounts (for example, self-efficacy and the social and environmental determinants of physical activity), which also shape exercise engagement and which we do not develop here. Second, the proposed framework remains largely hypothetical: much of the supporting evidence is indirect, drawn from the general population, other clinical groups, or animal models, and the in-session dynamics of pleasure and perceived exertion have rarely been measured directly in schizophrenia-spectrum disorders. These limitations underscore the need for the dedicated empirical tests outlined above.

Ultimately, breaking the cycle between negative symptoms and physical inactivity may require more than prescribing exercise; it may require designing exercise experiences that reshape how effort is predicted beforehand, felt during the activity, and recalled when the next occasion arises.
