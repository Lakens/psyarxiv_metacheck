Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Sense of agency (SoA) refers to the subjective feeling of controlling one's own actions and their outcomes. Previous studies have shown that sensorimotor discrepancies, such as spatial perturbations, reduce SoA, but it remains unclear whether such control disruptions also elicit online autonomic arousal during continuous action. In the present study, across two experiments, participants performed a continuous target-reaching task in which visual feedback of cursor movement was spatially perturbed. We recorded electrodermal activity during the task and used skin conductance level (SCL) as the physiological measure. We assessed subjective SoA and perceived stress after each trial. In Experiment 1, we manipulated perturbation magnitude and found that stronger perturbation reduced subjective SoA, increased perceived stress, and elicited a greater increase in SCL. In Experiment 2, we manipulated perturbation duration to examine whether the SCL increase in Experiment 1 depended on the continued presence of a strong perturbation or primarily on the early phase of disrupted control. Participants reported lower subjective SoA and higher perceived stress in the Long-term perturbation condition than in the Short-term perturbation condition, whereas mean SCL change did not reliably differ between conditions. Together, these findings suggest that spatial perturbations during continuous action are accompanied by changes in sympathetic electrodermal activity. The SCL was sensitive to perturbation magnitude, whereas evidence for a duration-related difference in mean SCL was limited.

Introduction

The sense of agency (SoA) refers to the subjective feeling of controlling one's own actions and their outcomes (Gallagher, 2000; Haggard, 2005). For example, when we press a light switch, if the light turns on immediately as expected, we experience SoA over the action and its outcome. Conversely, if the light turns on only after a long delay, SoA is diminished.

One influential account of SoA is the comparator model, which proposes that SoA arises from a comparison between predicted and actual sensory feedback (Blakemore et al., 1998). When an action is generated, an efference copy of the motor command is used to predict its sensory consequences. If the predicted and actual sensory outcomes match, SoA is maintained; if not, SoA is reduced. Consistent with this theory, numerous studies have shown that temporal and spatial discrepancies between predicted and actual sensory outcomes weaken SoA (Farrer et al., 2008; Kawabe et al., 2013; Metcalfe & Greene, 2007; Penton et al., 2022; Wen et al., 2015a). For example, Metcalfe and Greene (2007) introduced visuomotor perturbations during a continuous cursor-control task and found that disrupting the correspondence between participants' movements and visual feedback reduced agency judgments.

Recent theoretical accounts have extended the comparator model by proposing that SoA arises from multiple sources of information rather than sensorimotor signals alone (Chang & Wen, 2026; Synofzik et al., 2008; Wegner et al., 2004). According to the two-step account of agency, initial sensorimotor processing is followed by higherlevel inferential processes that integrate contextual cues, such as prior knowledge, task performance, effort, and outcome properties (Lin & Tanaka, 2026; Tanaka & Kawabata, 2021; Van den Bussche et al., 2020; Wen et al., 2015b Wen et al., , 2015c Wen et al., , 2017;; Yoshie & Haggard, 2017). For example, Wen et al. (2015c) showed that during a goal-directed continuous control task, improved performance with system assistance led to higher agency ratings, even though it disrupted the direct action-feedback association.

Similarly, Oishi et al. (2018) showed that participants reported higher SoA when they successfully achieved the goal than when they failed to do so, even when effort was controlled across conditions (see also Oishi et al., 2019).

Among these higher-level cues, recent studies have suggested that internal bodily states, including emotions and stress, may also modulate SoA-related measures in a context-dependent manner (Christensen et al., 2019; Render & Jansen, 2021; Villa et al., 2022). For example, Christensen et al. (2019) combined fear-and angerinduction procedures with a temporal binding task, an index of implicit SoA (Haggard et al., 2002), and found that participants showed weaker temporal binding when exposed to fear-and anger-inducing stimuli than in neutral conditions. In addition, several studies have examined SoA in relation to physiological responses (Chu et al., 2023; David et al., 2011; Herman & Tsakiris, 2020; Stern et al., 2020; Wen et al., 2015a). For example, Chu et al. (2023) employed the Trier Social Stress Test to examine the influence of acute psychosocial stress on SoA, and confirmed the effectiveness of the stress manipulation using subjective ratings and multiple physiological measures. Participants in the stress condition exhibited significantly greater subjective arousal and physiological responses than those in the control condition, as indicated by heart rate, electrodermal activity (EDA), and salivary cortisol. Moreover, temporal binding decreased in the stress group following stress induction, suggesting that acute psychosocial stress (accompanied by heightened emotional arousal) may weaken implicit SoA in subsequent actions (cf. Stern et al., 2020). As another example, Wen et al. (2015a) manipulated arousal by presenting a red stimulus as the high-arousal condition and a black stimulus as the low-arousal condition in a keypress-triggered visual-outcome task. Results showed that increased arousal in the high-arousal condition, as supported by subjective arousal ratings and EDA, enhanced temporal binding, although it did not affect explicit agency ratings. Taken together, these studies indicate that experimentally inducing specific emotional experiences or arousal states may modulate SoA-related measures during current or subsequent actions.

Although several studies have recorded physiological responses in SoA research, these measures have primarily been used to verify experimentally induced affective or arousal manipulations rather than to investigate the physiological consequences of sensorimotor perturbations themselves. An important exception is David et al. (2011), who examined skin conductance and heart rate during brief joystick movements with real or false visual feedback, but found no reliable physiological differences between the two feedback conditions. Investigating such physiological consequences may be particularly relevant in continuous actions, such as driving a car, in which individuals must continuously update motor commands while monitoring ongoing sensory feedback. Under such conditions, sensory perturbations may not only involve a mismatch between predicted and actual feedback but also increase effort, uncertainty, and autonomic arousal as the action unfolds. For example, Miller and Mandryk (2016) developed a Temple Run-like game in which participants continuously controlled a character for approximately 2 minutes per trial by swiping on a touchscreen.

The results showed that inserting delays between participants' swipe action and the character's movement increased participants' subjective frustration during gameplay.

These findings suggest that interference in continuous control may influence not only sensorimotor correspondence but also task-induced internal states. However, previous studies have primarily relied on retrospective self-report measures. Therefore, it remains unclear whether sensorimotor perturbations commonly used to modulate SoA also elicit increases in sympathetically mediated EDA.

In the present study, we examined whether control-related spatial perturbations during continuous action induce online physiological changes in EDA. Specifically, we focused on skin conductance level (SCL) as an index of sympathetic electrodermal arousal. Participants performed a continuous target-reaching task while the visual feedback of cursor movement was spatially perturbed. In two experiments, we measured subjective SoA, perceived stress, and SCL during task performance. In Experiment 1, we examined whether the magnitude of the perturbation modulates both subjective responses and physiological arousal by comparing Weak and Strong spatial perturbation conditions. We hypothesized that stronger perturbation would reduce subjective SoA and increase perceived stress. Furthermore, we predicted that stronger perturbation would elicit a larger increase in SCL relative to baseline. In Experiment 2, we further examined whether the SCL increase following a strong perturbation depends on the continued presence of the perturbation or persists after the perturbation ends. To this end, we compared Short-term and Long-term perturbation conditions. Together, these experiments aimed to clarify whether spatial perturbations commonly used to manipulate SoA are accompanied by online autonomic arousal and whether such physiological changes are shaped by perturbation magnitude or duration.

Experiment 1 Method

Sample size determination

We primarily focused on whether baseline-corrected SCL in the Strong perturbation condition was significantly higher than in the Weak perturbation condition.

Accordingly, we specified a one-factor within-participant design and conducted a power analysis for a one-tailed paired-sample t-test using G*Power (Faul et al., 2007). The analysis indicated that a sample of 45 participants would be required to detect an effect size of Cohen's dz = 0.5 with a statistical power of 0.95 (1 -β err prob) and an alpha error of 0.05. Based on this, our target sample size was set at 45 participants. Given the possibility of participant exclusion, we planned to recruit 48 participants for this experiment.

Participants

We recruited 48 participants (16 men, 32 women; 3 left-handed based on their selfreport; mean age = 22.13 years, SD = 3.42) from Kyushu University for the current experiment. All participants were naïve to the purpose of the experiment, had normal or corrected-to-normal vision, and provided written informed consent. Each participant received 1,000 JPY for the experiment (approximately 7 USD). Experiments 1 and 2 were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and were approved by the local ethics committee of the Graduate School of Human-Environment Studies, Kyushu University (Approval Nos. 2026-008, 2025-005, and 2023-038).

Apparatus and stimuli

All stimuli were presented on a 23.8-inch LCD monitor (EIZO Corporation, Hakusan, Japan) with a refresh rate of 60 Hz and resolution of 1,920 × 1,080 pixels. The viewing distance from the display was approximately 60 cm without a chin rest. The experiment was programmed using MATLAB (R2019a, The MathWorks, Natick, MA, USA) with the Psychtoolbox extension (Brainard, 1997; Pelli, 1997). During the experiment, a joystick (Ultra Joystick, Pretorian Technologies) was used to control the cursor.

EDA was recorded using an EDA100C amplifier and EL507 electrodes, and photoplethysmography (PPG) was recorded using a PPG100C amplifier and a TSD200 transducer. Signals were acquired at a sampling rate of 1,000 Hz using a Biopac MP160 data acquisition system and AcqKnowledge 5.0 software (BIOPAC Systems Inc., Goleta, CA, USA). The EDA100C gain was set to 5 µS/V. Both high-pass filter settings (0.05 and 0.5 Hz) were set to DC, and the hardware low-pass filter was set to 1.0 Hz. The physiological recording devices were attached to the participant's non-dominant hand: EDA electrodes were placed on the index and ring fingers, and a PPG optical sensor was attached to the middle finger. Note that PPG was recorded to monitor device operation but was not included in the preregistered or main analyses. We applied no additional software filtering to the EDA signal during acquisition.

Stimuli and Procedure

In the experimental task, a gray game window (19.8 cm × 19.8 cm) was presented at the center of the monitor against a black background. Five visual stimuli were presented within the game window: a red circular cursor (1.1 cm in diameter), three blue square distractors, and one yellow square target (each square measuring 1.1 cm × 1.1 cm). The four squares were randomly positioned within four different quadrants of the game window and remained fixed in those positions throughout the trial.

The experiment consisted of one practice trial and two experimental trials. Each trial consisted of a resting period followed by a continuous target-reaching task. At the beginning of each trial, a countdown timer was displayed on the screen, and participants were instructed to remain seated and relaxed and refrain from movement during the resting period. After the resting period, the task interface appeared, consisting of five visual stimuli: one red circular cursor, three blue square distractors, and one yellow square target. Participants controlled the red cursor using a joystick operated with their dominant hand. Their task was to move the cursor toward the yellow target while avoiding contact with the blue distractors. Once the cursor reached the target, one of the three distractors was randomly selected to change color from blue to yellow and became the new target, whereas the previous target reverted to a blue distractor. Participants continuously repeated this target-reaching task until the end of the trial.

The practice trial consisted of a 20-s resting period followed by a 60-s joystickcontrol period without perturbation. After the practice trial, physiological recording equipment was attached to the participant's non-dominant hand and remained in place throughout the experimental session. Before the first experimental trial began, the EDA and PPG signals were visually inspected to confirm that physiological signals were being recorded. Each experimental trial consisted of a 120-s resting period followed by a 593-s joystick-control period (i.e., approximately 12 min in total). The joystick-control period was further divided into a 360-s baseline period (i.e., no perturbation), a 143-s perturbation period, and a 90-s post-perturbation baseline period (i.e., no perturbation). A rest interval of approximately 3 min was provided between two experimental trials, during which the physiological recording equipment remained attached to the participant. The entire experiment lasted approximately 60 min.

Experiment 1 employed a fully within-participant design with one factor: perturbation magnitude (Weak vs. Strong). In each trial, participants experienced one of two levels of angular perturbation, applied to the visual feedback of cursor movement.

Perturbation was defined as a clockwise rotation of the visual feedback of cursor movement relative to the participant's joystick input. Participants were informed that computer-controlled interference might occasionally occur, resulting in disruptions to cursor movement. The order of perturbation conditions was counterbalanced across participants.

At the onset of the perturbation period (i.e., after the 360-s baseline period), the perturbation angle gradually increased from 0° to the condition-specific maximum angle (15° in the Weak condition and 75° in the Strong condition) over 5 s and then remained at that angle for 5 s, constituting the ramp-up phase of the perturbation period (Figure 1).

The perturbation then entered the cycle phase, during which the angle alternated between the maximum and minimum angles assigned to each condition. Specifically, the angle alternated between 15° and 6° in the Weak perturbation condition and between 75° and 30° in the Strong perturbation condition. This angle alternation in the present study was adopted to prevent participants from adapting to (learning) the specific rotated angle. In each cycle, the angle gradually decreased from the maximum to the minimum over 3 s, remained at the minimum for 5 s, increased back to the maximum over 3 s, and then remained at the maximum for another 5 s. Thus, each complete cycle lasted 16 s. In both perturbation conditions, the cycle phase consisted of eight complete cycles, lasting 128 s in total. Following the cycle phase, the perturbation angle gradually decreased from the maximum angle to 0° over 5 s, constituting the recovery phase of the perturbation period.

Thereafter, no perturbation was applied, and the trial continued during the 90 s postperturbation baseline period until its conclusion.

At the end of each trial, participants completed subjective ratings of SoA and perceived stress. SoA was assessed using the question, "How much control did you feel over the movement of the red circle?" Responses were provided on a 9-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (No control) to 9 (Full control). Perceived stress was assessed using the question, "How stressed were you about controlling the red circle on screen?" Responses were also recorded on a 9-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (Not at all) to 9 (Very much).

Data analysis

For each experimental trial, the perturbation onset was identified from the digital trigger recorded in the raw EDA data. The trigger was generated by a PLDM-20 device (Sankei Kizai Co., Ltd.) and corresponded to the onset of a white stimulus presented in the lower-left corner of the screen. For each trial, baseline SCL was defined as the median SCL value over the 60 s immediately preceding the perturbation onset. We used the median for the baseline period to obtain a robust estimate of the pre-perturbation SCL level that was less influenced by transient, nonspecific fluctuations during this control period. In contrast, we used the mean for the post-onset window because we aimed to quantify overall SCL activity after perturbation onset, including perturbation-related changes within the analysis window. SCL during the post-onset analysis window was calculated as the mean SCL during the 60 s immediately following perturbation onset (0-60 s). This window was selected to match the baseline window duration and capture the initial physiological response to the perturbation. We did not use the full perturbation period of 143 s because such a long window may include habituation or adaptation to the perturbation, which could obscure the initial change in SCL. Baseline-corrected SCL was then calculated separately for each condition by subtracting the baseline value from the mean value during the post-onset analysis window.

In the present study, we used R (version 4.5.1) in Visual Studio Code (version 1.124.2), primarily with the tidyverse package (2.0.0). Data cleaning and restructuring were performed by dplyr (1.1.4) and tidyr (1.3.1), and data visualization was performed using ggplot2 (3.5.2). To supplement the frequentist analyses by quantifying the strength of evidence, we computed Bayes factors in JASP (Version 0.97.1) for all directional paired-samples comparisons. One-sided Bayesian paired-samples t-tests with the default Cauchy prior (r = 0.707) were used for comparisons analyzed with paired-samples t-tests, whereas a one-sided Bayesian Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used for the exploratory nonparametric robustness check. We report BF10 to quantify the evidence for the alternative hypothesis relative to the null.

Manipulation checks were conducted to confirm that the perturbation manipulation affected participants' subjective experience as intended. First, one-tailed paired t-tests were conducted to examine whether subjective SoA rating was higher in the Weak perturbation condition than in the Strong perturbation condition, and whether subjective stress rating was lower in the Weak perturbation condition than in the Strong perturbation condition. In addition, participants' task performance was compared between the two conditions by examining the number of successful target reaches in each condition.

The main analysis focused on changes in SCL from baseline to the post-onset analysis window (i.e., baseline-corrected SCL). Directional hypotheses were tested using one-tailed paired t-tests. Following the preregistered testing sequence, we first examined whether SCL during the post-onset analysis window was higher than baseline SCL separately in the Weak and Strong conditions. If either test showed a significant difference, we then tested whether baseline-corrected SCL was greater in the Strong condition than in the Weak condition.

Results

Data Exclusions

Of the 48 participants recruited for Experiment 1, 10 were excluded from the analyses. Four participants were excluded because the digital trigger marking perturbation onset was not successfully recorded. We excluded five participants whose mean SCL during the continuous target-reaching task remained below 2 μS in either Trial 1 or Trial 2, using the lower bound of the typical SCL range (2-20 μS; Dawson et al., 2017) as a criterion. One participant was excluded because data collection could not be completed due to experimenter error. Thus, the final analyses included the remaining 38 participants, which was below the preregistered sample size. No missing values remained after these exclusions, and no missing data were imputed.

Manipulation Checks

To examine whether the perturbation manipulation affected subjective experience as intended, one-tailed paired t-tests were conducted on SoA and stress ratings. Subjective SoA rating (Figure 2A) was significantly lower in the Strong condition (M = 4.74, SD = 1.52) than in the Weak condition (M = 6.87, SD = 1.40), t(37) = -8.23, p < .001, Cohen's dz = -1.34, BF10 = 3.19×10 7 . Conversely, subjective stress rating (Figure 2B) was significantly higher in the Strong condition (M = 6.03, SD = 1.84) than in the Weak condition (M = 4.29, SD = 2.09), t(37) = 5.03, p < .001, Cohen's dz = 0.82, BF10 = 3257. These results indicate that the Strong perturbation condition successfully reduced participants' perceived control and increased their perceived stress. Because the SoA and perceived stress ratings were obtained retrospectively at the end of each trial, they cannot be attributed exclusively to participants' experiences during the perturbation period and may also have reflected their experiences during the baseline periods before and after the perturbation (see the General Discussion).

Task performance was also poorer in the Strong perturbation condition. The number of total successful reaches was significantly lower in the Strong condition (M = 295.79, SD = 76.73) than in the Weak condition (M = 351.55, SD = 72.27), t(37) = -6.60, p < .001, Cohen's dz = -1.07, BF10 = 3.06×10 5 . A similar pattern was observed for the number of successful reaches during the perturbation period (Figure 2C); participants made fewer successful reaches in the Strong condition (M = 25.87, SD = 15.77) than in the Weak condition (M = 79.68, SD = 19.45), t(37) = -17.67, p < .001, Cohen's dz = -2.87, BF10 = 7.21 × 10 16 . These results indicate that stronger angular perturbation impaired participants' task performance.

Skin Conductance Level

The primary analysis examined whether SCL increased from baseline to the postonset analysis window. In the Weak condition, SCL during the post-onset analysis window (M = 7.70 μS, SD = 4.39) was not significantly higher than baseline SCL (M = 7.70 μS, SD = 4.23), t(37) = 0.07, p = .471, Cohen's dz = 0.01, BF10 = 0.18, indicating that weak perturbation did not elicit a reliable increase in SCL. In contrast, in the Strong condition, SCL during the post-onset analysis window (M = 8.20 μS, SD = 4.46) was significantly higher than baseline SCL (M = 7.87 μS, SD = 4.49), t(37) = 3.34, p < .001, Cohen's dz = 0.54, BF10 = 34.97, indicating that strong perturbation elicited a reliable increase in SCL.

Next, we conducted a one-tailed paired t-test on baseline-corrected SCL (Figure 2D). We found that the SCL change was significantly larger in the Strong condition (M = 0.32 μS, SD = 0.60) than in the Weak condition (M = 0.01 μS, SD = 0.50), t(37) = 2.86, p = .003, Cohen's dz = 0.47, BF10 = 11.44. These findings support the hypothesis that stronger spatial perturbation during continuous control elicits greater electrodermal arousal. As an exploratory robustness check, we examined whether the condition difference in baselinecorrected SCL remained significant using a nonparametric test. A Shapiro-Wilk test indicated that the distribution of SCL differences between the two conditions significantly deviated from normality (W = 0.89, p = .001). Therefore, we conducted a one-tailed Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Consistent with the paired-samples t-test, the increase in SCL was significantly larger in the Strong condition than in the Weak condition, W = 580.50, p < .001, BF10 = 270.60. Figure 3A shows the descriptive time course of baseline-corrected SCL during the 60-s post-onset analysis window. To explore population-level time-dependent changes in baseline-corrected SCL and estimate peak timing for each perturbation condition, we used an exploratory generalized additive mixed-effects model (GAMM), implemented in the mgcv package (version 1.9-3). The GAMM allowed nonlinear temporal changes in SCL to be modeled while accounting for participant-level variability. The model included the perturbation condition as a parametric predictor, condition-specific smooths of time, and participant-specific smooths over time. To account for residual serial autocorrelation among the 1-s observations, we incorporated a first-order autoregressive [AR(1)] error structure. The AR(1) parameter was estimated from the lag-1 correlation of residuals obtained from the corresponding GAMM without an autoregressive error structure.

Adjacent residual pairs were pooled across independent participant-by-condition time series, excluding pairs that crossed series boundaries. The resulting estimate was ρ = .965, and the first observation of each time series was specified as the start of a new AR process. The estimated population-level trajectories during the 60-s post-onset analysis window are shown in Figure 3B. With the Weak condition as the reference level, the intercept did not differ significantly from zero, b = -0.001, SE = 0.08, t = -0.02, p = .985. After accounting for AR(1) residual autocorrelation, the parametric contrast for the Strong condition relative to the Weak condition was significant, b = 0.33, SE = 0.07, t = 4.58, p < .001, indicating a higher overall level of baseline-corrected SCL in the Strong condition across the modeled time window. The smooth term for time was not significant in the Weak condition, edf = 1.63, F = 0.11, p = .903, whereas the Strong condition showed a significant nonlinear temporal pattern, edf = 16.83, F = 9.77, p < .001. The participantspecific smooth was also significant, edf = 114.95, F = 0.33, p < .001, indicating participant-specific variation in temporal SCL trajectories. The model explained 50.8% of the deviance, with an adjusted R² = .493. Population-level predicted curves were then used to estimate peak timing for each condition, excluding participant-specific smooths from prediction. The Strong condition showed a model-estimated maximum approximately 11.4 s after perturbation onset.

Because the time smooth in the Weak condition was not significant, its numerical maximum was not interpreted as a reliable temporal peak. These exploratory results suggest that the Strong condition showed a reliable nonlinear temporal change in SCL, whereas no reliable time-dependent change was detected in the Weak condition.

Discussion

In Experiment 1, we examined whether spatial perturbations during continuous action elicit not only changes in subjective experience, but also online physiological activity indexed by SCL. Consistent with previous studies, the results of Experiment 1 showed that stronger spatial perturbation led to lower SoA ratings (Farrer et al., 2008; Penton et al., 2022) and higher perceived stress, broadly consistent with previous findings that disrupted or delayed control can increase frustration (Miller & Mandryk, 2016), confirming that the current manipulation successfully altered participants' subjective experience of control. Importantly, the SCL results showed that the Strong perturbation condition elicited a reliable increase in SCL relative to baseline, whereas the Weak perturbation condition did not. Moreover, the baseline-corrected SCL was significantly higher in the Strong condition than in the Weak condition. These results demonstrate that, in the present continuous-control task, stronger spatial perturbation can be accompanied by increased electrodermal activity.

The exploratory time-series analysis further suggested that the SCL in response to the Strong perturbation was not constant across the perturbation period. The GAMM analysis indicated a nonlinear temporal pattern in the Strong condition, with the population-level predicted peak occurring approximately 11.4 s after perturbation onset.

The relatively early peak, followed by a gradual decline in SCL despite the continued presence of the perturbation, may reflect habituation to the altered sensorimotor environment (Barry et al., 2023; Meir et al., 2024; Zimmer & Richter, 2023). Importantly, this temporal pattern raises two possible explanations for the increased SCL observed in the Strong condition. One possibility is that the continued presence of the strong perturbation contributes to maintaining increased SCL, although its influence gradually diminishes over time. Alternatively, the initial encounter with a strong perturbation may induce physiological arousal that persists for some time even after the perturbation itself is removed, potentially reflecting a physiological response triggered by the initial exposure to the perturbation or continued vigilance for further perturbations.

To distinguish between these possibilities, in Experiment 2, we examined whether the SCL following the initial increase depends on the continued presence of a strong perturbation or persists after the perturbation is removed. We therefore manipulated perturbation duration while keeping its magnitude constant, comparing a Short-term perturbation condition with a Long-term perturbation condition.

Discussion

In Experiment 2, we examined whether SCL increase after the peak depends on the continued presence of a strong perturbation or primarily on the early experience of that perturbation. The results showed that participants reported lower SoA and higher perceived stress in the Long-term condition than in the Short-term condition. Because the conditions also differed in the duration of unperturbed control before the retrospective ratings, these differences cannot be attributed uniquely to perturbation duration.

In contrast, the SCL results did not show clear evidence that longer perturbation exposure produced a larger physiological response. Baseline-corrected SCL was significantly greater than zero in both the Short-term and Long-term conditions, but it did not differ significantly between conditions during the primary critical analysis window.

Importantly, in the Short-term condition, the critical analysis window occurred immediately after the perturbation had ended, whereas in the Long-term condition, the same window occurred while the perturbation was still ongoing. This pattern suggests that SCL may remain elevated for some time after participants experience a strong perturbation, even after the perturbation itself disappears. Taken together, the findings suggest that exposure to a strong perturbation in the Short-term condition was sufficient to elicit an increase in SCL that persisted after perturbation offset, whereas continuing the perturbation did not produce a reliably greater mean SCL increase over the corresponding analysis windows.

General Discussion

The present study examined whether control-related spatial perturbations during continuous action influence not only subjective SoA, but also online autonomic arousal indexed by SCL. In Experiment 1, we manipulated perturbation magnitude and found that stronger perturbation reduced subjective SoA, increased perceived stress, and elicited a greater increase in SCL. In Experiment 2, we manipulated perturbation duration to examine whether the SCL increase depended on the continued presence of a strong perturbation or primarily on the early experience of disrupted control.

Participants reported lower subjective SoA and higher perceived stress in the Long-term than in the Short-term perturbation condition, whereas mean baseline-corrected SCL did not reliably differ between conditions. Together, these findings suggest that spatial perturbations during continuous action are accompanied by autonomic arousal, with the physiological response being sensitive to perturbation magnitude, whereas evidence for a duration-related difference in mean SCL was limited.

In Experiment 1, we found that SCL increased reliably relative to baseline in the Strong perturbation condition but not in the Weak perturbation condition. Previous studies have experimentally manipulated arousal through procedures independent of sensorimotor discrepancy (Christensen et al., 2019; Chu et al., 2023; Wen et al., 2015a). Therefore, to our knowledge, the present study is among the first to characterize tonic electrodermal changes and their temporal dynamics during graded visuomotor perturbation in continuous action. The present finding extends the conventional interpretation of spatial perturbations: such perturbations not only disrupt sensorimotor correspondence and reduce subjective SoA but can also elicit online changes in autonomic arousal while an action is ongoing. In addition, the exploratory GAMM analyses suggested that this physiological response unfolded dynamically in the Strong perturbation condition: the population-level predicted SCL rose steeply following perturbation onset, peaked approximately 11.38 s later, and then gradually declined.

The rapid initial increase may reflect an autonomic response to a salient sensorimotor discrepancy, and the subsequent decline, despite the continued presence of the perturbation, may reflect a reduction in the novelty and salience of the discrepancy.

In Experiment 2, we further clarified the temporal characteristics of these SCL changes. When participants experienced the same strong perturbation for either a short or long duration, retrospective subjective SoA and perceived stress differed between the two duration conditions: ratings indicated lower SoA and higher stress in the Long-term condition than the Short-term condition. In contrast, although SCL increased relative to baseline in both conditions, the magnitude of the increase did not differ significantly between them. Importantly, the critical analysis window in the Short-term condition occurred immediately after perturbation offset, whereas the same window in the Longterm condition occurred during ongoing perturbation; this pattern therefore suggests that the early experience of a strong perturbation may be sufficient to elicit an increase in SCL that persists for some time after the perturbation disappears. The exploratory GAMM analysis yielded a nonlinear trajectory in the Long-term condition and an approximately linear decline following the perturbation offset in the Short-term condition.

Taken together, the findings of Experiment 2 raise the possibility that continued exposure to a strong perturbation, as in the Long-term condition, may influence the temporal profile of the physiological response without necessarily amplifying its mean magnitude, but this possibility requires further confirmation. Theoretically, the differences between the Long-term and Short-term conditions in retrospective SoA and perceived stress, in the absence of a corresponding difference in SCL, suggest that subjective experience and autonomic arousal need not covary directly but may reflect different temporal aspects of disrupted control. The persistence of SCL elevation after perturbation offset also raises the possibility that the physiological consequences of disrupted control extend beyond the perturbation itself. As an implication, whether such residual arousal carries over to subsequent actions remains unknown, but this possibility may be relevant to post-error adjustments, in which errors or difficult control experiences influence subsequent behavior (e.g., Danielmeier & Ullsperger, 2011; Di Costa et al., 2018). Future studies could directly test whether perturbation-induced arousal predicts subsequent performance adjustments or agency judgments.

The present findings may extend the two-step account of SoA by suggesting that the consequences of spatial perturbation are not confined to sensorimotor processing.

Although the perturbation was introduced to alter the correspondence between predicted and observed cursor movements, it was accompanied by both reduced subjective SoA and increased SCL. Thus, a sensorimotor manipulation commonly used to investigate SoA may also alter the agent's autonomic state. Within the two-step account, bodily signals associated with this autonomic arousal could, in principle, provide an additional source of information for retrospective agency judgment (Herman & Tsakiris, 2020; Stern et al., 2020). From this perspective, even SoA judgments made in a relatively simple motor-control task may reflect the integration of sensorimotor prediction errors with bodily information arising from the disruption of control. Moreover, the larger SCL change in the Strong condition raises the possibility that bodily information was more salient under severe control disruption and could have contributed to retrospective SoA judgment. However, this possible contribution of bodily information does not imply a one-to-one correspondence between subjective judgments and SCL. Rather, subjective ratings and physiological arousal may be sensitive to partially different temporal aspects of disrupted control. Retrospective ratings may reflect the accumulated difficulty or loss of control experienced over the entire trial, whereas SCL may primarily reflect autonomic arousal elicited during the early phase of a salient control disruption. Future studies are therefore needed to determine whether and under what conditions perturbation-induced autonomic arousal directly contributes to retrospective agency judgments.

The exploratory GAMM analyses further suggest that mean-level comparisons alone may obscure the temporal dynamics of perturbation-induced arousal. Across the two experiments, SCL changes were characterized by time-varying trajectories rather than sustained elevations throughout the perturbation period. Although these findings are exploratory, they highlight the potential value of modeling continuous physiological dynamics in studies of ongoing action control.

In the present study, we developed an experimental paradigm that allowed EDA to be tracked over an extended period of continuous control. Unlike in typical SoA experiments where participants experienced perturbations across repeated trials lasting only a few seconds, participants in the present task rested for 2 minutes and performed a continuous control task for approximately 10 minutes. This extended duration allowed us to track changes in SCL over a sufficiently long period before, during, and after the perturbation. In addition, the perturbation angle was dynamically varied to avoid excessive repetition of identical perturbations. Repeated exposure to similar events is known to attenuate EDA responses through habituation of the orienting response (Barry et al., 2023; Meir et al., 2024; Zimmer & Richter, 2023). For example, Meir et al. (2024) found that the EDA response elicited by the final unexpected balance perturbation was smaller than that elicited by the first perturbation during treadmill walking, suggesting habituation of the electrodermal response following repeated balance perturbations.

Thus, although habituation to the perturbation itself cannot be entirely ruled out, varying the perturbation angle may have helped reduce habituation associated with repeated exposure to identical perturbations.

To date, both explicit and implicit measures of SoA have been developed, and their correlations have also been examined (Dewey & Knoblich, 2014; Imaizumi & Tanno, 2019; Siebertz & Jansen, 2022). Explicit measures, such as categorical agency judgments and agency rating scales, directly assess subjective experience of control, and are therefore simple and useful (Asai, 2016; Farrer et al., 2013; Miyawaki & Morioka, 2020; Takada et al., 2026). However, these measures may be influenced by response biases or task interpretation (Haggard, 2017; Moore, 2016). By contrast, implicit measures are intended to capture agency-related processes without requiring participants to directly report their experience. A representative example is temporal binding (Barlas et al., 2018; Barlas & Obhi, 2014; Moore & Obhi, 2012; Muth et al., 2022), the perceived compression of the temporal interval between a voluntary action and its outcome. Although temporal binding has often been used as an implicit index of SoA, accumulating evidence suggests that it does not necessarily correspond to subjective agency experience and may also reflect more general processes such as causal inference (Buehner, 2012; Dewey & Knoblich, 2014; Kirsch et al., 2019; Suzuki et al., 2019).

Given the limitations associated with both explicit and implicit measures of SoA, physiological measures may provide a complementary perspective on the processes accompanying changes in SoA, rather than an alternative measure of SoA itself. The present findings suggest that EDA can capture the online temporal dynamics of autonomic arousal during disrupted control, including an early increase after perturbation onset and sustained elevation after perturbation offset-temporal characteristics that could not be captured by retrospective ratings. Nevertheless, EDA should not be interpreted as a direct index of SoA on the basis of the present findings alone. As an index of sympathetic arousal, EDA is sensitive to multiple emotional or cognitive processes, including stress (Chu et al., 2023; Setz et al., 2010), attention (Hartnett et al., 2025), and mental workload (Hogervorst et al., 2014). Accordingly, the observed increase in SCL indicates that disrupted control was accompanied by autonomic arousal, but does not establish a causal relationship between subjective SoA and autonomic arousal. The perturbation may have independently reduced SoA and increased SCL. Alternatively, consistent with the two-step account of SoA, perturbationinduced arousal may have served as an additional bodily cue contributing to retrospective agency inference. Thus, combining physiological measures with established explicit and implicit measures may provide a more comprehensive characterization of the processes accompanying changes in SoA.

An important limitation is that perturbation magnitude also substantially affected task performance. Participants completed markedly fewer successful reaches in the Strong than in the Weak condition, particularly during the perturbation period. Therefore, the larger SCL response in the Strong condition cannot be attributed specifically to sensorimotor discrepancy or reduced SoA. Greater task difficulty, effort, error frequency, uncertainty, or frustration associated with the stronger perturbation may also have contributed to the electrodermal response. Future studies could dissociate these factors by manipulating control discrepancy while more closely matching task difficulty and performance across conditions.

Another limitation of the present study concerns the difference in the time periods captured by subjective and physiological measures. The SoA and perceived stress ratings were obtained retrospectively at the end of each trial and therefore represented summary evaluations of the entire joystick-control period (10 minutes), including the baseline periods before and after the perturbation. In contrast, the SCL analyses focused on predefined time windows relative to perturbation onset. Although this approach was necessary to measure stable EDA, these ratings cannot be mapped one-to-one onto the magnitude or time course of physiological response at specific moments. Accordingly, the apparent dissociation between subjective ratings and SCL across perturbation duration may partly reflect differences in measurement timescale rather than a fundamental dissociation between subjective and physiological responses.

In Experiment 2, the duration conditions also differed in the amount of unperturbed control between perturbation offset and the end-of-trial ratings. Consequently, the observed rating differences cannot be attributed uniquely to perturbation duration.

In conclusion, the present study provides evidence that spatial perturbations during continuous action influence not only subjective SoA but also online autonomic arousal indexed by SCL. The present findings suggest that the electrodermal response may remain elevated for some time after perturbation offset. Together, these results indicate that the electrodermal response accompanying disrupted control has temporal characteristics that are not fully captured by retrospective subjective judgments. By incorporating physiological measures into SoA research, the present study provides a complementary perspective on how disruptions of control unfold during ongoing action.
