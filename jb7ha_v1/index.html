How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). Statcheck might still miss one-sided tests that are described in another sentence, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. Abstract

Low) self-concept clarity and trait negative affectivity ostensibly capture distinct domains: a lack of clarity and stability of self-beliefs and the tendency to experience different kinds of negative emotions, respectively. Despite their clear theoretical distinctions, they are strongly correlated and show overlapping nomological networks. To date, there has not been a thorough evaluation of how facets of trait negative affectivity overlap with, and are distinct from, (low) self-concept clarity. Using an adult sample (N=493) who completed a series of baseline questionnaires followed by a 15-day ecological momentary assessment protocol, we evaluated the similarities and differences between the Self-concept Clarity Scale (SCCS) and a comprehensive collection of trait negative affectivity facets. Results from multilevel structural equation models indicated that the SCCS accounted for unique variance in a few select outcomes, most notably global depression and distress measures, state authenticity and rash inaction, providing evidence of unique predictive validity. However, our findings also indicated substantial empirical overlap between the SCCS and all facets of trait negative affectivity, with nearly identical patterns of empirical relations of momentary affective outcomes. Furthermore, much of the SCCS's relations with maladaptive functioning was accounted for by its overlap with trait negative affectivity. Overall, our findings clarify important nuances in the empirical overlap and distinctiveness between the SCCS and facets of trait negative affectivity, and we discuss the conceptual and practical implications of this overlap for future research on personality and self-functioning.

[div-01]

Self-concept is conceptualized as the understanding of one's traits (e.g., work ethic, intelligence), roles (e.g., parent, manager, spouse), and values (e.g., morals, faith, spirituality) which together guide the processing and interpretation of self-relevant information (Greenwald & Pratkanis, 1984; Kihlstrom & Cantor, 1984). More narrowly, self-concept clarity is an aspect of individuals' self-concept that captures the degree to which an individual's understanding of their self-concept is confidently and clearly defined, internally consistent, and temporally stable (Campbell et al., 1996). Associations between self-concept clarity and theoretically proximal aspects of the self, such as self-esteem, have been well established (e.g., Weber et al., 2023), and recent meta-analytic work has shown that self-esteem and self-concept clarity are strongly related (weighted r = .65; Lodi- Smith et al., 2026). These findings have led researchers to recommend methods that can carefully disentangle these related constructs (Lodi-Smith et al., 2026). However, self-concept clarity has also been shown to be similarly related to measures of trait negative affectivity (r = -.50 to -.63; Campbell et al., 1996; Connor, 2020), despite the theoretical distinctions between these latter two constructs. Furthermore, self-concept clarity and trait negative affectivity demonstrate considerable overlap in their associations with psychological outcomes (Manchiraju, 2026; Morey & Zanarini, 2000; Roepke et al., 2011; Watson et al., 1994), raising questions about the extent to which these constructs capture overlapping versus distinct aspects of psychological functioning.

To address this gap, the present study examined the extent to which the Self-Concept Clarity Scale (SCCS; Campbell et al., 1996) captures unique variance beyond a comprehensive set of trait negative affectivity measures. Specifically, we compared their associations with a range of external criteria, including established measures of depression and psychological distress, as well as momentary emotional, behavioral, and interpersonal experiences assessed through ecological momentary assessment (EMA), with the goal of better understanding the unique and overlapping characteristics of trait negative affectivity and self-concept clarity.

Conceptual and Empirical Background on Self-concept Clarity

The centrality of self-concept to personality and human functioning has been acknowledged for over a century (Cooley, 1902; Erikson, 1950; James, 1890). Through the lenses of self-concept and identity, individuals process and adapt to new experiences, adversity, emotions, and social interactions (Rosenberg, 1989). Although self-concept encompasses numerous dimensions, one aspect that has received considerable empirical attention is selfconcept clarity. Since its original conceptualization, self-concept clarity has been closely linked to psychological well-being (Campbell, 1990; Campbell et al., 1996; see also the more recent review by Manchiraju, 2026). Individuals higher in self-concept clarity report greater positivity in how they view themselves (Campbell et al., 1996) and greater subjective well-being (Ritchie et al., 2011). Conversely, lower self-concept clarity has been associated with poorer psychological functioning, including greater rumination, self-consciousness, and neuroticism (Campbell et al., 1996), as well as greater depression, anxiety, perceived stress (Smith et al., 1996), and negative affect (Nezlek & Plesko, 2001), alongside lower self-esteem (Campbell et al., 1996; Nezlek & Plesko, 2001; Smith et al., 1996). Self-concept clarity is also sensitive to life circumstances, fluctuating in response to transitions out of important social roles (e.g., divorce, job loss, and completing college; Light & Visser, 2013; Slotter et al., 2015).

These empirical findings extend to interpersonal functioning. Higher self-concept clarity is related to lower social anxiety (Stopa et al., 2010) and reduced loneliness (Richman et al., 2016), whereas individuals with lower self-concept clarity appear less likely to engage in moral behaviors (e.g., donating to charity) and more likely to engage in antisocial behaviors (e.g., cheating and dishonesty; Wang & Touré-Tillery, 2024). Within romantic relationships, lower selfconcept clarity is tied to difficulties in relationship formation and lower relationship satisfaction (Emery et al., 2015; Lewandowski et al., 2010). Thus, self-concept clarity is relevant both to broader psychological functioning and to the development and maintenance of social roles and relationships.

Conceptual and Empirical Background on Trait Negative Affectivity

Trait negative affectivity captures the tendency to experience unstable and unpleasant emotions and is represented in all prominent models of personality (Matthews et al., 2003; Leary & Hoyle, 2009), most notably as Neuroticism in the Five-factor Model (FFM) of personality (Gore & Widiger, 2013). It is also the FFM domain with the strongest empirical relations with various forms of internalizing psychopathology, including anxiety disorders, post-traumatic stress disorder, and major depressive disorder (Clark et al., 1994; Kotov et al., 2010), but also somatoform disorders, schizophrenia, and eating pathology (Malouff et al., 2005). Beyond psychopathology, its interpersonal correlates closely parallel those observed for self-concept clarity. Higher trait negative affectivity is related to elevated antisocial tendencies (Vize et al., 2019), heightened social anxiety (Newby et al., 2017), and diminished satisfaction within romantic relationships (Malouff et al., 2010), whereas low levels are associated with greater marital satisfaction (Gattis et al., 2004), occupational success (Ozer & Benet-Martinez, 2006), and overall quality of life (Lynn & Steel, 2006). Finally, trait negative affectivity has been theorized to manifest in heightened self-criticism, sensitivity to criticism from others, and feelings of personal inadequacy (Watson et al., 1994).

Self-concept Clarity and Trait Negative Affectivity in Clinical Personality Research

The overlap between trait negative affectivity and self-concept clarity also extends to personality pathology, particularly features of borderline personality disorder (BPD). Trait negative affectivity has been identified as the FFM domain with the strongest association with BPD (Morey & Zanarini, 2000). Relatedly, women with BPD exhibit significantly lower selfconcept clarity than healthy controls (Roepke et al., 2011). This difference is thought to largely reflect the diagnostic criterion of identity disturbance (defined as a markedly and persistently unstable self-image or sense of self)-itself a strong predictor of self-injurious and suicidal behavior (Yen et al., 2004). Higher self-concept clarity also appears to buffer against selfinjurious urges within this population, such that individuals higher in self-concept clarity report fewer urges when experiencing high negative affect (Scala et al., 2018). Furthermore, interventions targeting self-concept clarity have been accompanied by marked decreases in depressive symptoms and general psychopathology (Roepke et al., 2011).

The ambiguity surrounding where trait negative affectivity ends and identity-related constructs such as self-concept clarity begin is at the forefront of ongoing debates about the Alternative Model for Personality Disorders (AMPD) in DSM-5. The AMPD conceptualizes personality pathology using two complementary components. Criterion A assesses the severity of personality dysfunction, including disturbances in identity, self-direction, and interpersonal functioning (Skodol, 2012), making the coherence and clarity of one's self-concept a central aspect of personality functioning. Criterion B pertains to the presence of maladaptive personality traits. Although Criteria A and B were intended to capture distinct aspects of personality pathology (APA, 2013), accumulating evidence suggests that the distinction is less clear in practice, and debate continues over whether dysfunction in self and interpersonal functioning can be empirically distinguished from maladaptive traits, most notably trait negative affectivity (see Morey et al., 2022; Sleep & Lynam, 2022; Widiger et al., 2023).

Potential Explanations for Empirical Overlap

Taken together, the reviewed literature highlights considerable empirical overlap between measures of self-concept clarity and trait negative affectivity, despite clear conceptual distinctions between the constructs. This overlap is particularly surprising given the strong face validity of many measures of self-concept clarity that do not explicitly reference negative emotionality. Face validity refers to the extent to which a scale appears practical, pertinent, and related to its intended purpose (Mosier, 1947, p. 192). For example, SCCS items directly assess the clarity and coherence of one's self-concept without referencing negative emotionality (e.g., "I spend a lot of time wondering about what kind of person I really am," "My beliefs about myself seem to change very frequently," "In general, I have a clear sense of who I am and what I am"; Campbell et al., 1996). This stands in contrast to many clinically oriented measures of identity impairment, such as the Level of Personality Functioning Scale (LPFS; Morey, 2017), which contains many items likely to be saturated with trait negative affectivity (e.g., "My emotions rapidly shift around", "I can't even imagine living a life that I would find satisfying").

Thus, the substantial overlap between measures like the SCCS and measures of trait negative affectivity cannot be straightforwardly explained by shared item content. One possible explanation for this overlap is that difficulties maintaining a clear and coherent sense of self contribute to emotional distress; theorists have long noted that representations of the self and others are inherently intertwined with affective experience (e.g., Kernberg, 1984). Alternatively, chronic negative affectivity may undermine the stability and coherence of self-representations, such that persistent emotional distress contributes to difficulties maintaining a clear and consistent sense of identity. Consistent with this possibility, clinical theorists have proposed that persistent emotion dysregulation may disrupt a stable sense of self, particularly when emotional experiences are intense and rapidly shifting (Linehan & Kehrer, 1993). Thus, lower self-concept clarity may not simply represent a vulnerability contributing to emotional distress but may also emerge, in part, as a consequence of chronic negative affectivity.

A third possibility is that the overlap partly reflects shared evaluative variance across global self-report measures. Although low self-concept clarity is conceptually distinct from holding a negatively valanced self-concept (i.e., low self-esteem), such that individuals can theoretically maintain a markedly negative yet clear and consistent view of themselves, empirical research suggests that these constructs almost always covary (Campbell, 1990; Campbell et al., 1996). Measures of both self-concept clarity and trait negative affectivity may therefore reflect a general tendency to evaluate oneself in more favorable or unfavorable terms. Thus, some of the observed association between these constructs may reflect shared evaluative content rather than equivalence in their underlying psychological processes (e.g., Biderman et al., 2018; Kallio Strand et al., 2021).

In summary, although self-concept clarity and trait negative affectivity are conceptually distinct, there are good reasons to expect overlap among their measures. Because the two constructs are routinely used to predict the same outcomes (e.g., psychological well-being, mental health symptoms), it is important to establish how much of their predictive utility is shared versus unique. Designs that can disentangle the temporal relations between self-concept clarity and negative affectivity would be most informative, but cross-sectional self-report assessments are easy to administer, are the most popular way to assess both constructs, and will likely remain the most common assessment approach for the foreseeable future. Indeed, in a recent meta-analysis by Lodi- Smith and colleagues (2026) examining the overlap between selfesteem and self-concept clarity, over 90% of the included studies assessed self-concept clarity using cross-sectional self-report scales. It is therefore valuable to subject scales of self-concept clarity and trait negative affectivity to more rigorous tests of their unique predictive abilities, and examining them in relation to experiences assessed in daily life is a promising way to clarify when they capture meaningful individual differences. Researchers have already recommended controlling for self-esteem when studying self-concept clarity, given their strong empirical overlap (Lodi-Smith et al., 2026). The current study tests whether the same recommendation is worth extending to measures of trait negative affectivity.

Current Study

The primary aim of the present study was to comprehensively examine the extent to which two theoretically distinct constructs, self-concept clarity and trait negative affectivity, can be distinguished empirically. Despite their substantial empirical overlap and shared nomological networks across psychological and interpersonal domains, prior research has yet to clearly disentangle the two. In the current study, we tested the incremental validity of the SCCS relative to multiple operationalizations of trait negative affectivity across outcomes assessed both at baseline and in daily life. Baseline measures were depression symptoms and overall psychological distress, outcomes that both constructs are routinely used to predict. Outcomes from daily life comprised affective, behavioral, and interpersonal experiences, assessed over a 15-day EMA protocol. Examining outcomes that differ in both content and method of assessment allowed for a comprehensive evaluation of the two constructs' shared and distinct empirical relations.

Procedure

Participants were part of the Intensive Longitudinal Investigation of Alternative

Diagnostic Dimensions (ILIADD) study. Participants were considered eligible to participate if they owned a study-compatible Android or iPhone smartphone and were between 18 and 50 years old. Additionally, the sample was enriched for recent mental health treatment, with 63% reporting current or recent treatment. Participants completed baseline questionnaires prior to a 15-day ambulatory assessment portion of the study. All study procedures and consent were completed remotely, online. The 15-day ambulatory assessment period following the training involved both passive sensing and ecological momentary assessment (completed via the MetricWire smartphone application) to examine how transdiagnostic psychopathology symptoms relate to social, affective and contextual outcomes. EMA surveys included pseudo-random assessments (eight times per day) along with social interaction surveys. Participants were instructed to complete social interaction surveys after any relevant social interactions, which were defined as direct, real-time communication lasting at least five minutes between the participant and another person.

A total of N =552 individuals completed the baseline questionnaires and ambulatory assessment protocol. Of the total sample, 81% indicated a history of mental health treatment, with 63% indicating that they were currently in treatment (N=291) or had received mental health treatment in the past 12 months (N=55). To ensure reliable estimates of momentary-based outcomes, participants who provided fewer than 10 ambulatory assessment observations over the 15-day period were excluded from analyses. Thus, the final sample consisted of N=493 participants, the majority of whom were White (87%) and female (83%). The mean age of the sample used for analyses was 30.43 years (SD = 8.68).

Measures

Baseline Measures

Hierarchical Taxonomy of Psychopathology-Self-report Scale (HiTOP-SR).

The HiTOP-SR (Simms et al., 2022; https://www.3plab.org/hitop) is a 405-item self-report instrument that assesses psychopathology symptoms as represented in the HiTOP model (Kotov et al., 2021). The HiTOP-SR was designed to be broad but comprehensive in scope and includes subscale and factor scales that assess a wide variety of psychopathology symptoms and behaviors. Participants were asked to rate how much each item applied to them in the past month. Each item was rated using a one ("Not at all") to four ("A lot") Likert scale. Five broadbased factor scales (e.g., Distress, Emotionality), 85 specific symptom scales (e.g., Affective Lability, Depressed Mood), and 11 alcohol use scales (e.g., Craving, Hazardous Use) were computed using the HiTOP-SR items. Our focus was on the symptom/facet scales of Depressed Mood, Anxious Worry, Shame/Guilt, Affective Lability, and Irritability, which past structural research has demonstrated as most representative of trait negative affectivity (Mostajabi et al., 2026a).

Big Five Inventory-2 (BFI-2).

The BFI-2 (Soto & John, 2017) is a 60-item self-report measure of the Big Five personality domains. Each item was rated on a one ("Disagree strongly") to five ("Agree strongly") Likert scale, with participants being asked to rate their agreement with a series of statements about themselves (e.g., "I am someone who is moody, has up and down mood swings"). Items were averaged to create mean scales for each domain, as well as mean facet scales within each domain (three facet scales for each domain). Our analyses focused solely on the Negative Emotionality domain and its respective facets of Anxiety, Depression, and Emotional Volatility.

Self-concept Clarity Scale (SCCS).

The SCCS (Campbell et al., 1996) is a 12-item selfreport measure designed to assess self-concept clarity, which refers to the extent that self-beliefs are stable, clear and internally consistent. (sample item: "In general, I have a clear sense of who I am and what I am"). Participants were asked to rate their agreement with each item using a one ("Strongly Disagree") to five ("Strongly Agree") Likert scale. A total mean score was computed from the 12 SCCS items. To facilitate comparison with facet scales from the BFI-2 and HiTOP-SR, the SCCS was reverse coded, such that higher values on the SCCS represented lower selfconcept clarity.

Patient Health Questionnaire-8 (PHQ-8).

The PHQ-8 (Kroenke et al., 2009) is a brief self-report measure of depression symptoms and assesses common features of depression. The PHQ-8 was administered using eight Likert scale items ranging from zero ("Not at all") to three ("Nearly every day"). Participants were asked to rate how much they had been bothered by each symptom (e.g., "Feeling down, depressed, or hopeless") in the previous two weeks. A total score reflecting overall level of depression symptoms was computed by summing the eight items.

Kessler Psychological Distress Scale (K6).

The K6 (Kessler et al., 2003) is a brief selfreport measure of overall psychological distress. The K6 is comprised of six items, each rated using a zero ("None of the time") to four ("All of the time") Likert scale. Participants were asked to rate how often they had a particular feeling (e.g., nervous, hopeless, that everything was an effort) in the past 30 days. The total K6 score was used as an index of overall impairment, with past work demonstrating that total scores between 13-24 were indicative of a clinical degree of impairment (Kessler et al., 2003).

Momentary Measures

Momentary Affect and Stress. During each random prompt, participants rated their mood using four items. Positive affect was assessed with one item ("In the past 15 minutes, how POSITIVE have you felt?") using a zero ("Neutral") to ten ("Very Positive") sliding scale.

Participants also rated negative affect with one item ("In the past 15 minutes, how NEGATIVE have you felt?") using a zero ("Neutral") to ten ("Very Negative") sliding scale. In addition to rating positive and negative affect, participants rated their momentary stress ("In the past 15 minutes, how STRESSED have you felt?"). Stress was rated using a zero ("Not at all") to ten ("Extremely") sliding scale.

Impulsivity.

We assessed impulsivity as an outcome, as individuals with low self-concept clarity experience uncertainty and inconsistency in their self-views, which may undermine effective self-regulation (Finkenauer et al., 2005; Mostajabi et al., 2026b). Four items were used to assess momentary impulsivity. For each item, participants were asked whether they had engaged in the impulsive behavior in the past hour using a zero ("Neutral") to ten ("Very Much") sliding scale. Two items assessed rash action ("I did something on impulse"; "I did things without worrying about the consequences"), and two items assessed rash inaction ("I decided to put off something that I had to do"; "I avoided doing something despite the consequences").

Momentary Affect and Stress During Interpersonal Interactions.

Affect was assessed during interpersonal interactions with the same three items. Participants rated their own positive affect with the item "How POSITIVE were your emotions during the interaction?" with responses from zero ("Neutral") to ten ("Very positive"). Negative affect during the interaction was assessed with the item "How NEGATIVE were your emotions during the interaction?" with responses from zero ("Neutral") to ten ("Very negative"). Participants also rated how stressful they perceived the interaction ("How stressful was this interaction?").

Momentary Authenticity.

foot_0 Two items were used to assess authenticity during interpersonal interactions: "During the interaction, I was true to myself" and "During the interaction, I had a clear sense of who I was." Responses were provided on a sliding scale of 0 ("Not at all") to 10 ("Extremely"). The two items were highly correlated on both the betweenperson (r = .93, p <.001) and within-person (r = .67, p <.001) levels.

Analyses 2

Our primary analyses used multilevel structural equation models (MSEMs) to estimate the relations between baseline predictors (BFI-2 Negative Emotionality facet scales, HiTOP-SR facet scales, and the SCCS) and our selected outcome measures. MSEM allows the overall variability in individuals' responses to be divided into between-and within-person variance components like standard multilevel modeling approaches, but also provides additional benefits, including the latent decomposition of between-and within-person variance (Sadikaj et al., 2021). MSEM models were estimated in two steps. In the first step, each of our primary predictors were entered independently as predictors of all primary outcomes. In the second step, we tested MSEMs using BFI-2 Negative Emotionality facets and SCCS scale as simultaneous predictors of all primary outcomes, and MSEMs including HiTOP-SR Distress facets and the SCCS as simultaneous predictors of all primary outcomes. In total, the SCCS and trait negative affectivity scales were compared in relation to 14 outcomes. Two outcome variables were administered at baseline (PHQ-8 and K6), and twelve outcomes were derived from the EMA protocol. These twelve outcomes were average positive and negative affect (from random assessments and during interpersonal interactions), stress (from random assessments and during interpersonal interactions), rash action and rash inaction (from random assessments), momentary authenticity (during interpersonal interactions), and three random slopes. The random slopes were estimated by regressing momentary stress, momentary negative affect, and momentary positive affect on momentary authenticity, all assessed during interpersonal interactions. Random slopes allow individuals to differ in the strength of these within-person associations and allowed us to evaluate whether the strength of these within-person associations varied as a function of individual differences in our baseline predictors (i.e., a cross-level interaction). Using the standardized effects drawn from the MSEM results described above, we also examined indices of profile similarity between the SCCS and the facets from the BFI-2 and HiTOP-SR. We quantified profile similarity using double-entry correlations (r ICC ; Furr, 2010), which consider the shape (i.e., correlation), elevation (i.e., mean values), and scatter (i.e., variance) of the standardized regression coefficients and thus represent an index of global similarity. Bootstrapped 95% confidence intervals were also computed to evaluate whether r ICC values significantly differed from one another.

Discussion

The aim of the current study was to investigate the empirical distinguishability of the SCCS and facets of Negative Emotionality and trait negative affectivity derived from the Big Five Inventory-2 (BFI-2) and the Hierarchical Taxonomy of Psychopathology-Self-Report (HiTOP-SR), respectively. Despite their theoretical distinctions, previous research has demonstrated substantial negative correlations between trait negative affectivity and self-concept clarity (Campbell et al., 1996; Connor, 2020), with associations comparable in magnitude to those observed between self-concept clarity and self-esteem, a theoretically more proximal "sibling" construct (Lodi-Smith et al., 2026; Weber et al., 2023). However, researchers have yet to comprehensively examine the extent to which the SCCS accounts for unique variance beyond trait negative affectivity and its specific facets, particularly in predicting outcomes assessed in daily life.

To address this gap, we examined the empirical similarity of these measures in relation to well-validated clinical measures of depression and psychological distress assessed at baseline (PHQ-8 and K6), as well as relevant indices derived from a 15-day EMA protocol, including authenticity, impulsivity, affect, and stress. Reverse-coded self-concept clarity retained unique predictive validity beyond measures of trait negative affectivity. Specifically, reverse-coded SCCS scores remained uniquely and significantly associated with PHQ-8 and K6 scores, reflecting global depression and psychological distress, as well as momentary authenticity, rash inaction, and rash action, after accounting for trait negative affectivity operationalized using both the BFI-2 and HiTOP-SR. Reverse-coded self-concept clarity also retained unique associations with momentary stress and negative affect during interpersonal interactions, as well as general momentary stress, in models incorporating the BFI-2 operationalization of negative affectivity; however, these associations did not hold in the corresponding HiTOP-SR models. In contrast, the SCCS demonstrated more limited incremental validity for momentary positive and negative affect, both generally and within interpersonal contexts.

Taken together, these findings suggest that although the SCCS and measures of trait negative affectivity demonstrate considerable empirical overlap, particularly in relation to affective outcomes, they are not entirely redundant. Although the strong zero-order relations between the SCCS and our primary outcomes were partly explained by its overlap with trait negative affectivity scales, self-concept clarity appears to provide distinct predictive utility concerning momentary authenticity, impulsivity-related experiences, and global depression and psychological distress beyond trait negative affectivity. Implications for these findings are discussed below.

Evidence of SCCS' Incremental Validity

The SCCS demonstrated evidence of incremental validity for the outcomes of momentary authenticity, rash inaction and rash action, and the cross-level moderation of within-person effects involving momentary authenticity and positive affect, all of which have important theoretical ties to self-concept clarity. Remarkably, the SCCS demonstrated unique predictive abilities in relation to both the K6 and PHQ-8 operationalizations of global depression and distress, despite the inclusion of specific scales from the BFI-2 and HiTOP-SR explicitly tied to these outcomes (e.g., BFI-2-Depression). However, these incremental effects varied across operationalizations of trait negative affectivity. Specifically, after accounting for the HiTOP facets, the SCCS remained the sole significant predictor for momentary authenticity.

Momentary Authenticity

SCCS's unique association with momentary authenticity after accounting for trait negative affectivity provides useful insight into the nature of the overlap between these constructs. Specifically, one of the items used to assess momentary authenticity, "I had a clear sense of who I was," closely resembles an item from the SCCS, "In general, I have a clear sense of who I am and what I am." This overlap in item content provides an opportunity to examine trait-state convergence, as the association between trait self-concept clarity and momentary authenticity may reflect the extent to which individuals' stable self-concept clarity corresponds with their momentary experiences of self-understanding (Fleeson, 2001). This interpretation is consistent with previous research demonstrating correspondence between trait-level and daily experiences of self-concept clarity (Nezlek & Plesko, 2001). We extend these findings by clarifying the SCCS's unique association with momentary authenticity beyond trait negative affectivity suggests that this relationship is not entirely attributable to shared variance with general negative emotionality.

Additionally, the moderating role of SCCS in the association between momentary authenticity and positive affect offers further insight into its function as a psychological resource. Ellison and colleagues (2020) found that low self-concept clarity predicted impatience and state negative affect for some, but not all, individuals, supporting the conceptualization of self-concept clarity as a psychological resource that facilitates behavioral and emotional regulation (Campbell, 1996). Our findings help clarify this heterogeneity by suggesting that the psychological resource function of self-concept clarity may be specific to individuals with higher levels of self-concept clarity. Specifically, self-concept clarity moderated the within-person association between state authenticity and positive affect, such that higher self-concept clarity was associated with a stronger positive relationship between momentary authenticity and positive affect. This moderation did not extend to stronger decreases in negative affect when reporting higher momentary authenticity, suggesting that self-concept clarity may function as a resource that amplifies the positive affective benefits of authentic self-expression among individuals who possess higher levels of self-concept clarity.

Impulsivity

In addition, the SCCS showed incremental validity when predicting rash inaction after accounting for both the HiTOP-SR and BFI-2 facets. These findings support Kashyap and Lal's (2026) hypothesis that self-concept clarity may function as a psychological resource for impulse control and suggest that impulsive behavior is a function of both identity-related processes and trait negative affectivity. Indeed, SCCS's unique association with rash action remained significant after accounting for both operationalizations of trait negative affectivity. In the HiTOP-SR model, however, Affective Lability was also uniquely associated with rash action, and its association was comparable in magnitude to that of the SCCS. This suggests that, when trait negative affectivity is assessed more comprehensively as opposed to a general domain score, individual facets such as Affective Lability may account for as much of the association with rash action as self-concept clarity does. Thus, the incremental validity observed for the SCCS in the BFI-2 models compared to the HiTOP-SR models may partly reflect differences in breadth across the two operationalizations and suggests that different measures may produce different results regarding incremental utility of self-concept clarity relative to trait negative affectivity.

For example, NEO-based instruments of neuroticism include facet scales of Self-consciousness and Vulnerability (Costa & McCrae, 1995), which do not have direct analogues in the facets we investigated here, and may provide further insight into areas of overlap between self-concept clarity and trait negative affectivity.

Moreover, the differing patterns across rash action and rash inaction are consistent with the view that impulsivity is a multifaceted construct (e.g., Carver & Johnson, 2018) and with recent work suggesting that it may be useful to distinguish between active and inactive components (Mostajabi et al., 2026b, preprint). The present findings extend this literature by suggesting that rash inaction may be more closely related to identity functioning, whereas rash action may be more closely associated with trait negative affectivity, particularly affective lability. Future research should examine the differential associations of rash action and rash inaction with identity constructs and trait negative affectivity to further clarify the distinct nomological networks of these impulsivity components.

Global Depression and Distress

The retention of unique predictive utility for self-concept clarity across two operationalizations of depressive symptoms and psychological distress (the PHQ-8 and K6), even after accounting for facets of trait negative affectivity, is particularly noteworthy given the well-established associations between negative affectivity and depressive symptoms (e.g., Kotov et al., 2010 , Lakhtakia et al, 2024), as well as the inherently distressing nature of negative emotional experiences. These findings suggest that the coherence and stability of one's selfconcept account for unique variance in depression and psychological distress beyond individual differences in the tendency to experience negative affect. Thus, although self-concept clarity and trait negative affectivity are empirically related, their associations with depressive symptoms and psychological distress are not entirely reducible to the same underlying construct.

Overlap and the Perils of Partialing

The limited incremental validity of SCCS beyond trait negative affectivity in predicting momentary outcomes of positive and negative affect, and the shared variation found across all outcomes points to important empirical overlap between these two constructs that should be considered in future research contexts. Specifically, it is important for researchers attempting to capture identity disturbance using the SCCS to account for its overlap with trait negative affectivity and take deliberate steps to parse variance distinct to identity disturbance from that attributable to broader negative affectivity. Indeed, in their recent meta-analysis, Lodi-Smith and colleagues (2026) found a correlation of r = .65 between self-esteem and self-concept clarity and in turn argued that it was critical to assess both constructs in studies on either self-esteem or selfconcept clarity to provide a more precise characterization of each. Our findings dovetail nicely with their recommendations and are worth emphasizing given the present findings-the pattern of correlations between the SCCS and most trait negative affectivity scales are on par with the correlation between self-esteem and self-concept clarity.

Importantly, this does not mean researchers can make clear, unambiguous inferences after controlling for trait negative affectivity in analyses of self-concept clarity. As cautioned by Lynam and colleagues (2006; see also Hoyle et al., 2023), researchers must consider what remains of a construct after controlling for highly correlated neighboring constructs. For example, if aspects of trait negative affectivity contribute to lower self-concept clarity, then statistically controlling for trait negative affectivity in an effort to self-concept clarity would lead to meaningful components of self-concept clarity being partialed out. Alternatively, if they are not constitutive of one another (e.g., they covary because of a general negative evaluative tendency), controlling for trait negative affectivity may be useful for determining whether associations attributed to self-concept clarity are better explained by a broader tendency to experience negative emotions, and vice versa. Given the nuanced pattern of our findings, it is likely that these constructs are partly, but not fully, constitutive of one another. Therefore, researchers will benefit from using multiple analytic approaches, including zero-order associations alongside unique associations to appropriately contextualize findings and ensure that associations attributed to self-concept clarity are interpreted alongside the substantial variance it shares with trait negative affectivity (and vice versa).

Limitations and Future Directions

Results should be interpreted with several limitations in mind. First, the present sample is primarily White (87%) and majority female (83%) which affects the generalizability of our findings. Given that women have been found to exhibit lower levels of self-concept clarity and that self-concept clarity has been found to differ across Western and Eastern cultures (Campbell et al., 1996), research is needed to investigate whether our findings generalize across other populations and sociocultural contexts.

Second, although the SCCS developed by Campbell and colleagues (1996) is a widely used and well-validated measure of self-concept clarity, the use of alternative measures of this construct may have yielded different results (e.g., Burger & Guadagno, 2003; Hamid & Cheng, 1995; Slotter et al., 2010). Additionally, the SCCS captures a relatively narrow aspect of identity functioning. Measures that assess other dimensions of self-belief, such as identity disturbance (Self-Concept and Identity Measure; SCIM; Kaufman et al., 2015), self-esteem (Rosenberg, 1965), or self-efficacy (Chen et al., 2001), may demonstrate different patterns of predictive utility across the outcomes examined. Given longstanding concerns about construct proliferation in the self-belief literature (Marsh et al., 2019), future research should investigate the empirical distinctiveness of trait negative affectivity from a broader range of measures of identity functioning and self-beliefs.

Additionally, both self-concept clarity and affective instability (i.e., the HiTOP affective lability facet) were conceptualized and assessed at the trait level. Given theoretical perspectives suggesting that impairments in the unity and coherence of the self-concept may contribute to affective instability and negative affect (Horowitz, 1977; Kernberg, 1975; Stein, 1995), it is plausible that the SCCS's limited incremental predictive utility for affective outcomes beyond the affective lability facet reflects, in part, the difficulty of disentangling their potentially interrelated processes using exclusively trait-level measures. If difficulties in maintaining a coherent selfconcept contribute to affective instability, these constructs may be difficult to distinguish when assessed as relatively stable individual differences. Future research should therefore investigate how self-concept clarity and affective instability relate to one another and to affective outcomes using measures collected over shorter timescales. Such approaches may help clarify the temporal dynamics linking difficulties in maintaining a coherent self-concept with fluctuations in affective experience.

Although the present study included adults ranging from 18 to 50 years of age, thereby extending prior self-concept clarity research beyond the adolescent and young adult samples typical in this line of work, this broader age range may have also weakened some associations.

Identity development is particularly salient during adolescence and young adulthood, when individuals experience substantial identity exploration and life transitions. Consequently, selfconcept clarity may play a more prominent role in daily emotional and identity functioning during this developmental period than in adulthood. Future research should therefore examine the incremental validity of self-concept clarity beyond broader measures of negative affectivity within adolescent and young adult samples to determine whether the present pattern of findings generalizes to populations in which identity development is most active.

Conclusion

Overall, the SCCS and trait negative affectivity demonstrated unique associations with momentary authenticity, impulsivity, and global measures of depression and psychological distress, above and beyond their shared variance; however, the SCCS's associations with momentary affective experiences were more limited, with much of its relation to these outcomes attributable to its shared variance with trait negative affectivity. These findings provide evidence that, despite their considerable overlap, the two constructs retain distinct associations with several important aspects of psychological functioning. Given these nuanced findings, researchers using the SCCS to investigate identity-related functioning and psychological wellbeing should consider its overlap with trait negative affectivity and take deliberate steps to distinguish variance uniquely attributable to self-concept clarity from that shared with broader negative emotionality. This work provides a valuable step towards further clarifying the relationship between self-concept clarity and trait negative affectivity.
