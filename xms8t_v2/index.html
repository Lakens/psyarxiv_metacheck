The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. Introduction

Among the many cognitive developments which occur across childhood, vocabulary growth is perhaps the most visible. By age five a child might know ~5,000 words (Segbers & Schroeder, 2017), suggesting a learning rate of 2-3 new words per day. What is less obvious, but no less striking, is the fact that children must also develop the ability to recognize these words in real-time. This is not trivial for several reasons. Words are temporarily ambiguous-the sound /kæ/ at the onset of cat is also consistent with cap, catch, cattle, and more-meaning listeners must cope with insu icient information until more input arrives. A consequence of this is competition; when a listener has only heard the initial portion of a word there are many candidates available for interpretation. Moreover, sentences unfold on the order of 2-3 words per second, placing enormous demands on the system to deal with this ambiguity e iciently.

A wealth of research has asked how real-time lexical access develops through childhood and adolescence (Apfelbaum et al., 2023; Fernald et al., 2006; Fernald et al., 1998; Kutlu et al., 2024; Rigler et al., 2015; Sekerina & Brooks, 2007) and beyond (Colby & McMurray, 2023). This is usually framed relative to adult models of word recognition (Marslen-Wilson, 1987; McClelland & Elman, 1986; Norris, 1994). These posit that when hearing speech, listeners activate multiple candidate words in parallel depending on their match to the input at that moment (/kæ/ activates cat, cap, catch, etc.). This set is subsequently winnowed down as additional information arrives, until only one word remains (Allopenna et al., 1998; Marslen-Wilson, 1987; Marslen-Wilson & Zwitserlood, 1989).

Developmental studies find that lexical competition in children di ers quantitatively from adults-they are less e icient in activating candidates and do not suppress competitors as quickly. However, there may also be a larger qualitative di erence. Many models of spoken word recognition argue that lexical candidates actively inhibit one another during spoken word recognition (Luce et al., 2000; McClelland & Elman, 1986; Norris, 1994). That is, highly active words inhibit less-active ones. This is commonly referred to as lateral inhibition (as it's posited to be the result of direct, word-to-word inhibitory links, in contrast to domain-general cognitive control).

Work on the development of lateral inhibition has found far weaker e ects in early childhood, leading to claims that lateral inhibition is not robustly present in children (Gür et al., 2025) or that children engage lateral inhibition inconsistently (Blomquist & McMurray, 2023) (though see Paquette-Smith et al., 2016). These results suggest children have an immature system and that lateral inhibition may have a protracted developmental trajectory (c.f., McMurray et al., 2022).

In contrast, as we argue here, it is possible that the typical paradigm used to index lateral inhibition is fairly insensitive when word recognition is not yet robustly developed (e.g., in younger children). Here, we introduce a more powerful variant of this task which may be able to detect inhibition in young children and thus is better at characterizing the nature of developmental change. We begin by outlining the developmental trajectory of word recognition and lateral inhibition before describing this new paradigm.

The Developmental Trajectory of Word Recognition

A hallmark of lexical access is that as a word unfolds, multiple items are considered in parallel. Evidence for this in adults comes from a variety of tasks, including gating and cross-modal priming (Grosjean, 1980; Marslen-Wilson & Zwitserlood, 1989), though most recent work has used the Visual World Paradigm (VWP; Cooper, 1974; Tanenhaus et al., 1995). In this paradigm, listeners' eyes are tracked as they locate an image of a target word (cat) from an array of images representing both the target and phonologically related words (e.g., cap, hat). Listeners must find the target to complete the task; consequently, they direct eye-movements to items they are considering. Classic studies suggest that listeners fixate both the target and its phonological competitors before narrowing their fixations to just the correct object as the input arrives (Allopenna et al., 1998; Dahan, Magnuson, & Tanenhaus, 2001; Magnuson et al., 2003).

Work on the development of lexical access has leveraged the VWP to ask how realtime lexical access develops in children. This has found that as children age, the rate at which target words are activated increases. The earliest work demonstrating development in activation rate comes from Fernald et al. (1998), who used a simplified eye-tracking paradigm to demonstrate that 15-month-old infants were significantly slower and less e icient at fixating a target than 18-month-olds, who had slower activation rates relative to 24-month-olds. This suggests a rapid developmental trajectory.

Further research with older children has used a more sophisticated version of the task that can simultaneously index the speed of activating the target and of suppressing competitors. This demonstrates that while young children exhibit rudimentary lexical access skills, this ability continues to refine over development. For instance, 5-and 6-yearold children recognize words at a slower pace relative to adults (Sekerina & Brooks, 2007) and adolescents show more e icient target activation than younger children (Apfelbaum et al., 2023; Rigler et al., 2015). In these studies, changes in target activation are typically accompanied by developmental di erences in the speed of suppressing competitors. They suggest that the development of lexical access is protracted. Though it is present at a young age, it takes decades to become fully operational (perhaps not reaching peak ability until early adulthood; Colby & McMurray, 2023).

Models of word recognition posit that the suppression of candidates is in part dictated by a system of inter-lexical inhibition, in which items directly inhibit one another (Dijkstra & Van Heuven, 2013; Hannagan et al., 2013; Luce et al., 2000; McClelland & Elman, 1986; Norris, 1994; Norris et al., 2000; Shook & Marian, 2013). At any given moment in processing, words that are most active (e.g., because they have a stronger bottom-up match to the input or are more frequent) inhibit other items more than the reverse. This drives the system towards the most likely interpretation when multiple candidates are considered.

Given evidence for the protracted development of competition resolution, it stands to reason that lateral inhibition may also be developing across these ages. Importantly, there is evidence that beyond its role in lexical access, lateral inhibition may play a broader role in language development. For instance, it's been argued that novel words are fully integrated into the lexicon as learned units when they begin to produce inhibitory e ects (Gaskell & Dumay, 2003; Weighall et al., 2017) and weak or absent lateral inhibition has been implicated as a key locus of di erence in Developmental Language Disorder (DLD; McMurray et al., 2019). Thus, assessing the presence and development of lateral inhibition may have far more consequential implications that simply describing the developmental trajectory of word recognition.

Lateral Inhibition and its Development

Empirical evidence for inhibition in adults largely derives from two paradigms. First, research with lexical decision times has found that listeners are slower to recognize words with larger lexical neighborhoods (words which di er in a single phonemic deletion, addition, or substitution from many other words) than those with smaller neighborhoods (Luce et al., 2000; Luce & Pisoni, 1998). For instance, the words cat and leaf are each disambiguated from other words in the lexicon at their final sound; before this, they are consistent with multiple candidates. However, while cat is similar to a substantial number of other words (at, chat, cot, catch, cap, etc.) leaf is not (e.g., leap, beef). Listeners are slower to recognize cat compared to leaf, implying an inhibitory process-the more candidates that are active, the more inhibition acts on the target word, slowing word recognition.

A more direct demonstration of lexical inhibition has been shown with the subphonemic mismatch paradigm (Dahan, Magnuson, Tanenhaus, et al., 2001; Marslen-Wilson & Warren, 1994). In this task, the onset of a target word (net) is replaced with the onset of another candidate (neck). The resulting stimulus (neckt) matches the form of the target but contains subtle coarticulatory cues to a competitor. These cues boost competitor activation at early moments in processing, which in turn inhibits the target. When the final disambiguating phoneme (in this case, the /t/) arrives, listeners must then recover from this inhibition, in which case, processing should be slowed.

In modern versions of this paradigm, the timecourse of activation of the target word (net) is then monitored using a VWP task. Critically, only the target word is ever depicted on screen-eye-movements are delayed even in the absence of a reasonable alternative. Typically, listeners are slower to fixate an image of the target word (and often fixate that word to a lower degree) when they hear stimuli in which two words are cross-spliced (e.g., neckt which we refer to as a word-splice condition; Figure 1). This is not driven by acoustic deviation introduced via the splicing procedure itself; listeners are slower when hearing neckt (where the spliced onset neck is a known word) relative to when hearing nept (where the spliced onset nep is a nonword). This is particularly important, as it suggests that slower fixations in the word-splice condition are due to direct inhibition from the competitor word on the target, as opposed to a kind of template mismatch between the heard target word and internal representations caused by splicing.

Sub-phonemic mismatch thus indexes how lateral inhibition a ects real-time lexical access, and unlike paradigms which focus on the number of competitors of a word, it manipulates competition within-item. That is, it modulates the degree of competition for the same target word. This sensitivity has made it the paradigm of choice for research examining lateral inhibition's development.

Developmental work with sub-phonemic mismatch suggests that the development of lateral inhibition has a protracted trajectory. For instance, Blomquist and McMurray (2023) tested younger (7-8 year-olds) and older (12-13 year-olds) children and found that both groups showed a significant delay/reduction in fixations overall in the word-splice condition relative to the nonword-splice. However, this was inconsistent and seemed to be partially a product of task demands or learning. An analysis of the first half of the trials (when task or learning e ects are likely minimal) found no evidence for inhibition in the younger children, but they did show evidence in the second half. This suggests that younger children have immature inhibition, potentially only able to apply it when environmental demands to di erentiate words are high (as in the sub-phonemic mismatch task) and only then after some degree of learning has occurred (or as the result of exposure to di icult stimuli increasing inhibition, c.f. As stated, younger children tend to be slower to build activation for items than older children and adults, and they are less sensitive to fine-grained phonetic di erences (e.g., coarticulation in the stimuli; Kutlu et al., 2026; McMurray et al., 2018). Together, these results imply that lateral inhibition emerges slowly over childhood, potentially only reaching maturity in adolescence. This might explain the quantitative di erences observed in word recognition across these ages-both activation rate and competitor resolution can be theoretically linked to a strengthening of inhibition over time, as initially weak inhibition in children allows alternative candidates to remain active for longer, leading to slower recognition and less robust competition resolution. Simulations with a computational model support the idea that lateral inhibition may underlie some aspects of the increases in e iciency of spoken word recognition observed across childhood (Blomquist & McMurray, 2023).

However, before concluding that inhibition is not present in younger children, it is important to consider another limitation of sub-phonemic mismatch. This paradigm relies on a very small window of time in which to see an e ect-listeners have only a brief moment during which the spliced onset contains coarticulatory properties that favor a competitor (e.g., neckt), before the arrival of the disambiguating phoneme, /t/. In this short window, the competitor must become more active than the target and inhibition must spread from one to the other. In children, word recognition may simply be too slow to demonstrate a strong e ect. Although children are clearly sensitive to the activation of a competitor via sub-phonemic manipulations (Gür et al., 2025), it is not clear if this is fast enough to drive inhibition in the short period before the disambiguating phoneme. This may explain why the e ects of lateral inhibition are hard to observe or appear inconsistent in young children. This raises key questions: while most of the prior work has focused on whether the strength of inhibition develops, it is also possible that the timecourse of inhibition develops as well.

The Present Study

The ideal paradigm should use a within-item manipulation, as in in sub-phonemic mismatch, but one that o ers more bottom-up support for a competitor and more time for its activation to build. Thus, we introduce a variant of the sub-phonemic mismatch paradigm where instead of manipulating only the first two phonemes of a monosyllabic word we manipulate the first portion of a multi-syllabic word. This creates a larger time window over which competitors can become active and inhibit the target.

For instance, if we start from the target word battleship, we can manipulate the first syllable so that it is ambiguous with the onset of another word (bottlecap) to create a stimulus approaching bottle-ship.foot_0 This functions similarly to the word-splice condition in the sub-phonemic mismatch paradigm (boosting activation for a competitor, bottlecap), but it has an extended time frame for which competitors (bottle; bottlecap) may be active. This may provide more time for the competitor to inhibit the target word (battleship) making it possible to see delayed or reduced fixations to its referent. This can be compared to a control stimulus where a target word remains intact (battleship) and a nonword-splice condition where the target is manipulated to resemble a nonword (bettleship). Critically, fixation delays in this condition should not be of similar magnitude as the word splice, as a nonword does not activate a competitor which could inhibit the (eventual) target.

This o ers three critical contrasts. The di erence between the nonword-splice and the word-splice conditions reflects the degree to which children are delayed by the presence of any phonetic mismatch. The di erence between the word-splice and the control condition necessarily reflects the contribution of this mismatch along with the additional e ects of inhibition. The di erence between the two splice conditions can then be used to isolate the e ect of inhibition.

We tested children across grades 2-5 (ages 7-11) in this paradigm, assessing the presence of inhibition and its development across ages. We show robust inhibition across ages. However, a close characterization of the timecourse of the e ect suggests a surprising locus for development in the duration of the inhibitory e ect, not its magnitude. Next, as an additional exploratory analysis we asked whether children with lower overall language skills in this study demonstrate inhibition. As stated, a lack of inhibition has been linked to DLD (McMurray et al., 2019), but the inability to robustly test the presence of inhibition in young children has limited ongoing research. As a proof-of-concept, we demonstrate here that inhibition can be detected in young children with low language ability, suggesting that this task may be sensitive to di erences in this population and thus a plausible approach to studying the link between inhibition and DLD in future work. Finally, in Experiment 2 we test adults to fully characterize the developmental timecourse identified in Experient 1.

Methods Participants

Two-hundred and forty-seven children participated in this experiment. These were recruited across grades 2-5 (age range: 7.5 to 12.1; mean = 9.91) as a part of a long-term longitudinal study in which data was collected on a variety of language tasks and measures. Recruiting and testing was conducted in accord with IRB-approved procedures.

Participation was constrained such that children: (1) had normal hearing (verified by an audiogram at study onset); (2) had normal or normal-corrected vision; (3) were monolingual speakers of English; and (4) did not have major intellectual disabilities, though we did not exclude for developmental language disorder or for dyslexia, as these were populations of interest in the larger project. The last three criteria were verified by the child's parent.

As part of the larger longitudinal project, children returned every year for repeated testing. A small number of these children completed this task twice (two years apart). However, this was only about a third of the total sample. Thus, we took a cross-sectional approach, using the first available year from this longitudinal sample.

Prior to analysis, we dropped five participants who chose the wrong referent on 5% of control trials. This high threshold for accuracy was motivated by the fact that the three foils for the target were completely unrelated, and the target word was intact, making the task relatively easy. We similarly removed three participants who made few fixations to the target referent (<50% of looks across correct trials made to the target), as this is likely a task-specific strategy (e.g., briefly looking at the target image before fixating the center of the screen again to prepare for the next trial) as opposed to a reflection of lexical processing. An additional three participants were removed for having unusable or missing data.

With these criteria established, the resulting sample of participants used for our initial analyses (including the first visit from the longitudinal group) was 236 (67 tested in grade 2, 39 in grade 3, 72 in grade 4, and 58 in grade 5).

Demographic information collected from parents reported 115 female children, 118 male, and 3 non-binary. Two-hundred and ten children were reported as white, 6 as Asian, 10 as Black or African American, 1 as Native Hawaiian or Pacific Islander, and 9 as none of the above. Fifteen were reported as being Hispanic or Latinx.

Task Design

This task broadly followed the design of the sub-phonemic mismatch paradigm. In these paradigms, participants are tasked with finding and selecting a referent of a target word (battleship). Stimuli were manipulated to construct three conditions: a control (battleship), nonword-splice (bettleship) and word-splice (bottle-ship) condition.

We began with identifying a set of 18 multisyllabic word-pairs, each of which consisted of two words whose first syllable di ered by a single vowel but which could be disambiguated by substantial material in the later syllable(s). For example, the first portion of battleship and bottlecap are minimally di erent, but -cap and -ship allows for easy identification of the whole word. For each pair we also identified another onset syllable (e.g., bettle-) that di ered from that of the two word-pairs by only the vowel, but for which if it were appended to the codas of either word would make a nonword (see Table 1 for the complete list of sets).

For the visual display on each trial, each target item (e.g., battleship) was paired with three words from this list that were phonetically and semantically unrelated to the target and one another (e.g., magazine, sundae, and checkers), creating a word set (see Appendix A for full list).

Each item in a set served as a target word in all three conditions. For example, when battleship was the target (with potential forms of bottleship and bettleship as the word-and nonword-splice conditions), the other pictures might be magazine, sundae and checkers. However, on other trials with this set, magazine could be the target (with megazine and magazine as the word-and nonword-splice conditions). Note that in constructing these sets all four items selected for a set were phonetically unrelated, and the cross-spliced word never appeared on the screen with the target (e.g., bottlecap was not on the screen alongside battleship).

To maximize the number of available items, the two cross-spliced words (battleship and bottlecap) reversed roles in independent sets. For instance, a pair like manatee and monitor were used to construct six stimuli: two controls (manatee, monitor), two wordsplices where the onsets of the pair were swapped (monatee, manitor), and two nonwordsplices where a common nonword was given at onset (menatee, menitor). Auditory Stimuli. Audio stimuli were constructed from natural recordings through a series of controlled steps. First, words and nonwords to be used in the splicing procedure (battleship, bettleship, and bottleship) were recorded by a male speaker of English. The best exemplar of each word was chosen and subsequently cleaned using Praat (Boersma, 2023) to remove any clicks or other idiosyncratic elements.

Simply splicing the onsets of one word to another to create our conditions would not be appropriate, as this would likely result in unnatural sounding stimuli with a complete phonemic mismatch (e.g., a mispronunciation). The original sub-phonemic mismatch paradigm avoids this issue by only manipulating coarticulatory cues in the vowel (a subphonemic change) without ever introducing a phonemic di erence that would indicate the competing word (e.g., the onset ne-from neck is used to create a word-splice neckt but listeners never hear the actual /k/, only coarticulatory cues that predict it). We sought to mimic this approach (though with words that had considerably longer periods of ambiguity) to create stimuli that provided strong (but only partial) cues towards this competitor.

To achieve this, we created continua between the control target word (battleship) and its word/nonword splices (battleship to bottleship; and battleship to bettleship) using Tandem STRAIGHT (Kawahara et al., 2008). We then used an intermediate point (based on listeners' judgements) between battle-and bottle-or between battle-and bettle-as the basis of the final stimulus. This meant that the vowel would be biased more to the competitor (or nonword) than to the target word, even as it did not sound like an obvious mispronunciation.

Tandem STRAIGHT uses spectral information to interpolate between two endpoints, creating a continuum from one to the other. However, STRAIGHT is best suited for interpolating between sonorant segments (i.e. vowels, liquids, nasals). Thus, tokens were trimmed so that only the target vowel and surrounding sonorant sounds were included. We refer to this spliced section as the base of the word, and the surrounding context as the remainder. The splicing locations within a word were chosen to include the maximum number of segments surrounding the target vowel while only including sonorants. For example, the base was /-æ-/ for battleship, /ælə-/ for alligator, and /maʊ-/ for mousetrap. Nine-step continua were then generated between the base of a target word and the base of its word-splice competitor (e.g., the base of battleship to the base of bottleship), and between the base of a target and its nonword-splice competitor (base of battleship to base of bettleship).

Once these initial continua were created, the amplitude of each step was scaled to the maximum amplitude of the relevant remainder (the part(s) of a word around the base) using a MATLAB script. If necessary, steps were cleaned to remove acoustic clicks obtained from the continua-generation procedure. Steps were then concatenated to their respective remainders using Praat. This resulted in a final continuum for each target-word and target-nonword pair.

We next generated the auditory tokens used for each condition in the experiment. The originally recorded stimulus (after cleaning) was used as the stimulus for control trials. For the word-splice and nonword-splice stimuli, a token from each relevant continuum was chosen via a pilot study. Members of the lab heard each token in the presence of its written referent and rated how well the auditory stimulus matched the written form. For instance, listeners heard a continuum from battleship to bottleship and rated each step in terms of how well it represented the word battleship (they did so again with a continuum from battleship to bettleship, rating how alike each step was to the written word battleship).

Steps were chosen depending on how evocative they were of the intended word, while maintaining ambiguity (this was step five for practically all continua). The result was wordsplice and nonword-splice stimuli that both sounded natural and provided cues toward the spliced candidate, maintaining the spirit of sub-phonemic mismatch. Stimuli are available on the OSF page for this project.

Assessing Acoustic Di erences Across Conditions.

Our experiment depends on listeners demonstrating di erent behavior across various splicing conditions; in particular, that eye-movements are delayed and/or reduced in word-splices relative to nonwordsplices. However, a similar e ect might occur if the word-splice is simply more acoustically distinct from the expected token than a nonword-splice. For instance, the control word "cricket" was modified with the onset of both the competing word "cracker" (in the wordsplice case) and the nonword "crecker" (in the nonword-splice case) on separate trials. The vowel in "cricket" is more similar to the vowel in "crecker" than in "cracker", however, potentially leading to delayed recognition of the word-splice token regardless of its lexical identity. If word-splice stimuli are systematically dissimilar to the control in this way, this might explain potential results without invoking inhibition.

To test this, we extracted five auditory cues to vowel identity from the onset of each auditory stimulus used in the experiment. We first identified the initial vowel. We then selected the center of this vowel (with minimal transition to or from neighboring phonemes) and extracted estimates of fundamental frequency and the first three formants at that time-point. Finally, we compared control stimuli with their word-and nonword-splice counterparts across these four dimensions using Euclidian distance. Estimates of acoustic cues were log-scaled prior to calculating distance, as formant estimates can di er in an order of magnitude (fundamental frequency is in the range of 140-200 Hz, while the third formant is typically over 3000).

Average log-scaled Euclidian distance was almost identical in word-splices (0.086) and nonword-splices (0.087), suggesting that words were not more acoustically dissimilar to the control overall. A paired t-test comparing Euclidian distance across splice conditions found no significant di erence (t(35) = 0.11, p = 0.911), arguing that one was not systematically more dissimilar to the target than the other. This supports the interpretation that any potential e ects observed in the eye-track record are the result of inhibition.

Visual Stimuli. Visual stimuli were roughly equal in style and salience. Images came from a database of referents, each entry of which followed the same criteria of inclusion. First, a series of potential referents were downloaded from commercial clipart libraries. Then, a focus group of lab members selected the best exemplar of each and o ered guidance on edits to be made to the image. This process repeated as many times as necessary until a prototypical referent was agreed upon, at which point the image was approved by a senior member of the lab (often the principal investigator).

Procedure

Eye-movement data was collected using an SR Research Eyelink 1000 desktopmounted eye-tracker. Participants were placed in a chin-mounted head rest to ensure as little movement as possible. Prior to the initiation of experimental trials, a 9-point calibration procedure was completed. Every 54 trials a drift correct procedure was performed. Failure to pass a drift correct led to recalibration, with changes to the head rest if needed.

Trials were presented at 1280 by 1024 resolution on an LCD monitor. Images were scaled to be 300  300 pixels when presented on screen. Audio was played over headphones, powered by an M-Audio AIR 192|4 USB sound card. Prior to the onset of experimental trials, listeners performed a familiarization task where they saw each visual stimulus in the study alongside its auditory label. This ensured that children were aware of what each referent represented. Auditory stimuli provided in this phase came from the control condition and so contained no manipulated acoustic variance.

Analytic Approach

Eye-tracking data was preprocessed using EyelinkAnalysis (McMurray, 2023) a custom Microsoft Access application which operates using events (blinks, saccades) in the fixation record. EyelinkAnalysis first collates a saccade with a subsequent fixation to form a 'look'-as listeners cannot redirect a saccade after it has been launched, and do not move their eyes again until a fixation ends. For the purpose of analyses, image boundaries were extended by 150 pixels on each side so that a look to the area around an image counted as a look to that image. This did not cause overlap between the original image locations.

Next, the proportion of eye-movements made to each item on screen was calculated on the basis of these looks. This was averaged across each trial in a condition for that subject, sampling every 4 msec. These were then analyzed with a nonlinear curve fitting technique. Looks to targets typically follow a logistic function, which can be described with four parameters: a baseline Minimum value (reflecting the degree of fixations made prior to recognizing a word), a ceiling Maximum value (reflecting fixations made to an image as it is selected to end the trial), the Slope of the function (reflecting how quickly fixations are made to the image across time), and a Crossover point (reflecting the point in time where the function reaches half of its maximum value; Figure 2). This function was fit to these curves using custom MATLAB software, which utilizes a constrained gradient descent method to minimize the root mean squared error between a logistic function and raw fixation data (McMurray, 2024). This resulted in a set of parameter values for each participant in each condition, which were used in subsequent analyses.

The Minimum value o ered little in theoretical importance, as it reflects fixations to the target prior to hearing the word. Since Slope and Crossover both index speed of activation, we combined these parameters into a single Activation Rate measure (following procedures outlined by Kutlu et al., 2024). This was calculated by first log-scaling Slope values, then z-scoringfoot_1 both the transformed Slope and Crossover values; we then multiplied Crossover by -1 (to rescale its direction, as a higher crossover reflects slower recognition, but a higher slope reflects faster recognition); and finally, we averaged the parameters. We also analyzed the target Maximum, as an estimate of the degree to which the target is ultimately active (or if its asymptote was disrupted by one of the splice conditions). These were first transformed using the empirical logit function, as they are constrained between 0 and 1. Since the empirical logit function is undefined when raw values are exactly 0 or 1, a correction was applied, defined by adding half of a trial to the numerator and denominator of the function. Our cross-sectional analysis analyzed these estimated parameters with a mixede ects model (Bates et al., 2015). Each index was predicted by grade as a continuous variable (centered) and its interactions with two contrasts for condition. The first compared the control condition against the nonword and word-splices (contrast coded with the control as +2/3 and each splice condition as -1/3) while the second compared the nonword and word-splices to one another (nonword-and word-splice coded as +1 and -1 respectively, with the control coded as 0). As condition was within-subject, we included a random intercept of participant and a random slope of both condition contrasts. No covariance terms were included, as the condition terms were derived from the same factor.

Next, we conducted an additional analysis examining the relationship among overall rate of activation, and early and late components of competition. This was intended to help understand how inhibition propagates through the system over time and how this might change developmentally.

Finally, we followed this with exploratory analyses asking whether inhibition is present in children with low language skills. This leveraged the larger longitudinal study, with measures of language including sub-measures from the Woodcock Johnson Test of Oral Language (IV) (vocabulary; Schrank & Wendling, 2018), the Clinical Evaluations of Language Fundamentals (following directions and repeating sentences; Semel et al., 2003), and the Comprehensive Test of Phonological Processing (nonword repetition; Wagner et al., 1999). As all of these measures yield standard scores (mean of 100, SD=15) and were robustly correlated, we created a composite by averaging them together. We chose a cuto of 90 to represent low language ability. This was done based on our prior results with sub-phonemic mismatch showing that the most robust di erences in the degree of inhibition were observed at the low end of the scale (McMurray et al., 2019). We then ran an additional mixed-e ects model including a categorical factor of language ability (with high language as a comparison group) interacting with the two contrasts for condition. If we saw an interaction, this would suggest that children in the low language group demonstrate di erences between conditions that are not observed in the higher language group. If there was no interaction, this would suggest the e ect across language groups does not di er, suggesting inhibition is operative in children at risk for DLD.

Results

We first examined overall performance in the task. Accuracy was quite high, with listeners correctly identifying the target referent on 99.06% of trials across conditions (SD = 0.10%). The grand average for each condition across age demonstrates the typical pattern of looks to the target over time, with a smooth sigmoidal curve building over time from low to maximal proportion of fixations (Figure 3A). Importantly, there was a clear e ect of condition, with the nonword-splice displaying slowed processing relative to the control stimulus and the word-splice slower than both. This accords with the expectation from lateral inhibition; while mismatching acoustic information impacts target looks in the nonword condition, the addition of lexical inhibition in the word condition further depressed target looks.

The task was also clearly sensitive to developmental di erences in the overall rate of lexical access. When we compare fixations in the control condition across grades, there is a clear increase in the speed of lexical access in older relative to younger children (Figure 3B).

Interestingly, there was no interaction of grade and condition, suggesting that across ages children demonstrated similar evidence for inhibition in Activation Rate. These conditions are matched most closely on their acoustic deviation from the target, implying that any additional delay or target reduction during processing in the word splice condition is due to inhibitory activity from an active lexical competitor. Note that a higher di erence score thus suggests a greater degree of inhibition, as the word condition is more delayed/reduced.

This timecourse is quite similar early, with both older and younger children showing similar inhibitory e ects around 800 msec (approximately 500 msec after the onset of the word, given the timing of the stimulus and oculomotor delays). However, somewhat paradoxically, in younger children this interference drops to zero-they fully activate the correct word and seem to recover from inhibition. In contrast, in older children the degree of interference stays high and never drops to zero. For them, inhibitory e ects are not necessarily stronger (initially), but they persist longer. This matches our previous findings, with no interaction between grade and condition for Activation Rate (which indexes the early portions of this curve) but a significant interaction for Maximum (indexing the later portion). These analyses suggest a number of potentially inconsistent e ects. First, there was a fairly robust e ect of the inhibitory manipulation on both early (Activation Rate) and late (Maximum) parts of the timecourse. However, only Maximum showed any moderation by grade. This raises the question as to whether there is a single inhibitory process or whether this pattern might reflect two distinct inhibitory mechanisms. In this latter case, one could think of this in terms of time (early and late application of inhibition). Alternatively, it may be a matter of what is inhibiting what. In this view, the early index reflects the competitor's influence on the target, while the later index reflects the target's ability to inhibit the competitor and become fully active. We investigate this here by investigating the degree to which the magnitude of the inhibitory e ect on Activation Rate predicts the magnitude of the inhibitory e ect on Maximum.

Second, in the control condition, we found robust developmental e ects on Activation Rate. Consistent with prior work (Kutlu et al., 2024; Rigler et al., 2015), older children activated the target word more quickly than younger children. It is not clear if this change in overall speed of word recognition is related to the development of the inhibitory di erences in Maximum. Experiment 2: Replication with Adults

The primary developmental finding was unexpected: Developmental e ects on inhibition e ects were only observed in the late, asymptotic portion of the timecourse. This was unexpected because typically e ects are more robust at the transition window. Moreover, while it was expected that inhibition might grow with development, in some ways this late e ect represents poorer word recognition: older children were not fully recovering from the inhibitory e ects, whereas younger children were.

There are two possibilities for this e ect. First, this might be a Type-I error. Indeed, the e ect was numerically small (though highly significant) and at an unexpected portion of the timecourse. Second, if the e ect is real, this raises questions about the developmental timecourse: is this a transition state in which inhibition has grown but children have not developed the ability to fully cope with it? Or is this the developmental endpoint? Moreover, the finding of a negative association between early and late inhibition in Experiment 1 was also unexpected. Again, it is unclear if this would replicate in a separate population, and it is unclear whether it is unique to the developmental period studied here (if it is transitory) or if it is a proper description of the mature system. To address these questions, we ran the same task on a sample of adults.

Methods

Participants. 31 undergraduate students participated in Experiment 2. Participants received class credit and followed procedures outlined by the University of Iowa IRB. Participation was restricted along the same four criteria: (1) normal or normal-corrected vision; (2) normal hearing; (3) first-language English speakers; and (4) no history of neurological or intellectual disorders. Discussion

Previous research on the development of lateral inhibition has suggested that it is not robustly present in children, only coming online in early adolescence (Blomquist & McMurray, 2023; Gür et al., 2025). We posited that these findings could be the result of limitations in the commonly used sub-phonemic mismatch task, which disrupts lexical access via the presentation of coarticulatory cues for a competitor early in a word. We reasoned that children might not show strong evidence in this task because the brief competition provided by standard sub-phonemic mismatch approaches may not provide enough time for inhibition to drive significant changes in target fixations. Our results support this hypothesis. We introduced a variant of the sub-phonemic mismatch task in which the window in which to activate a competing candidate is extended by providing cues to the competing word throughout the initial portion of a multisyllabic word. We found that, relative to an unmanipulated control stimulus, looks were delayed when a target contains cues to a nonword and were even further a ected when cues point towards another lexical item. Moreover, we demonstrated that children show robust evidence of inhibitory processes even at young ages (seven and eight years old).

Importantly, we also found that while the speed at which children recognize words increased over time, the delays in lexical access associated with the nonword-and wordsplice conditions did not (at least in the early portion of the curve). That is, the e ect of the inhibitory manipulations on these early portions of the timecourse was largely the same in each grade. Critically, these were numerically the most robust markers of inhibition.

This presents a dissociation-even as children rapidly develop in the e iciency of word recognition in this sample, the magnitude of the early inhibition e ect does not seem to change. Thus, similar to earlier work with sub-phonemic mismatch, this suggests that whatever internal changes to the system are driving overall gains in lexical competition e iciency, they are distinct from any changes in lateral inhibition (Blomquist & McMurray, 2023; Gür et al., 2025).

The null e ect of grade on the earliest components of inhibition suggests the most rapid aspects of inhibition are largely in place by this age. One alternative possibility is that our task induced maximal inhibition, thus stifling developmental e ects that could have been observed otherwise. Beyond the di erences in stimuli between ours and standard sub-phonemic mismatch tasks, another critical di erence is that here a word and its competitor (battleship and bottlecap) reversed roles in separate trials-sometimes the initial word was the control (battleship; bottlecap) and sometimes it was a word-splice (bottle-ship; battle-cap). In contrast, typical versions of sub-phonemic mismatch do not use previously competing words as a target. In our task, then, participants may have learned to activate these words more strongly across multiple exposures (akin to a temporary increase in their frequency, leading to stronger activation; c.f. Magnuson et al., 2003). As a result, when they encountered the word again as a competitor-splice this increased activation induced greater inhibition on the target. This may have masked potential e ects, artificially inflating inhibition observed in young children and thus obscuring developmental changes.

However, it is not the case that there was no evidence for the development of inhibition at all, nor that inhibition was entirely unrelated to word recognition e iciency. When we examined the e ect of inhibition late in processing (at the end of the trial) we found evidence that inhibition changes with age. In particular, di erences in maximum target looks between the nonword-and word-splice conditions increased across grade, meaning older children showed suppressed target looks late in processing in the wordsplice condition. That is, inhibition seems harder to overcome in this age group-younger children recover from the word-splice to make maximal looks to the target while older children do not.

One interpretation of this is a straight-forward increase in inhibition over age. In older children there is stronger inhibition from the splice competitor to the correct target word, making it more di icult to overcome when disambiguating information arrives. This results in reduced looks towards the target referent at the end of the trial. These lead to the somewhat unexpected conclusion that older children are less well equipped to deal with interference than younger ones. Critically, Experiment 2 showed that this unexpected e ect was not an aberration-it replicates in adults (with the caveat that e ects were marginal), suggesting this pattern is robust and stable developmentally after about 12.

Additional exploratory work suggests a more nuanced story. We found a negative association between inhibition magnitude between early and late processing. Particularly in older children, listeners who showed a greater e ect of inhibition early showed a smaller e ect late. In combination with the finding that older children and adults showed greater inhibition late in processing and that recognition speed predicted stronger late inhibition, this argues that children might not develop in terms of the strength of inhibition but in its timing or duration: younger children, children with slower recognition speeds, and children with stronger early inhibition demonstrate little e ect of inhibition late not because inhibition is necessarily weaker overall but because it runs its course earlier or more rapidly. In comparison, older children, adults, children with faster recognition speeds, and children who have weaker early inhibition show an e ect late simply because inhibition is still active.

This suggests a new way of thinking about lateral inhibition. Typical models of word recognition posit that lateral inhibition is a derivative of the dynamics of lexical access (coming online proportionate to the level of candidate activation). In contrast, our results suggest that inhibition may have a largely separable time course. Under this view, lexical dynamics unfold according to the input, inhibition unfolds according to its own dynamics, and these convolve to influence word recognition (though it is important to note inhibition cannot be mechanistically independent from lexical access, as nothing can be inhibited unless at least two words are first activated). The dynamics of lateral inhibition may have their own developmental trajectory, changing in the timing of when it is operable or in the duration it is applied over age.

A 'growth spurt' may be a useful analogy for explaining these findings. Young children are generally slow word recognizers but already maintain strong operative inhibitory skills. As children age the speed at which they recognize words develops quickly, but the timing of inhibition may not; quicker selection of a candidate does not imply quicker inhibition. If inhibition remained slow and comes online later, it would disrupt later lexical processing. This would explain why each age group demonstrated strong inhibition while simultaneously older children showed stronger e ects of inhibition late, as well as why early and late inhibition are anti-correlated; those listeners whose inhibition becomes active early necessarily do not engage inhibition late. This does not explain why adults demonstrate a similar e ect, however. If inhibition was simply slow to catch up to the timing of overall word recognition, it would presumably have done so by this age. The replication of a negative association thus suggests an (at once simpler and more complex) answer: There are individual di erences in when inhibition is applied, and this is set early in development (around the age of our older children, ~10-12 years old). Notably, this still implies that inhibition may develop somewhat independently from general word recognition-the timing of when inhibition is most active changes over the course of childhood, but the end state of such changes may be reached fairly rapidly.

Other explanations for these results are worth exploring. For instance, it may be that late regions of the time course are simply more sensitive to overall developmental changes. That is, it may be that changes are occurring in the magnitude of inhibition early in processing, but that this has an extremely protracted developmental trajectory (c.f. Colby & McMurray, 2023 for work which shows word recognition skills may still be developing into middle adulthood). The age range studied here (roughly 7-12) may have been too small to demonstrate reasonable change in this metric. This would explain why we see e ects in target Maximum but not Activation Rate. This would also argue against our earlier conclusion, that development in the timing of inhibition reaches maturity by age 10; it is possible the timing of inhibition has an extremely protracted trajectory, and that the negative association between early and late inhibition goes away in even older adults (consistent with work showing very gradual rise and fall of general word recognition across the lifespan; Colby & McMurray, 2023). Additional work is required to detangle these possibilities.

There are also several unresolved questions regarding the nature of lateral inhibition which are worth noting here. Lateral inhibition is typically thought of as a system that is ubiquitously active during recognition and always a suppressive force, but this may not be the case. For instance, recent research suggests that in sentence contexts phonological neighbor words may not inhibit subsequent recognition but facilitate it-participants are faster at detecting a verb in both grammatically correct and incorrect sentences when the previous word is phonologically related (Dufour et al., 2024). While this does not necessarily argue against lateral inhibition (such facilitation may be due to shared sublexical acoustic cues from the previous word[s] activating the subsequent one) it is unclear how lateral inhibition operates in this context where inhibition (as well as phonological facilitation) may have to extend across di erent slots in a sentence.

Other research has similarly found a facilitation e ect. In bimodal bilinguals (people who communicate in both a spoken and signed language), listeners activate words which share manual properties with a heard target (Villameriel et al., 2022). For example, when hearing the Spanish word vino listeners activate the word bruja. Though vino and bruja share no phonological overlap, they do share manual properties (hand placement and shape). The fact that listeners show facilitation across these words raises questions about lateral inhibition, which would predict that the word vino should suppress bruja due to their overlapping features. Further research into how lateral inhibition develops might provide insight into these unresolved issues.

Implications for the Development of Word Recognition

Beyond informing our understanding of the development of inhibition, the present study also provides important implications for broader work on the development of lexical processing. We earlier posited that changes in word recognition over development may be the result of a developing inhibitory system; as inhibition becomes stronger children, are less disrupted by competitors (as they leverage inhibition to suppress their activation), thus becoming faster at recognizing the target and better at disregarding alternatives.

Our results suggest this cannot be the full explanation. When examining the di erence in Activation Rate between the nonword-and word-splice conditions (a measure of inhibition magnitude) we find no change across grade, despite robust increases in Activation Rate overall. That is, while the speed of lexical access clearly develops, the e ect of inhibition on that speed does not. This suggests that these are largely separablegrowth in Activation Rate is not associated with growth in inhibition.

This leaves it unknown what mechanism(s) are associated with increased Activation Rate across age. It is well known that children increase in the e iciency of processing in other cognitive domains as they grow older-it is possible that the mechanisms underlying this general growth are the same as those which underlie development in word recognition specifically. However, recent work suggests that growth of Activation Rate cannot be fully attributed to general speed of processing (Kutlu et al., 2024). In fact, age-related changes in Activation Rate are almost fully mediated by general growth in language. One way to consider this is that the development of Activation Rate is largely a product of refining bottom-up connections between the input and words (a conclusion supported by computational modeling; McMurray et al., 2010), whereas the development of lateral inhibition derives from connections between words, which can further refine lexical processing via their function on competition dynamics.

Implications for Language Disorders

Inhibition has been suggested as a key locus of di erence in listeners with DLD. Using a standard sub-phonemic mismatch paradigm, McMurray et al. (2019) showed that typically developing adolescents demonstrate clear inhibition, with a nonword-splice slower than a control and a word-splice slower than the nonword. In contrast, adolescents with DLD demonstrate no di erence between the nonword-and word-splice conditions, though both were slower than a control. This suggests that these listeners were a ected by the acoustic variance introduced by these cues but did not have robust inhibition.

As we've argued, however, the standard sub-phonemic mismatch paradigm may be insensitive to inhibition in some populations. It may be that the task variant described here could demonstrate such e ects in listeners with DLD. To this end, we performed exploratory analyses testing a sub-sample of participants in this study who demonstrated low language skills (indexed via an average score across multiple assessments below 90). We find that these children show no di erences in the e ect of condition relative to those with higher language ability. This suggests that inhibition is active and present in this population.

It is important to note that this group of children were not diagnosed with DLD, and so our findings are speculative. However, some approaches to language disorders-and DLD in particular-argue for a framework in which these populations are not typologically distinct from others but simply represent the lower end of some spectrum (Dollaghan, 2004; Leonard, 1987; McMurray et al., 2023; Tomblin, 2011; Tomblin et al., 1999). From this perspective, our findings are instructive; when examining the lower end of language ability, we still find evidence for inhibition.

Nonetheless, this null e ect must be considered carefully in light of two things. First, our children were younger than most of the prior studies; if lateral inhibition is still in development, it may be that these deficits only emerge later in adolescence. Second, most robust developmental e ects of inhibition were on late, asymptotic portions of the timecourse-the exact portions that were linked to DLD in older children. This suggests that the origins of inhibitory deficits in DLD may have a complex developmental path.

Implications for Models of Word Recognition

Lateral inhibition has long been a staple of models of spoken word recognition. These codify inhibition as an independent parameter of the system, implying that it may serve as a locus for development. Here, however, we demonstrate that inhibition is largely present at young ages. This questions interpretations in which inhibition meaningfully develops at all; it may be that any su iciently large lexicon shows at least partial inhibitory e ects. Instead of a separable parameter, then, it may be that inhibition is an emergent property of interactions among lexical items which naturally occur during spoken word recognition.

In contrast to traditional interactive activation models (Hannagan et al., 2013; McClelland & Elman, 1986), distributed connectionist models may be able to instantiate this theoretical framework. For instance, the Distributed Cohort Model (DCM; Gaskell & Marslen-Wilson, 1997) proposes that inhibitory e ects might derive from interference among lexical items, as opposed to direct suppressive connections. This model imagines each word in the lexicon as a pattern of activity across some set of neuron-like units. Importantly, this set of units encodes all words-it is the processing space over which lexical access occurs. When ambiguous input arrives the model might activate a pattern of activity which is similar to many possible words. As more information is presented this activity converges on a single interpretation. Thus, the degree of interfering activity could replicate inhibitory e ects without the need for inhibition: The more that the model must sort through competing patterns the longer it takes to converge. Providing the model with a word-splice would induce greater competition from alternative patterns, resulting in what looks like inhibition.

This alternative model might explain work in novel word learning, which demonstrates that words begin to show inhibitory e ects as soon as they are su iciently integrated (Gaskell & Dumay, 2003; Weighall et al., 2017). In classic architectures this would require a novel word to build inhibitory connections with other items. Here, it could be explained straightforwardly as a novel word joining the pool of possible activity patterns a model might consider. As soon as this pattern is learned the word can begin to exert interference and subsequently exhibit inhibitory-like e ects. (In the context of the current study, it could be fruitful to use the modified sub-phonemic mismatch paradigm presented here to examine what items exert inhibitory activity in children and how early this can occur. However, eye-tracking can be di icult in very young children; other techniques may be more appropriate).

This perspective, in which inhibitory e ects are operative as soon as any degree of lexical items have been codified, could explain the lack of growth in inhibition as indexed by Activation Rate in this study. However, it does not describe why the late indices of inhibition (indexed by target Maximum) grow over development. One possibility is that these reflect di erent emergent properties from a single system. For instance, it may be that late inhibitory processes arise via a feedback mechanism. If activation for some pattern of activity feeds back to itself to further improve activation, the result would be a system which continues to build evidence for its current best interpretation even absent evidence from the input (c.f. "digging-in" e ects in other domains of language processing; Tabor & Hutchins, 2004). In a word-splice condition, such a model might adopt a pattern of activity that represents a probabilistic mix of both candidates. Feedback might entrench the model in this pattern, making it more di icult to adopt the correct interpretation when more input arrives. The result might be a reduction in activity late in processing, as the model struggles to manage interference from an alternative candidate which has been artificially inflated via feedback.

This approach, in which activity patterns interfere with one another and feedback improves the model's current estimate of which word it has heard, could explain our findings without the need for positing development of an independent inhibitory mechanism. As soon as a reasonably sized lexicon is in-place interference among activity patterns might lead to inhibitory-like e ects. Presumably this would be observable early in development, and the magnitude of this might not necessarily grow as new words are learned and the lexicon gets larger. 4 The later stages of word recognition dynamics might then be dictated by a feedback mechanism, in which the system's present activity is boosted. If the strength of this feedback mechanism develops over time, it might explain why older children demonstrate stronger inhibitory-like e ects late in processing; feedback allows alternative interpretations to interfere for longer. This is obviously speculative, with a number of unsolved questions. What sized lexicon would be reasonably large enough to begin demonstrating inhibitory e ects? Topdown feedback (from lexical to sub-lexical structures) is a controversial topic (Elman & McClelland, 1988; Luthra et al., 2021; Norris et al., 2000); it is likely that a kind of "lateral feedback" in which activity boosts itself within the same layer would be even more so, to say nothing of whether such an architecture would even result in the e ects described. Moreover, we find a negative association between inhibition magnitude when indexed early and when indexed late; how might the combination of interference and feedback result in this finding?

Ultimately, adopting a more parsimonious approach-in which we accept common models and assume direct lateral inhibition operating among lexical units-might be the most fruitful approach. Still, critically examining alternative explanations (and building alternative models) may be a useful exercise in developing a deeper understanding of these phenomena.

Conclusion

Given the importance of lateral inhibition to word recognition (as well as other domains of language), it is important to develop a greater understanding of its development. The present study contributes to this enterprise, discovering that inhibition may be relatively mature even at young ages, though it grows in certain aspects, most notably its persistence over time. By utilizing a version of sub-phonemic mismatch with a larger period of ambiguity, we find evidence for robust inhibitory activity at all age groups, and for listeners at risk for DLD, a population previously thought to be limited in inhibitory skills.

Moreover, we find that inhibition as a mechanism may develop somewhat independently of other properties of spoken word recognition. The magnitude of early inhibition did not seem to develop over time (children in grade two demonstrated as large of an e ect between the nonword-and word-splice conditions as children in grade five), e ects of later inhibition were negatively predicted by e ects of earlier inhibition, and older children demonstrated stronger later inhibition overall. Together, this suggests that development in inhibition is largely defined by its timing, as opposed to its magnitudeolder listeners either induce inhibition at a di erent time or apply inhibition for longer, but the strength of inhibition does not seem to grow.

A replication of the study with adults found a similar negative association between early and late inhibition, providing additional evidence for a unique timing of inhibition from word recognition more generally and arguing that the development of the timing of inhibition occurs rapidly, reaching maturity before adolescence (again in contrast with overall word recognition, which still grows at this age; Rigler et al., 2015).

This argues for a model of word recognition in which inhibitory actions are not completely dependent on competition dynamics among lexical items. It is unknown whether extant models are able to accommodate this, as the standard approach is for inhibition to be directly tied to lexical activity. Novel frameworks may ultimately be necessary for explaining these e ects.
