Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. Full text

Abstract

Cognitive decline, cardiometabolic dysfunction, and psychological disorders (particularly late-life depression and anxiety) are highly prevalent in aging, often co-occur, and predict each other over time.

Importantly, stress exposure across the lifespan and dysregulation of the hypothalamic-pituitaryadrenal (HPA) axis and the autonomic nervous system (ANS), the two main biological stress systems, are considered causes and exacerbators of these age-related conditions. However, the stress-to-disease pathway in aging has proven hard to demonstrate consistently, and health trajectories vary widely between individuals. We argue that this partly reflects how the problem has been modeled, with cognitive, cardiometabolic, and psychological health problems in aging studied as separate downstream outcomes of stress and HPA axis and ANS dysregulation. We review and propose several neurocognitive, neurovisceral, and psychophysiological mechanisms that may explain how these three conditions form a mutually reinforcing system. Importantly, these mechanisms also feed back onto the HPA axis and ANS, disrupting their circadian and context-sensitive regulation. We identify middle adulthood as the period in which these reciprocal influences strengthen. Moreover, we propose that the domain first affected and individual reserve shape the health trajectory and which conditions cooccur. In this framework, we argue that stress-system dysregulation in aging should be considered both a cause and a consequence of cognitive decline, cardiometabolic dysfunction, depression and anxiety, forming a self-maintaining loop that may provide a systems-level mechanism through which stressrelated multimorbidity contributes to trajectories commonly associated with accelerated biological aging.

1. Introduction.

Aging is characterized by the convergence of cognitive decline, cardiometabolic dysfunction, and psychological disorders, such as depression and anxiety (Hedden and Gabrieli, 2004; Hirode and Wong, 2020; GBD 2019 Mental Disorders Collaborators, 2022). Age-related cognitive change is detectable well before old age, with longitudinal declines in reasoning evident among adults aged 45-49 years and increasing with advancing age (Singh-Manoux et al., 2012). Changes in cognition have been most frequently investigated and documented in executive functions, which are cognitive processes supported by the prefrontal cortex (Cabeza and Dennis, 2013), including mental flexibility, inhibitory control, and working memory (Fisk and Sharp, 2004; Hedden and Gabrieli, 2004), but also in episodic memory, which is thought to be partially explained by age-related decline in executive functions (Crawford et al., 2000; Lee et al., 2012). Such agerelated change is not, in itself, a sign of dysfunction, but there is considerable interindividual variability in the timing and magnitude of these changes (Reuter-Lorenz and Park, 2014), with some individuals showing a decline that exceeds what would be expected for a person's age and education (i.e., what we refer to in this review as 'cognitive decline'), even though clinical thresholds are not reached, and such decline may or may not reflect emerging neurodegenerative or cerebrovascular disease (Boyle et al., 2013). Cardiometabolic burden follows a similarly marked age gradient. In US population data, metabolic syndrome was present in 39.4% of adults aged 40-59 years and 48.6% of those aged 60 years or older, compared with 19.5% of adults aged 20-39 years (Hirode and Wong, 2020). Depression and anxiety, two of the most frequent mental disorders, also remain major sources of disability throughout the second half of life (GBD 2019 Mental Disorders Collaborators, 2022). In contrast to age-related cardiometabolic disorders and cognitive decline, which are observed particularly in late adulthood, depression and anxiety are highly prevalent across the lifespan (Kessler et al., 2007; Liu et al., 2024). However, some specific forms of depression and anxiety have been associated with aging. Late-life depression (LLD) is conventionally defined as a depressive disorder, most often major depressive disorder, but also dysthymia and clinically significant subthreshold syndromes, occurring in adults aged 60 years or older (Butters et al., 2008; Koenig et al., 2014; Teixeira et al., 2025).

Approximately 5% of community-dwelling older adults meet full DSM-5 criteria, while a further 10-16% show clinically significant depressive symptoms that do not meet full criteria (for a review, see Szymkowicz et al., 2023). LLD comprises early-onset cases (conventionally those with a first episode before age 50-60) that recur or persist into later life, and late-onset cases in which the first lifetime episode appears after that age (Butters et al., 2008; Koenig et al., 2014). Compared with depression in younger adults, LLD is less likely to present with prominent sadness and is more likely to show somatic symptoms such as gastrointestinal distress, sleep disorders, or fatigue (Gallo et al., 1997; Hegeman et al., 2012; see also Koenig et al., 2014).

Late-life anxiety (LLA) has received considerably less attention despite being more frequent than depression, with 12-month prevalence estimates of up to 11.6% and lifetime estimates as high as 15.1% in the United States (Kessler et al., 2005; Reynolds et al., 2015; Ramos and Stanley, 2018) and estimates of 1.2-15% across DSM-5 categories (Hellwig and Domschke, 2019). Subthreshold anxiety appears at least as prevalent as anxiety disorders in later life (Witlox et al., 2021), and community estimates of clinically significant anxiety symptoms range from 15% to 52% (Bryant et al., 2008; Yochim et al., 2013). Agoraphobia, panic disorder, and generalized anxiety disorder are the most common in older adults (Hellwig and Domschke, 2019). Like LLD, LLA appears to follow a bimodal onset distribution, with a substantial group reporting symptoms since late adolescence or early adulthood and another reporting first onset after approximately age 50 (Le Roux et al., 2005).

Importantly, these conditions do not occur in isolation, as cognitive decline, cardiometabolic dysfunction, depression and anxiety increasingly converge in the second half of life. In this context, population-based evidence increasingly demonstrates links (in some cases bidirectional) among cognitive, psychological, and metabolic health problems (Pan et al., 2012; Bangen et al., 2019; Firth et al., 2019; Szymkowicz et al., 2023; van Nieuwkerk et al., 2023; Ijaz et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2025). These associations are not simply explained by health-risk behavior as a common exposure, as they persist after adjustment for age, sex, education and selected health-related factors (e.g., smoking, physical activity and body mass index), although the covariates considered vary across studies and residual confounding, particularly by broader social determinants, cannot be excluded. (e.g., Batelaan et al., 2016; Schmitz et al., 2018; Firth et al., 2019; Koutsonida et al., 2022) Still, the mechanisms by which these conditions become intertwined and the factors that drive the considerable interindividual variability in their emergence and progression remain poorly understood, limiting our ability to identify at-risk individuals, detect early intervention targets, and develop effective prevention and treatment strategies.

Stress and its physiological mediators are considered key drivers and exacerbators of these agerelated health outcomes (Belvederi Murri et al., 2014; Liu et al., 2014; Schlaich et al., 2015; De Looze et al., 2024). The hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis and the autonomic nervous system (ANS) coordinate the allocation of physiological resources necessary for adaptive responses to environmental and internal demands (Sapolsky et al., 2000). The HPA axis exerts many of its effects through glucocorticoid release, whereas the ANS regulates physiological activity through the coordinated action of its sympathetic and parasympathetic branches (Thayer and Lane, 2000; Sapolsky et al., 2000; Ulrich-Lai and Herman, 2009; Russell and Lightman, 2019). Sympathetic activation involves the release of catecholamines, particularly adrenaline and noradrenaline (heightening blood pressure, heart rate, and inflammation, among others), while parasympathetic regulation is largely mediated by the vagus nerve, which provides inhibitory control over cardiac and other visceral functions (Sapolsky et al., 2000; Ulrich-Lai and Herman, 2009; Lamotte et al., 2021). Beyond stress responses, diurnal variation in HPA-axis and ANS functioning also modulates adaptation to the environment (Adam et al., 2017; Hidalgo et al., 2021; Mather, 2024). Problems emerge when individuals are exposed to chronic or repeated stressors accumulated across their lifespan (including ongoing psychosocial demands and stressful life events), as such exposure progressively disrupts HPA axis and ANS regulation, leading to less context-sensitive and flexible regulation, which may manifest as elevated or blunted activity, altered circadian organization, exaggerated or insufficient reactivity, and impaired recovery (Gaffey et al., 2016; Pulopulos and Kozusznik, 2018; Thayer et al., 2021). Dysregulation of these stress-related systems triggers a chain reaction across interconnected biological systems, leading to a collective collapse as individual biomarkers decline and trend toward disease (Juster and Misiak, 2023). This cumulative physiological burden has been conceptualized as allostatic load (McEwen and Stellar, 1993; McEwen, 1998; McEwen, 2002; Juster et al., 2010). Within this framework, dysregulation of primary stress systems progressively alters metabolic, cardiovascular, and immune parameters that have shifted but remain subclinical, such as glucose and lipid profiles, insulin sensitivity, visceral adiposity, and blood pressure.

Alterations in these secondary systems, in turn, are held to give rise to tertiary outcomes, that is, cardiometabolic disease, cognitive impairment, psychiatric morbidity, and mortality (Juster et al., 2010; see also McEwen, 2008). Importantly, allostatic load overlaps with biological aging, understood as the timedependent decline in physiological integrity that varies among people of the same chronological age (Hastings et al., 2022; Zhang, 2023). Higher allostatic load is linked to accelerated biological aging, especially in middle age (Teferi et al., 2026; Liu et al., 2026), and cognitive decline, cardiometabolic dysregulation, and late-life depression and anxiety can be conceptualized as conditions characterized by accelerated biological aging (Alexopoulos, 2019; Gonzales et al., 2022; Szymkowicz et al., 2023).

Empirical evidence and theoretical models from different fields suggest that there are strong mechanistic reasons to consider dysregulation of the HPA axis and the ANS as major contributors to cognitive decline, cardiometabolic dysfunction, depression, and anxiety in middle-aged and older adults (e.g., Baeken and De Raedt, 2011; Belvederi Murri et al., 2014; Liu et al., 2014; Schlaich et al., 2015; Thayer et al., 2021).

Sustained or poorly regulated HPA axis and ANS activity can influence glucose and lipid metabolism, insulin sensitivity, visceral adiposity, blood pressure, endothelial function, and other processes central to cardiometabolic disease (Schlaich et al., 2015; Ortiz et al., 2022). The same biological stress systems have also been linked to structural and functional differences in the brain, including prefrontal and hippocampal circuits critical for executive functions and memory, providing a pathway through which cumulative stress exposure may contribute to cognitive decline (McEwen, 2002; Frewen et al., 2013; Gupta and Morley, 2014; Pulopulos et al., 2014; Pulopulos et al., 2016; Montoliu et al., 2018; Zeki Al Hazzouri et al., 2018). Both depression and anxiety are associated with dysregulation (observed in stress-induced responses, circadian activity and resting conditions) in HPA axis and ANS activity in young and older adults (Kemp et al., 2010; Stetler and Miller, 2011; Belvederi Murri et al., 2014; Chalmers et al., 2014; Puig-Perez et al., 2016; Goncharova, 2020; Rashid et al., 2023), and several frameworks have proposed that dysregulation of stress biological systems may lead to the development, maintenance, and recurrence of depression and anxiety via its structural and functional effects on frontolimbic regions involved in emotion and stress regulation (Baeken and De Raedt, 2011; Hellwig and Domschke, 2019; Szymkowicz et al., 2023). Thus, the HPA axis and autonomic dysregulation have been conceptualized as mechanistic contributors and candidate causal mediators linking stress exposure throughout the lifespan to cognitive, cardiometabolic, and psychological problems later in life.

However, it is important to note that this apparently straightforward stress-to-disease pathway in aging has proven difficult to demonstrate consistently (e.g., Comijs et al., 2010; Gaysina et al., 2014; Pulopulos et al., 2014; Singh-Manoux et al., 2014; Pulopulos et al., 2016; Christian et al., 2023; Li et al., 2026; Wilson et al., 2024), and a large interindividual variability exists, with older adults showing markedly different physiological profiles and health trajectories (e.g., Puig-Pérez et al., 2015; Puig-Pérez et al., 2016; Liu et al., 2022; Maksyutynska et al., 2024; Wilson et al., 2024). Thus, although stress exposure can accelerate cognitive changes and lead to and exacerbate cardiometabolic dysfunction and psychological disorders, the mapping from stress to HPA axis and ANS dysregulation and from this dysregulation to specific health outcomes in aging remains poorly understood. In this review, we propose that part of this inconsistency may stem from how the problem itself has been modeled. Cognitive decline, cardiometabolic dysfunction, depression, and anxiety are typically conceptualized as consequences and are generally examined as separate downstream outcomes of stress exposure throughout the lifespan, and especially in aging. An alternative possibility we will elaborate on in this review is that, from middle adulthood onward, these conditions increasingly become part of the regulatory process that generates and maintains dysregulation. Thus, they may not simply share stress-related causes; they may also act on one another and feed back into the same stress-related systems that contributed to their emergence.

In this review, we will discuss the idea that what appears epidemiologically as comorbidity among these stress-related health problems in late adulthood may, at least in part, reflect a self-maintaining failure of shared regulatory systems. We move beyond comorbidity to consider cognitive decline, cardiometabolic dysfunction, depression, and anxiety in older adults as components of a potentially mutually reinforcing system. In the next sections, (i) we examine the evidence for the directional pathways linking cognitive decline, cardiometabolic dysfunction, depressive and anxiety symptomatology (including both clinically diagnosed disorders and dimensionally at levels below diagnostic thresholds); (ii) we discuss and hypothesize how mechanisms central to stress regulation, including neurocognitive, neurovisceral and psychophysiological processes, could provide the substrate through which changes in one domain influence the others; (iii) we discuss and hypothesize how these altered processes may, at the same time, affect stressregulatory systems in aging, contributing to the maintenance of HPA axis and ANS dysregulation, and (iv) we then argue that, from middle adulthood onward, the effects of stress and allostatic load on health may be understood not only as the cumulative consequence of stress acting on the organism, but also as the accumulating expression of a regulatory system in which cognitive decline, cardiometabolic dysfunction, depression, and anxiety increasingly sustain one another as people age. Building on findings from relatively independent fields, we aim to synthesize empirical evidence, elaborate on new mechanistic links, provide a unified perspective on how cognitive, cardiometabolic, and psychological health problems evolve as people age, and generate new research questions to guide future research. Although the HPA axis and the ANS do not exhaust the biology of stress and aging, we focus on these two systems because they constitute rapid bidirectional regulatory interfaces through which the brain, metabolic organs, and environmental demands interact. Immune, inflammatory, and cellular-aging processes represent closely interacting parallel pathways outside the primary scope of this framework.

2. Depression and anxiety as drivers of cardiometabolic dysfunction and cognitive decline.

>Depression and anxiety as drivers of cardiometabolic dysfunction and cognitive decline.

2.1. From depression and anxiety to cardiometabolic dysfunction.

Among the directional pathways considered in this review, the contribution of stress-related psychological dysfunction to subsequent cardiometabolic and cognitive problems is arguably one of the most extensively investigated. A large body of meta-analytic research indicates that depression predicts the later development of metabolic syndrome (MetS; a cluster of conditions including impaired glucose levels, hypertriglyceridemia, abdominal obesity, low high-density lipoprotein cholesterol levels, and hypertension; Wang et al., 2020) and its independent and related components (e.g., type 2 diabetes, insulin resistance, obesity, and other cardiovascular outcomes) (Mezuk et al., 2008; Gan et al., 2014; Cao et al., 2022). For instance, Pan et al. (2012) observed that baseline depression predicts incident MetS (OR = 1.52), while another meta-analysis found a 60% higher risk of developing type 2 diabetes among individuals with depression (Mezuk et al., 2008). Similarly, prospective meta-analytic studies consistently associate depression with an increased risk of coronary heart disease (Gan et al., 2014; Cao et al., 2022). Anxiety has received comparatively less attention but shows a similar pattern. A meta-analysis of 37 prospective studies including more than 1.5 million individuals found that anxiety was associated with a 52% higher incidence of cardiovascular disease, independently of several conventional cardiovascular risk factors and depression (Batelaan et al., 2016). Importantly, although the studies included in these meta-analyses spanned a wide age range (18 to 99 years), the mean age in most studies was around the fifth decade or older, and results were similar when researchers considered participants older than 65 years (Cao et al., 2022). Still, more research is needed to understand whether LLD and LLA carry a prospective cardiometabolic risk different from that associated with depression and anxiety diagnosed in younger adulthood.

A central mechanism proposed to account for the prospective associations is that depression and anxiety (even at subclinical levels) can prolong and repeatedly activate the physiological stress response, increasing cumulative exposure to HPA-axis and autonomic dysregulation. Depression and anxiety are characterized not only by maladaptive responses to stressors but also by psychological processes such as rumination, worry, anticipatory threat processing, and maladaptive appraisal that may maintain the representation of a stressor in the absence of the stressor itself (e.g., De Raedt and Koster, 2010; De Raedt and Hooley, 2016; Brown et al., 2023; Pulopulos et al., 2023). The perseverative cognition hypothesis proposes that processes associated with rumination and worry prolong stress-related physiological activation over time (Brosschot et al., 2006). Consistent with this hypothesis, a meta-analysis of 60 studies found that rumination and worry were associated with higher blood pressure, heart rate, and cortisol, and with lower vagally mediated heart-rate variability (HRV; a marker of parasympathetic cardiac control) (Ottaviani et al., 2016). Importantly, this link seems to be explained by state rumination and worry, not trait levels (see e.g., Li et al., 2024a). Depression itself has been associated with altered HPA-axis activity and reduced cardiac vagal control, while anxiety disorders are similarly characterized by reduced HRV (Kemp et al., 2010; Stetler and Miller, 2011; Chalmers et al., 2014; Puig-Perez et al., 2016). Late-life generalized anxiety disorder has been associated with elevated diurnal cortisol secretion relative to non-anxious controls, with cortisol levels correlating with worry severity (Mantella et al., 2008; Hellwig and Domschke, 2019). Thus, depression and anxiety (clinically diagnosed but also subclinical symptomatology) may increase cardiometabolic burden by prolonging the duration and increasing the frequency of glucocorticoid, sympathetic-adrenergic, and parasympathetic responses, repeatedly engaging the metabolic and cardiovascular systems to deal with (real or imagined) stressors and leading to their impairment.

2.2. From depression and anxiety to cognitive decline.

Depression and anxiety have been consistently associated with cognitive decline in aging. In a metaanalysis, Hudon et al. (2020) observed that depressive symptoms and diagnosis of depression were incident predictors of both cognitive decline and dementia in cognitively normal middle-aged and older adults, and Santabárbara et al. (2020) showed that depression was associated with a 63% higher risk of subsequent dementia. These associations are clearer for late-life phenotypes than for midlife. In LLD, 20% to 50% of individuals experience cognitive impairment exceeding that of matched controls based on age and education, with processing speed and executive functions being most consistently and severely impacted (Butters et al., 2004; Sheline et al., 2006; Koenig et al., 2014; Szymkowicz et al., 2023). Such deficits are attributed to disruption of the cognitive control network, including the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex, dorsal and rostral anterior cingulate, and parietal association regions (Aizenstein et al., 2009; Alexopoulos et al., 2012; Morimoto et al., 2015). Importantly, these cognitive deficits are not solely due to low mood, as they often continue after remission (see Koenig et al., 2014 for a review). Moreover, a study combining tau PET and amyloid PET found that patients with LLD showed no excess tau accumulation and no higher rate of amyloid positivity than cognitively unimpaired, non-depressed older adults (Vande Casteele et al., 2025), suggesting that cognitive difficulties in LLD are not necessarily attributable to early Alzheimer's pathology, although the contribution of cerebrovascular pathology or other neurodegenerative processes cannot be ruled out.

Regarding anxiety, the evidence is less extensive but suggests a similar trend. Meta-analytic evidence in older adults indicates that anxiety is associated with an increased risk of cognitive impairment and dementia, with stronger associations observed at higher ages (Gulpers et al., 2016). The evidence, though inconclusive, suggests the effects are particularly evident for executive functions (Gulpers et al., 2016).

Prospectively, clinically significant LLA has predicted accelerated cognitive decline in studies that adjusted for baseline cognitive performance, whereas trait neuroticism has not, suggesting that the clinical significance of anxiety, rather than an anxious disposition per se, may be the key factor (for a review, see Beaudreau and O'Hara, 2008). Finally, anxiety symptoms have been proposed as a prodromal marker of, or an independent risk factor for, both Alzheimer's disease and vascular dementia (Becker et al., 2018; Hellwig and Domschke, 2019).

The mild behavioral impairment framework proposes that the longitudinal associations between psychological symptoms and cognitive deterioration may reflect, at least partly, shared underlying pathology (Ismail et al., 2016). Another possibility is that a causal relationship exists in which depression and anxiety contribute to cognitive decline in older adults. The same processes linking depression and anxiety to cardiometabolic disorders may provide a plausible pathway from psychological dysfunction to cognitive decline. Repeated and maladaptive activation of the HPA axis as observed in depression and anxiety may affect brain regions critical for cognition, including the prefrontal cortex, hippocampus, and amygdala, all of which contain glucocorticoid receptors and undergo functional and structural remodeling after prolonged stress (McEwen, 2002; McEwen, 2008; Lupien et al., 2018). In humans, chronic stress, prolonged glucocorticoid exposure, and dysregulated HPA axis activity have been associated with alterations in hippocampal and prefrontal structure and function, impairing memory and executive functions (e.g., Pulopulos et al., 2014; Hidalgo et al., 2016; Pulopulos et al., 2016; Lupien et al., 2018; Montoliu et al., 2018).

Importantly, these changes may also impair the ability of the same brain regions to regulate subsequent stress responses (Baeken and De Raedt, 2011), leading to maladaptive stress regulation and exacerbating HPA axis dysregulation. This cascade of events may provide a potential mechanism through which depression and anxiety (even subclinically) progressively compromise both cognition and stress regulation. This mechanism is particularly relevant from middle adulthood onward, when stress-related alterations occur against a background of declining neural resilience (Reuter-Lorenz and Park, 2014) and an allostatic burden that accumulates across adulthood and remains high in later life (Crimmins et al., 2003; Juster et al., 2010).

However, although the route from prolonged or dysregulated HPA activity to cognitive decline is relatively well documented in the general population, it has not been examined prospectively in LLD and LLA, and more research is needed to understand the long-term impact of these psychological conditions on HPA regulation and its later impact on cognitive aging.

The noradrenergic system, a main component of the sympathetic system, provides a second, complementary pathway. The locus coeruleus-noradrenaline (LC-NA) system plays an important modulatory role on the PFC and hippocampus and is essential for attention, working memory, and adaptive responding to environmental demands (Mather and Harley, 2016). Moderate levels of NA support PFC function, whereas high tonic LC activity and high NA levels associated with intense stress recruit lower-affinity adrenergic receptors and rapidly impair PFC-dependent cognition (Arnsten, 2009; Datta and Arnsten, 2019). With chronic stress or repeated stress activation, as observed in depressive and anxious states, these signaling processes may become persistent and contribute to dendritic and synaptic alterations in the PFC (McEwen, 2007; Arnsten, 2009; McEwen et al., 2016; Datta and Arnsten, 2019). Moreover, chronic stress may shift the LC-NA system into a high-tonic mode, affecting phasic responses (Aston-Jones and Cohen, 2005; Krohn et al., 2023). Neuroimaging studies have also shown that adequate LC integrity (which may lead to appropriately timed NA release) predicts cognitive functioning in later life, and experimental evidence suggests that NA can protect neurons against inflammation, excitotoxicity, and amyloid-related toxicity (Mather and Harley, 2016).

Importantly, depression and anxiety have themselves been associated with abnormalities in the adrenergic system (for reviews, see Morris et al., 2020; Reyes, 2025; Korukonda and Weinshenker, 2026), and particularly in aging, LLD has been linked to worse structural integrity of the LC (Guinea-Izquierdo et al., 2021) and lower functional connectivity between the LC and the anterior cingulate cortex, which correlates with the severity of depression (del Cerro et al., 2020). Aging has also been linked to increased mean diffusivity in the LC in older adults (Valdés Cabrera et al., 2026). Importantly, LC microstructural integrity is linked to processing speed and executive function in aging regardless of depression status (Valdés Cabrera et al., 2026), and the functional topography of the LC changes throughout adulthood in ways associated with emotional memory and emotion regulation (Veréb et al., 2023). This indicates that aging, affective dysfunction, and cognitive control all involve the same noradrenergic system.

Taken together, the epidemiological and mechanistic evidence supports depression and anxiety as important potential amplifiers of both cardiometabolic dysfunction and cognitive decline in aging, acting in part by further dysregulating the same HPA, autonomic, and central noradrenergic systems through which stress links to psychological, cognitive, and cardiometabolic conditions.

3. Cardiometabolic dysfunction as a driver of depression, anxiety and cognitive decline.

>Cardiometabolic dysfunction as a driver of depression, anxiety and cognitive decline.

3.1. From cardiometabolic dysfunction to depression and anxiety.

Compared with the well-established literature linking depression and anxiety to cardiometabolic dysfunction, the opposite pathway has received less systematic attention. Prospective epidemiological evidence indicates that the direction of influence is not exclusively psychological-to-metabolic. Chourpiliadis et al. (2024) followed more than 200,000 individuals (mean age at baseline 42.1, SD 12.6) for a mean of 21 years and observed that higher glucose and triglyceride levels predicted a greater subsequent risk of depression, anxiety, and other stress-related disorders, whereas higher high-density lipoprotein cholesterol was associated with lower risk. Furthermore, metabolic differences between individuals who later developed psychiatric disorders and controls were detectable up to two decades before diagnosis. These findings suggest that metabolic abnormalities may precede the clinical emergence of depression and anxiety rather than merely arise as a consequence of it. Importantly, Liu et al. (2022) also demonstrated that this metabolic signature is itself age-dependent. In a community cohort of adults aged over 50, they identified seventeen plasma metabolites that were simultaneously associated with depressive symptoms and correlated with age, suggesting that the metabolic contribution to depression becomes progressively more relevant as metabolic disturbance accumulates. This idea is also supported by evidence obtained specifically in later life. Among 40,080 initially depression-free participants of the Swedish Twin Registry (mean age 60) followed for eighteen years, one cardiometabolic disease was associated with a 52% higher hazard of incident depression and two or more with an 83% higher hazard (Yang et al., 2024). Importantly, the risk effect was larger when the cardiometabolic disease had developed in midlife (before age 60) than in late life, suggesting a midlife transition point.

Regarding anxiety, while evidence in LLA is limited, findings suggest that certain multimorbidity patterns involving cardiometabolic disease can predict the onset of an anxiety disorder (Palmese et al., 2026), and that cardiometabolic disorders may increase the likelihood of the first onset of internalizing disorders (including any mood or anxiety disorder) six years later in more than 4,000 participants aged 18-64 at baseline (mean age 44.3; SD=12.5) (Nuyen et al., 2021). However, the evidence regarding the predictive value of cardiometabolic conditions for depression and anxiety has not always been observed (e.g., Nuyen et al., 2021), which highlights the large heterogeneity in physiological profiles and health trajectories.

Importantly, cardiometabolic dysfunction is an umbrella domain rather than a unitary biological entity. Different components are expected to engage different pathways and to produce partially distinct psychological and cognitive phenotypes. Along this line, researchers have proposed different mechanisms underlying the prospective association between cardiometabolic dysfunction and depression and anxiety in aging. The 'vascular depression' hypothesis postulates that cerebrovascular disease may predispose, precipitate, or perpetuate some geriatric depressive syndromes (characterized by retardation, anhedonia, lack of insight into their illness, and disability, and fewer reports of feelings of guilt) by impairing frontolimbic networks supporting affective processes (Alexopoulos, 2019). Given the clinical overlap between depression, apathy, and executive-behavioral syndromes in aging, distinguishing affective distress from motivational and behavioral changes is essential. These phenotypes may reflect partly different mechanisms and may not engage the hypothesized stress-regulatory feedback loops in equivalent ways. Some authors have also proposed the definition of (immuno-)metabolic depression as a bio-clinical dimension defined jointly by atypical energy-related depressive symptoms (increased appetite and weight, sleep problems, fatigue, motivational impairment, and anhedonia), together with low-grade systemic inflammation and metabolic abnormality, and associated with higher cardiometabolic disease risk and with poorer response to standard antidepressant treatment (Penninx et al., 2025). Thus, cumulative decline across physiological systems, including endocrine-metabolic regulation, is not only a core feature of biological aging (Clegg et al., 2013), but also a major mechanism for conceptualizing LLD as a disorder of accelerated biological aging (Alexopoulos, 2019).

Insulin may play an important role in explaining how metabolic dysfunction may lead to depression and anxiety. Type 2 diabetes, obesity, and MetS (and its components) are independently associated with insulin resistance, which on its own has been associated with more severe mood disorders (Meng et al., 2025). A potential pathway involves metabolic and endocrine signaling to the brain, as insulin modulates neuronal and glial metabolism, synaptic plasticity, memory, reward, and emotional functioning. Peripheral insulin resistance and type 2 diabetes can coexist with impaired insulin signaling in the brain, which may compromise synaptic, metabolic, and immune functions and has been implicated in both cognitive impairment and psychiatric symptoms (Arnold et al., 2018). Moreover, glucose metabolism in medial prefrontal regions during psychosocial stress is associated with the magnitude of the cortisol response (Kern et al., 2008). Brain insulin resistance has therefore been proposed as one possible biological hub connecting metabolic dysfunction with depression and anxiety, particularly with cognitive symptoms and anhedonia (Kleinridders et al., 2015; Hamer et al., 2019). MetS may also promote neuroinflammation and disrupt hypothalamic regulation of hormonal balance (Van Dyken and Lacoste, 2018).

A second, less-explored pathway involves autonomic dysregulation. MetS, obesity, insulin resistance, and hypertension are associated with increased sympathetic activity, and sympathetic overdrive has itself been proposed as an important component in the development and progression of MetS (Grassi, 2006; Schlaich et al., 2015). Cardiometabolic dysfunction is also associated with reduced cardiac vagal regulation (for a meta-analysis, see Ortiz-Guzmán et al., 2023). Importantly, lower cardiac vagal control has been associated with less flexible emotional responding and less effective regulation of negative affect (Williams et al., 2015), and our research also shows that lower HRV in anticipation of a stressor is associated with a higher stress-induced cortisol response (Pulopulos et al., 2018; Pulopulos et al., 2020b). Thus, we argue that the autonomic relationship may itself become bidirectional. That is, depression and anxiety promote prolonged sympathetic activation and vagal withdrawal, as discussed in the previous section, while an increased metabolic burden may shift autonomic regulation toward sympathetic dominance and reduced vagal control, shifting the brain's stress response away from prefrontal regions toward salience regions, and potentially decreasing the physiological flexibility required for adaptive emotion and stress regulation.

A related mechanism may arise from the afferent side of autonomic and metabolic regulation. The vagus nerve serves as a two-way communication pathway between the body and the brain. Vagal afferents, which represent almost 80% of the vagal fibers, sense cardiometabolic signals via their endings in the viscera and relay this information to the nucleus tractus solitarius in the brainstem, which processes it and transmits it to other brain regions. To trigger vital homeostatic reflexes essential for cardiometabolic control, these neurons send strong projections to vagal motor neurons in the dorsal motor nucleus of the vagus or the nucleus ambiguus. Additionally, they project to regions that generate sympathetic output, such as the ventrolateral medulla and the intermediolateral cell column, as well as to respiratory centers in the brainstem and pons. They also connect indirectly to other brain regions such as the hypothalamus, amygdala, and prefrontal cortex (particularly via the LC), where peripheral information is integrated with other circuits to regulate autonomic functions and behaviors during stress responses (Holt, 2022; Boychuk and Wang, 2026; Roy and Vanderhasselt, 2026). Importantly, cardiometabolic dysfunction remodels stress systems. Beyond the sympathetic overdrive and reduced cardiac vagal control, cardiometabolic stressors and disease states alter inhibitory GABAergic signalling within the vagal reflex circuits themselves (vagal afferents, the nucleus tractus solitarius, and vagal motor neurons), which has been proposed as a mechanism of the vagal dysfunction associated with cardiometabolic disease (Boychuk and Wang, 2026). Moreover, vagal afferent pathways transmit nutrient-, glucose-, gastrointestinal-, and hormone-related signals that are critical for regulating energy and glucose homeostasis (Waise et al., 2018; Borgmann and Fenselau, 2024). These neurometabolic interfaces become dysfunctional in conditions such as obesity and diabetes (Masi et al., 2018). We therefore hypothesize that in individuals with accumulating cardiometabolic burden, the same objective stressor elicits a less flexible and less well-timed HPA-axis and autonomic response, with less capacity to recruit the prefrontal resources needed to regulate it.

Recent theoretical works provide a framework for understanding how such alterations could lead, in the long term, to depression and anxiety. The interoceptive energy-allostasis model proposes that the brain continuously integrates peripheral metabolic signals with contextual information and previous experience to estimate the body's energetic state and prospectively regulate physiological resources (Mehrhof et al., 2025).

Within this framework, metabolic dysfunction could alter either the peripheral signals available to the brain or their central interpretation, thereby degrading the internal model that underpins allostatic regulation.

Inaccurate or unreliable information about energetic states could consequently affect motivation, reward, effort allocation, affective experience, and subsequent physiological regulation. Crucially, the consequence would be that cardiometabolic dysfunction could impair the feedback information the brain uses to regulate both metabolism and psychological responses in emotional and stressful situations, creating conditions in which metabolic and psychological dysfunction reinforce one another. This self-reinforcing process would sustain less flexible autonomic and HPA axis regulation, maintaining or exacerbating their dysregulation. This perspective also aligns with predictive allostatic-interoceptive frameworks, which propose that disruptions in integrating central predictions and peripheral bodily signals may compromise adaptive physiological regulation across psychiatric and neurological disorders (Santamaría-García et al., 2025). Similarly, evidence from behavioral variant frontotemporal dementia suggests that alterations in interoceptive brain responses are associated with cognitive and affective impairments, as well as structural and functional abnormalities in the allostatic-interoceptive network. (Birba et al., 2022).

3.2. From cardiometabolic dysfunction to cognitive decline.

MetS and related components (e.g., type 2 diabetes, insulin resistance, hypertension, obesity, and other vascular risk factors) have repeatedly been associated with poorer cognitive functioning and an increased risk of cognitive impairment and dementia (Henn et al., 2022). For instance, Qureshi et al. (2024) showed in 37,395 dementia-free adults from the UK Biobank database that MetS was associated with lower total brain, gray matter, and hippocampal volumes, and that participants with MetS performed worse on cognitive tests of executive function, verbal declarative memory, processing speed, and verbal and nonverbal reasoning. More MetS components were also associated with lower brain volume, greater white matter hyperintensity, and poorer cognition across all domains. Similarly, about one in three patients with cardiac disease shows cognitive impairment, most often affecting executive functions and memory (van Nieuwkerk et al., 2023).

Longitudinal studies suggest that these associations already become evident in middle-aged adults.

In the Framingham Offspring Study, both midlife and late-life MetS were associated with poorer cognitive functioning, with midlife MetS already associated with poorer cognition around age 55 (Bangen et al., 2019).

More recently, the Guangzhou Biobank Cohort Study followed 7,775 middle-aged and older adults for up to 12 years and found that a higher MetS index was associated with faster decline in delayed recall and global cognition (MMSE), including attention and calculation, whereas persistently elevated MetS trajectories were associated with faster memory decline (Tian et al., 2025). In a meta-analysis, González-Castañeda et al. ( 2021)also observed lower global cognitive scores in adults with MetS than those without MetS. Moreover, pooled data from four cohorts in China, the United Kingdom, the United States, and continental Europe (n = 40,883, mean age 63.4) showed that cardiometabolic multimorbidity is associated with worse levels and faster deterioration of physical disability, depressive symptoms, and cognitive function, with the highest odds observed for concurrent worsening across all three functional domains rather than for deterioration in any single domain (Zhang et al., 2025). These results are in line with the idea that the psychological and cognitive consequences of cardiometabolic dysfunction occur in parallel, rather than in a fixed sequence. Nevertheless, some studies report weaker associations than initially hypothesized, and results show high interindividual variability (e.g., Siervo et al., 2014; Koutsonida et al., 2022), suggesting that other factors may contribute to the link between cardiometabolic conditions and cognitive decline. Together, the current evidence suggests that early disruptions in systemic metabolism can cause persistent and progressive effects on cognition later in life. However, the precise mechanisms leading to cognitive impairment are still not fully understood.

Several interrelated mechanisms may account for these associations. Cardiometabolic dysfunction contributes to cerebral small-vessel disease, endothelial dysfunction, impaired cerebrovascular reactivity and cerebral perfusion, blood-brain barrier disruption, glial disruption and inflammation, while insulin resistance may also disrupt insulin signaling and glucose metabolism within the brain (Alfaro et al., 2018; Arnold et al., 2018; Horton and Barrett, 2021; Al Haj Ahmad et al., 2022; Henn et al., 2022; Li et al., 2023; Jost and Kujach, 2025). These cardiometabolic dysfunctions may compromise the brain's ability to obtain and dynamically allocate the metabolic resources required to cope with cognitive challenges. Insulin resistance has also been associated with lower cerebral glucose metabolism, including in prefrontal, parietal, and medial temporal regions, and with poorer working, immediate, and verbal memory (Willette et al., 2015; Deery et al., 2024a).

Importantly, insulin resistance and aging also alter the coupling between cerebral blood flow and glucose metabolism, suggesting reduced efficiency in the mechanisms that supply energetic resources to the brain (Deery et al., 2024b).

We propose that cardiometabolic dysfunction may also affect stress-related biological systems that are crucial to cognitive performance. The same supply mechanisms meet the energetic demands of cognitive work and the stress response because both cognitive performance and adaptive stress responses require the brain to dynamically allocate resources between central cognitive processes and peripheral physiological systems (Pulopulos et al., 2020a; Van Boven et al., 2026). Prefrontal regions particularly regulate this brainbody interaction, coordinating these adjustments through interactions with the central autonomic network (including the LC-NA system) and vagal pathways (Aston-Jones and Cohen, 2005; McKlveen et al., 2015; Smith et al., 2017). As described above, these systems are crucial for cognitive processes, particularly prefrontal-related executive processes. Evidence for this coupling comes from a study with 389 patients with stable coronary artery disease showing that greater stress-induced peripheral vasoconstriction, higher inferior frontal lobe activation, and larger increases in norepinephrine and interleukin-6 were each independently associated with worse switching performance (as measured by the Trail Making Test B), and each also predicted the decline in executive function over the following two years (Moazzami et al., 2021).

Neuroimaging evidence shows that in patients with coronary artery disease, stress-induced peripheral vasoconstriction is accompanied by increased perfusion of the insula and parietal cortex, along with decreased perfusion of the medial prefrontal cortex (Shah et al., 2019; see Wang et al., 2025 for a review).

As mentioned above, cardiometabolic disease may therefore shift the brain's stress response away from prefrontal regions that coordinate regulation and toward salience regions. Together, these findings support the idea that cardiometabolic dysfunction, by compromising prefrontal control and autonomic flexibility, may reduce the capacity of prefrontal systems to recruit the resources necessary for adaptive cognitive and physiological regulation during stress.

4. Cognitive decline as a driver of depression, anxiety and cardiometabolic dysfunction.

>Cognitive decline as a driver of depression, anxiety and cardiometabolic dysfunction.

4.1. From Cognitive decline to depression and anxiety.

Among the pathways considered in this review, the possibility that cognitive decline in aging contributes to subsequent depression and anxiety in aging has received comparatively less attention. This is particularly striking for executive functions (working memory, switching, and inhibition), which are among the most affected by aging and stress, and are considered fundamental regulatory capacities required to adapt thoughts, emotions, and behavior to changing demands (De Raedt and Koster, 2010; De Raedt and Hooley, 2016; Pulopulos et al., 2023). Moreover, because of their cross-cutting role in standardized cognitive tasks, declines in executive function can affect performance in other cognitive domains and contribute to global cognitive decline (Crawford et al., 2000; Lee et al., 2012). Importantly, the question here is not whether individuals with lower cognitive ability have poorer health (a relationship examined extensively; see e.g., Batty et al., 2007; Deary, 2010) but whether within-person decline in executive functions from middle adulthood onward, particularly when it exceeds what would be expected for age, leads to psychological and cardiometabolic health problems.

Evidence in middle-aged and older populations is consistent with this hypothesis, although it remains mixed and has rarely focused specifically on executive function. In the Leiden 85-plus Study, poorer baseline performance on the Stroop test (reflecting interference control) and on immediate and delayed memory predicted an accelerated increase in depressive symptoms over four years (Vinkers et al., 2004). Using ten years of data from more than 16,000 adults aged ≥65, poorer prior global cognition predicted subsequent increases in depressive symptoms, with sleep quality partially mediating that path (Guo et al., 2022), and a symptom-level cross-lagged network analysis to three waves collected in 2015, 2018, and 2020 of 14,961 adults aged ≥45 found that memory and orientation predicted future depressive symptoms (Ma et al., 2025).

In 2,605 participants (mean age 55, range 33-84) followed for approximately nine years, poorer inhibition, set-shifting, working-memory updating, inductive reasoning, and global cognition each predicted generalized anxiety disorder at follow-up (Zainal and Newman, 2018). In 721 non-demented twins followed with up to eight cognitive assessments over 26 years, worse processing speed, attention, working memory, and nonverbal memory each predicted larger increases in anxiety three years later, with the largest effects for attention and working memory (Petkus et al., 2017). The evidence is nevertheless not uniformly supportive, as global cognitive impairment is not associated with incident anxiety or depression in some studies (Potvin et al., 2011; Best and Cosco, 2022). Together, these studies suggest that associations between depression or anxiety and cognitive performance are most consistently observed when executive functions are specifically assessed, whereas they tend to be weaker or absent when cognition is assessed using global cognitive screening measures or episodic memory tests.

Research focused on mechanisms underlying the association between cognitive decline and depression and anxiety is scarce in older adults, but the evidence from younger populations provides an important starting point. Experimental and theoretical evidence proposes that executive functions support inhibiting irrelevant or negative information, switching away from maladaptive thought patterns, updating representations as circumstances change, and maintaining goal-relevant information during stressful situations (e.g., De Raedt and Koster, 2010; De Raedt and Hooley, 2016; Vanderhasselt et al., 2023; De Smet et al., 2024). In a prospective study, poorer executive cognitive control over emotional information strengthened the association between stressful experiences and subsequent depressive rumination (De Lissnyder et al., 2012). Moreover, Snyder and Hankin (2016) showed that weaker executive functions led to stressful situations partly caused by an individual's actions, which then heightened rumination and, ultimately, symptoms of depression and anxiety. In middle-aged and older adults, Ng et al. (2022) showed that higher executive functioning predicted both lower baseline rumination and a faster decline in rumination across eight days, consistent with more successful regulation of ruminative thought. Along these lines, a depression-executive dysfunction syndrome has been described in older adults characterized by anhedonia, psychomotor retardation, pronounced disability, lack of insight, suspiciousness, and mild vegetative syndrome, associated with worse performance in tests of verbal fluency (often conceptualized as measuring executive functioning; e.g., Henry & Crawford, 2005a , 2005b; Lezak et al., 2012), response inhibition, novel problem solving, cognitive flexibility, working memory, and/or ideomotor planning, and consistent with disruption of frontal-subcortical networks (Alexopoulos, 2019). No study has investigated how executive function predicts changes in stress perception and regulation in aging. However, cross-sectional evidence indicates that worse working memory, inhibition, and shifting are linked to more perceived stress in middleaged and older adults (Caswell et al., 2003; Korten et al., 2017; Pertl et al., 2017; Chen et al., 2018; Guevara et al., 2019; D'Amico et al., 2021). As discussed above, perseverative cognition provides an established mechanism through which psychological stress extends HPA-axis and autonomic activation over time.

Together, executive functions may influence psychological health by shaping how much stress individuals generate, how they appraise it, and how long its cognitive representation persists after the event ends.

Cognitive decline could therefore enter the allostatic process upstream of psychological stress dysregulation, rather than functioning exclusively as a downstream consequence.

The contribution of cognitive functioning to stress regulation may extend beyond executive control.

Socio-affective processes, such as recognizing emotional signals, interpreting others' intentions, and adapting behavior to interpersonal situations, may also shape the appraisal and generation of social stressors but have been overlooked (Hostinar et al., 2014; Saxbe et al., 2020). Impairments in these processes could contribute to interpersonal misunderstandings, conflicts, or reduced social support, potentially increasing exposure to stress and limiting opportunities for interpersonal emotion regulation (Vanderhasselt et al., 2025). Thus, alterations in socio-affective functioning and their interpersonal consequences may represent an additional, yet insufficiently investigated, pathway through which cognitive changes contribute to stress-system dysregulation (Saxbe et al., 2020), which may, in turn, lead to depression and anxiety disorders (Vanderhasselt et al., 2025).

Beyond these behavioral and interpersonal pathways, another potential mechanism involves prefrontal regulation of the HPA axis and autonomic functioning. Executive function and physiological stress regulation depend partly on overlapping prefrontal systems. The medial and lateral PFC contribute to executive control and emotion regulation while also regulating autonomic and neuroendocrine responses through interactions with the amygdala, hypothalamus, brainstem, LC-NA system, and vagal pathways (McKlveen et al., 2015; Smith et al., 2017; De Wandel et al., 2020; Razza et al., 2024; Van Boven et al., 2026; Keppens et al., 2026). Several lines of evidence support close coupling between executive function demands and these neurovisceral systems. The LC-NA system plays a central role in executive function and adapts noradrenergic signaling as task demands change (Grueschow et al., 2020). Anticipating cognitive and physical effort is accompanied by dorsomedial PFC activity and pupil dilation (a marker associated with the LC-NA system), consistent with anticipatory central energization (Kurniawan et al., 2021; Pulopulos et al., 2022; Van Boven et al., 2026). Experimentally modulating the DLPFC can alter effort-related cardiovascular mobilization during demanding cognitive performance (Framorando et al., 2021). Similarly, increasing executive demands produces phasic cardiac vagal withdrawal, and the magnitude of this withdrawal moderates the relationship between cognitive performance and PFC hemodynamic recruitment (Wei et al., 2024). Together, these findings indicate that successful executive function involves dynamic coordination between prefrontal activity, central noradrenergic signaling, and peripheral autonomic regulation rather than cognition operating independently of physiological resource allocation.

We propose that age-related deterioration of prefrontal-related cognitive-control systems may progressively reduce the efficiency of this coordination, a process that may be exacerbated by stress exposure throughout the lifespan. As prefrontal regulatory capacity declines, the organism may become less able to match central and peripheral resource mobilization to actual environmental demands. Reduced topdown control could result in less flexible LC-NA activity, inappropriate vagal withdrawal or recovery, and less precise modulation of HPA-axis responses. The consequence might not be a constant increase in activation of all stress systems. Instead, it would involve a loss of regulation that depends on the context, leading to activation that is excessive, too little, mistimed, or lasts longer than needed relative to the real demands.

From this perspective, prefrontal-related cognitive control decline could contribute simultaneously to psychological dysfunction (through poorer appraisal, emotion regulation, perseverative cognition, socioaffective processes) and to physiological dysregulation through increasingly inefficient neurovisceral control, which will ultimately lead to psychological disorders such as depression and anxiety.

4.2. From cognitive decline to cardiometabolic dysfunction.

Although cognitive functioning is usually treated as an outcome of cardiometabolic health, a separate epidemiological literature indicates that cognition can also precede metabolic and cardiovascular outcomes. Vishwanath et al. (2026) examined cognitive trajectories before incident cardiovascular events in 9,435 initially cardiovascular disease-free older adults from the Aspirin in Reducing Events in the Elderly randomized clinical trial and the extension observational study. Individuals who subsequently experienced a cardiovascular event showed faster cognitive decline several years before the event. Importantly for this framework, composite executive function declined significantly faster in future cardiovascular disease cases, with trajectories beginning to diverge about six years before the cardiovascular event. These findings provide important evidence that changes in cognition, particularly executive functioning, precede clinically manifest cardiovascular disease. Leng et al. (2018) reported similar results in the Women's Health Initiative Memory Study. Among 5,596 women aged 60 and over who were free of cardiovascular disease at baseline and followed for an average of 7.1 years, every 5-point lower baseline score on the Modified Mini-Mental State Examination was associated with a 12% higher risk of incident cardiovascular disease, a 37% higher risk of heart failure, a 35% higher risk of cardiovascular death and a 24% higher risk of all-cause mortality. More importantly, every additional 1-point-per-year decline was associated with a 4% higher risk of incident cardiovascular disease, 10% for coronary heart disease, 9% for stroke or transient ischaemic attack, 17% for cardiovascular death and 13% for all-cause mortality.

One proposed explanation for this prospective association is that subclinical vascular pathology could already be present years before clinical diagnosis and could simultaneously impair cognition and increase cardiovascular risk (Vishwanath et al., 2026). Indeed, this explanation is compatible with the vascular mechanisms discussed in the previous sections. However, at least three other mechanisms could translate declining executive function into greater cardiometabolic risk. The first is behavioral. Managing cardiometabolic risk successfully requires planning, prospective memory, inhibition of habitual behavior, updating health information, medication adherence, symptom monitoring, and sustained regulation of diet and physical activity. A systematic review concluded that poorer executive function and other cognitive abilities in older adults with type 2 diabetes are associated with poorer medication adherence, glucose monitoring, appointment attendance, insulin-management skills, and other self-care behaviors (Tomlin and Sinclair, 2016). Likewise, in heart failure, cognitive impairment is associated with poorer treatment adherence and self-care, and cardiac patients with cognitive impairment experience more recurrent cardiovascular events, which are in turn associated with further cognitive decline (van Nieuwkerk et al., 2023). However, meta-analytic evidence indicates that the effect of executive function on health behavior is modest (Gray-Burrows et al., 2019), and other factors may also play an important role. In this context, while cognitive abilities may influence health-related behaviors, their translation into effective disease management likely depends on contextual (i.e., sociological) resources, including access to healthcare, social support, and opportunities to implement recommended behaviors.

The second mechanism is the stress-regulatory pathway described in the previous sections. We argue that reduced prefrontal control and executive function may increase perceived stress and perseverative cognition, extending HPA-axis and autonomic activation (Pulopulos et al., 2019; Pulopulos et al., 2020a; Pulopulos et al., 2023), increasing allostatic load and leading to cardiometabolic dysfunction (Ortiz et al., 2022). The third mechanism involves the neurovisceral pathway. We hypothesize that declining prefrontal regulatory capacity may itself contribute to increasingly inefficient autonomic, noradrenergic, and HPA-axis regulation, producing repeated unnecessary or mistimed central vs peripheral metabolic mobilization. This pathway is supported by neuromodulation studies showing that increasing prefrontal activity (particularly dorsolateral and medial regions) increases HPA axis regulation under stress, but also noradrenergic and parasympathetic regulation during executive control tasks, and baroreflex function (a central-peripheral system involved in the control of blood pressure) (Pulopulos et al., 2020a; Razza et al., 2024; van Boven et al., 2026; Montoro et al., 2025; Keppens et al., 2026). Thus, deterioration of the prefrontal systems supporting cognitive control may reduce the efficiency with which metabolic resources are dynamically allocated between central and peripheral systems during challenge. Over time, these processes could contribute to the allostatic burden that promotes insulin resistance, hypertension, endothelial dysfunction, and other cardiometabolic dysfunctions.

Taken together, the available evidence suggests that cognitive functioning should not be considered exclusively a downstream consequence of psychological and cardiometabolic problems. Cognitive decline (particularly executive functions) may represent a turning point in the transition from accumulated allostatic burden to self-maintaining dysregulation. As the regulatory capacity required to manage stress begins to deteriorate, psychological and cardiometabolic dysfunction may become progressively more capable of sustaining (and being sustained by) the same failing regulatory system. Importantly, we do not propose that executive dysfunction captures the full cognitive-aging phenotype. Rather, among cognitive functions, executive control is particularly relevant to the present framework because it directly participates in appraisal, emotion regulation, behavioral self-regulation, socio-affective processes, and neurovisceral control.

5. From depression, anxiety, cognitive decline, and cardiometabolic dysfunction to HPA axis and ANS dysregulation.

The mechanisms described above explain how depression, anxiety, cognitive decline, and cardiometabolic dysfunction may affect one another. Importantly, we want to emphasize that these mechanisms may also negatively affect HPA axis and ANS regulation, thereby sustaining dysregulation of the biological stress systems responsible for these conditions (Figure 1).

Regarding the psychological node, stress-related appraisal and perseverative cognition, associated with prefrontal control, prolong endocrine, cardiovascular, and metabolic activation beyond the stressor itself and are associated with maladaptive stress responses (Brosschot et al., 2006; Ottaviani, 2018; De Raedt and Hooley, 2016), increasing the burden on the HPA axis and the ANS, and affecting cardiometabolic and cognitive processes. Repeated exposure to glucocorticoids remodels hippocampal and prefrontal neurons involved in terminating the HPA-axis stress response and autonomic outflow (McEwen, 2002; McEwen, 2007; Smith et al., 2017). Additionally, chronic stress shifts the locus coeruleus into a high tonic state and impairs phasic noradrenergic signaling (Aston-Jones and Cohen, 2005; Krohn et al., 2023). The consequence is therefore not only cognitive and metabolic damage, but also a reduced capacity to trigger adaptive physiological responses to future stressors in older adults (Pulopulos et al., 2018; Mikneviciute et al., 2023).

The cardiometabolic node may act on the same systems from the periphery. Autonomic dysregulation, as reflected in sympathetic overactivity and diminished cardiac vagal control, is not only linked to metabolic syndrome but is considered an integral part of it (Grassi, 2006; Schlaich et al., 2015; Ortiz-Guzman et al., 2023). Additionally, cardiometabolic diseases can impair inhibitory GABAergic signaling within the vagal reflex circuits themselves (Boychuk and Wang, 2026). Since vagal afferents transmit the metabolic information on which central allostatic estimates rely (Waise et al., 2018; Borgmann and Fenselau, 2024; Mehrhof et al., 2025), their dysfunction impairs both regulatory input and its subsequent output. Vagal activity, in turn, exerts an inhibitory influence over glucose regulation, HPA-axis activity, sympathetic activation, and inflammatory processes, with the prefrontal cortex and amygdala as the central structures linking these systems to the vagus (Thayer and Sternberg, 2006; Pulopulos et al., 2018; Pulopulos et al., 2020b; Keppens et al., 2026). Reduced vagal control can therefore increase HPA axis and sympathetic activity, contributing to their dysregulation.

Regarding cognitive decline, executive function impairment may alter stress perception and regulation (e.g., Osmanovic-Thunström et al., 2015; Pertl et al., 2017; Korten et al., 2017; Chen et al., 2018; Guevara et al., 2019; D'Amico et al., 2021), and perseverative cognition (De Lissnyder et al., 2012; Snyder and Hankin, 2016; Ng et al., 2022), increasing HPA axis and ANS activation. Moreover, prefrontal cortex-related control is crucial for context-sensitive vagal, sympathoadrenal, and HPA-axis responses to challenges (e.g., Pulopulos et al., 2020a; Framorando et al., 2021; van Boven et al., 2026). The effects extend beyond the individual through interpersonal regulatory processes. Changes in cognitive and affective functioning can modify social behavior and relationship dynamics, potentially generating additional stressors or reducing opportunities for interpersonal emotion regulation. In turn, these changes in the social environment may influence subsequent psychological and physiological responses (Vanderhasselt et al., 2025).

Together, dysregulation of the HPA axis and ANS should be seen as both a cause and a consequence of the feedback loop. From this perspective, accelerated biological aging linked to depression, anxiety, cognitive decline, and cardiometabolic dysfunction is not only caused or exacerbated by stress but is also sustained internally by a regulatory system whose failures contribute to its own inputs (see Figure 1).

6. Middle adulthood as a transitional period.

The framework proposed here raises an important question: when do psychological, cognitive, and cardiometabolic problems begin to operate as a mutually reinforcing system? We propose that during middle adulthood (between the fourth and sixth decades of life), several processes that have previously developed relatively independently increasingly overlap, creating conditions in which reciprocal influences may become progressively stronger and coupling increases. Middle adulthood may represent a particularly likely transition period.

Several observations make middle adulthood a plausible candidate for such a transition. First, although changes in the HPA axis and ANS are frequently observed in older adults, these changes are progressive, with pronounced dysregulation emerging in middle adulthood, around the fifth decade of life (Jandackova et al., 2016; Thayer et al., 2021; Miller et al., 2016). Second, cardiometabolic burden increases markedly during this period. As noted above, the prevalence of MetS in US adults is approximately twice as high at ages 40-59 as at ages 20-39 (Hirode and Wong, 2020), while many of its components (including insulin resistance, hypertension, visceral adiposity, and vascular dysfunction) may remain subclinical for years before producing overt disease. Third, subtle reductions in cognitive functioning, including reasoning and executive processes, can be detected from the fifth decade onward, even though most individuals remain cognitively healthy and can compensate for these changes (Singh-Manoux et al., 2012; Reuter-Lorenz and Park, 2014). Fourth, the physiological consequences of cumulative stress exposure are becoming increasingly apparent in this period. In a classic population-based analysis, allostatic load increased sharply between young adulthood and approximately age 60 before reaching a relative plateau at older ages (Crimmins et al., 2003). Midlife also involves the coexistence of multiple social roles and demands, including work, parenting, and, for many individuals, caregiving for aging relatives, increasing exposure to stressors (Lachman, 2004; Lachman et al., 2015). Finally, recent evidence suggests that neural aging itself may also change during this period. Antal et al. (2025) examined the organization of functional brain networks in over 19,000 participants and found that these networks become increasingly unstable throughout life, and that this destabilization follows a nonlinear pattern, with identifiable temporal milestones of brain aging beginning in midlife (the 40s). We therefore propose that in young adulthood, the relative preservation of cognitive control, metabolic flexibility, and physiological reserve serves as a buffer, preventing disruptions in any one area from spreading systemically. In middle adulthood, accumulated stress often coincides with increased cardiometabolic burdens, along with slight declines in executive control and neurovisceral regulation capacity. This can make perturbations propagate more easily, with psychological, cardiometabolic, and cognitive issues affecting each other and the HPA axis and ANS systems. These mutual influences may become deeply integrated, resulting in synergistic effects (e.g., Khambaty et al., 2022). Therefore, as people age, psychological, cognitive, and metabolic dysfunction not only rises and worsens with stress, but their mutual interactions also strengthen.

Longitudinal studies beginning in middle adulthood are therefore particularly important because they can capture the formation of the loop rather than only its consequences.

Importantly, increasing coupling among these domains does not imply that psychological (depression and anxiety), cognitive, and cardiometabolic conditions will necessarily deteriorate together in every individual. Indeed, research has shown that the profile of these age-related conditions is heterogeneous (Kokkeler et al., 2022; Liu et al., 2022; Szymkowicz et al., 2023; Maksyutynska et al., 2024). In some individuals, the predominant coupling may occur between cognitive decline and depression or anxiety, whereas in others, psychological and cardiometabolic conditions or cognitive and cardiometabolic deterioration may become more strongly interconnected. Some individuals may show relatively isolated dysfunction within a single domain, with limited propagation to the others. We propose that these different trajectories may reflect variation in the availability of protective and compensatory resources that can interrupt or weaken cross-domain feedback. We recently proposed that factors such as physical activity, diet, sleep, social connectedness, socioeconomic resources, and engagement in cognitively (including socially) stimulating activities may preserve physiological and cognitive regulatory capacity (Vanderhasselt et al., 2025), thereby reducing the likelihood that dysfunction in one domain spreads to another. This idea is consistent with the revised Scaffolding Theory of Aging and Cognition, which emphasizes that trajectories of cognitive aging emerge from the balance between age-and disease-related neural challenges and compensatory or protective influences, including cognitive reserve, neural resource enrichment, physical activity, intellectual engagement, and other life-course exposures (Reuter-Lorenz and Park, 2014).

Importantly, social and socioeconomic conditions may not only act as protective or compensatory resources but may also shape the chronic environmental demands to which individuals must adapt. Recent allostaticinteroceptive frameworks have emphasized the integration of biological regulation with social and environmental adversity (Migeot and Ibáñez, 2025). Consistently, multidimensional social exposome measures have been associated with cognitive, neuropsychiatric, and brain alterations in aging and neurodegenerative diseases (Migeot et al., 2025). These findings suggest that social conditions may influence both the emergence of dysfunction and the resources available to compensate for its consequences.

Together, middle adulthood may increase the opportunity for the three domains to become coupled, while the interplay between individual reserve and broader social and socioeconomic conditions may determine or moderate whether this coupling remains limited, involves two domains, or develops into a broader selfmaintaining regulatory failure.

We argue that another important source of variation is related to the main condition initially affected. A similar idea has been proposed for cognitive impairment, physical frailty, and cardiovascular disease, where the condition that develops first may indicate the pathway driving pathological aging (Ijaz et al., 2024). As mentioned above, researchers have identified at least three different profiles for LLD, which have been called depression executive dysfunction syndrome, vascular depression, and (immuno-)metabolic depression (Alexopoulos, 2019; Penninx et al., 2025). These profiles are characterized by different symptomatology and might also be explained by different mechanisms and by different points of entry into the system. When cardiometabolic burden is predominantly vascular, the resulting depressive syndrome tends to be dominated by psychomotor retardation, apathy, anhedonia, and executive dysfunction, a presentation that overlaps substantially with the depression-executive dysfunction syndrome and is consistent with frontostriatal disruption (Alexopoulos, 2019). When obesity, insulin resistance, and lowgrade inflammation predominate, the atypical, energy-related profile of immuno-metabolic depression is more likely (Penninx et al., 2025). Genetic evidence is consistent with this distinction, since polygenic risk for stroke is associated only with later-onset depression, whereas polygenic risk for body mass index, coronary artery disease, and type 2 diabetes is associated with both early-and later-onset depression (Hagenaars et al., 2020). A comparable specificity may apply to the cognitive consequences of cardiometabolic dysfunction.

Hypertension has been associated with poorer executive function and processing speed (Pacholko and Iadecola, 2024), and moderates the association between cortisol regulation and processing speed (Pulopulos et al., 2016), whereas insulin resistance has been linked to lower glucose metabolism in medial temporal and prefrontal regions and to poorer working and short-term memory (Willette et al., 2015; Deery et al., 2024a), and brain insulin resistance has been proposed as a shared substrate of anhedonia and cognitive impairment in depression (Hamer et al., 2019). When the first domain affected is cognitive, stress exposure interacting with factors that reduce neural resilience may favor a depression-executive dysfunction presentation. For example, in healthy older adults, higher cortisol levels were associated with poorer declarative memory, particularly in carriers of the ApoE-ε4 allele, a major genetic risk factor for Alzheimer's disease, suggesting that this allele may confer vulnerability to the cognitive effects of HPA-axis dysregulation (Montoliu et al., 2018). In LLD, early onset is associated with a greater decline across all cognitive areas compared to late onset (Riddle et al., 2017), suggesting that if the psychological domain is affected initially and depression is recurrent, the cognitive effects tend to be more extensive. Importantly, potentially traumatic experiences and particularly early-life adversity may further shape these entry points. Adverse childhood experiences have been associated with enduring alterations in HPA-axis activity, prefrontal and hippocampal structure, and inflammation that may increase allostatic load and accelerate biological aging (Danese and McEwen, 2012). Such early biological embedding could therefore accelerate the processes described here, influencing not only vulnerability within each domain but also how early, and how strongly, the three domains become coupled. Moreover, the first clinically affected cognitive domain may not correspond to the earliest underlying biological disturbance. Neurodegenerative and cerebrovascular processes can remain subclinical for extended periods, potentially influencing several domains before overt symptoms emerge, and involve compensatory mechanisms. Identifying the initial point of entry into the proposed system may therefore require longitudinal biological and clinical characterization rather than reliance on the temporal order of symptom detection.

The same principle may help explain why some individuals develop one condition, others two, and others all three. Longitudinal studies have linked the three domains in different sequences. In the Rotterdam and Whitehall II cohorts, poorer cardiometabolic status predicted later depressive symptoms, which in turn predicted subsequent cognitive decline, alongside a direct cardiometabolic-cognitive association (Schmitz et al., 2018). In the 1958 British birth cohort, affective symptoms accumulated across early and mid-adulthood were associated with midlife memory and verbal fluency through cardiometabolic risk (John et al., 2021).

Moreover, depressive symptoms were associated with steeper increases in glycaemic markers only among individuals with lower executive function in 1,257 African American and White, urban-dwelling adults from the Healthy Aging in Neighborhoods of Diversity across the Life Span (Khambaty et al., 2022). These findings align with the idea that dysfunction may start at any node, with the first affected node shaping which others are recruited next. Whether and how dysfunction spreads likely depends on the interaction between this initial perturbation and individual risk and protective factors. To our knowledge, however, no study has examined, within the same cohort, the probability of developing none, one, two, or all three of these conditions as a function of the first affected domain and the risk and protective factors present. Such studies will be necessary to determine whether the point of entry into the system predicts the combination of conditions an individual eventually develops.

7. Future research.

In the previous sections, we have described how cognitive, cardiometabolic and psychological health problems associated with aging can be exacerbated and self-maintained by stress and the dysregulation of stress-related biological systems. It is important to note that most cross-domain pathways reviewed here are supported by prospective observational evidence, whereas direct causal evidence remains limited. Thus, the proposed feedback system should be understood as a testable mechanistic hypothesis rather than an established causal network. Moreover, several gaps still need to be addressed to better understand how these conditions evolve with aging and the mechanisms behind their progression.

First, the directional pathways between domains should be examined with all three domains (cognitive, cardiometabolic, and psychological) measured, even when only one pathway is of primary interest. We argue that the associations between the three conditions are not independent. An association between cardiometabolic dysfunction and subsequent depressive symptoms may be direct, may be routed through declining cognitive control, or may be inflated because cognitive decline contributes to both. A two-domain design cannot distinguish these possibilities, and the unmeasured domain then becomes an important and uninterpretable source of between-person variability. Measuring the third domain is therefore valuable even when it is not the target, as it allows estimation of the pathway of interest while accounting for alternative routes. Where repeated measures of all three domains are available, randomintercept cross-lagged panel models and related approaches can separate within-person change from stable between-person differences. However, although such longitudinal models can help disentangle withinperson associations from stable between-person differences, they do not by themselves establish causal relationships, particularly in the presence of unmeasured time-varying confounding or measurement error.

Second, further studies testing the mechanisms proposed in the preceding sections, and whether their contribution changes with age, may offer valuable insights for developing effective preventive strategies. In this context, age should not be treated as a confounder, as it is important to understand whether cross-domain coupling changes with age. Age-moderated longitudinal models and time-varying network approaches, among other statistical approaches, could estimate whether psychological-tometabolic, metabolic-to-cognitive, and cognitive-to-psychological/metabolic pathways become progressively stronger across adulthood. In such analyses, the parameter of primary interest is not an age difference in mean depression, metabolic syndrome, or cognitive performance, but the change in the magnitude of the prospective relationships among these domains. Similarly, protective and compensatory resources should be modeled as moderators of the cross-domain pathways, as they may explain when individuals are at risk of developing relatively isolated dysfunction, coupling restricted to two domains, and a broader self-maintaining regulatory failure. Such analyses should also consider interpersonal and socioeconomic conditions, including social support, education, occupational characteristics, and material resources, as potential determinants of stress exposure and moderators of cross-domain associations (Vanderhasselt et al., 2025). This would help distinguish individual regulatory capacities from the social conditions that may facilitate or constrain their expression.

Third, given the large interindividual variability, more research is needed to understand specific health profiles, focusing on specific symptoms rather than general diagnoses. As emphasized by Amad et al.

(2026) in the context of psychiatric disorders, a more precise characterization of symptom expression, including observable behavioral and physiological manifestations, may help identify clinically meaningful subgroups and provide a stronger foundation for investigating their underlying biological mechanisms. For instance, patients sharing a diagnosis of depression may present opposite symptoms (e.g., insomnia vs. hypersomnia, weight loss vs. weight gain; Goldberg, 2011), and in a sample of more than 1,500 patients, the diagnostic criteria for major depressive disorder were met through 170 different symptom combinations (Zimmerman et al., 2015). In LLD, three profiles have been linked to executive, vascular, and (immuno-)metabolic dysfunction (Alexopoulos, 2019; Penninx et al., 2025). We may also expect different symptomatology profiles in other conditions depending on the specific mechanisms involved. Future studies should therefore test whether distinct symptom profiles, in the psychological as well as in the cardiometabolic and cognitive domains, are associated with distinct patterns of HPA-axis and ANS dysregulation and with different trajectories. A more comprehensive cardiometabolic and neuropsychological characterization could also include relevant disease biomarkers and neuroimaging measures, when available. Regarding cognitive decline, when combined with age-and education-adjusted norms and repeated assessments, such characterization would also allow testing whether stress-system dysregulation accelerates age-related cognitive change in the absence of overt pathology, increases vulnerability to (or hastens the expression of) neurodegenerative and cerebrovascular processes, or both.

Within this context, it is important to note that given the heterogeneity of cognitive assessments across studies, stronger associations involving executive functions could partly reflect differences in measurement sensitivity or the domains preferentially investigated. Thus, cognitive assessment should extend beyond executive functions and global cognitive scores to encompass other cognitive domains, including episodic memory, language, visuo-constructive, and socio-affective functioning.

Fourth, studies should directly test whether dysfunction in the mechanisms linked to the three domains contributes to maintaining the dysregulation of the stress systems themselves. Most of the research has focused on how dysregulation of the HPA axis and ANS predicts cognitive, cardiometabolic, and psychological health problems. No research has investigated whether these conditions and their related mechanisms contribute to stress regulation and HPA axis and ANS profiles in aging. Along this line, we argue that these conditions are linked to reduced context-sensitive regulation, leading to a weaker match between demand and response (i.e., activation that is excessive, insufficient, mistimed, or prolonged relative to the situation) rather than a simple elevation of any single marker. Future studies could combine multidimensional allostatic load indices, integrating cardiovascular, metabolic, and neuroendocrine, and inflammatory markers, with measures of central allostatic-interoceptive processing, such as heartbeatevoked potentials and functional connectivity of the allostatic-interoceptive network. Longitudinal assessments could help determine whether alterations in these physiological and neural systems precede, accompany, or follow cognitive and psychological changes, including in populations at risk of neurodegenerative disease (Birba et al., 2022; Marin-Díaz et al., 2025). Including measures of biological aging would further allow testing whether stronger coupling among the three domains, and the stress-system dysregulation that sustains it, predicts accelerated biological aging. Such designs should account for the fact that different organs and systems may age at different rates (Zhang, 2023). Fifth, prevention and intervention studies should target the shared regulatory mechanisms. We predict that interventions targeting shared regulatory mechanisms should produce effects across more than one condition. Small nonrandomized studies already point to such cross-domain effects, with cognitive improvement after treating cardiac dysfunction (e.g., aortic valve replacement or cardiac resynchronization therapy; van Nieuwkerk et al., 2023). Bottom-up approaches, acting on the peripheral and afferent side of the neurovisceral circuit, and top-down approaches, acting on its prefrontal node, may be particularly important to change the mechanisms described above. Exercise and physical activity are obvious examples because they can simultaneously improve cardiometabolic function, cognitive function, and psychological well-being, including in middle-aged and older populations, although the exact mechanisms and the types of patients who benefit most are still not well understood (Penedo and Dahn, 2005; Pérez-López et al., 2017; Van Damme et al., 2026). Similarly, voluntary slow-paced breathing, considered both a bottom-up and topdown approach, reliably increases vagally mediated HRV both acutely and following repeated practice (Laborde et al., 2022; Li et al., 2024b). Five weeks of HRV biofeedback involving slow-paced breathing has been shown to alter indices related to LC-noradrenergic and sympathetic functioning in younger adults and to influence LC-innervated hippocampal subregions in older adults (Bachman et al., 2023; Yoo et al., 2023).

Non-invasive prefrontal stimulation offers another mechanistically informative approach, as meta-analytic evidence indicates that cortical stimulation can alter heart rate, HRV, and stress-induced cortisol regulation, supporting the causal involvement of cortical systems in HPA axis and autonomic regulation (Pulopulos et al., 2020a; Schmaußer et al., 2022). Transcutaneous vagus nerve stimulation offers another route to the afferent pathways, as it has been shown to affect LC activity and interoception (Roy and Vanderhasselt, 2026).

Importantly, middle-aged adults could represent a particularly important preventive window, when dysfunction is emerging but compensatory neural and physiological mechanisms remain available. This possibility is consistent with both the revised Scaffolding Theory of Aging and Cognition and recent evidence that midlife is a turning point when metabolic plasticity of brain networks begins to decline (Reuter-Lorenz and Park, 2014; Antal et al., 2025). Beyond these individual-level approaches, interventions targeting interpersonal processes and social support could also be investigated as potential means of modifying stressrelated feedback mechanisms (Vanderhasselt et al., 2025). Such studies could examine whether improvements in relationship quality, interpersonal emotion regulation, or psychological support are accompanied by changes in psychological and physiological stress regulation.

Crucially, future intervention studies should not simply determine whether these approaches improve one isolated outcome, but whether improvements in vagal regulation, prefrontal functioning, or HPA and adrenergic flexibility explain improvements across domains. Such a design would directly test whether modifying the shared regulatory substrate changes the coupling between the three nodes. Conversely, if an intervention substantially improved one domain but repeatedly failed to influence the others or their prospective coupling, this would constitute evidence against the stronger form of the selfmaintaining-system hypothesis. Importantly, such studies should also assess whether changes in cognitive and physiological regulation translate into clinically meaningful improvements in everyday functioning and psychological well-being.

8. Conclusion.

Depression and anxiety, cognitive decline, and cardiometabolic dysfunction are common in older adults and have been independently linked to dysregulation of the HPA axis and ANS. In this review, we suggest that what appears epidemiologically as comorbidity among stress-related health problems in late adulthood may partly stem from a failure of a shared regulatory system that maintains them. We extend the discussion from mere comorbidity to view depression and anxiety, cognitive decline, and cardiometabolic dysfunction as interconnected components of a potentially reinforcing system. Existing evidence indicates directional pathways connecting these health issues. Key stress-regulation mechanisms may underlie how changes in one area affect others, potentially influencing stress-regulation systems during aging and contributing to ongoing HPA axis and ANS dysregulation. Middle adulthood emerges as a critical period when the impact of stress and allostatic load on health can be seen not only as cumulative effects of stress, but also as the progressive manifestation of a regulatory system in which psychological, cognitive, and cardiometabolic issues increasingly sustain each other.
