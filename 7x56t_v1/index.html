Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

The marginal module searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The list of terms is a subset of those listed in a blog post by Matthew Hankins. The module returns all sentences that match terms describing ‘marginally significant’ results.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Abstract

Psychological research increasingly draws on resources inherited from historical traditions, with the potential both to advance contemporary psychological knowledge and to sustain and further develop those traditions. Existing methodological discussions have often emphasized fidelity to inherited meanings in reconstructing and transforming traditional resources. Yet when contemporary research substantially revises inherited responses, can such work still count as inheritance, and if so, where does the boundary between continuation and departure lie? Focusing on traditional Chinese psychology, this conceptual article identifies a shared developmental logic across theoretical, practical, and methodological cases that go beyond the reconstruction of inherited responses. On this basis, it conceptualizes continuative development as a mode of inheritance distinct from reconstructive transformation. Its central argument is that continuity lies at the level of inquiry rather than in the preservation of particular inherited responses. The inquiry itself may even develop to address new questions under changing conditions. What distinguishes continuation from mere influence or use of traditional resources is an intelligible developmental relation to an inherited inquiry. The article further argues that, compared with reconstructive transformation, continuative development opens a broader space for ongoing knowledge generation by allowing new answers, practices, methodological approaches, and questions to emerge beyond the historical repertoire. This also means that contemporary psychology need not treat inherited traditions only as repositories of existing intellectual resources, but can also feed back into and further develop those inquiries. In turn, such development may contribute to cultural continuity while enabling inherited traditions to generate new psychological knowledge that may extend beyond their original cultural contexts. In sum, the article articulates the principles and potential contributions of continuative development as a distinct, methodologically defensible research orientation and clarifies the relationship between inheritance and innovation in traditional psychology.

Continuative Development of Traditional Psychology: Rethinking Inheritance and Innovation through Traditional Chinese Psychology

Contemporary psychological research increasingly draws on intellectual and practical resources inherited from historical traditions, including Chinese, Indian, Islamic, and other traditions (e.g., Li et al., 2026; Rao et al., 2008; Rassool, 2023). Such engagement is valued not only for its contributions to contemporary psychology but also, in some contexts, as a means of sustaining and further developing inherited intellectual and cultural traditions (e.g., Cornelissen et al., 2014; Luo & Lin, 2026; Rassool, 2023). In the case of traditional Chinese psychology, this engagement spans a range of ideas and practices. Themes such as oneness and self-transcendence have been taken up in transpersonal psychology (Lajoie & Shapiro, 1992; Li et al., 2026), while constructs inspired by Daoist or Buddhist thought, such as dialectical thinking and appreciative joy, have been operationalized and studied empirically (Peng & Nisbett, 1999; Zeng et al., 2017). Traditional practices such as mindfulness meditation and Tai Ji have likewise been adapted for contemporary use (Creswell, 2017; Solloway et al., 2016). This emphasis on historically inherited intellectual resources differs in focus from much of the earlier Chinese indigenous psychology movement, whose primary concern was to develop psychological knowledge grounded in the cultural and social realities of Chinese societies (e.g., Bedford & Yeh, 2020; Yang, 1999). In recent years, Chinese policy has explicitly emphasized the inheritance and further development of fine traditional Chinese culture (Ministry of Education of the People's Republic of China, 2022). Against this background, research on traditional Chinese psychology has gained increasing prominence as an explicit direction for the development of Chinese psychology. Such research is valued for its potential both to advance contemporary psychological knowledge and to contribute to the continuation and further development of Chinese intellectual and cultural traditions (Luo & Lin, 2026; Ye & Su, 2023).

Yet the principles and boundaries governing inheritance and innovation in traditional Chinese psychology remain insufficiently articulated. For example, Liu (2018) explicitly called for the creative transformation of traditional Chinese psychology, treating interpretation as necessarily creative while also stressing that it should remain grounded in inherited texts rather than become arbitrary invention. Ye and Su (2023) inherited. On the one hand, carefully reconstructing a classical construct on the basis of classical texts and then measuring it or examining its contemporary effects already involves substantial creativity: interpretation, operationalization, and empirical investigation inevitably generate forms of knowledge that did not exist in the original tradition. On the other hand, revisiting a question historically addressed by the tradition and proposing a new answer may likewise be regarded as innovation or creative transformation. That is, existing discussions leave a fundamental question unresolved: what must remain continuous with a tradition, and how much change is compatible with genuinely inheriting and developing it?

This question becomes especially important because fidelity to inherited meanings has already emerged as a substantive methodological concern in traditional Chinese psychology.

For example, Liu (2019), in criticizing empirical research on zhongyong, argued that contemporary operationalizations can depart from the classical construct when they rely on present-day understandings or omit its ethical prerequisites; he therefore stressed the need to begin from careful interpretation of classical texts. Tu and Guo (2016) similarly warned that factor-based measurement could fragment theoretically interdependent dimensions and conflict with the integrated Daoist conception of human nature. Such critiques reflect a fidelity-oriented expectation: when contemporary researchers claim to study an inherited construct or practice, its modern form should remain recognizably related to its historical meaning, structure, and context. Comparable concerns with authenticity, continuity, and grounding in inherited intellectual traditions also appear in contemporary Indian and Islamic psychology (Cornelissen et al., 2014; Rassool, 2023).

However, not all contemporary developments seek primarily to preserve inherited responses. As illustrated below, some studies have already reconsidered inherited theoretical answers, substantially redesigned inherited practices, or developed new methodological approaches from traditional starting points. In such cases, fidelity to inherited responses is no longer the appropriate primary concern, since those responses are themselves being reconsidered or revised. More importantly, these developments bring the boundary between inheritance and departure into sharper focus. Must research drawing on traditional psychology remain confined to reconstructing inherited responses? If not, on what grounds can developments that revise these responses still count as inheritance, and how far can such revisions go without breaking continuity? Beyond differences in the extent of revision, how do these developments differ in their underlying approach from research centered on reconstructing inherited resources, and what additional contributions might they make to contemporary psychology and to the further development of inherited traditions? Notably, modern Chinese philosophy and contemporary Confucian thought have already discussed the further development of inherited traditions, including the revision of inherited views in light of contemporary knowledge and the pursuit of questions left unresolved by earlier thinkers (e.g., Angle, 2022; Fu, 1990; Li, 2023). However, such discussions have primarily addressed the continuation and development of philosophical traditions and do not by themselves provide a corresponding account of these developments within psychological research.

Against this background, the present article distinguishes two broad orientations in the development of traditional Chinese psychology. Reconstructive transformation refers to work that seeks to reconstruct inherited concepts, theories, practices, ways of knowing, or other resources while remaining grounded in the tradition. By contrast, continuative development refers to work that reopens questions, problems, or practical aims historically addressed by the tradition and allows inherited responses to be further developed. Focusing primarily on continuative development, the article identifies its shared developmental logic, examines the grounds on which it can count as inheritance, and explores its potential contributions. The aim is not to displace reconstructive transformation or to argue that continuative development is inherently superior, but to establish continuative development as an explicit and methodologically defensible research option. In doing so, the article seeks to facilitate further work along this line and advance broader methodological discussions of inheritance and innovation in traditional Chinese psychology. While the present study focuses on traditional Chinese psychology, its framework may also illuminate a broader problem in psychology's engagement with historically inherited intellectual traditions.

In the following sections, the article first illustrates theoretical, practical, and methodological forms of continuative development, classified according to the primary domain of contemporary development, and contrasts each with reconstructive transformation.

Forms of Continuative Development

Theoretical Development

Much contemporary theoretical research drawing on traditional Chinese psychology begins from inherited concepts, constructs, or psychological accounts. Here, theoretical is used broadly to encompass research on concepts, propositions, explanatory models, and other theoretical structures. Representative examples include research on zhongyong thinking (Wu & Lin, 2005), ren (Wang & Wang, 2010), filial piety (Ge, 2021), Junzi personality (Ge et al., 2021), Daoist personality (Tu & Guo, 2016), and Confucian coping (Li & Hou, 2012). Some studies aim to recover classical meanings and structures as faithfully as possible, whereas others explicitly examine how inherited concepts have changed or are understood in contemporary populations. Research on Junzi personality illustrates the former orientation: Ge et al. (2021) sought to clarify the structure and meaning of the Junzi ideal in Confucian thought, with the initial questionnaire items adapted directly from passages in the Analects.

By contrast, research on filial piety has explicitly examined differences between classical and contemporary forms (Ge, 2021), while research on ren has combined classical textual analysis with contemporary understandings and treated its meaning and structure as open to ongoing construction (Wang & Wang, 2010). These studies differ in how much continuity they assume between classical and contemporary forms, but they share a common focus: the inherited construct remains the principal reference point, and the research asks what it meant, how it has changed, how it can be operationalized, or how its contemporary form functions psychologically.

In contrast, Liu and Guo (2016) began not with a single inherited construct to be recovered, but with a long-standing question in Chinese thought: what makes human beings distinctive enough to ground the traditional claim that humans are "supreme" among other beings? They identified multiple historical answers within Chinese intellectual traditions, including accounts based on the endowment of qi, upright posture, labor, social organization, emotion, language, intelligence, and morality, and reconsidered these answers in light of contemporary scientific research. They argued that many capacities traditionally invoked to distinguish humans-such as tool use, social organization, emotion, language-related abilities, and intelligence-also appear in nonhuman animals, even if often in less developed forms, and therefore do not straightforwardly establish a difference in kind. By contrast, they treated morality as a stronger candidate for human distinctiveness, noting that nonhuman animals exhibit altruism, care, and mutual assistance while arguing that human morality involves normative standards and forms of rational self-regulation. They ultimately concluded that morality and faith provided the strongest basis for human distinctiveness, thereby lending renewed support to strands of Confucian thought associated with Mencius and later Neo-Confucian discussions of the human-animal distinction. For present purposes, the important point is not whether this conclusion is ultimately correct, but the structure of the inquiry. Liu and Guo reopened an inherited question and used contemporary evidence primarily to reassess answers that had already emerged within the historical tradition.

Beyond reassessing inherited answers, theoretical continuative development may also offer new answers to unresolved questions within an inherited inquiry. For example, Reber and Slingerland (2011) revisited a Confucian problem: how can deliberate training lead to effortless virtue? In addressing one aspect of this problem-how people can come to love a moral way of life they do not initially embrace-they examined historical internalist, externalist, and rationalist responses and argued that none was fully adequate. Drawing on contemporary cognitive research on processing fluency and affect, they proposed that repeated engagement with rituals and other cultural practices could gradually foster positive feelings toward them. Rather than merely explaining an inherited practice in modern psychological terms, their analysis offered a new candidate answer to a problem left unresolved within the tradition.

Beyond such explicit reconsideration of inherited questions, contemporary research may also substantially reformulate an inherited theoretical account without explicitly restating the inquiry to which it responded. A case in point is modern research on the Five-Pattern account of personality derived from traditional Chinese medicine. The inherited account classified persons into five patterns in terms of differing configurations of yin and yang. As discussed by Yang and Xue (2006), this line of research revised the psychological characterizations of the five patterns and, more fundamentally, reformulated the inherited typology itself. The five patterns were no longer treated simply as mutually exclusive personality types. Instead, a person could exhibit characteristics associated with several patterns to different degrees, and personality characteristics were theorized as reflecting differing proportions of yin and yang.

Taken together, these examples clarify the theoretical form of continuative development. The essential contrast with reconstructive transformation is not that one preserves tradition whereas the other changes it. Rather, reconstructive transformation primarily seeks to interpret, reconstruct, or adapt inherited theoretical content, whereas continuative development treats that content as a revisable response to an underlying question that remains open to further development. Practical Development

Traditional practices such as mindfulness meditation and Tai Ji have entered contemporary psychology in substantially adapted forms (Creswell, 2017; Solloway et al., 2016). Their instructions may be translated into modern language, training may be shortened or standardized, religious or cosmological elements may be reduced, and delivery may be reorganized for clinical or educational settings. Yet such adaptations are typically still presented as modern forms of an inherited practice, with some core operations, meanings, or aims retained as the basis of continuity. Mindfulness provides an especially revealing example. Modern mindfulness-based interventions have secularized and recontextualized Buddhist-derived meditation for contemporary clinical and psychological settings (Creswell, 2017; Kabat-Zinn, 2003). Recent comparative work has continued to evaluate contemporary mindfulness in terms of what is preserved, omitted, or transformed relative to Buddhist conceptualizations, particularly regarding ethical orientation, liberative aims, and the broader system of contemplative practice (Kang et al., 2026). More explicitly, Van Gordon and Shonin (2020) framed second-generation mindfulness-based interventions as a move toward more "authentic" mindfulness practice and teaching by reintegrating Buddhist ethical and contemplative elements. These debates illustrate a reconstructive orientation at the practical level: despite substantial modernization, the inherited practice itself remains the object to be interpreted, adapted, and carried forward.

A different orientation is evident in Tang et al. (2026), who developed a new form of mindfulness of death meditation (MDM) through deliberate practical redesign. Traditional MDM encompasses diverse practices, but many involve deliberately bringing death to awareness and using related contemplation to shape how practitioners live, including by generating urgency and motivation for practice (Buddhaghosa, 2010; Tang et al., 2026).

Drawing on these inherited practices, Tang et al. designed their own meditation scripts and a structured 16-day intervention rather than reproducing any single traditional protocol. This initial step resembles the modern adaptations described above. More consequentially, however, the redesign extended to what the practice was intended to cultivate. The new MDM did not simply seek to intensify the motivational effects of death awareness. The intervention deliberately balanced such arousal against excessive anxiety, while later practice sought to cultivate a calmer attitude toward everyday difficulties by placing them within a broader perspective on life and death. Crucially, these expanded aims were incorporated into the meditation operations themselves through purposefully designed contemplations, rather than appearing only as additional psychoeducation or new applications attached to an otherwise unchanged practice. Furthermore, the study explicitly emphasized the deliberate design and cultivation of responses to death, rather than merely asking whether an inherited MDM protocol was effective. In this case, the inherited practice was treated as a revisable practical response to a broader cultivation concern: how awareness of death might be used to cultivate more desirable responses to life.

Other cases retain the inherited cultivation aim while deliberately redesigning the practical route used to pursue it. For example, Hao et al. (2026) analyzed and compared several classical Daoist meditation practices and then integrated elements from Inner Observation and Sitting in Oblivion to develop a new practice, which they called "Merging Oblivion Meditation." Rather than merely simplifying or combining existing exercises, the authors reconfigured the difficult apophatic operations of Sitting in Oblivion by incorporating guided imagery from Daoist Inner Observation as an alternative route toward self-dissolution and union with the Dao. At the same time, the resulting practice retained a broadly inherited Daoist aim of reducing the sense of self and facilitating union with the Dao, interpreted in contemporary psychological terms as a nondual experience. This case shows that deliberate practical redesign need not require changing the cultivation aim itself: just as theoretical development may ultimately reaffirm an inherited answer, a traditional cultivation aim may be retained while a different practical route toward it is developed.

Taken together, the redesigned MDM and Merging Oblivion Meditation help clarify the practical form of continuative development. This form is not defined by the extent of modification, nor by whether a newly designed practice receives a new name. Rather, it treats an inherited practice as one revisable response to the practical or cultivation task it historically addressed. Contemporary work may retain the inherited aim while developing a different practical route toward it, as in Merging Oblivion Meditation, or may reconsider what the practice itself should cultivate, as in the redesigned MDM. This parallels the theoretical pattern described above: just as theoretical development returns from an inherited answer to the question it addressed, practical development returns from an inherited practice to the practical or cultivation task behind it. Thus, at the practical level, the distinction between continuative development and reconstructive transformation does not turn on whether a different practice is produced. Instead, the inherited practice is not fixed in advance as the practical response that contemporary work must preserve: it may be redesigned, or retained if renewed consideration of the cultivation task still supports it. This helps explain why the two orientations, although analytically distinct, may be difficult to distinguish in individual studies. Reconstructive adaptation can itself involve substantial redesign, while continuative development may ultimately retain much of an inherited practice. Therefore, the resulting protocol alone may be insufficient to determine the orientation of the research; evidence about how the cultivation task was framed and why particular design choices were made may also be needed. As in the theoretical case, how far the practice, its aims, or even the underlying task itself may change while the work still counts as continuation is a boundary issue addressed later.

Methodological Development

Contemporary scholars have also developed psychological methodologies from resources within traditional Chinese psychological traditions. For example, Peng and Hu (2012) proposed neizheng (inner verification). They examined how Confucian, Daoist, and Buddhist traditions had pursued experiential knowledge through practices such as quiet observation, visualization, and meditation, and then abstracted shared features of these practices into a contemporary methodological formulation. Liu (2021 Liu ( , 2022) ) Taken together, transforming these inherited ways of knowing into explicit psychological methodologies is itself innovative, since it requires conceptual clarification, systematization, and adaptation to contemporary research. Yet the orientation remains primarily reconstructive: these proposals are organized around identifying, articulating, formalizing, and further developing ways of knowing already manifested in the traditions, rather than reopening the underlying inquiries within which those ways of knowing were developed.

In contrast, ideal psychology illustrates how a traditional concern with clear methodological relevance can be reopened and further developed into a contemporary methodological framework. Confucian, Daoist, and Buddhist traditions did not merely describe psychological states already present in ordinary life, but systematically pursued ideal mentalities, such as nirvana and tianren heyi (the unity of heaven and humanity), through sustained psychological training (Ivanhoe, 2000; Li et al., 2026; Rahula, 1974; Roth, 2016).

Ideal psychology continues the broader concern with whether and how human mentality can be deliberately transformed toward ideal forms. Correspondingly, it proposes a circular process of design, achievement, and evaluation for ideal mentalities, in contrast to the more conventional sequence of description, explanation, prediction, and control applied to existing mentalities (Zeng, 2025). At the same time, ideal psychology departs from some assumptions embedded in traditional cultivation models. Traditional accounts may present ideal states as having already been realized by exemplary figures-for example, sages in Confucianism and the Buddha in Buddhism (Angle, 2010; Rahula, 1974). Ideal psychology does not attempt to adjudicate these historical claims; methodologically, it treats ideal mentalities as not yet empirically verified as fully realized, making whether and how they can be realized part of the research itself (Li et al., 2026). Furthermore, ideal psychology has subsequently developed from a methodology for studying ideal mentalities into a more general futureoriented approach to psychology (Zeng, 2025). This perspective regards the deliberate design of new and potentially better mentalities as a legitimate aim of psychological inquiry, treats existing mentalities as open to criticism and transcendence rather than as fixed givens, and emphasizes active interventions aimed at realizing such designs and transformations (Tang et al., 2026; Zeng, 2025). More fundamentally, this perspective assumes that the present forms and regularities of human mentality need not define the limits of its future development and therefore commits ideal psychology to the deliberate design and open-ended transformation of mentality (Zeng, 2025). This differs from the traditional cultivation models considered here, which are oriented toward realizing pre-articulated ideals and, in some traditions, toward recovering or fulfilling an inherent human nature (Ivanhoe, 2000; Rahula, 1974; Roth, 2016). In this sense, ideal psychology reopens the traditional concern with whether and how human mentality may be transformed toward better or ideal forms, revises inherited assumptions about its nature, plasticity, and limits, and leaves both its possible future forms and the means of transformation open to further development. The cases above help clarify the methodological form of continuative development.

Reconstructive transformation may identify, articulate, and formalize inherited ways of knowing into explicit psychological methodologies, as illustrated by neizheng and tizheng.

By contrast, continuative development reopens broader concerns associated with traditional resources and further develops their methodological relevance under contemporary conditions. This may involve developing a traditional concern with relatively clear methodological relevance into a new methodological framework, as in ideal psychology, or deriving new methodological questions from inherited concerns whose methodological relevance is less explicit, as in methodological relationalism. Notably, the present discussion does not ask whether neizheng, tizheng, ideal psychology, methodological relationalism, or related proposals are novel relative to the full history of modern psychological methodology.

Its purpose is simply to illustrate different ways in which contemporary methodological development may relate to traditional Chinese psychology. As with theoretical and practical development, how far such methodological development may depart from inherited resources while still counting as continuation is a boundary issue addressed in the next section.

The Boundary of Continuation Continuity at the Level of Inquiry

As repeatedly noted in the preceding section, continuative development may substantially transform what it inherits: theoretical answers may be revised or replaced, practices may be redesigned or their aims reconsidered, and methodological approaches may develop beyond inherited ways of knowing or other traditional resources. These possibilities make a common boundary question unavoidable: if continuation does not require preserving particular inherited responses, what must remain continuous for such developments to count as continuations of traditional Chinese psychology rather than departures from it?

The present article argues that continuative development requires substantive continuity at the level of inquiry. An inquiry refers here to a sustained intellectual or practical endeavor organized around a substantive question, problem, or practical aim. Specific theories, practices, and methodological approaches are responses developed within such an inquiry. Liu and Guo's (2016) discussion of human distinctiveness provides a simple illustration. The inquiry concerns what makes human beings distinctive or especially valuable, while intelligence and morality are possible answers to this inquiry.

On this account, continuative development permits particular responses, as well as the grounds for justifying or preferring them, to be reconsidered in light of contemporary knowledge while the inquiry itself remains continuous. This structure is visible across the cases discussed above. Liu and Guo (2016) reassess inherited answers to this theoretical inquiry. The redesigned MDM revises the practice and its cultivation targets while continuing the practical inquiry into how awareness of death can be used to shape how one lives (Tang et al., 2026). Ideal psychology revises assumptions about the nature, plasticity, and limits of human mentality, develops new methodological procedures, and broadens its scope into a more general future-oriented psychology, while continuing an inherited inquiry into whether and how human mentality can be deliberately transformed toward better or ideal forms (Li et al., 2026; Zeng, 2025). In each case, continuity lies not in preserving a particular inherited response, but in inherited and contemporary responses remaining intelligible as contributions to the same continuing inquiry.

Notably, continuity at the level of inquiry is a substantive rather than declarative requirement: the inquiry need not be explicitly formulated or claimed as such by either historical or contemporary researchers. It may also be reopened less explicitly through the reasoned revision of an inherited response, as illustrated by the Five-Pattern case discussed above. At the same time, not every change to an inherited response constitutes continuative development. Some changes may arise within reconstructive transformation itself, for example through inaccurate reconstruction, semantic drift, or alterations introduced by measurement and operationalization. Such changes do not by themselves indicate continuative development and may instead be better understood in terms of the fidelity concerns noted earlier (Liu, 2019; Tu & Guo, 2016). Furthermore, research may deliberately examine how an inherited construct is understood differently under contemporary conditions and even identify elements that were not present in its classical formulation. For example, Ge's (2021) research on filial piety explicitly examined historical-contemporary differences and identified a dimension centered on accompanying parents that was largely absent from the classical texts examined. Such work may provide important materials for further development, but the fact that a construct has changed does not by itself establish that the underlying inquiry has been reopened. That further step requires treating inherited and contemporary formulations as revisable responses within an identifiable continuing inquiry, rather than simply describing their differences.

Development of the Inquiry Itself

If continuation depends on continuity at the level of inquiry, a further question is whether the inquiry itself may also develop. Inquiries are historically situated, and changes in knowledge, concepts, social conditions, and competing intellectual traditions may bring into view questions that earlier formulations did not explicitly address. Such development is already evident within Chinese intellectual history. Song-Ming Confucianism did not simply preserve the questions and formulations of pre-Qin Confucianism; in responding to new intellectual challenges, especially those posed by Buddhism, Confucian thinkers further developed questions concerning mind, human nature, cultivation, and the relation between moral life and the larger order of reality (Angle & Tiwald, 2017; Hon, 2023). Contemporary continuation of traditional Chinese psychology may develop in a similar way. For example, traditional pursuits of ideal mentalities were often justified within the normative and metaphysical frameworks of the traditions themselves. In contemporary pluralistic settings, especially in psychological interventions that do not presuppose a specific traditional background, an important question is how to justify the pursuit of such ideal mentalities beyond their endorsement within the originating traditions (Liu & Zeng, 2023).

At the same time, such extension is not arbitrary. What is required is an intelligible developmental transition: a new question should arise from the inherited inquiry in a way that can be explained by changes in knowledge, concepts, social conditions, or practical requirements, and should become relevant to what must now be addressed for that inquiry to be pursued adequately under contemporary conditions. The value-justification problem noted above provides one example. By contrast, merely examining how a reconstructed traditional construct relates to new contemporary variables, or applying it to a population or setting that historical thinkers did not study, may generate genuinely new research questions, but such novelty does not amount to an extension of the inherited inquiry in the sense of continuative development.

Distinguishing Continuation from Other Engagements with Tradition

If continuative development allows inherited responses to be revised and even an inherited inquiry itself to be extended, a further boundary question arises: would any modern psychological work influenced or inspired by traditional Chinese thought qualify as continuative development? In other words, what is required for a particular work to count as continuative development rather than merely being influenced by or drawing inspiration from the tradition? The present article argues that what distinguishes continuative development from other forms of engagement with tradition is a developmental relation to an inherited inquiry: contemporary work must remain substantively connected to an inquiry historically pursued within the tradition and further develop it under contemporary conditions. This depends on what the contemporary work actually does-how it engages an inherited inquiry and how it further develops that inquiry-not on whether its authors describe their project as a continuation. Traditional materials may also contribute to modern psychology in other ways, such as by confirming an independently developed view, providing an analogy, or serving as a conceptual resource. Such contributions do not constitute continuative development unless a developmental relation to an inherited inquiry can also be established.

A clear example is Rogers's reception of Daoist thought within his own personcentered psychology. Hermsen (1996) notes Rogers's association of wu-wei with the attitude of the facilitator in encounter groups and identifies important limitations and misunderstandings in Rogers's interpretation of Daoism. Wu-wei, however, was historically embedded in a broader Daoist inquiry concerning effortless action, self-cultivation, and the proper relation between human agency and the Way (Hermsen, 1996; Slingerland, 2003). For present purposes, the key issue is not whether Rogers interpreted Daoism accurately, or whether he explicitly described his work as a continuation, but what role the inherited Daoist inquiry played in the psychological development itself. In Hermsen's account, Rogers drew on wu-wei primarily as an intellectual resource for articulating the attitude of person-centered facilitation, rather than reopening and further developing the inherited question to which wuwei was itself a response. The case therefore illustrates how traditional thought may genuinely influence modern psychology without the resulting development constituting continuative development.

A different kind of contribution appears when traditional material is used to provide historical exemplification or support for a modern psychological construct. Csikszentmihalyi (1990), for example, interpreted yu in the Zhuangzi through the concept of flow and treated Cook Ding as an early example of the same experiential dynamics. The comparison is not groundless: both descriptions involve highly skilled, absorbed, and apparently effortless activity. However, more recent comparative work has emphasized that the Cook Ding episode is embedded in a broader Daoist understanding of life, skilled action, and the relation of the person to the social and natural world, whereas flow is formulated primarily within an individual psychological framework of optimal experience and enjoyment (Herzog et al., 2024). In this context, Cook Ding provides a historical analogy and illustration for flow, but the psychological inquiry is not a further development of the inherited inquiry to which the episode contributed. Therefore, it does not constitute continuative development in the sense proposed here.

Continuing an Inquiry and Continuing a Tradition

A further issue concerns the relation between continuing an inherited inquiry and continuing the tradition in which that inquiry historically developed. First, must a contemporary development derive primarily or exclusively from the inherited tradition in order to count as its continuation? Second, must the resulting theory, practice, or methodological approach remain identifiable with that tradition, or may a line of continuation eventually contribute to a more general or cross-traditional framework? These questions concern different ends of the developmental relation-where a continuation may draw its resources from, and where it may ultimately lead.

Regarding its sources, continuative development does not require exclusive derivation from the inherited tradition. Contemporary work may draw simultaneously on multiple intellectual traditions. What matters is not whether the inherited tradition is the sole or even dominant source, but whether its inquiry remains a substantive line of development within the resulting work. The historical development of Song-Ming Confucianism provides a familiar precedent: substantial engagement with Buddhist thought introduced new conceptual resources and challenges while Confucian thinkers continued to develop questions inherited from earlier Confucianism (Angle & Tiwald, 2017; Hon, 2023). Similarly, ideal psychology explicitly acknowledges multiple sources for its future-oriented perspective, including traditional Chinese psychology, Marxist psychology, Platonism, transhumanism, and the development of artificial intelligence (Li et al., 2026; Zeng, 2025). This plurality does not negate its continuative relation to traditional Chinese psychology, because the inherited inquiry into whether and how human mentality can be deliberately transformed toward better or ideal forms remains a substantive line of its development, as discussed above. In principle, this continuative relation to traditional Chinese psychology does not preclude the same framework from also standing in continuative relations to other traditions, provided that each relation can likewise be established.

The second question concerns where continuative development may ultimately lead.

A line of continuation need not remain culturally specific to the tradition from which it develops. For example, ideal psychology recognizes traditional Chinese psychology as an important foundation of its development, while explicitly positioning itself as a general future-oriented psychological approach rather than one limited to Chinese or Eastern psychology (Li et al., 2026). The Values in Action (VIA) classification provides an even clearer example, because the resulting framework is explicitly cross-traditional rather than remaining within any one inherited tradition. In developing the VIA classification, Peterson, Seligman, and colleagues drew on a cross-traditional search for recurrent virtues across Confucianism, Daoism, Buddhism, and several other philosophical and religious traditions (Dahlsgaard et al., 2005; Peterson & Seligman, 2004). The recurrent virtues identified through this work contributed directly to the construction of a general psychological framework in which 24 character strengths were organized under six broad virtues. The resulting framework, in turn, provided a basis for subsequent empirical research on character strengths and their relations to psychological outcomes (e.g., Park et al., 2004). In this way, inherited answers concerning virtue and human excellence helped shape a broader contemporary psychological inquiry, even though the resulting framework was no longer specific to any one historical tradition. Continuative development may therefore lead to a general or cross-traditional framework while still retaining a substantive developmental relation to inherited inquiries.

At the same time, moving beyond a tradition-specific framework may come at a cost.

Critics of positive psychology have argued that extracting common virtues across traditions can obscure culture-specific meanings and the larger conceptual structures in which particular virtues are embedded, and some have questioned whether ostensibly universal positivepsychological frameworks nevertheless retain Western individualistic assumptions (e.g., Christopher & Hickinbottom, 2008). These concerns are important, but they do not by themselves negate a continuative relation. A development may qualify as continuative because it genuinely extends an inherited inquiry, while still being criticized for simplifying, decontextualizing, or marginalizing distinctive elements of the tradition. Thus, continuing an inquiry from a tradition should be distinguished from continuing that tradition as a culturally distinctive intellectual tradition. The former concerns a developmental relation; the latter additionally concerns what is preserved, transformed, or lost in the course of that development.

Notably, some contemporary projects explicitly impose this additional tradition-level requirement. Contemporary Confucian philosophers, for example, have argued that substantial innovation should remain connected to central Confucian commitments, inherited sources, or internal lines of development (e.g., Angle, 2022; Li, 2023). The psychological work examined in this article generally does not impose an analogous requirement. Whether this contrast reflects broader differences in disciplinary aims, intellectual histories, or simply the current development of these fields remains unclear and falls beyond the present analysis.

It is therefore treated here as a descriptive rather than normative observation: the present account does not claim that psychology ought either to preserve or to relinquish traditionlevel identity. Whether maintaining or further developing such identity is itself an important goal is a separate question considered in the next section.

Contribution of Continuative Development From Inherited Resources to Ongoing Knowledge Generation

Existing discussions of traditional Chinese psychology have already treated engagement with tradition as a project of contemporary knowledge development rather than merely historical recovery (Liu, 2018; Luo & Lin, 2026; Ye & Su, 2023). Reconstructive transformation and continuative development share this orientation, and both involve innovation. However, they differ in what is treated as open to further development, with consequences for the range of knowledge that subsequent work can generate.

As illustrated above, reconstructive transformation takes inherited theories, practices, ways of knowing, or other traditional resources as its principal objects. It can produce genuinely new psychological knowledge. The operationalization of inherited concepts, empirical investigation of traditional theories, application and adaptation of practices under modern conditions, and psychological research drawing on traditional ways of knowing can all generate findings and applications unavailable to the historical tradition. Yet their primary task remains to interpret, reconstruct, adapt, or investigate the inherited resources themselves.

By contrast, continuative development reopens the underlying inquiry, so that the historically inherited repertoire no longer defines in advance the range of responses that subsequent development may take. Contemporary work may introduce new candidate answers, develop new practical responses, generate new methodological approaches, or even address new questions by extending the inquiry itself as discussed above. In this sense, continuative development makes the inherited repertoire a starting point rather than the boundary of further theoretical, practical, and methodological development, thereby opening a broader space for innovation.

Additionally, continuative development may contribute to reconstructive transformation. Establishing a continuative relation typically requires attention not only to particular inherited responses, but also to how those responses were situated within a broader pattern of questions, competing answers, assumptions, objections, and later developments. This broadens the historical and theoretical materials available for reconstructive transformation. More importantly, attending to these relations can in turn deepen the reconstruction of particular theories or constructs by clarifying why they were proposed, what problems they were intended to address, and how their meanings depended on surrounding theoretical or ethical commitments. This is especially important given existing concerns about decontextualized reconstruction in traditional Chinese psychology (Liu, 2019; Tu & Guo, 2016).

Further Development of Inherited Inquiry

Continuative development can also promote further examination of issues already embedded in inherited inquiries. Research that draws on traditional resources may legitimately select those concepts, theories, or practices that are useful for a particular contemporary purpose. However, when researchers seek to continue an inherited inquiry, assumptions, tensions, or practical difficulties that bear directly on the proposed development cannot simply be set aside. For example, Zhou et al. (2024) note that attempts to cultivate ideal mentalities drawn from traditional sources raise questions about whether such ideals are attainable, what intermediate stages should count as meaningful progress, how attainment should be assessed, and how potentially conflicting value commitments should be handled.

Addressing such questions does more than adapt inherited resources for present use; it can also feed contemporary psychological findings back into the inherited inquiry, helping to clarify its claims, evaluate unresolved problems, and revise inherited assumptions where necessary.

Continuative development also allows psychology to participate in the continuing development of the broader Chinese intellectual tradition from its own disciplinary position.

Themes historically addressed in traditional Chinese thought, such as human nature, moral cultivation, and ideal forms of personhood, have continued to develop after the emergence of modern academic disciplines. For example, modern New Confucian and contemporary Confucian philosophers have continued to develop such issues primarily through conceptual and normative argument, including renewed accounts of moral psychology, cultivation, sagehood, and the good life (e.g., Angle, 2010 Angle, , 2022;; Li, 2023). Such developments are important continuations of the inherited intellectual tradition, but they do not exhaust how these inquiries may develop under modern conditions. Contemporary psychology can participate in them in its own ways, through psychological theorizing, empirical investigation, the design and evaluation of practices, and methodological development.

Psychology therefore need not merely translate conclusions reached elsewhere into psychological terminology; it can contribute additional questions, evidence, practices, and methodological approaches to inquiries whose historical development preceded the modern disciplinary division between philosophy and psychology.

Notably, this implies a different stance toward tradition itself. If traditional Chinese psychology is approached only as a body of inherited theories, practices, ways of knowing, and other resources to be reconstructed for present use, it can easily appear as something historically completed. Continuative development instead treats inherited inquiries as capable of acquiring new answers, practices, methodological approaches, and even new questions in the present. Contemporary psychological work can thereby become part of the intellectual inheritance that later researchers may themselves interpret, challenge, and further develop. In other words, traditional Chinese psychology is not only something contemporary psychology receives from the past, but also something to whose future development it may contribute.

Cultural Continuity and Global Contribution

As discussed above, the outcome of continuative development need not remain culturally specific to the tradition from which it develops. However, maintaining and further developing the tradition while preserving its cultural and intellectual distinctiveness may itself remain an important goal. In contemporary China, building an independent knowledge system in philosophy and social sciences has become an explicit intellectual and institutional goal, within which the inheritance and further development of fine traditional Chinese culture are explicitly emphasized (Ministry of Education of the People's Republic of China, 2022).

Similar concerns are also visible in Indian and Islamic psychology, where the development of modern psychological knowledge is explicitly connected to inherited intellectual traditions and their continued development (e.g., Cornelissen et al., 2014; Rassool, 2023). Thus, the importance of sustaining inherited psychological traditions as distinctive intellectual traditions is not unique to the Chinese case.

Specifically, reconstructive transformation already contributes to the inheritance and development of such traditions by recovering, articulating, and adapting inherited resources for contemporary use. Continuative development can further extend these contributions: by reopening inherited inquiries, it allows new answers, practices, methodological approaches, and even new questions to become part of a tradition's subsequent intellectual history. More fundamentally, it can change how contemporary researchers understand their relation to tradition: rather than seeing themselves only as interpreters or users of a historical legacy, they may also understand their work as participating in its ongoing intellectual development.

For the tradition itself, this means that it may remain a living intellectual project rather than merely a body of inherited achievements. Looking further ahead, because both responses and inquiries may continue to develop under changing historical conditions, continuative development gives the tradition an open-ended capacity for future development, so that its future need not be confined to what it has already produced. Additionally, by organizing otherwise fragmented lines of research around the inquiries they address, continuative development may also support the construction of more coherent knowledge systems grounded in historically inherited traditions.

At the same time, cultural continuity need not imply intellectual closure.

Contemporary discussions of China's independent knowledge system have also emphasized the importance of producing knowledge that is grounded in Chinese intellectual resources while contributing to broader academic inquiry and addressing questions of wider human significance (Ministry of Education of the People's Republic of China, 2022). This outward orientation resonates with indigenous, decolonial, and global approaches to psychology, which have challenged the dominance of a single intellectual tradition and sought to broaden psychological knowledge by bringing culturally grounded theories, epistemologies, and practices into the discipline (e.g., Berry, 2013; Bhatia et al., 2025; Blume & Diop, 2025).

Continuative development can further expand such contributions by generating new theories, practices, methodological approaches, and questions that were present neither in the historical tradition nor in existing psychology. By doing so, it not only introduces distinctive intellectual resources from particular traditions, but also generates new psychological knowledge that may ultimately extend beyond those traditions' original cultural contexts.

Conclusion

Contemporary psychological research increasingly draws on intellectual and practical resources inherited from historical traditions. Yet the boundary between inheritance and innovation remains insufficiently clear, especially when inherited responses themselves are substantially revised. Existing methodological discussions have often centered on fidelity to inherited meanings and structures. The present article extends this discussion by examining another way in which traditions may be inherited and further developed.

First, through cases of theoretical, practical, and methodological development, the present article conceptualizes continuative development as a mode of inheritance distinct from reconstructive transformation. Its defining feature is continuity at the level of inquiry rather than the preservation of particular inherited responses. Inherited responses may therefore be revised, and the inquiry itself may even develop to address new questions under changing conditions.

Second, this account clarifies the relation between inheritance and innovation.

Substantial innovation need not constitute a departure from inheritance simply because inherited responses are changed; conversely, not every innovation inspired by a tradition counts as its continuation. Continuative development requires an intelligible developmental relation to an inherited inquiry, thereby providing a basis for distinguishing continuation from mere influence or use of traditional resources.

Third, the framework highlights a broader role for tradition in contemporary psychology. Tradition need not function only as a repository of inherited resources for present use, but can also remain a site of ongoing knowledge generation. Contemporary psychology can thereby not only inherit traditional psychological inquiry, but also contribute to its future development by generating new theories, practices, methodological approaches, and even new questions. Such developments may in turn support cultural continuity while generating contributions to psychology that extend beyond the traditions' original cultural contexts.

It is also worth noting that the present analysis is conceptual and illustrative rather than a systematic review of research engaging with traditional Chinese psychology. The cases examined here should therefore not be taken to exhaust the possible forms or instances of continuative development. Nevertheless, the present article brings together and analyzes a set of studies that have not previously been examined as sharing a common developmental structure beyond the reconstruction of inherited responses. By identifying this structure and conceptualizing it as continuative development, the article seeks to encourage researchers to further develop inherited inquiries more deliberately and to provide a basis for broader methodological discussion of inheritance and innovation in traditional psychology.
