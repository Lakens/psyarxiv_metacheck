Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Marginal Significance works

List all sentences that describe an effect as ‘marginally significant’.

The marginal module searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The list of terms is a subset of those listed in a blog post by Matthew Hankins. The module returns all sentences that match terms describing ‘marginally significant’ results.

Some of the terms identified might not be problematic in some contexts, and there are ways to describe ‘marginal significance’ that are not detected by the module.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. Some differences come from the ground truth: it lacks p-values with a correction name (“p Bonf = .061”), and counts partial eta squared written with its subscript after it (“η 2 p = .05”), “p = .05”, and some table notes and figure legends (“† p < .10, *p < .05”), which this module leaves out, as non-significant p-values. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

The Effect Size module checks if effect sizes are correctly reported in t-tests and F-tests.

The Effect Size check searches for regular expressions that match typical ways in which effect sizes are reported. It subsequently checks different ways in which Cohen’s d, g, ηp2, and ωp2 can be computed against the reported value. If effects are missing, or might be incorrect, you the module provides a warning. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. Abstract

While the benefits of physical activity for physical and mental health are well-established, it is less clear whether it also boosts resilience, defined as preserved mental health after exposure to stressors. Resilience in adolescents and young adults is critical given the high prevalence and lasting impact of mental health problems that emerge during development. Despite increasing recognition of the importance of physical activity for resilience, a structured overview is missing.

Therefore, a systematic review of the literature was conducted including records published until December 2025 in PsycInfo, PubMed, Embase and Web of Science. We included 90 intervention and longitudinal studies on physical activity, and resilience in adolescents and young adults (10-25 years) without a diagnosed mental health problem. In general, controlled intervention studies (n=39) showed various physiological and psychological benefits of physical activity in youth exposed to adversity and stressors. Intervention studies without a control group (n=26) and longitudinal observational studies (n=25) yielded comparable results. These findings imply that physical activity may causally impact resilience in youth exposed to adversity and stressors. It should be noted, however, that more preregistered, well-powered studies are required to corroborate these conclusions before formulating clinical or policy recommendations.

Relationship between physical activity and resilience to stressors in non-clinical samples of adolescents and young adults a systematic review We all encounter some degree of stress in our daily lives; whether it is the looming pressure of upcoming exams, navigating social conflicts with peers, parents, or siblings, or coping with more significant adversities such as the loss of a close family member. While sometimes individuals appear to weather these challenges with little disruption to their mental well-being, other times they may struggle, highlighting variation in how people adapt to stressors. Such variability is referred to as resilience and understanding how we can boost resilience would buffer against decreases in mental health at a societal level. It also highlights resilience is not a static trait, but a dynamic process that unfolds over time and across contexts.

Although there might be temporally stable factors that make resilient outcomes more likely (e.g., certain cognitive skills or personality traits), these resilience factors are not to be confounded with resilience itself. Hence, in this study, we consider resilience as a dynamic process and define it as preserved mental health, or quick recovery thereof, after exposure to adversity (Kalisch et al., 2017).

How resilience is best measured, however, is far from trivial. Following Kalisch et al. (2017), resilience is best understood as a dynamic process that must be studied longitudinally.

This has clear methodological implications: resilience is modelled as the differential change in mental health or well-being as a function of exposure to a stressor, requiring multiple assessments of mental health or well-being and a gradual measure of adversity exposure as well.

At the time of their review, only a handful of studies were available where resilience was operationalised in accordance with their rationale (Kalisch et al., 2017). In the current study, we adopt and extend this definition, including not just changes in mental health after exposure to serious adversity (e.g., accidents, catastrophes, loss of a significant other) but also to minor stressors in daily life (Zietse et al., 2025) and in laboratory settings (Skoluda et al., 2015). Our rationale for this is twofold. First, the intra-individual mechanisms triggered by adversity and stressors likely overlap despite their different timescales. Second, evidence shows that exacerbated psychological reactivity to minor stressors can culminate into long-standing mental health problems (Cohen et al., 2005; Mandel et al., 2015; Parrish et al., 2011; van Nierop et al., 2018; Van Rijsbergen et al., 2013).

While exposure to adversity or any stressor is critical in this framework, their definition and measurement remain heterogeneous in the literature. Here, we define a stressor as any event that causes the stress response, which is "a perturbation of our mental and physical equilibriums " (p. 331, Hermans et al., 2025) with physiological, behavioural, and phenomenological components. Stressors are usually adverse, uncontrollable, and unpredictable (Hermans et al., 2025; Mineka & Kihlstrom, 1978), and may come in various 'flavours', including physical (e.g., cold, heat, noise), psychological (learned response), social (e.g., being excluded or dominated by others), and direct challenges to cardiovascular or metabolic homeostasis (e.g., exercise, hypoglycaemie; Pacák & Palkovits, 2001). To acknowledge this variability, in the current study, we considered resilience in the context of this wide array of stressors.

Resilience during adolescence and young adulthood is particularly important since this period is marked by significant biological, social, and emotional changes (Worthman & Trang, 2018) and enhanced environmental sensitivity (Fuhrmann et al., 2015; Sisk & Gee, 2022). This a highly vulnerable period as three quarters of mental health disorders commence before the age of 25 (Kessler et al., 2005 (Kessler et al., , 2007) ) and globally, one in seven adolescents is estimated to experience mental health conditions (WHO, 2025). Similarly, young adults' well-being has been a growing concern (Sharp & Theiler, 2018), with more than half of young adults reporting experiencing mental health problems in some countries such as the Netherlands (RIVM, 2023).

These mental health issues developed during youth have a substantial impact on daily life and later development (Thapar et al., 2012) and increase the risk of psychopathology later in life (Clayborne et al., 2019; Kim-Cohen et al., 2003). Therefore, a better understanding of resilience and causal resilience factors in adolescents and young adults is a public health priority.

Physical activity might be a resilience factor that helps youth cope with stress. This idea is grounded on meta-analyses showing an association between physical activity and mental health in children and youth during the COVID-19 pandemic (B. Li et al., 2023) and positive effects of school-related physical activity interventions on the mental health of children and adolescents (Andermo et al., 2020). Physical activity may exert its beneficial impact on mental health via modulating the physiology of the stress response and psychological mechanisms of stress coping. More specifically, the Cross-Stressor Adaptation (CSA) hypothesis (Hamer et al., 2006; Sothmann, 2006) proposes that regular exercise, a (homeostatic) stressor in itself (Pacák & Palkovits, 2001), leads to biological adaptations. These, in turn, contribute to a reduced physiological reaction of the sympathetic nervous system (SNS) and the hypothalamicpituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis to stressors in general (Hamer et al., 2006; Tsatsoulis & Fountoulakis, 2006). A reduction in physiological stress reaction is reflected by lower stress reactivity (i.e., the deviation from the physiological baseline is smaller) and faster stress recovery (i.e., less time is needed to return to the physiological baseline) (Forcier et al., 2006).

In addition, physical activity could also boost resilience at the psychological level. For example, it may increase self-esteem and self-efficacy (Joseph et al., 2014; McAuley et al., 2000; Ryan, 2008) and a wide array of psychosocial factors that are potentially mediated by physical fitness (McAuley et al., 2000; Sonstroem, 1984).

Despite the increasing recognition of the importance of physical activity for improved resilience, a structured overview of existing empirical studies is lacking. Here, we aim to consolidate the available evidence on how physical activity is related to coping with stressors and preserving mental health. Therefore, we conducted a systematic review on how physical activity relates to resilience, in non-clinical populations of adolescents and young adults. In line with the rationale advocating for studying resilience as a process (Kalisch et al., 2017), we focus on longitudinal studies and intervention studies with and without controls.

3.1 Intervention Studies With a Control Group

Among the 39 intervention studies with a control group, 12 focused on adolescents, 26 on young adults, and 1 on both. The studies differed in the type of outcome measure: Six used a biological or physical measure for resilience, 16 a psychological measure, and 17 a combination of those.

Biological Outcomes.

The six studies that used a biological measure all focused on young adults (average age of 19 -24) and were conducted in Western countries (USA, UK, Germany, and the Netherlands). Four of the six studies had only male participants. All used a mental and/or physical laboratory stressor. Physical activity was an intervention in all studies, except for Huang et al. (2014), who compared low versus high fit groups (N = 17 each) based on maximum oxygen consumption (VO2 max).

Three of these studies found an association between physical activity and stress reactivity. More specifically, a small-scale longitudinal training study by Claytor (1991) showed an attenuation in the cardiac output response to a behavioral challenge following 10 weeks of cycle ergometry and treadmill running. In addition, in a randomized controlled trial (N = 42 per group; Wunsch et al., 2019), both acute and habitual exercise were related to a reduction in stress activation as measured using salivary alpha-amylase and cortisol. Only habitual exercise was related to lower peak cortisol levels. More recently, in a randomized crossover trial (N = 17), Chauntry et al. (2023) found that interrupting prolonged sitting with frequent light intensity body-weighted resistance activity resulted in lower reactivity to stressors as measured by systolic blood pressure and inflammatory markers. There was no significant difference in cortisol markers.

One study (de Geus et al., 1990) reported mixed results: Pre-existing differences in aerobic fitness were related to physiological response patterns to stress, but a 7-week training program was not. However, the study had a relatively small sample (N = 14 in the training and N = 8 in the waiting list control group).

Finally, a few studies yielded null results. In Huang and colleagues' (2014) study, reactivity to a combination of mental and physical stressors (compared to physical stressors alone) did not differ between individuals with low vs. high fitness. We note that the critical appraisal score for this study was low, as it did not report a power calculation and drop-out rates, for example (see Table 2). However, in a study with a larger sample (M. C. Thomas & Kamarck, 2023; N ≈ 60 per group), physical activity in the form of treadmill walking was also not associated with blood pressure responses to stress.

In conclusion, while the overall picture seems mixed, if we concentrate on the three studies with relatively high quality scores (Chauntry et al., 2023; M. C. Thomas & Kamarck, 2023; Wunsch et al., 2019; see Table 2a)), both short-term and habitual exercise might have a positive influence on stress reactivity, but light exercise might not be sufficient.

Psychological Outcomes. There were 16 controlled intervention studies that used solely self-reported psychological outcome measures, most of which were conducted in Western countries (n = 13; mostly USA) and three in non-Western countries (Brazil, China, and Liberia).

Most focused on young adults, five on adolescents and one on both adolescents and young adults.

All 16 studies included randomization, except for Glaser et al. (2022 Glaser et al. ( , 2024)), which were nonrandomized repeated measures-controlled trials (see Table 2a).

In all cases, the controlled intervention targeted physical activity. However, interventions were rather heterogeneous regarding their timescales and specificity. These included exercise sessions lasting 15-30 minutes in a lab setting (Bernstein et al., 2020; Bernstein & McNally, 2018; Jacquart et al., 2020; Morais et al., 2025) and yoga, dance, and exercise courses with multiple sessions spreading multiple weeks or months (e.g., Chang et al., 2022; Friedman et al., 2022; Marenus et al., 2023; Pandya, 2022 Pandya, , 2024;; Tao et al., 2025; D. G. Thomas et al., 2025), However, other studies evaluated more complex intervention programs that spanned months or a year and included additional psychoeducational or psychosocial components (Beaman et al., 2021; Glaser et al., 2022 Glaser et al., , 2024;; Greco, 2021; Wilson et al., 2025). About half of the studies applied an active control (e.g., stretching), while the other half used a passive control (e.g., waiting or unspecified).

In 12 studies, the stressor exposure was an observed variable, either operationalized as being (at risk of) exposed to various forms of adversity (Beaman et al., 2021; Glaser et al., 2024; Greco, 2021; Pandya, 2022 Pandya, , 2024;; Tao et al., 2025; D. G. Thomas et al., 2025; Wilson et al., 2025) or experiencing the COVID-19 pandemic (Chang et al., 2022; Friedman et al., 2022; Glaser et al., 2022; Marenus et al., 2023). Four studies induced stress in a lab setting with a standardized protocol (Bernstein et al., 2020; Bernstein & McNally, 2018; Jacquart et al., 2020; Morais et al., 2025), yet none of them had a control condition for the stress intervention.

Overall, physical activity interventions seemed to carry a wide range of psychological benefits in individuals (potentially) exposed to adversity. These include increased self-reported resilience scores (Glaser et al., 2022; Greco, 2021; Pandya, 2022 Pandya, , 2024)), prosocial behaviour (Pandya, 2024), fortitude (Pandya, 2024), self-efficacy (Greco, 2021), life satisfaction (Glaser et al., 2024), well-being (Chang et al., 2022), improved emotion regulation and affective responses (Morais et al., 2025; Tao et al., 2025) as well as reduced psychosomatic symptoms (Glaser et al., 2024) and stress (Chang et al., 2022). On the other hand, there were some inconsistent findings but those mainly came from low-quality studies (Friedman et al., 2022; Glaser et al., 2022; Marenus et al., 2023; Wilson et al., 2025). What is more, in a study, physical activity (reaching daily step targets) following two days of rest after mild traumatic brain injury did not have a positive influence on the quality of life and even led to increased symptoms (D. G. Thomas et al., 2025), indicating that there also might be situations in which physical activity could be harmful.

Finally, there was one large-scale intervention field study that found that taking part in the program did not impact resilience (Beaman et al., 2021).

Lab-based studies using social stressors have nuanced this picture by shedding light on which psychological processes physical activity might influence. After exercise, maladaptive emotion regulation interfered less with emotional recovery after the stressor (Bernstein & McNally, 2018), and self-criticism was not as strongly associated with other aspects of rumination (such as negativity, brooding, replaying, and perseveration) (Bernstein et al., 2020). Additionally, when people were instructed to reappraise their arousal, either alone or combined with exercise, this was related to more adaptive stressor appraisals and quicker recovery after stressor exposure (Jacquart et al., 2020). All in all, these few studies hint that exercise might change how people regulate their emotional responses to minor social stressors.

Biological and Psychological Outcomes. Seventeen intervention studies with control groups employed both biological and self-reported psychological outcome measures. Seven included adolescents; 10 involved young adults. Studies with adolescents were conducted in highincome countries (Spain, USA, Germany, Switzerland, and Australia) and included one nonrandomized controlled trial (San Juan et al., 2008), four RCTs (Fishbein et al., 2016; Lambiase et al., 2010; Mucke et al., 2020; Smith et al., 2025), and two randomized crossover studies (Hanke et al., 2023 (Hanke et al., , 2025)). Most samples had balanced gender distributions, except one trial that included boys only (Mucke et al., 2020). Stressors included real-life challenges-pediatric bone marrow transplantation (San Juan et al., 2008) -and school-based stressors in high-risk adolescents (Fishbein et al., 2016) as well as laboratory stress induction paradigms such as the Trier Social Stress Test for Children, psychosocial stress tasks, and a cognitive Stroop task (Hanke et al., 2023 (Hanke et al., , 2025;; Lambiase et al., 2010; Mucke et al., 2020; Smith et al., 2025).

The types of interventions that were used varied to a large extent and included an 8-week intrahospital strength and aerobic program (San Juan et al., 2008), a 7-week school-based yoga program (Fishbein et al., 2016), single-session simulated active versus passive commuting (Lambiase et al., 2010), cycling versus a resting control condition (Hanke et al., 2025; Mucke et al., 2020), and comparison of various intensity physical activity groups (Hanke et al., 2023; Smith et al., 2025). Physical activity was measured objectively in four studies (Hanke et al., 2023 (Hanke et al., , 2025;; San Juan et al., 2008; Smith et al., 2025) and subjectively in two (Lambiase et al., 2010; Mucke et al., 2020), while one combined these approaches (Fishbein et al., 2016). Outcome measures included health-related quality of life, mood, externalizing problems, regulatory processes, perceived stress, psychological distress, and biological stress reactivity (cortisol, alpha-amylase, heart rate, heart rate variability, perceived stress).

Results consistently indicated that higher or increased physical activity was associated with more adaptive stress responses. Children receiving intrahospital exercise training showed greater quality-of-life improvements than healthy controls (San Juan et al., 2008). The yoga intervention produced some stress-related benefits, though broader emotional and behavioral outcomes did not differ significantly from care-as-usual (Fishbein et al., 2016). Simulated active commuting reduced cardiovascular and perceived stress reactivity compared with passive commuting (Lambiase et al., 2010), and higher habitual activity predicted lower cortisol reactivity to psychosocial stress (Hanke et al., 2023). Acute cycling before stress affected alpha amylase, but not heart rate responses (Mucke et al., 2020). It also positively influenced cortisol reactivity, but only in cases with high levels of chronic stress (Hanke et al., 2025). Light or moderate to vigorous activity did not influence cortisol reactivity, perceived stress or psychological distress in Smith et al. (2025), but this could be due to the small sample size (N = 12-13 per group). Overall, structured programs and higher habitual activity generally reduce biological stress reactivity and improve resilience-related quality of life, though effects on self-reported emotional and behavioral outcomes remain mixed.

The 10 studies conducted in young adults recruited primarily university students or healthy volunteers in Spain, the USA, Germany, Belgium, Australia, Canada, and China. Some studies included only women (Rogers et al., 2023) or only men (von Haaren et al., 2016; Zschucke et al., 2015). Seven were parallel-group RCTs (Calvo et al., 1996; Hartig et al., 2003; Luo et al., 2025; Morava et al., 2025; Rogers et al., 2023; von Haaren et al., 2016; Zschucke et al., 2015), two used a counterbalanced crossover design (Benvenutti et al., 2017; Schoonjans et al., 2025), and one a quasi-experimental design (Periasamy et al., 2025). Activity intensity ranged from single exercise or yoga bouts to multi-week aerobic, cycling or yoga programs (4-24 weeks). Stressors included both real life stressors, such as adverse childhood experiences (Rogers et al., 2023) and study-related stress (Periasamy et al., 2025; von Haaren et al., 2016), and laboratory paradigms such as mental arithmetic, the Montreal Imaging Stress Task, the Paced Auditory Serial Addition Task, the Trier Social Stress Test, and viewing negative versus neutral images (Benvenutti et al., 2017; Calvo et al., 1996; Hartig et al., 2003; Luo et al., 2025; Morava et al., 2025; Schoonjans et al., 2025; Zschucke et al., 2015).

Interventions included structured aerobic training, yoga, cycling and (treadmill) walking. Physical activity was assessed via objective fitness indices (Luo et al., 2025; Morava et al., 2025; Periasamy et al., 2025; Schoonjans et al., 2025; von Haaren et al., 2016; Zschucke et al., 2015) or subjective reports and treatment assignment (Benvenutti et al., 2017; Calvo et al., 1996; Hartig et al., 2003; Rogers et al., 2023). Outcomes included psychological measures (e.g., Connor-Davidson Resilience Scale, state anxiety, stress appraisal) and biological indices (cardiovascular and inflammatory markers, cortisol, alpha-amylase, brain activation).

In general, data from these studies suggest that physical activity often improves stressrelated outcomes. Eight weeks of structured exercise reduced serum endothelin-1 and improved resilience and hope in women with adverse childhood experiences (Rogers et al., 2023). Twenty weeks of aerobic training enhanced autonomic stress reactivity during examination stress (von Haaren et al., 2016). Acute exercise before stress reduced cortisol responses, particularly in participants who had higher aerobic fitness (Zschucke et al., 2015). It also decreased mind wandering, but did not affect stress responses (Morava et al., 2025). A 12-week program reduced cognitive and behavioral anxiety responses to mental arithmetic, especially in women (Calvo et al., 1996). Walking in natural settings increased positive affect and reduced anger following prior stress (Hartig et al., 2003). A single yoga session did not alter acute cardiovascular stress reactivity (Benvenutti et al., 2017), but a 24-week yoga program did clearly improve perceived stress and cortisol levels (Periasamy et al., 2025). A 4-week cycling intervention improved state anxiety levels and reward sensitivity (Luo et al., 2025) and slow-paced breathing after a cycling intervention reduced worry and cardiovascular reactivity (Schoonjans et al., 2025). Taken together, physical activity interventions in young adults appear to improve psychological resilience indicators and reduce physiological stress reactivity (Calvo et al., 1996; Hartig et al., 2003; Luo et al., 2025; Periasamy et al., 2025; Rogers et al., 2023; von Haaren et al., 2016; Zschucke et al., 2015), though evidence for the effects of a brief yoga intervention remain inconsistent (Benvenutti et al., 2017).

Study Quality and Conclusion. We noticed some general patterns in study quality (see Table 2a and Figure 2a). A clear strength of the field is that almost all studies used reliable and validated measures, and most studies were randomized (although the quality of the randomization, or its reporting, varied). Due to the nature of physical activity interventions, blinding of participants and individuals delivering the intervention is very difficult if not impossible, making it hard to fully exclude any expectation or placebo effects, even in the presence of a well-thought control condition. There is room for some improvement regarding adherence to intervention protocols, which was often not reported, except for laboratory studies.

A serious limitation of the field is that, with one exception (Schoonjans et al., 2025), none of the studies reported that it was preregistered and only a few reported a power calculation. Furthermore, only one study adopted a process-based approach to resilience (Beaman et al., 2021) while most relied on self-reported resilience scores or changes in mental health but without considering variation in stressor exposure, going against the rationale advocated by Kalisch and colleagues (2017).

In conclusion, the intervention studies with a control group showed that exercise positively influences stress reactivity. This was found in studies with biological, psychological or combined outcome measures and in both adolescents and young adults. There seemed to be a few exceptions, however, as light exercise or a brief yoga intervention had no significant effect in a few studies. Based on lab-based studies, one of the mechanisms behind this could be that exercise changes how people regulate their emotional responses to minor social stressors.

3.2 Intervention Studies Without a Control Group

In this review, 26 intervention studies without a control intervention group were included. These studies used different designs; 13 studies used a within subject design of which ten studies described a single group that all received the same intervention (no wait-list control or control experiment; Cruce et al., 2024; Draper et al., 2024; Gatz & Kelly, 2018; Godfrey et al., 2015; Hilgendorf, 2017; McKenzie et al., 2021; Olson et al., 2024; Pinellas, 2021; Popescu et al., 2025; van der Mee et al., 2023), while three studies used a within-subjects experimental design, in which participants completed exercises with different levels of physical activity in a counterbalanced order (Bernstein & McNally, 2017a; Lowe et al., 2017a; Webb et al., 2011).

Additionally, 13 included studies used a between-subjects design. Those studies provided the same intervention to two or more subgroups (e.g., athletic versus non-athletic participants) and compared results among those subgroups (Britton et al., 2019; Claytor, 1991; Claytor et al., 1988; Gerber et al., 2017; Gogola et al., 2025; Hu et al., 2024; Klaperski et al., 2013; Lackner et al., 2016; McCubbin et al., 1992; Rimmele et al., 2007; Smits et al., 2011; Spalding et al., 2000; Vetter et al., 2010).

Of the 13 studies using a within-subjects design, seven focused on adolescents aged 10 to 18 years (Cruce et al., 2024; Draper et al., 2024; Gatz & Kelly, 2018; Godfrey et al., 2015; Hilgendorf, 2017; McKenzie et al., 2021; Pinellas, 2021) and six on young adults aged 18 to 25 years (Bernstein & McNally, 2017b; Lowe et al., 2017a; Olson et al., 2024; Popescu et al., 2025; van der Mee et al., 2023; Webb et al., 2011). Biological Outcomes. There were no studies included that used a biological measure to assess resilience in adolescents. In young adults, there was only one study that used a biological measure (Lowe et al., 2017b). Lowe et al. A Stroop task was used to quantify both the initial perturbation and subsequent recovery effect on inhibitory control. Findings of this study supported the hypothesis that exercise promoted cortical flexibility, especially in the brain regions that support inhibitory control.

Psychological Outcomes.

Nine studies used a psychological outcome measure to assess resilience, of which six focused on adolescents (Cruce et al., 2024; Draper et al., 2024; Gatz & Kelly, 2018; Godfrey et al., 2015; McKenzie et al., 2021; Pinellas, 2021) and three focused on young adults (Bernstein & McNally, 2017b; Olson et al., 2024; Popescu et al., 2025). All studies, except one (Cruce et al., 2024) showed a positive effect of physical activity on resilience or mental health outcomes after the administration of naturally occurring or laboratory-induced stressors. Various outcome measures for resilience and mental health were used (e.g., self-reported Brief Resilience Scale, Perceived Stress Scale, the Positive Affect and Negative Affect Schedule -Child Form [PANAS-C] -see Table 3b) and positive effects were shown both in adolescents and young adults. In all studies, the intervention focused on physical activity with interventions with varying frequency; from three laboratory visits with counterbalanced inactive, active and moderate exercise conditions (Bernstein & McNally, 2017b), to 8 sessions of yoga (Pinellas, 2021), to 12 x 2-hour sessions of bilateral movement for 12 times (Draper et al., 2024). The interventions described varied from surf-therapy (Godfrey et al., 2015; McKenzie et al., 2021), to triathlon training and group fitness (Gatz & Kelly, 2018) and online yoga sessions (Cruce et al., 2024; Pinellas, 2021). Only the study of Cruce et al. (2024) showed no significant changes in psychological outcomes from baseline to end of survey, but drop-out was substantial from baseline to post-intervention (i.e., 83%; from 99 at baseline to 16 post-intervention). Participants that attended all nine yoga sessions did state that they were likely to use yoga in the future to deal with stress and to calm down.

Using a within-subjects design, there were three studies that examined biological and psychological outcomes simultaneously (Hilgendorf, 2017; van der Mee et al., 2023; Webb et al., 2011). The results of Webb et al. (2011) suggest that a stressor may have a more pronounced effect when performing physical activity. More specifically, they found that the initial mental challenge might have enhanced the overall adrenal response to the subsequent low-level physical challenge, suggesting that activities requiring low-level physical activity that is preceded by mental stress could be impacted by enhanced cortisol responses.

Van der Mee et al. Study Quality and Conclusion. Considering all 13 intervention studies using a withinsubjects design, a strength of the included studies is the clarity of the study question, clear description of the intervention and the use of valid and reliable outcome measures (see Figure 2b). But most did not report whether all eligible participants were enrolled and often it was not possible to blind the people assessing the outcomes because many studies used self-reported questionnaires. Notably, two of the three larger studies showed a positive effect of physical activity on resilience (Bernstein & McNally, 2017b; Godfrey et al., 2015) while the one by Van der Mee (2023) did not find evidence that physical activity decreased the overall stress response. Overall, the quality of these three larger studies was not different from the other intervention studies without control group (see Table 2b). Furthermore, physical activity was mainly measured using subjective measures; only Webb et al., (2011) employed an objective measure of physical activity.

In conclusion, experimental studies that used a within-subject design a clear beneficial effect of PA on resilience in terms of psychological outcomes, both in adolescent and young adults. There was only one study that focused solely on a biological outcome, also showing that exercise promoted cortical flexibility. The results from the three studies combining biological and psychological measures of resilience were mixed, implying that timing of the physical activity might be critical.

3.2.2 Studies Using a Between-Subject Design all Receiving Same Intervention

We reviewed 13 studies that used a between-subjects design and provided the same intervention to two or more subgroups and compared results among those subgroups (Britton et al., 2019; Claytor, 1991; Claytor et al., 1988; Gerber et al., 2017; Gogola et al., 2025; Hu et al., 2024; Klaperski et al., 2013; Lackner et al., 2016; McCubbin et al., 1992; Rimmele et al., 2007; Smits et al., 2011; Spalding et al., 2000; Vetter et al., 2010). The majority of these studies focused on young adults; only three studies focused on adolescents (Gogola et al., 2025; Hu et al., 2024; Vetter et al., 2010). Six studies included both genders (Britton et al., 2019; Gerber et al., 2017; Gogola et al., 2025; Hu et al., 2024; Smits et al., 2011; Vetter et al., 2010), the two studies of Klaperski et al. (2013) and Lackner et al. (2016) only included females, and only males were included in five studies (Claytor, 1991; Claytor et al., 1988; McCubbin et al., 1992; Rimmele et al., 2007; Spalding et al., 2000). All studies were conducted in Western countries (see Table 3b) such as the USA, UK (Britton et al., 2019), Switzerland (Gerber et al., 2017; Rimmele et al., 2007), Poland (Gogola et al., 2025) and the Netherlands (Hu et al., 2024). Total sample size varied from 16 participants (Claytor et al., 1988) to 168 participants (Lackner et al., 2016). Again, in the following paragraphs the studies will be described based on the outcome measures that were used in the studies: biological measures, psychological measures, or both.

Biological Outcomes.

Four studies used a biological outcome to measure resilience and focused on young adults (Britton et al., 2019; Claytor, 1991; Claytor et al., 1988; McCubbin et al., 1992). There were no studies in adolescents using just a biological outcome measure. In the studies, a division was made between fit or trained athletes and untrained participants. Those two groups participated in the same intervention varying from computerized arithmetic tasks (McCubbin et al., 1992) to a cold pressor test (Britton et al., 2019). In Britton et al. (2019), no significant differences in heart rate variability were observed between the rest and task periods, or the task and post-task periods. In Claytor et al. (1991), the trained group showed lower heart rate, and higher resting stroke volume and cardiac output at baseline compared to the untrained group. In Claytor (1988), there were no differences in cardiovascular response to laboratory stressors depending on aerobic fitness. Finally, in McCubbin et al., (1992), opioidergic modulatory effects on stress reactivity may comprise an important mechanism in fitnessassociated risk reduction for cadiovascular disease (fit participants showed less stress reactivity than non-fit participants). On the whole, elite sportsmen showed reduced reactivity to the psychosocial stressor, characterized by lower adrenocortical, and psychological stress responses.

These results suggest that physical activity may provide a protective effect against stress-related disorders. Overall, although not unanimous, the included studies provide some evidence for showing that those more fit or trained show less pronounced biological responses to stressors.

Psychological Outcomes.

Three studies focused on adolescents (Gogola et al., 2025; Hu et al., 2024; Vetter et al., 2010) and only Smits et al. (2011) focused on young adults. In the study of Gogola et al. (2025), adolescents with lower-limb disability who experienced transient injuries during training (pain group) were compared with those who did not. In Hu et al. (2024), a subgroup of adolescents with lower and higher resilience levels were identified, while in Vetter et al. (2011) there was a group of hostages and a group of non-hostages in a terrorist attack. In the study on young adults by Smits et al. (2011) different levels of physical activity were compared. In three out of four studies a positive effect of physical activity on resilience was found. In the study of Gogola et al. (2025) adolescent with lower-limb disability who experienced transient injuries during the training (pain group) showed fewer negative emotions related to disability after 39 sessions of sit-down wake boarding. This effect was significantly larger in the pain group than in the no-pain group. In the study of Smits et al. (2011), physical activity was significantly related to stress reactivity, but only among persons with elevated levels of anxiety sensitivity and at high levels of physical activity, not at low levels of physical activity. Finaly, in Vetter et al. (2010), at post-intervention and six-month follow-up, resilience scores increased compared to the baseline after a resilience enhancement program camp.

Hostages and participants in the highest losses and injuries categories had lower resilience scores at baseline and after the end of the program and greater gains in resilience six months after the completion of the program compared to non-hostages and participants in the lowest losses and injuries categories. However, the resilience program tested in Hu et al. (2024) did not result in a significant overall improvement of psychological resilience. Still, the participants with lower resilience scores at baseline, showed an increase in the sense of relatedness levels on the post-test, indicating that adolescents with lower resilience levels may be more responsive to supportive environments compared to adolescents with higher resilience scores at baseline.

Overall, the studies in the psychological outcomes domain indicate largely positive outcomes, particularly for those most susceptible (in terms of those (a) experiencing pain from transient injuries, (b) more sensitivity to anxiety, and (c) being held hostage). Some results also show that higher doses of physical activity may be more beneficial.

Biological and Psychological Outcomes. Although there were no studies reporting both biological and psychological outcomes in adolescents, there were five studies reporting both resilience outcomes in young adults (Gerber et al., 2017; Klaperski et al., 2013; Lackner et al., 2016; Rimmele et al., 2007; Spalding et al., 2000). In all five studies, participants of different types of activity were compared. In Rimmele et al. (2007), trained men exhibited significantly lower cortisol and heart rate responses to the stressor compared with untrained men. In addition, trained men showed significantly higher calmness and better mood, and a trend toward lower state anxiety during the stress protocol. In Gerber et al. (2017), it was found that young adults with low vigorous physical activity levels and high levels of perceived stress were associated with an increased salivary cortisol response, but not heart rate, to stress. They also experienced less favorable affect throughout the entire testing session. Similarly, in Klaperski et al. (2013), physiological stress reactions were lower in the vigorously active group after the stress induction compared to the inactive and moderately active groups. Psychological stress reactions deteriorated under stress for the vigorously active group, indicating a dissociation between physiological and psychological stress responses. Again, similarly, in Lackner et al. (2016), non-exercising women showed a stronger second accelerative component of the transient heart rate response to the terrifying scenes in a film compared to regularly exercising women, indicating greater sensitivity of the avoidance (defensive) motivational system and heightened sensitivity to aversive stimuli. Moreover, more frequently exercising participants reported least mood deterioration compared to less frequently exercising and nonexercising participants. In Spalding et al. (2000), no effect was found: Aerobic capacity was not related to reactivity and recovery results were similar. The study indicates that trained individuals do not experience less subjective stress compared to the untrained ones.

Overall, although somewhat mixed findings, we see that biologically, those more active show more reduced reactions to stress. Although this could also generalize to the psychological level, these results were less clear.

Study Quality and Conclusion.

Considering all 13 studies that used a betweensubjects design, two of the larger studies showed positive effects of physical activity on resilience (Lackner et al., 2016; Smits et al., 2011), while Hu et al. (2024) showed no significant overall improvement of psychological resilience, but only in terms of relatedness levels for those with lower resilience scores at baseline. The quality of these three studies was moderate, as representativeness of the sample for the population of interest was often not reported (except for Smits et al., (2011), and sample size calculations were lacking. However, these data quality indicates were not very different from the other between subjects studies, as they often lacked in the other studies as well (see Table 2b and Figure 2b). Overall, whether people assessing the outcomes were blinded to the participants' group status was often not reported. For studies using self-reported questionnaires for psychological outcomes, blinding was not possible. Outcome measures were often taken multiple times after the intervention, but not multiple times before the intervention.

In conclusion, the between-subjects studies generally showed that biologically, those more active showed less reactivity to stressors. In the studies that solely used a psychological measure of resilience most studies also showed that physical activity helped to improve psychological resilience. However, for the studies that combined biological and psychological measures, results for the biological measures were positive (PA improved resilience) but for the psychological measures results were less clear.

3.3 Longitudinal Studies

Across all included longitudinal studies, we identified 13 articles focusing on adolescents and 12 focusing on young adults. None of the studies among adolescents used a biological outcome measure. Among the studies focusing on young adults, nine used solely psychological outcome measures while three used both psychological as well as biological outcomes.

Studies examining physical activity and resilience during adolescence were methodologically heterogeneous. Designs ranged from short-term two-wave studies with follow-up periods of several weeks to multi-wave prospective cohort studies spanning multiple years, including recent three-wave cohort studies. Sample sizes varied substantially, from small, often high-risk cohorts (n = 48-262) to five large population-based samples (n = ~2,000 -12000). Mean baseline age of participants ranged approximately between 12 and 16 years, covering early to late adolescence and most studies included roughly equal proportions of boys and girls (see also Table 3c). Studies comprised between two and four assessment waves, with intervals ranging from several weeks to multiple years. Physical activity was typically assessed using self-report measures capturing participation, frequency, or intensity. Outcome measures covered a broad spectrum of mental health and well-being indicators. Several studies focused on resilience in the context of major stressors, including the COVID-19 pandemic (Chen et al., 2022; H. Li et al., 2025; J. Thomas et al., 2021), adverse childhood experiences (Easterlin et al., 2019; Goff, 2017), parental depression (Collishaw et al., 2016; Padaigaite-Gulbiniene et al., 2024), poverty (Gillis et al., 2023), homelessness (J. Thomas et al., 2021), and illness (Richard et al., 2018). Others examined resilience during more normative developmental transitions, such as the transition from early to middle adolescence (Marquez et al., 2023) or entry into high school (Ruffin, 2013).

Studies focusing on young adults likewise employed diverse methodological approaches. Designs ranged from several intensive daily-diary studies spanning 14 to 21 consecutive days to two-or three-wave longitudinal studies conducted over several months including studies covering different phases of the COVID-19 pandemic (including prepandemic, pandemic, and post-restriction periods). Sample sizes ranged from small and medium-sized university or community samples (n = 56-274) to large multi-site or populationbased cohorts of more than 1,000 participants. Across studies, mean ages generally ranged between 18 and 25 years, although some studies included adults up to their early forties.

Samples were generally female-skewed, with women comprising approximately 55-65% of participants (see Table 3c). Longitudinal studies included two or three survey waves separated by several weeks to more than a year, whereas diary studies relied on repeated daily assessments. Most studies examined resilience in the context of the COVID-19 pandemic and related lockdowns, restrictions, and lifestyle disruptions (Ma et al., 2023; Romm et al., 2021; Shanahan et al., 2022; Turner et al., 2023; Wong et al., 2023). Other stressors included major life transitions such as entry into medical school (Kötter et al., 2016) or completion of tertiary education (Lines et al., 2021), as well as social media use (Fruehwirth et al., 2024), prolonged academic stress (Reschke et al., 2024), examination periods (O'Flynn et al., 2018), daily stressors (Flueckiger et al., 2016) and minority status as a psychosocial stressor (Bai & Yang, 2025). Physical activity was assessed almost exclusively through self-report instruments capturing frequency, intensity, active days per week, or perceived change in physical activity, although one study employed accelerometry-based daily step counts. Outcome measures encompassed a broad range of psychological and well-being indicators, including depressive symptoms, internalizing symptoms, psychological distress, affect, general mental health, subjective well-being, and resilience. Three studies additionally incorporated biological outcomes.

Psychological Outcomes. 13 studies focused on psychological outcomes in adolescents. Among adolescents in Sweden, Chen et al. (2022) observed increased stress and psychosomatic symptoms over time, but found no differential impact of COVID-19 exposure, nor significant associations between PA and resilience-related outcomes across the two-year period. Similarly, in a three-wave U.S. cohort, Gillis et al. (2023) found no significant associations between PA and changes in internalizing symptoms, nor evidence that PA buffered the impact of income-related stress. In contrast, a large three-wave Chinese cohort study conducted during the later stage of the COVID-19 pandemic found that lack of exercise was one of several factors predicted membership of dysfunctional depression and anxiety trajectories, suggesting that physical activity may protect against persistent mental health problems under prolonged pandemic-related stress (H. Li et al., 2025). Likewise, a smaller UK study among homeless youth during the early COVID-19 lockdown (J. Thomas et al., 2021) detected beneficial effects: increases in overall PA and moderate PA were associated with improved wellbeing across a short-term interval.

More consistent positive associations emerged in studies examining adversity exposures. In two independent cohorts of adolescents with a parental history of depression (Collishaw et al., 2016; Padaigaite-Gulbiniene et al., 2024), higher PA participation (typically measured as frequency of sports or moderate-to-vigorous activity) predicted greater mental health resilience. Specifically, adolescents who consistently exercised were more likely to maintain good mood-related mental health over time (Collishaw et al., 2016), and PA contributed uniquely to a broader constellation of protective factors associated with mental health resilience (Padaigaite-Gulbiniene et al., 2024). Studies among adolescents exposed to Adverse Childhood Experiences (ACEs) demonstrated similar patterns: in the Add Health cohort, Easterlin et al. (2019) found that team sport participation predicted significantly lower odds of depression and anxiety well into adulthood among ACE-exposed youth. In a smaller sample of previously institutionalized youth, higher PA at baseline was associated with fewer depressive symptoms at both timepoints (Goff, 2017). Moreover, in the LONGSCAN cohort, participation in organized activities (including sports) at age 16 predicted better academic performance and fewer internalizing and externalizing problems despite prior maltreatment exposure (Kwak et al., 2018).

Studies examining resilience-related processes during typical developmental transitions also identified protective effects. In the large #BeeWell dataset, Marquez et al. (2023) showed that higher PA predicted better mental health resilience over one year in early to middle adolescent transition, even when controlling for other internal and external protective resources, with slightly stronger effects among boys. However, PA did not significantly predict life satisfaction resilience after adjustment. Similar findings were reported by Ruan et al. (2026), who studied Chinese left-behind children aged 9-14 years during the transition from childhood to adolescence. These children remain in rural communities while one or both parents migrate for work. In this population, higher levels of physical activity were associated with more favorable developmental trajectories of depressive symptoms. Conversely, in a U.S. sample transitioning from middle to high school, PA did not predict depressive symptomatology across one year (Ruffin, 2013), suggesting that effects may depend on the specific outcome domain, measurement approach, or contextual factors.

Finally, a small perioperative cohort study (Richard et al., 2018) focusing on adolescents undergoing hip preservation surgery provides a unique perspective: although the primary intervention was medical and interdisciplinary rather than PA-based, improvements in physical function and return to activity were accompanied by reductions in anxiety, emotional problems, and stress-related symptoms, aligning with broader evidence that regained or maintained physical activity capabilities support psychological recovery.

Nine studies focused on psychological outcomes in young adults. Several studies reported associations between PA patterns and changes in depressive or internalizing symptoms in the context of the COVID-19 pandemic. In a retrospective three-period assessment during lockdown periods in 2022 in China, Ma et al. (2023) found that shorter daily exercise time predicted increases in depression severity in the second and third period. Similarly, in a U.S.

multi-city cohort, Romm et al. (2021) observed that young adults who increased PA during the early COVID-19 period experienced less deterioration in depressive symptoms between survey waves than peers who decreased activity. In Switzerland, Shanahan et al. (2022) reported that higher engagement in PA during the 2020 lockdown was associated with lower internalizing symptoms and lower perceived stress at follow-up, despite PA being measured at only one timepoint. Comparable results were found in Hong Kong, where Wong et al. (2023) showed that reductions in vigorous PA predicted increased psychological distress during the Omicron outbreak, with PA exerting both independent and interactive effects alongside rising smartphone overuse. In a U.S. university cohort studied before and throughout the COVID-19 pandemic, Fruehwirth et al. (2024) identified that higher social media use predicted declines in PA and poorer mental health, whereas PA itself represented a protective factor within an evolving digital and pandemic context.

Beyond COVID-related contexts, intensive daily diary designs demonstrated robust within-person associations between PA and affective reactivity to daily stressors. In two studies spanning 14 and 21 days, Flueckiger et al. (2016) found that on days involving higher stress, individuals who engaged in more PA reported greater positive affect and less negative affect, indicating that daily fluctuations in PA buffer stress-related emotional responses. Similarly, a cohort of first-year medical students showed that higher PA at university entry predicted better general and mental health at the end of the freshman year (Kötter et al., 2016), suggesting that PA is a moderating resource during demanding academic transitions. Similarly, in a three-wave study among Chinese university students, Bai and Yang (2025) found that higher levels of physical exercise predicted subsequent improvements in subjective well-being over a ninemonth period, suggesting that regular physical activity contributes to sustained psychological well-being.

One study did not provide clear evidence for protective effects of physical activity. Reschke et al. (2024) found no consistent longitudinal associations between physical activity, study-related stress and well-being among a small sample (n=56) of German law students preparing for a prolonged high-stakes examination. Associations varied across time points without a clear longitudinal pattern.

Taken together, longitudinal studies among adolescents indicate that while associations between PA and resilience-related mental health outcomes are not entirely consistent, a substantial proportion of studies, particularly those involving adversity-exposed or vulnerable adolescents, identify PA as a protective factor for resilience-related mental health outcomes.

Among young adults, eight out of nine studies observed a longitudinal association between physical activity and mental health indicators, whereas two studies did not find clear patterns.

Psychological and Biological Outcomes. A smaller subset of studies (Lines et al., 2021; O'Flynn et al., 2018; Turner et al., 2023) incorporated both psychological indicators and biological resilience measures. Two studies used accelerometry to capture PA objectively.

Turner et al. (2023) compared three university cohorts assessed before, during, and after the initial COVID-19 onset. Across cohorts, young adults who developed depressive symptoms exhibited fewer daily steps at baseline, and step counts declined with the onset of pandemic restrictions. While the study's primary focus was on genetic and psychosocial predictors of stress resilience, they also observed covariation between reduced PA and emerging mood symptoms. Similarly, in an Australian measurement-burst study, Lines et al. (2021) found that higher perceived daily stress was associated with lower engagement in both light and moderateintensity activity on the same day. Although PA was not the central resilience variable, the daylevel coupling between stress and reductions in objectively captured PA suggests that periods of higher stress coincide with lower levels of physical activity. One study incorporated biological stress indices directly. O'Flynn et al. (2018) assessed cortisol awakening response (CAR) during an examination period in addition to psychological symptoms. Elevated anxiety and poorer mood during exams were accompanied by decreased PA and altered CAR profiles. Although causality cannot be inferred, the combined changes in PA, affect, and biological stress markers illustrate how reductions in activity can coincide with heightened physiological stress reactivity during acute academic pressure. Together, these studies demonstrate that when biological measures are included, PA emerges as part of a coordinated pattern of behavioral and physiological stress responses. Objectively lower PA levels tend to co-occur with elevated stress physiology or emerging mood symptoms, providing convergent evidence across measurement modalities.

Study Quality and Conclusion.

Considering the longitudinal studies, several general patterns in study quality were observed in both studies with adolescents and young adults (see Table 2c and Figure 2c). Most studies clearly described their research question and study population and used clearly defined and consistently implemented measures of mental health outcomes. In most studies, physical activity was assessed prior to the mental health outcome, although it was mostly measured using self-report. Limitations often included incomplete reporting of participation and representativeness, and substantial loss to follow-up (exceeding 20%). Repeated assessment of stressor exposure and adjustment for potential confounding variables varied across studies.

In conclusion, across both developmental groups, the majority of studies indicated that higher levels of physical activity were associated with more favorable mental health trajectories under stress, although methodological approaches and stressor types varied substantially.

4. Discussion

While the benefits of physical activity for physical and mental health are wellestablished, it is less clear whether physical activity boosts resilience in youth. Therefore, in this systematic literature review, we filled this gap and reviewed 90 intervention and longitudinal observational studies of adolescents and young adults. Overall, these studies suggest that engaging in physical activity yields a wide range of psychological and biological benefits in individuals exposed to stressors and adversity. The strongest evidence comes from controlled intervention studies, implying that physical activity may causally impact resilience in youth.

This picture was corroborated by findings from intervention studies without a control group and longitudinal observational studies, which yielded associations consistent with a causal effect of physical activity on resilience. The convergence across physiological and psychological levels of analysis and various timescales is especially promising given the multifaceted nature of the stress response and suggest that physical activity might exert its benefits via multiple mechanisms.

Several potential mechanisms may account for the beneficial effect of physical activity on resilience. First, in line with the Cross-Stressor Adaption (CSA) hypothesis, in betweensubject studies, those more fit or physically active in general showed less pronounced biological responses to stressors (Gerber et al., 2017; Klaperski et al., 2013; Lackner et al., 2016; McCubbin et al., 1992; Rimmele et al., 2007). This supports the tenet of the CSA hypothesis that regular exercise leads to biological adaptations that contribute to a reduced physiological reaction of the SNS and HPA axes to stressors (Hamer et al., 2006; Tsatsoulis & Fountoulakis, 2006). Second, exercise might change how people regulate their emotional responses to minor social stressors. Lab studies suggested that exercise might prevent negative emotional cascades by shaping the dynamics of maladaptive emotion regulation, halting their downstream effects (Bernstein et al., 2020; Bernstein & McNally, 2017b , 2018; Jacquart et al., 2020). Studies into the psychological impact of daily stressors provide further support for this conjecture. For example, in the study of Greco et al. (2021), psychoeducational activities combined with physical exercise training and team games seemed to improve resilience and self-efficacy of adolescents, and made them less likely to behave aggressively or be bullied. Moreover, longitudinal studies suggest that increasing physical activity may buffer the adverse effects of stress on positive and negative affect (Flueckiger et al., 2016). Third, exercise might be a "resource" for increased self-esteem and self-efficacy. For example, Gatz & Kelly (2018) showed that in girls aged 11-14 years at risk for low self-esteem, a sedentary lifestyle, and/or classification as overweight, a triathlon training and health promotion program resulted in increased confidence and self-acceptance. Girls learned to self-regulate learning and set goals that promoted fitness, better attitudes and resilience.

Our systematic review has limitations in terms of scope, confounding factors and quality of included studies. First, it remains to be established whether our conclusions generalize to clinical populations, as studies of adolescents and youth with diagnosed mental disorders were not included. It is possible that such individuals would present a stronger or more pervasive stress response that is harder to regulate via exercise. Additionally, since mental health problems co-occur with lower socioeconomic status and urban deprivation (Reijneveld & Schene, 1998; Xu et al., 2023), individuals with a psychiatric diagnosis might have less opportunity for engaging in physical activity due to socioeconomic and urban constraints.

Second, we did not assess whether observational studies accounted for key potential confounders, as a wide range of studies with various independent and dependent variables were included hindered the comparison of confounder adjustment. Finally, future studies should apply quantitative meta-analytic tools to summarize effect sizes, estimate their uncertainty, characterize their heterogeneity, and evaluate a potential publication bias in the literature. Our systematic review provides a strong foundation for such work.

Regarding the quality of the available evidence, the picture is rather mixed. A key strength of the work surveyed was that most studies used reliable and validated measures and interventions, facilitating the accumulation and synthesis of knowledge. However, for the field to move forward, there is a clear need for more preregistered, well-powered studies.

Furthermore, we urge authors and journals to report all the relevant information on study quality, in particular the participation and dropout rates, representativeness, adherence, and details of blinding and the randomization process (see Figure 2a -2c). While ensuring the availability of this information is necessary for building a solid evidence base, it was missing from a large fraction of the studies. Finally, barely any of the reviewed intervention studies operationalized resilience by quantifying the association of repeated and graded measures of stressors with mental health, as recommended by the process-based resilience framework (Kalisch 2017). An exception was a study evaluating the Sports for Change program in thousands of children in Liberia (Beaman 2021). Notably, a couple of longitudinal studies followed this principle (Collishaw 2016; Shanahan 2022; Wong 2023; Flueckiger et al. 2016; Fruehwirth et al. 2024; Lines et al. 2021). Considering the questionable validity of one-shot, self-reported measures of resilience (Kalisch 2017), most of the published literature might be limited in terms of construct validity, that is, whether they indeed captured resilience. This identifies a specific and crucial methodological improvement for future work, especially for intervention studies.

Reviewing the literature also revealed several critical knowledge gaps. First, no study has directly compared how physical activity impacts resilience across different timescales. For instance, engaging in sport during a crisis might be helpful in the short-term by regulating the physiological stress response and providing a momentary relief by means of distraction. At the same time, in the long-term, it might also build resilience factors by strengthening coping resources such as self-esteem and self-efficacy, not to mention slower physiological adaptations.

Future studies integrating lab-based and daily life resilience assessments would facilitate understanding how the benefits of physical activity unfold at multiple timescales. In addition, no study has systematically differentiated whether physical activity is particularly useful against specific types of stressors in humans, e.g. physiological, physical, or psychosocial. However, such research would require procedures that can reliably elicit and measure specific stress pathways in isolation, which might be challenging to design and raise concerns about ecological validity, as stressors rarely occur in isolation. Finally, most studies were conducted in Western, high-income countries. It remains to be evaluated how the present findings generalize to countries where the natural and built environment might imply different constraints to engage in physical activity, where cultural habits and norms regarding physical activity and fitness might be different, or where different coping strategies might be more prevalent.

The present review also has implications for policy and practice. Although, collectively, the available evidence suggests that physical activity may support resilience following adversity, the overall quality and methodological rigor of the current literature remains mixed. While we would suggest that physical activity remains justified given its well-established general physical and mental health benefits (Andermo et al., 2020; B. Li et al., 2023), stronger evidence is needed before making stronger, more specific recommendations regarding physical activity interventions to foster resilience during or after adversity in youth. We consider investing in large-scale, pre-registered, adequately-powered intervention studies a priority, as this will help build an evidence-base that ensures future policy recommendations to be grounded in robust evidence. Put more broadly, progress in this field will depend on closer integration of exercise science, stress physiology, psychology, and mental health research to develop a comprehensive understanding of how physical activity shapes resilience across biological and psychological levels of functioning in youth.

In conclusion, this systematic review indicates that physical activity is associated with a wide range of psychological and biological benefits in non-clinical/mentally healthy youth exposed to adversity and stressors. Future studies should repeatedly assess both exposure to stressors and mental health outcomes over time, especially for intervention studies.

Furthermore, examining the effectivity of physical activity when exposed to different types of stressors could provide valuable insights into potential protective effects. Finally, preregistered, high powered studies and rigorous adherence to data quality reporting guidelines are critical for advancing the field.
