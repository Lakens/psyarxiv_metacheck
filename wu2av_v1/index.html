To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 6 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Highlights: 1) Eye-tracking responses to semantic expectancy violations and self-referential processing successfully classified depression diagnosis and risk with up to 83.8% accuracy using machine learning models.

2) Saccade dynamics (particularly displacement and velocity measures), entropy, and pupil dilation emerged as key features distinguishing individuals with depression from healthy controls.

3) Self-referential stimuli elicited slightly stronger predictive signals for depression classification, highlighting the importance of personalized cognitive-affective processing in objective assessments.

Introduction

Major depressive disorder (MDD) manifests as changes in mood, sleep, and cognitive function, among other symptoms (American Psychiatric Association, 2013). In 2020, 18.5% of the US population had received a diagnosis of depression (Lee, 2023), resulting in large health and economic impacts (McLaughlin, 2011). Currently, screening relies on self-report of symptoms, which are susceptible to misreporting (e.g., due to stigma, cognitive impairments, or difficulty expressing oneself). An objective method to assess symptoms of depression would improve clinical diagnoses, especially for individuals who are unable to report symptoms or motivated to underreport their symptoms.

Multiple lines of research have investigated whether a relatively unobtrusive and comfortable sensor, such as eye tracking, could be used to objectively diagnose depression. Some have used simple gaze-based tasks, such as pro-saccade and anti-saccade tasks or executive function tasks (e.g., Stroop, trail-making, or digit-span tasks). Most found behavioral differences (e.g., reaction time) (Barsznica et al., 2021a; Carvalho et al., 2015). Although some found shorter saccade duration and lower peak saccade frequency during a smooth pursuit task, results for eye tracking data were inconsistent (Takahashi et al., 2021a).

Most studies using affective stimuli have employed facial images to examine eye-tracking differences between individuals with depression and healthy controls (HCs). Kellough et al. (2008) studied participants experiencing a major depressive episode and HCs with no depression history. Participants viewed sad, threatening, positive, and neutral faces. Individuals with depression showed more fixations on negative faces, fewer on positive faces, and were more likely to direct their first fixation toward threatening faces. Similarly, Lu et al. (2017) asked MDD and HC participants to view paired images of the same individual displaying neutral and emotional expressions. Patients with MDD exhibited a negative attentional bias.

These findings demonstrate reliable attentional biases in depression, particularly increased attention to negative stimuli and reduced attention to positive stimuli. Building on this foundation, recent work has applied machine learning to eye tracking data to detect individuals with depression. For example, Xinfang et al. (2019) showed participants four valanced images at a time, including dysphoric, threatening, neutral, and positive images achieving 65% accuracy using Random Forest. Likewise, Stolicyn et al. (2022) achieved 65% accuracy using eye-tracking during cognitive tasks with emotional distractors (neutral, positive, and negative words).

However, these studies focus on attentional allocation to emotional stimuli and do not address whether individuals with MDD differ in their expectations of positive or negative outcomes. Evaluating gaze responses to affective expectancy violations may provide greater sensitivity by directly probing predictive neurocognitive processes. To our knowledge, only one eye-tracking study has applied this approach using affectively valent lexical stimuli designed to violate expectations (Kobo et al., 2024). Using sentences with congruent and incongruent emotional endings, a long short-term memory (LSTM) network achieved 65% accuracy. While promising, several limitations constrain interpretation. The stimuli were framed in the third person and likely did not engage participants' self-relevant affective expectations. Depression severity was defined using a median split on PHQ-9 scores rather than clinically validated thresholds. In addition, affectively salient words varied in position and analyses were not time-locked to the critical expectancy-violating word. These factors may have reduced sensitivity to expectancy-specific gaze responses.

Critically, detecting expectancy-related differences requires tightly controlled linguistic stimuli. Electroencephalography (EEG) studies conducted in HCs demonstrate that semantic expectancy violations (e.g., "the lifeguard looked into the ocean and saw a car" vs. "…a shark") elicit robust, time-locked physiological responses (Kuperberg et al., 2020). In addition, self-relevant contexts preferentially bias expectations toward positive outcomes (e.g., "A man knocks on your hotel room door. You see that he has a gift/gun/tray in his hand" (Fields and Kuperberg, 2015)). These paradigms rely on precise experimental control, including balanced selfrelevant and non-self-relevant stimuli, fixed placement of the affectively valent critical word (CW), and analyses time-locked to CW onset. Lexically controlled, time-locked stimuli may maximize sensitivity to expectancy violations and provide a principled framework to enhance detecting depression-related alterations in predictive processing.

In the current study, we applied these principles to eye tracking using rigorously controlled linguistic stimuli and clinically validated assessments of depression (MINI, BDI, PHQ-9).

We addressed three questions: 1. Expectancy detection: Can eye tracking detect expectancy violations in non-self-relevant (NSR) stimuli? We hypothesized that gaze responses would differ between expected and unexpected CWs.

Stimuli

Participants were presented with vignettes of 3-4 sentences (Sameni et al., 2025). Scenario framing (thirdperson non-self-referential (NSR) vs. first-person self-referential (SR)) and CW valence (positive vs. negative) defined four conditions (Table 1). NSR scenarios resolved with an expected (NSR-E) or unexpected (NSR-U) CW. SR scenarios resolved with a self-positive (SR-P) or self-negative (SR-N) CW. Eighty vignettes were presented per condition. SR vignettes were presented first, followed by NSR vignettes. CW congruency was counterbalanced across vignettes within each block. Vignettes comprised a scenario, a resolution, and a CW. The scenario was presented as a text block that participants read at their own pace. The resolution (the last sentence of the vignette) was presented word-by-word in rapid serial visual presentation (RSVP), with each word shown for 350 ms interleaved with a 150 ms black screen. While examination of eye-tracking behavior during natural reading can yield rich data on cognition (Bax, 2013; Carpenter and Just, 1981) and semantic processing (Hohenstein and Kliegl, 2014; Phillips et al., 2012), we optimized the study design and stimulus presentation for multimodal physiology measurement with particular emphasis on EEG-based event related potentials, motivating RVSP. The sentence-final CW was shown for 700 ms. The vignettes were designed such that the CW made the resolution either congruent or incongruent with the scenario, when interpreted by a healthy individual. Lexical parameters, including concreteness, orthographic length, orthographic neighborhood frequency, and orthographic neighborhood size were balanced between all stimuli, as described in (Karimi et al., 2026). To ensure that participants maintained attention to the stimuli, 1/3 of vignettes were followed by comprehension questions probing vignette content (never referencing the CW) and were randomly spaced to limit predictability.

Eye-tracking Data Acquisition and Feature Extraction

Eye-tracking data were collected in conjunction with several other physiological data streams including EEG, electrocardiogram (ECG), photoplethysmogram (PPG), seismocardiogram (SCG), electrodermal activity (EDA), and continuous blood pressure. These other data streams, in addition to multi-modal evaluation of physiological predictors for depression, are evaluated elsewhere (Karimi et al., 2026; Stone et al., 2025). Eye tracking data were collected at a sampling rate of 1000 Hz using an Eyelink 1000 Plus eye tracker (SR Research, Ltd.). The Eyelink's native calibration sequence was performed at the beginning of the experimental session and at designated breaks. The monitor was 1920 x 1080 pixels, and participants were restrained by a chin rest to stabilize their head. The distance from the participant's eye to the top of the display monitor was approximately 1440mm, and the distance from the participant's eye to the bottom of the display monitor was approximately 1470mm.

Eye tracking was time-locked with respect to CW onset to assess gaze responses to semantic expectancy and self-referential processing. Eye-tracking data were separated into epochs from -150 ms to 1100 ms relative to CW onset. Blinks were identified and removed from the data, and a linear interpolation was applied across blink periods. For blinks occurring at the beginning or end of each epoch, data were removed. We then computed 19 measures specific to each epoch (Table 2). Features in each epoch were then mean-aggregated by participant across trials within the four stimulus type conditions (NSR-E, NSR-U, SR-P, SR-N), resulting in 76 total features used as input to the classification models (19 measures x 4 stimulus type conditions). Epochs that had no data for a given feature (<6% of the data) were not included in aggregation.

Feature Selection and Classifier Design

We evaluated three classification tasks: clinical diagnosis (MDD vs. HC), BDI-defined risk (Low vs. High), and PHQ-9-defined risk (Low vs. High). Multiple classifiers were evaluated, including the Random Forest, XGBoost, CatBoost, Support Vector Machine, Logistic Regression, and Linear Discriminant Analysis. Performance was comparable across models (Supplemental Table 13). Random Forest was selected for primary analyses due to its stable performance across tasks and its suitability for high-dimensional, nonlinear data while mitigating overfitting (Breiman, 2001). Model performance was evaluated using stratified 20-fold cross-validation to preserve class balance while maximizing training data utilization.

Within each fold, 60% of the data were used for training and 40% for testing. To prevent leakage, all preprocessing steps were performed using training data only and then applied to the held-out test data. Features were first standardized using z-score normalization based on the training set mean and standard deviation. To address class imbalance, the Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique (SMOTE) was applied to the training data only. The data was then re-standardized following SMOTE. Feature selection was then performed using Random Forest-based recursive feature elimination (RFE), retaining the top 25% of features (19 total). This procedure iteratively removed the least important features based on model-derived importance scores until the target feature set size was reached (Guyon et al., 2002). Finally, model hyperparameters were optimized within each training fold using grid search. Classification was performed using a probability threshold of 0.5.

Feature Analysis

We first evaluated whether site-related differences were present. Because both GT and Emory collected HC participants, we conducted mixed ANOVAs for each feature with site as a between-subjects factor and stimulus condition (NSR-E, NSR-U, SR-P, SR-N) as a within-subject factor. Fourteen of 19 features (73.7%) showed a significant or marginal main effect of site (p <0.10), indicating substantial site-related variability. To account for these differences, site effects were removed using linear regression. Each feature was modeled as a function of site, group, condition, and their interaction, and residualized values were used for subsequent analyses. Emory was used as the reference site. This procedure removes additive site-related variance while preserving experimental effects. Following correction, reanalysis of HC participants showed no remaining site effects (all p >0.10), confirming effective removal of site-related variability. Site-corrected features were used for all subsequent analyses.

Do gaze patterns to unexpected versus expected stimuli differ by diagnostic group or depression risk?

We next evaluated whether eye tracking could detect violations of expectation by examining group and stimulus condition effects for each feature. To isolate expectancy violation, analyses were limited to NSR stimuli. We first examined which variables were associated with diagnosis. Mixed ANOVAs were conducted for each feature with group (HC vs. MDD) as a between-subjects factor and stimulus condition (NSR-E vs. NSR-U) as a within-subjects factor. To control for multiple comparisons, family-wise p-values were adjusted via a Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (BH-FDR) correction. For the main effect of group, five features (saccade maximal peak velocity, saccade average peak velocity, saccade maximal displacement, saccade average displacement, and peak velocity) were significant (p<0.05). HC participants showed larger and faster saccades than MDD participants. Center displacement was significant prior to correction, but after BH-FDR correction was at trend level (p<0.1). For the main effect of condition, two features (pupil dilation, and pupil dilation percent change) were significant, with greater pupil dynamics observed for NSR-U stimuli. No effects were found for the group x condition interaction. These findings indicate that saccade-related parameters differ by group, while pupil dilation may reflect differences in responses to unexpected stimuli.

Mixed ANOVAs with BDI risk (low vs. high) as a between-subjects factor and stimulus condition as a withinsubjects factor showed significant main effects of BDI risk for five features (saccade maximal peak velocity, saccade average peak velocity, saccade maximal displacement, saccade average displacement, and peak velocity; p<0.05). Two features (pupil dilation, and pupil dilation percent) showed main effects of condition (p<0.05). No BDI risk x stimulus condition interactions were observed. These effects mirrored those of diagnosis analysis, with greater gaze dynamics observed for low-risk BDI and greater pupil dilation for unexpected stimuli.

Finally, we evaluated PHQ-9 risk for each feature. Mixed ANOVA with PHQ-9 risk (low vs. high) as a betweensubjects factor and stimulus condition as a within-subjects factor. Again, five features (saccade maximal peak velocity, saccade average peak velocity, saccade maximal displacement, saccade average displacement, and peak velocity) showed a significant main effect of PHQ-9 risk (p<0.05). For the main effect of condition, two features (pupil dilation and pupil dilation percentage) were again significant (p<0.05). Saccade maximal displacement and entropy were at trend level (p<0.1). No group x condition interaction was observed. Once again, greater gaze dynamics were observed in low-risk as opposed to high-risk individuals, while greater pupil dynamics was associated with expectancy violation. These effects followed the same pattern as for diagnosis and BDI, such that no single feature's association with expectancy depended on risk level. Taken together, these analyses suggest that differences in depressed state may be best indexed by saccade dynamics, while expectancy may be best indexed by pupil dynamics.

Do self-referential incongruency and depressed state relate to different gaze behaviors?

Next, we evaluated whether gaze features differed when lexical stimuli were congruent or incongruent with self-representation in positive versus negative contexts. Mixed ANOVAs were conducted with group (HC vs. MDD) as a between-subjects factor and stimulus condition (SR-P vs. SR-N) as a within-subjects factor. Six features (saccade maximal peak velocity, saccade average peak velocity, saccade maximal displacement, saccade average displacement, peak velocity, and center displacement) showed a significant main effect of group (p<0.05). Healthy participants showed higher gaze dynamics. The number of fixations was initially significant, but was at trend-level after correction for multiple comparisons. No significant main effect of condition was observed. Saccade maximal duration and saccade average duration showed significant group × condition interactions prior to correction, but only saccade average duration was at trend level following BH-FDR correction (p<0.10).

Mixed ANOVAs with BDI risk (low vs. high) as a between-subjects factor and SR condition as a withinsubjects factor. Six features (saccade maximal peak velocity, saccade average peak velocity, saccade maximal displacement, saccade average displacement, peak velocity, and entropy) showed significant main effects of BDI risk (p<0.05). Larger saccade dynamics were observed for HC participants, while high risk individuals showed greater entropy. No significant main effect of SR stimulus condition was observed. Initially, significant BDI risk × condition interactions were observed for saccade maximal duration and saccade average duration, while interactions for main sequence ratio and entropy approached significance (p<0.10), but none of these findings survived correction for multiple comparisons.

For PHQ-9 risk (low vs. high), five features (saccade maximal peak velocity, saccade average peak velocity, saccade maximal displacement, saccade average displacement, and peak velocity) showed significant main effects of PHQ-9 risk (p<0.05). Greater gaze dynamics were observed for HC individuals. Main sequence ratio and entropy were initially significant but did not survive BH-FDR correction. No main effects of condition were observed, although pupil dilation approached significance. Significant PHQ-9 risk × condition interactions were initially observed for saccade maximal duration and main sequence ratio, but they did not survive correction.

Taken together, these results indicate that SR expectancy engages complex gaze dynamics. Features associated with saccade dynamics were consistently associated with depression diagnosis and risk. These findings complement the NSR results and suggest that self-referential stimuli may amplify depression-related differences in gaze behavior. However, interdependence among features highlights the need for multivariate approaches to fully characterize these relationships.

Can eye tracking predict depression diagnosis and risk?

Finally, we used Random Forest classifiers to predict clinical diagnosis (HC vs. MDD), BDI risk (Low vs. High), and PHQ-9 risk (Low vs. High). Our goals were to: (1) determine whether event-locked eye tracking could accurately distinguish healthy vs. depressed states in three classification tasks; and (2) determine variables that more greatly contributed to model conclusions. Confusion matrices are shown in Figure 1, and full classification results are provided in Table 4. For classifying MDD vs. HC (Figure 1A), the Random Forest classifier performed well, averaging 83.3% (SD=4.8%) accuracy across the 20 folds. Sensitivity was M=60.8% (SD=15.2%) and specificity was 93.0% (SD=6.4%) indicating somewhat better performance correctly classifying healthy individuals. The macro F1 score was M=0.782 (SD=0.070), and the mean precision was M=0.851 (SD=0.048) for HC and M=0.815 (SD=0.145) for MDD. Feature importance analysis showed that saccade-related measures, particularly saccade displacement, were consistently among the most influential features. Saccade peak velocity, center displacement, and saccade main sequence ratio also contributed strongly. SR features dominated the top-ranked features, with nine of the ten most important features originating from SR conditions.

For classifying high vs. low BDI risk (Figure 1B), the Random Forest again performed well, averaging an accuracy of 82.3% (SD=6.2%). Sensitivity for BDI was better than the diagnosis classifier at M=76.2% (SD=16.7%) and specificity was 86.2% (SD=9.6%). The macro F1 score was M=0.810 (SD=0.074), and the mean precision was M=0.857 (SD=0.083) for low risk and M=0.808 (SD=0.11) for high risk. SR negative entropy was the most important feature, followed by SR saccade displacement measures. Center displacement and peak velocity also contributed. Seven of the ten most important features were from SR conditions, with the remaining three from NSR stimuli.

Finally, classification of PHQ-9 risk (Figure 1C) showed lower performance, with mean accuracy of 71.0% (SD=9.6%). Sensitivity was essentially chance at M=50.8% (SD=23.8%) while specificity was higher at M=79.6% (SD=7.9%), indicating that the model was biased towards labelling observations as low risk. The macro F1 score was M=0.645 (SD=0.13), and the mean precision was M=0.797 (SD=0.086) for low risk and M=0.506 (SD=0.173) for high risk. Feature importance analysis again highlighted saccade displacement, saccade peak velocity, entropy, and peak velocity, with seven of the ten features from SR condition.

Discussion

We examined whether eye-tracking responses to expectancy violations and self-referential processing differ in depression and whether these responses predict diagnosis and symptom severity. Saccade features were consistently associated with diagnosis and depression severity, while pupil dilation was most sensitive to expectancy violation. In NSR contexts, gaze responses to unexpected stimuli were largely independent of diagnosis, suggesting that expectancy violations alone may not fully differentiate depressed individuals. No features showed an interaction between diagnosis and condition, suggesting that on a feature-by-feature basis, expectancy violations in non-self-referential contexts may reflect general cognitive processing differences rather than depression-specific alterations.

Our second question examined whether self-referential expectancy violations differed in depressed individuals. Again, saccade-related features showed associations with diagnosis and depression risk. Saccade duration features initially showed interactions between SR congruency and diagnosis, BDI risk, and PHQ-9 risk, though these findings did not survive correction for multiple comparisons. While they should be interpreted with caution, these findings suggest that in depression, saccades may be associated with expectancy processing specifically when evaluating SR information. This aligns with theories that depression is characterized by maladaptive self-schema, in which negative SR outcomes may be more expected and positive outcomes less expected, altering cognitive and attentional responses during self-evaluation.

Our third question examined whether eye-tracking features alone could classify depression diagnosis and severity. Random Forest classifiers achieved moderate to high accuracy across all tasks, with the highest performance for diagnosis (83.3%), followed by BDI risk (82.3%) and PHQ-9 risk (71%). Saccade features were most consistently important. Self-referential features contributed disproportionately to classification performance, especially for BDI classification, suggesting that gaze responses to self-relevant expectancy violations may be particularly informative for detecting depression. Lower PHQ-9 classification performance likely reflects differences in symptom constructs assessed by PHQ-9 compared with BDI, including differences in emphasis on somatic versus cognitive-affective symptoms (Kung et al., 2013; Moriarty et al., 2015).

Importantly, these findings converge with our prior work using EEG in the same expectancy-violation paradigm, where event-related potentials time-locked to the critical word accurately classified depression diagnosis and severity (Stone et al., 2025). Similarly, recent work using wearable ECG and PPG recordings collected during this paradigm demonstrated that cardiovascular features can classify depression risk and severity using machine learning approaches (Karimi et al., 2026). Together, these results suggest that expectancy-locked physiological responses, spanning neural, autonomic, and oculomotor systems, capture a shared disruption in predictive processing in depression. While EEG provides direct neural measurement and ECG/PPG reflect autonomic function, eye tracking offers a scalable, non-invasive modality that may be particularly suitable for clinical screening and monitoring.

Our findings align with prior research linking eye-tracking dynamics to depression. As in this study, alterations in saccade kinematics have been consistently reported in depressed individuals (Barsznica et al., 2021b; Carvalho et al., 2014; Gao et al., 2023; Li et al., 2016; Sweeney et al., 1998; Takahashi et al., 2021b). These alterations may reflect disruptions in frontostriatal and frontoparietal networks involved in executive function, attentional engagement, and affective regulation. Entropy, which was associated with both diagnosis and expectancy effects in this study, has also been used to characterize gaze variability in depression and may reflect altered attention allocation or cognitive load (Di Stasi et al., 2016; Goodridge et al., 2024; Jiang et al., 2024; Shiferaw et al., 2019). Pupil dilation, which was sensitive to expectancy violation, is known to reflect autonomic nervous system activation and prediction error processing (Braem et al., 2015; Demberg and Sayeed, 2016; Hyönä et al., 1995; Kadem et al., 2020; van der Wel and van Steenbergen, 2018; Van Steenbergen and Band, 2013), suggesting that depressed individuals may exhibit altered autonomic responses to expectancy violations.

Taken together, these findings support the hypothesis that depression involves disruptions in predictive processing, particularly in self-referential contexts. Expectancy violations appear to engage both cognitive and affective systems and are detectable through eye-tracking dynamics. The convergence of gaze-based and neural biomarkers suggests that predictive processing deficits may represent a fundamental mechanism underlying depression.

Several limitations should be considered. First, the sample size was modest, particularly for the MDD group, and replication in larger cohorts is needed. Second, the research-grade eye tracker used in this study may limit immediate clinical deployment, although lower-cost and more portable eye-tracking systems are increasingly available. Third, classification models relied on clinical diagnosis and self-report measures as ground truth, which may not fully capture the multidimensional nature of depression. Finally, although site effects were carefully controlled, residual site-related variance cannot be completely excluded.

Despite these limitations, this study represents an important step toward developing objective biomarkers of depression. Our classification performance exceeded prior eye-tracking studies which reported near 65% (Ding et al., 2019; Kobo et al., 2024; Stolicyn et al., 2022), highlighting the importance of event-locked, expectancybased paradigms. These results suggest that eye tracking may serve as a scalable, non-invasive tool for objective depression assessment and monitoring. Future work integrating eye tracking with other physiological modalities, such as EEG, may further improve predictive performance and advance development of multimodal diagnostic tools for depression.
