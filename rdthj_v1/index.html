To fix Exact P-Values

What we found

We found 2 imprecise p values out of 62 detected p values.

Show details

Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (e.g., “decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

The APA manual states: Report exact p values (e.g., p = .031) to two or three decimal places. However, report p values less than .001 as p < .001. However, 2 decimals is too imprecise for many use-cases (e.g., a p value meta-analysis), so report p values with three digits.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Patterns are designed to capture:

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module retrieves information from repositories.

Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. Abstract

Climate policy acceptance and sustainable behavior partially depend on accurate CO₂ estimates and perceived policy effectiveness. We tested whether seeding, an intervention in which people learn accurate CO₂ values for representative foods that serve as anchors for estimating other foods' emissions, can increase carbon numeracy (i.e., the ability to estimate carbon footprints of foods without explicit calculations).

Two pre-registered experiments examined whether increased carbon numeracy also enhances perceived effectiveness and acceptance of a value-added tax (VAT) reform on food and food choice. Study 1 (N = 974) tested whether seeding increased the effectiveness and acceptance of a VAT increase for meat. Study 2 (N = 1,023) extended this by explaining the policy mechanism. Food choice was assessed by asking participants to select a food box (vegan, vegetarian, or meat-based) in a lottery.

Both studies found that seeding improved carbon numeracy (ηp² = .55-.58, p < .05).

However, neither seeding nor policy explanations significantly increased policy acceptance or food choice, although explaining the policy increased perceived effectiveness (β = .21-.24, p < .05). In both studies, perceived effectiveness (r = .31-.41, p < .001) and policy acceptance (r = .30-.49, p < .001) were significantly related to selecting a non-meat food box.

Exploratory analyses revealed that perceptions of a potential price change were the strongest predictor of policy acceptance (β = -.47, p < .001).

Conclusion

These findings suggest that VAT policy changes could require both explaining environmental benefits and addressing perceived personal costs.

Highlights

• Seeding improves CO₂ estimates of foods through reference anchors • Seeding successfully improved carbon numeracy in a representative online sample • Carbon numeracy had no effect on perceived effectiveness or acceptance of the VAT • Perceived effectiveness and acceptance correlated with non-meat food choices • Perceived price change was the strongest predictor of policy acceptance

1 Introduction

Even though most individuals have a comprehensive understanding of the causes and consequences of climate change, it remains difficult for many to identify which measures and behaviors effectively reduce carbon emissions (Cologna et al., 2022; Frings et al., 2025; Johnson et al., 2024; Truelove & Parks, 2012). This is partly attributed to low carbon numeracy (Grinstein et al., 2018), meaning that people have difficulty estimating the CO₂ emissions of the products they consume (Cologna et al., 2022; Ludwig et al., 2025). Every day, individuals emit CO₂ in multiple domains: due to energy use, transportation or food consumption (Brandenstein et al., 2023; Ivanova et al., 2016 Ivanova et al., , 2020)). Food alone accounts for approximately a quarter of the total households' CO₂ emission in the European Union (Plinke et al., 2026; Tukker & Jansen, 2006). Being able to identify and assess which measures are most effective to mitigate climate change is crucial, as it enables people to make impactful environmentally friendly decisions. More importantly, it allows one to accurately evaluate whether proposed policy measures contribute to effectively reducing emissions (Grinstein et al., 2018; Johnson et al., 2024; Ludwig et al., 2025).

One such effective policy is a value-added tax (VAT) reform on foods. For example, an increase in the value-added tax on animal-based products is expected to lead to a decrease in meat purchases and consequently in less meat consumption (Springmann et al., 2025).

However, the effects of changes in meat consumption on CO₂ emissions are systematically underestimated, whereas the effects of low-impact behaviors, e.g., installing efficient light bulbs, are frequently overestimated (Camilleri et al., 2019; Cologna et al., 2022; Johnson et al., 2024; Kretschmer, 2024; Panzone et al., 2020; Wynes et al., 2020). This underestimation presumably also influences acceptance of climate policies (Frings et al., 2025; Ludwig et al., 2025), as suggested by research showing a significant link between perceived policy effectiveness and policy acceptance (see Bergquist et al., 2022; Grelle & Hofmann, 2023; Tiede et al., 2025). It is crucial to understand the reasons behind the acceptance of political measures, since in democratic societies, the implementation of climate policy depends on public support. Political decision-makers are likely to avoid measures that could alienate voters in order to increase their chances of winning elections. (Anderson et al., 2017; Cullerton et al., 2016; Matthews & Wynes, 2022; Schaffer et al., 2022). Enhancing carbon numeracy for food may therefore be one stepping stone in fostering policy acceptance.

We therefore make three contributions: First, we experimentally test whether improving carbon numeracy for food increases acceptance of a tax change policy aiming at reducing CO2 emissions and whether it affects actual food choice. Second, we examine whether perceived policy effectiveness is affected by changes in carbon numeracy. Third, we explore perceived financial costs of a policy change, as perceived higher cost seem to reduce public support for other climate policy measures (e.g., Fairbrother, 2022; Kudrnáč & Fairbrother, 2025).

Carbon numeracy refers to the ability to estimate the carbon footprint of everyday behaviors without explicit calculations, much like how people interpret calories or temperatures to make decisions without performing formal calculations (Grinstein et al., 2018; Wynes et al., 2020). It is a domain of the broader ability of carbon competence, which is defined as the capacity to evaluate the mitigation effectiveness of climate mitigation policies (Johnson et al., 2024). Both concepts represent specific forms of knowledge that can presumably be summarized under the broader crystallized intelligence domain of climate change knowledge (Geiger et al., 2019; Temme et al., 2026). Considering these dependencies, we expect that improving individuals' estimates of CO₂ emissions of behaviors could therefore increase the readiness to change behavior and support for policies regulating emission behaviors (Merten et al., 2026; Vandenbergh & Nielsen, 2019; Wynes et al., 2020).

Increasing VAT on high emission products is a regulatory measure that can shift consumption patterns at scale. For example, making animal-based products more expensive can reduce their consumption (Springmann et al., 2025). Simultaneously reducing VAT on plant-based products can further encourage a shift toward plant-based diets by lowering the financial barrier to healthier and more sustainable food choices (Plinke et al., 2026; Springmann et al., 2025). Consequently, the perceived effectiveness of such policies should increase by improving carbon numeracy.

An intervention called 'seeding' has been proposed as a pathway to increase carbon numeracy (Bröder et al., 2023): participants learn accurate CO₂ values for a small set of representative reference food items, which then serve as anchors (seeds) for estimating the emissions of other foods. Unlike traditional informational approaches that simply present facts, seeding engages learners in the process by offering accurate information for only a limited number of representative food items. This focused approach supports the construction of an internal reference scale for food-related emissions necessary for carbon numeracy and therefore carbon competence (Bröder et al., 2023). Using this experimental procedure that has been shown to increase carbon numeracy allows testing whether increased carbon numeracy transfers to greater acceptance of a policy as well as real-world choices of food.

1.1 Overview

Considering the assumed links between carbon numeracy, perceived policy effectiveness and acceptance of the VAT policy, we pre-registered the following hypotheses (see Zenodo): we expected that seeding increases carbon numeracy (H1), and that this increased carbon numeracy transfers to greater perceived effectiveness to reduce CO2 emissions (H2) and, in turn, greater acceptance of a VAT reform on food (H3). Additionally, we expected that improved carbon numeracy for food also shifts individual food choices to more environmental-friendly alternatives (H4). Furthermore, we hypothesized that within participants who did not receive the seeding intervention initially, perceived policy effectiveness and acceptance of the VAT policy would increase from pre-to post-seeding measurement, reflecting within-person change as a result of exposure to the intervention (H5).

Due to the nested nature of carbon numeracy in higher-order knowledge domains, we control for individual differences in climate change knowledge and examine how this influences the accuracy of CO₂ estimates and the learning increase in carbon numeracy. We are also examining whether explicitly explaining the policy mechanism, that is, how a VAT change translates into reduced meat consumption and lower emissions, increases perceived policy effectiveness, and how strongly perceived financial costs shape policy acceptance in the context of a food-related VAT reform. To test the hypotheses, Experiment 1 employed a classical seeding intervention to enhance carbon numeracy, assessed as the accuracy when estimating the CO₂ footprint of different food products. Experiment 2 extended this approach by additionally providing participants with explicit explanations of how VAT changes on meat and dairy translate into emission reductions, supporting the development of carbon competence. By integrating carbon estimation training with information about the mechanisms underlying food-related emissions we aimed at understanding the boundary conditions of carbon competency translating into policy acceptance.

2 Experiment 1

In Experiment 1, we replicated the procedure by Bröder et al. (2023), which, in their study, led to increased accuracy of CO₂ estimates for food items. We examined whether seeding affects policy-relevant attitudes and food choices relative to a control group without seeding.

Additionally, we also pre-registered positive within-subject effects of seeding on the perceived effectiveness of the VAT policy in reducing CO₂, the acceptance of the VAT policy and on food choice. These variables were measured before and after the seeding intervention.

2.1 Method

All materials, pre-registrations, quantitative data, analysis codes, and output are available at Zenodo.

2.1.1 Participants

Participants were recruited via the online-panel provider Bilendi. The sample (N = 974) was non-probabilistic and quota-representative for age × gender and federal state. Participants were 18 to 74 years old (M = 45.9, SD = 15.4) and included 52.1% males, 47.7% females and 0.2% non-binary individuals. Most participants (56.2%) had completed secondary education with university entrance qualifications. Participants who did not consume meat were excluded from all analyses of the dependent variables (N = 63), as the proposed VAT reform's downsides (increased costs for meat) primarily affect meat consumers, yielding a more conservative test of the hypotheses. design with perceived effectiveness of the policy to reduce emissions, acceptance of the policy and food choice as dependent variables. To keep participation time across conditions of similar length, the no-seeding condition also received the seeding intervention, but only at the end of the experiment, so that both groups had ultimately read the same information. This also served as a within-subject design, allowing to test pre-post changes.

2.1.2 Design

2.1.3 Experimental Manipulations

Policy. To explain and contextualize the policy at hand, participants in both conditions learned that agriculture, and animal husbandry in particular, contributes significantly to the emissions of climate-damaging greenhouse gases and that, therefore, the German Advisory Council on the Environment recommends increasing the value-added tax on animal-based products (this latter half was fictitious). Half of the participants were told that the federal government would adopt the recommendation and change the VAT rate for animal-based products from 7% to 19%, while the other group was told that the recommendation would not be adopted (remaining at 7%). At the end of the study, all participants were informed that the federal government currently has no intention of implementing the recommendation.

Intervention. To experimentally manipulate carbon numeracy, we employed the same seeding intervention used by Bröder et al. (2023). It consists of 48 food items divided equally across four food groups: Fruit/Vegetables; Meat/Fish; Dairy and Alternatives (to meat and dairy). Carbon footprint estimates for all items were derived from the IFEU carbon footprint database for foods and meals in Germany (Reinhardt et al., 2020). The seed item set consisted of 3 items from each food group, while the remaining 36 items served as transfer items.

Each participant completed two rounds estimating carbon footprints. In the first round, participants estimated the CO₂ footprint in grams for all 48 food items. To help participants become familiar with the response format, an example with the correct answer was provided (1 kg of carrots has a CO₂ footprint of 100 g). After completing the first round, participants received the correct CO₂ values for the 12 seed items. In the second round, all participants reestimated the CO₂ footprints for all 48 items, including both the seed and transfer items. This procedure allows us to assess learning for seed items and whether that knowledge transfers to novel items for which correct values were not provided.

2.1.4 Measures

Carbon numeracy. We define carbon numeracy as the capacity to accurately estimate emissions of different single foods. To calculate the estimation accuracy we followed (Brown, 2002) Food choice. In a choice task, participants were offered the chance to win a professional mail-in food box containing various pre-portioned dishes and ingredients. The box was worth 100€ and raffled between participants after the study had ended. Participants chose between a vegan, vegetarian, or meat option, reflecting varying degrees of animalbased food content to assess the likelihood of a pro-environmental, emission-reducing food choice. For the analysis, responses were dichotomized into meat (coded as 0) versus non-meat choices (1), as vegan choices were too rare for a meaningful separate analysis.

Climate change knowledge was measured with the 15-item Climate Change Knowledge (CCK) scale (Temme et al., 2026). The CCK scale includes ecological, social as well as climate action knowledge. The CCK score was calculated as the sum of correct answers across all items. A two-parameter logistic (2PL) item response theory model confirmed unidimensionality and good model fit supporting the use of a total score (for details, see the supplementary material) with good reliability (ω = .75). This allowed us to test whether the intervention was equally effective for participants with different levels of climate change knowledge.

2.1.5 Procedure

Participants were randomly assigned to one of the four experimental conditions as shown in Fig. 1. First, participants answered sociodemographic and knowledge questions and provided information about their diet. Subsequently, half of the participants completed the seeding procedure to manipulate carbon numeracy; then information about the VAT policy was provided. Finally, dependent variables were measured. The other half of the participants were first informed about the proposed VAT policy, and then the dependent variables were measured.

To investigate whether seeding improves policy-relevant attitudes and food choices at the within-subject level, the no-seeding control group received the seeding intervention after completing the dependent variables.

2.2 Statistical Analyses

All statistical analyses were conducted using R version 4.2.1. Significance threshold ɑ was set to .05 for all tests.

To test whether the intervention increased carbon numeracy (H1), we conducted a two-factorial repeated-measures ANOVA with time point (before vs. after seeding) and item type (seed items vs. transfer items) as factors and mean Order of Magnitude Error (OME) as the carbon numeracy dependent variable (Bröder et al., 2023). We used the entire sample for this (including vegans; N = 974). To provide a more conservative test of genuine competence gain beyond simple memorization of the seeding values, an additional exploratory paired ttest was conducted comparing pre-and post-seeding OME for transfer items only.

Furthermore, in an exploratory analysis, the interaction between time point and item type was examined to assess whether the improvement in carbon numeracy differed between seed and transfer items.

For the transfer effects (H2, H3, H5) we analyzed both between-subjects effects (seeding vs. no seeding) and within-subjects effects (pre-vs. post-seeding). For the betweensubjects analyses, we conducted separate univariate regressions for each experimental factor, followed by a full model including both factors and their interaction. For food choice (H4), a binary logistic regression with the same predictors was calculated.

The within-subject experimental effects were only examined in the no-seeding condition. A one-tailed pairwise t-test assessed within-person changes (H5). To examine whether food choice shifted toward plant-based options after the seeding, a McNemar test was calculated.

To examine the relationships between perceived effectiveness, acceptance of the VAT policy, and food choice, we conducted Pearson correlations for continuous variables and point-biserial correlations for the association with the dichotomous food choice variable.

To explore the relation between pre-existing environmental knowledge and carbon numeracy, we regressed OME on time point (effect-coded as -0.5 for pre-seeding and +0.5 for post-seeding), centered CCK score, and their interaction. To avoid spurious effects due to simple memorization of the correct values shown during the intervention, we used transfer items only, which provides a more conservative test of competence improvement and genuine transfer attainment.

3.1 Carbon numeracy

For carbon numeracy, zero indicates perfect accuracy. Higher absolute values indicate greater deviations and thus lower accuracy; +1 represents a 10-fold overestimation and -1 represents a 10-fold underestimation. Prior to the seeding intervention, participants systematically underestimated CO₂ emissions across all food groups (MOME.sign = -0.93, SD = 0.79), indicating that their estimates were on average approximately nine times lower than the true emission values. As shown in Figure 2A, the seeding intervention successfully improved participants' estimation accuracy across all food groups, shifting carbon numeracy towards the zero line, F(1, 435) = 523.68, p < .001, ηp² = .55, supporting H1, which predicted that seeding would increase carbon numeracy. Furthermore, there was a significant main effect of item type, showing that seed items were estimated more accurately than transfer items, F(1, 435) = 95.38, p < .001, ηp² = .18. Additionally, the time point effect was also present when transfer items were analyzed separately, demonstrating genuine competence gain beyond simple memorization of the seeding values, t(1743) = 35.96, p < .001, d = .82. The improvement in carbon numeracy was larger for seed items than for transfer items as indicated by the interaction effect between time point and item type, F(1, 435) = 41.11, p < .001, ηp² = .09.

3.2 Perceived policy effectiveness and policy acceptance for a VAT reform on food

Figure 3 shows that despite the improvements in carbon numeracy, seeding neither increased perceived effectiveness nor acceptance of the VAT policy change, contradicting H2 and H3.

For perceived effectiveness, VAT policy showed a marginal effect, with participants in the VAT increase (vs. no VAT change) condition tending to rate the policy as slightly more effective (β = .14, t(909) = 2.07, p = .038). Seeding did not significantly predict perceived effectiveness (β = -.01, t(909) = -.20, p = .839). In the full model, neither seeding (β = .03, t(907) = .30, p = .767), the VAT policy (β = .18, t(907) = 1.96, p = .050), nor their interaction (β = -.09, t(907) = -.68, p = .499) reached significance. Within-subjects comparisons also revealed no significant change in perceived effectiveness from pre-to post-seeding (t(466) = .56, p = .289, d = .02), contrary to H5.

For acceptance of the policy, participants in the no VAT change condition showed higher acceptance than those in the VAT increase condition, (β = -.22, t(909) = -3.32, p = .001).

Seeding did not significantly influence the acceptance of the VAT policy (β = .05, t(909) = 0.73, p = .467). In the full model including both factors and their interaction, again, neither seeding (β = .12, t(907) = 1.33, p = .183), the VAT policy (β = -.15, t(907) = -1.65, p = .100), nor their interaction (β = -.14, t(907) = -1.07, p = .285) reached significance. Within-subjects comparisons also revealed no significant change in acceptance of the VAT policy from pre-to post-seeding (t(466) = -.44, p = .670, d = -.01), again contrary to H5.

The correlation between perceived effectiveness and acceptance of the VAT policy was significant and positive (r = .55, p < .001).

3.3 Food Choice

Food choice was not significantly affected by seeding (OR = .93, d = -.04, p = .678), the VAT policy (OR = 1.06, d = .03, p = .748), or their interaction (OR = .84, d = -.10, p = .519), contrary to H4, which predicted that improved carbon numeracy would shift food choices toward plant-based options. Food choice was also highly stable from pre-to post-seeding. Of the 467 participants, only n = 30 changed their selection and no significant shift toward plant-based options (n = 16) was observed (χ²(1) = .033, p = .855). Furthermore, perceived effectiveness (r = .31, p < .001) and acceptance of the VAT policy (r = .30, p < .001) were significantly related to the likelihood of selecting a non-meat box.

3.4 Exploratory Analyses: Role of Prior Climate Change Knowledge

Regressing carbon numeracy on time point, centered CCK score, and their interaction showed that carbon numeracy improved after the seeding intervention and was higher among participants with greater climate change knowledge, as indicated by significant main effects of both time point (β = -.43, t(866) = -22.42, p < .001) and pre-existing climate change knowledge (β = -.40, t(866) = -11.77, p < .001). The significant interaction (β = .11, t(866) = 5.58, p < .001) suggested that the improvement from pre-to post-seeding was stronger for participants with low CCK (-1 SD: β = -.57, t = -19.80, p < .001) than for participants with high CCK (+1 SD: β = -.34, t = -11.90, p < .001).

4. Discussion

The results show that carbon numeracy, i.e., the accuracy in estimating carbon emissions of single food items, can be effectively increased by seeding, a cognitive intervention that provides anchors and feedback on emissions in a repeated estimation task. Consistent with Bröder et al. (2023), the study demonstrates that seeding works, even for individuals with a good understanding of climate change, i.e., individuals with higher scores on the Climate Change Knowledge scale. Individuals with less prior knowledge benefited more from the intervention, suggesting the influence of a ceiling effect: those with greater knowledge had less room for improvement. Contrary to our expectations, increasing carbon numeracy had no transfer effect on perceived effectiveness or acceptance of the VAT increase on animal-based foods. The seeding intervention also did not shift participants' food choices toward plantbased options.

This lack of transfer may also occur because knowing the CO₂ emissions of specific foods does not automatically imply understanding how increasing VAT on animal-based products would reduce them. Participants in the VAT increase condition rated the policy as slightly more effective in reducing CO2 emissions than those in the no VAT change condition, yet this did not increase acceptance. Uncertainty about personal consequences may be another reason for people to cling to the status quo instead (Samuelson & Zeckhauser, 1988).

Although perceived effectiveness and acceptance of the policy were correlated at the individual level, the manipulation was perhaps too weak to shift effectiveness perceptions sufficiently to increase acceptance across conditions.

Experiment 2 therefore added a detailed explanation about the VAT increase and its complete effect chain: how the VAT change and resulting price increase influences consumption behavior, how reduced meat consumption affects agricultural production, and how this ultimately reduces emissions (Springmann et al., 2025; Stewart et al., 2023). This way we will assess whether explicit explanations of policy mechanisms can enhance the effect of seeding, or whether knowledge about the policy mechanisms alone may be sufficient to improve perceived policy effectiveness and support.

5. Experiment 2

In addition to the VAT increase for animal-based products, Study 2 also implemented a VAT combination condition, where animal-based foods would be taxed at 19% (compared to the current rate of predominantly 7%) and plant-based foods at 0% (compared to the current rate of predominantly 7%). This VAT combination condition was selected as it represents the most effective approach for simultaneously reducing animal-based product consumption and increasing plant-based food intake (Springmann et al., 2025), thereby reducing both environmental and health burdens. In addition, an explanation of the mechanism behind the VAT reform was added to the procedure. Finally, we explored how people perceived the price change due to the VAT change as perceived financial costs of a policy change seem to reduce public support for climate policy measures (Fairbrother, 2022; Kellner et al., 2025; Kudrnáč & Fairbrother, 2025).

We hypothesized and pre-registered that policy acceptance would be higher in the VAT combination condition than in the VAT increase condition (H6). Regarding intervention type, we hypothesized that the conditions in which the mechanism was explained, both with and without seeding, would show higher perceived policy effectiveness and acceptance of the VAT policy as compared to the seeding only condition (H7, H8). Likewise, participants in the explanation plus seeding and explanation only conditions were expected to be more likely to select a vegan or vegetarian meal box over a meat box compared to the seeding only condition (H9). Finally, drawing on the pattern observed in Study 1, we hypothesized that higher perceived effectiveness of the VAT policy in reducing CO₂ emissions would be associated with greater policy acceptance (H10).

6.3 Food Choice

Food choice was again not significantly affected by the intervention (explanation plus seeding: OR = 1.05, d = .03, p = .735; explanation only: OR = 1.21, d = .11, p = .212), contradicting H9. 6.4 Costs

Exploratory analyses showed that combining a VAT increase on animal-based foods with the reduction of VAT on plant-based foods attenuated the perceived financial burden of the policy (participants in the VAT increase condition: M = 4.79, SD = 1.59; VAT combination condition: M = 4.37; SD = 1.67; β = -.26, t(1021) = -4.15, p < .001). Although participants in the VAT combination condition perceived the policy as less costly overall, cost perception remained a strong negative predictor of acceptance within this condition (β = -.53, t(526) = -14.44, p < .001). The explanation intervention did not significantly predict perceived price change, with neither the explanation plus seeding condition (β = .03, t(1020) = .35, p = .730) nor the explanation only condition (β = -.09, t(1020) = -1.17, p = .243) differing significantly from the seeding only condition and no significant interactions between intervention type and VAT condition emerged.

Perceived price change was significantly related to both policy evaluation outcomes.

Participants perceiving the policy as more costly, rated it as less effective (β = -.38, t(1021) = -13.16, p < .001) and showed lower acceptance (β = -.48, t(1021) = -17.43, p < .001).

Including perceived price change in the full model including both factors and their interactions substantially improved explained variance: for perceived effectiveness, R² increased from .019 to .154; for policy acceptance, R² increased from .013 to .230.

The findings of Study 2 partially replicate those of Study 1: Participants' systematic underestimation of CO₂ emissions of different foods can be improved by the seeding learning intervention. However, Study 2 again did not find the expected transfer effects of carbon numeracy on policy evaluations. While explaining the policy mechanism significantly increased perceived policy effectiveness, it could not enhance the effect of carbon numeracy on the acceptance of the VAT policy, which suggests that simply informing people about CO₂ emissions and their political implications is not enough to gain greater policy acceptance (see Tiede et al., 2025).

In exploratory analyses, perceived costs emerged as the strongest predictor of both policy acceptance and perceived effectiveness, explaining substantially more variance than the experimental manipulations. Although participants in the VAT combination condition perceived the policy as less costly overall, individual differences in cost perception remained a strong negative predictor of acceptance even within this condition. These results highlight a fundamental challenge: perceived costs could outweigh the effects of carbon numeracy and policy explanations in shaping policy acceptance for a VAT reform on food, which could make it difficult to conduct an informed evaluation of climate policy measures (Herberz et al., 2026; Merten et al., 2026).

8 General Discussion

Regulatory instruments to mitigate climate change, such as VAT increases on meat and animal-based products, have great potential to shift consumption patterns at scale, with documented effects on CO₂ reductions, health outcomes, and public revenues (Springmann et al., 2025). Across two pre-registered studies, we found that training people's carbon numeracy with an elaborated method (seeding, Bröder et al., 2023) effectively improved carbon numeracy. However, this improvement did not translate into greater related policy acceptance or changes in food choice behavior. As suggested by prior research, carbon-related knowledge seems to be a helpful, although not sufficient, predictor for pro-environmental behavior (see e.g., Colombo et al., 2023; Kollmuss & Agyeman, 2002). Therefore, we also provided explanations on how food-related VAT policies can reduce CO₂ emissions, assuming that this chain of information is needed for transfer effects to occur.

Explicit explanations of policy mechanisms indeed increased perceived effectiveness of the VAT policy, but not its acceptance. This disconnect reveals that policy comprehension alone may not automatically generate support for it. However, the significant positive correlation between a policy's perceived effectiveness and its acceptance underscores the importance of increasing effectiveness perceptions as a potential pathway to greater policy acceptance (see e.g., for other policies Bergquist et al., 2022; Tiede et al., 2025). The failure of the interventions to increase acceptance, despite successfully raising perceived effectiveness, demonstrates that effectiveness perceptions alone could be insufficient for generating support for fiscal policies.

In both studies, perceived effectiveness and acceptance of the policy were significantly related to the likelihood of choosing a non-meat food box. Yet, neither carbon numeracy nor policy explanations directly affected food choices. This null effect aligns with broader evidence showing that even when awareness of meat's environmental impacts increases, consumer food decisions may remain driven by e.g., taste, affordability, convenience or social norms (Drewnowski & Monsivais, 2020; Fernqvist et al., 2024; Żurek et al., 2026; Brasington et al., 2025). While carbon numeracy alone does not drive food choice, policy attitudes may represent a meaningful pathway toward the behavioral goal the VAT reform is ultimately designed to achieve: a reduction in meat consumption.

In exploratory analyses, perceived price change emerged as the strongest predictor of policy acceptance and effectiveness, explaining substantially more variance than the experimental manipulations. This highlights that people may not only be sensitive to costs, but may also systematically overestimate the financial burden of economic policy measures, just as they misjudge the effectiveness of mitigation behaviors (Behrens et Stylianou et al., 2021; Sun et al., 2022). This pattern is consistent with economic assumptions of loss aversion, suggesting that perceived financial losses weigh more heavily than equivalent gains (Kahneman & Tversky, 1979), and with related research showing that increasing support for carbon pricing is, too, heavily influenced by perceived personal costs (Kellner et al., 2025). As such, theories and empirical findings both highlight the necessity to meticulously consider personal costs and benefits in communicating climate change mitigation measures.

In sum, our interventions changed perceived policy effectiveness but not acceptance of the VAT policy. Combined with the finding that perceived costs dominate policy evaluations, fiscal policy interventions thus face a dual challenge: communicating environmental benefits while managing perceived economic costs. Future studies should examine the role of perceived costs, particularly where cost estimates are inherently uncertain.

8.1 Limitations

Several limitations of the present research should be noted. First, the findings only concern food VAT policies and cannot yet be generalized to climate change mitigation policy acceptance overall. Yet, related experiments on other policies (e.g., Kellner et al., 2025; Kudrnáč & Fairbrother, 2025) show similar results, emphasizing the role of perceived price change could outweigh effectiveness information in determining public acceptance. Second, both studies were conducted with online samples, which limits internal validity due to reduced experimental control compared to laboratory settings. At the same time, the use of an incentivized food choice task, in which participants could only win the food box they actually selected, strengthens the ecological validity beyond hypothetical intentions. Third, the seeding intervention represents a relatively low-dose exposure compared to the commercial environment involved in real-world food purchasing decisions, thus, reduced effects compared to habits, for example, are to be expected. Future research should investigate whether more intensive or repeated training on carbon numeracy produces stronger effects.

9 Conclusion

While prior research demonstrates that perceived policy effectiveness is a strong predictor of policy acceptance, our results show that this association may be weakened when cost concerns dominate. Neither improving carbon numeracy nor explaining how policies reduce emissions substantially influenced policy acceptance nor food choice. Instead, perceived personal financial burden was a stronger predictor of acceptance. In sum, these findings suggest that for fiscal policies, effective communication should go beyond explaining effects on environmental outcomes. Just as people struggle to accurately estimate the CO₂ impact of climate policies, they may also systematically misjudge the personal financial consequences of economic measures. Future research should address potential misconceptions about its personal economic implications.
