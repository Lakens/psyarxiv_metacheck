To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 27 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Abstract

Social and temporal comparisons inform self-evaluation. In educational and work settings, social comparison information seems to affect performance self-assessments more strongly than temporal comparison information. We investigated whether this "higher assigned priority of social comparison information" (HAPSCI) also occurs in people with symptoms of depression, a condition associated with aberrant feedback processing. In a false feedback experiment, we studied the effects of social and temporal comparison information on selfassessments in a sample of N=159 individuals with dysphoria. Participants received concurrent social and temporal comparison feedback on their cognitive performance in a visuospatial 2-back task, which was either favorable (improvement over time or outperforming other participants) or unfavorable (deterioration or being outperformed). Only temporal comparison feedback affected performance self-assessments, while only unfavorable social comparison feedback impacted objective performance. Our results suggest that HAPSCI may not generalize across populations. Individuals with depressive symptoms might be more receptive to temporal than social comparison feedback. We discuss how appraisal of the motivational significance of comparison outcomes, sample characteristics, and experimental procedures may shape effects on self-evaluation.

Temporal comparison information is prioritized over social comparison information in individuals with symptoms of depression Introduction

Self-assessment relies heavily on comparison information (Mussweiler & Epstude, 2009; Unkelbach et al., 2023; Vlaev et al., 2011). Social and temporal comparisons are commonplace and inform judgments on a broad range of dimensions (Morina et al., 2022; Smith et al., 1989; Steffel & Oppenheimer, 2009): We may, for instance, evaluate our cognitive abilities relative to that of other people (social comparison; Festinger, 1954) or to our performance at a different point in time (temporal comparison; Albert, 1977).

Comparison motives and achievement goals

The general comparative processing model (gComp; Morina, 2021) holds that a prior self-representation is compared with a standard representation, yielding a comparison outcome, which is then evaluated with respect to the comparer's motives and coping abilities.

This implies that the appraisal of the comparison outcome, rather than standard type and direction per se, determines its effects. Nevertheless, there is evidence for systematic asymmetries in comparison direction (i.e., whether the self is compared to a better-off or worse-off standard) and effects of social and temporal comparisons. For instance, temporal comparisons are more likely to be favorable than social comparisons (Wilson & Ross, 2001), whereas social comparisons tend to be directed upward more often than downward (Gerber et al., 2018). A possible explanation is that upward social comparisons may support accurate self-assessment (Freund & Kasten, 2012) and are especially useful in motivating selfimprovement (Gibbons et al., 2000). Conversely, temporal comparisons seem to be more commonly directed downward, with past selves typically judged less favorably than present selves, and may therefore mainly serve self-enhancement motives in support of a view of ourselves as continually improving (Wilson & Ross, 2001; Zell & Alicke, 2009). Although temporal comparison can also provide actionable moment-to-moment feedback about progress toward a goal (Carver, 2006), Zell and Alicke (2009) suggest that social comparison is generally more informative for objective self-assessment than temporal comparison, linking temporal comparison to self-serving bias. However, if self-enhancement is in fact often prioritized over self-assessment (Sedikides & Strube, 1997), this should lend weight to temporal comparisons. While upward comparisons can often be adaptive (Boissicat et al., 2022; Buunk & Dijkstra, 2017), their frequency has been linked to reduced psychological well-being and self-esteem, and increased psychopathology (Morina et al., 2026; Schlechter et al., 2024). Unfavorable comparisons are common in clinical settings (Morina et al., 2022) and are associated with elevated levels of psychopathology, regardless of whether unfavorable comparison standards are social or temporal (Morina & Schlechter, 2023).

Moreover, favorable social comparisons may generally help preserve psychological wellbeing in the face of declining health (Kohn & Smith, 2003), as favorable competence-based self-evaluations are associated with improved achievement outcomes and lower anxiety and depression (Ackerman & Wolman, 2007; Allott et al., 2020; Arens et al., 2017; Meyer et al., 2023).

Social and temporal comparisons are thought to generally serve different purposes (Wilson & Ross, 2001; Wolff et al., 2018; Zell & Alicke, 2010). If this is the case, achievement goals should play a part in selecting and weighting comparison standards (van Yperen & Leander, 2014). Achievement goals can be classified along two dimensions, their focus, distinguishing performance-from mastery-orientation, and their valence, distinguishing approach-from avoidance-orientation (Elliot & Hulleman, 2017; Elliot & McGregor, 2001).

Approach goals are defined in positive terms (e.g., attaining a desired outcome like scoring in the top 10% in an exam or improving one's own grades). Avoidance goals are defined in negative terms (e.g., preventing failure, like not wanting to score worse than others or not wanting one's grades to slip ). While performance goals typically emphasize competition and outward demonstrations of competence (Edwards, 2014), mastery goals tend to be directed inward toward one's continued development and are therefore sometimes referred to as learning goals (Grant & Dweck, 2003 1 ). Approach orientation seems to be associated with better performance and self-evaluation (van Yperen & Orehek, 2013), and a more positive impact of social comparisons on self-judgments and academic performance (Boissicat et al., 2022) than avoidance orientation.

Mastery orientation, and mastery-approach goals specifically, have been considered as a source of resilience in the face of setbacks (Grant & Dweck, 2003; van Yperen & Leander, 2014), prosocial behavior (van Yperen & Orehek, 2013), intrinsic motivation (Rawsthorne & Elliot, 1999, but see also Edwards, 2014), and protection against negative affect and work dissatisfaction (Elliot & McGregor, 2001; van Yperen & Janssen, 2002). Performanceapproach orientation, too, is linked to desirable outcomes, such as favorable competence selfevaluation, self-efficacy and performance attainment. However, these benefits appear to be contingent on perceived success and thus more tenuous and equivocal (Senko & Dawson, 2017). Fittingly, unfavorable feedback based on external rather than internal standards appears to affect learners' motivation and self-concept more negatively (Dickhäuser et al., 2017; Meyer et al., 2023). This core distinction between reliance on inter-versus intrapersonal standards suggests different roles for social and temporal comparisons depending on an individual's goal: Social comparison may be particularly relevant for evaluating progress towards performance goals, whereas temporal comparison may serve a comparable function in relation to mastery goals (e.g. van Yperen & Leander, 2014).

Mastery goals are widely endorsed (van Yperen & Orehek, 2013) and feedback that matches learners' own standards seems to better support learning and motivation (Janson et al., 2022). However, people's utilization and weighting of comparison information do not always match their goal orientation. Social comparisons can impact competence-based selfjudgments more strongly than temporal comparisons, even when at odds with stated goals: In evidence of this, van Yperen and Leander (2014) investigated the effects of combined social and temporal comparison information on performance self-assessments. They provided arbitrary feedback about participants' performance in two blocks of the d2 , a test of sustained visual attention (Brickenkamp, 1962; for a process-oriented discussion, see Blotenberg & Schmidt-Atzert, 2019). Social and temporal false feedback always appeared simultaneously and each could be either favorable (better performance than most participants or improvement between blocks), or unfavorable (worse performance or deterioration). Social feedback impacted self-assessments more strongly than temporal feedback, even though most participants endorsed mastery goals. Bases on this descriptive difference in effect size, van Yperen and Leander termed this "the overpowering effect of social comparison information" or TOESCI. However, this may be an overstatement since temporal feedback still impacted self-evaluations alongside social feedback (van Yperen & Leander, 2014; Zell & Alicke, 2009). We therefore propose the more cautious term HAPSCI ("higher assigned priority of social comparison information").

How universal is HAPSCI? Cognitive performance in depression

It remains to be determined whether HAPSCI generalizes to other settings and populations. If social comparison information is indeed intrinsically prioritized, this effect should be stable across settings and populations. Conversely, if the relative importance of social and temporal information depends primarily on the appraisal of the comparison outcome (Morina, 2021), this asymmetry may hinge on context and population variables. To date, research on this topic has focused on students and young professionals (Janson et al., 2022; van Yperen & Leander, 2014; Wolff et al., 2018), for whom social comparisons and performance goals may be especially salient, given their regular exposure to performance evaluations (Garcia et al., 2013; Wolff & Möller, 2022). Achievement goals may change across the lifespan, with increasing emphasis on mastery orientation, intrapersonal comparison standards and community-over competence-based goals in older working age (Buchinger et al., 2022; Caldwell et al., 2004; Heidemeier & Staudinger, 2015; Lange et al., 2010) 2 . It remains unclear whether HAPSCI generalizes beyond these settings.

To our knowledge, HAPSCI has not been explored yet in clinical contexts, although distinct patterns of comparative thinking have been linked to psychopathology, such as symptoms of post-traumatic stress, anxiety, and depression (Arigo et al., 2024; Morina et al., 2026; Sheeran et al., 1995). Depression is characterized by low mood, anhedonia, demotivation, and negative self-views (American Psychiatric Association, 2013) and imposes a considerable personal and societal toll (Kessler, 2012). Compared to controls, people with depression exhibit slowed information processing, lower attentional control, and impaired working memory performance (LeMoult & Gotlib, 2019; Nikolin et al., 2021). They show negative biases in self-evaluation, memory, interpretation of ambiguous information, and attentional disengagement from unpleasant material (LeMoult & Gotlib, 2019; Phillips et al., 2010; Wisco, 2009). They also tend be more susceptible to negative feedback and less responsive to positive feedback (Kube, 2026; Mueller et al., 2015; Rapo et al., 2025; Xie et al., 2022). (Disner et al., 2017) may contribute to maladaptive mental habits in depression (Spinhoven et al., 2018), such as frequent engagement in aversive comparisons (McCarthy & Morina, 2020). Importantly, people with dysphoria tend to compare themselves less favorably than the general population (Butzer & Kuiper, 2006; Morina et al., 2022; Morina & Schlechter, 2023) and decreases in social comparison frequency were found to predict symptom reduction (Kelly et al., 2007). People with dysphoria are less likely to identify with upward, and more likely to identify with downward standards, which may serve to maintain negative affect and an unfavorable self-view (Buunk & Dijkstra, 2017).

Importantly, the above-mentioned retrospective self-report studies on comparison have mainly focused on dimensions such as well-being or physical appearance. Hence, the findings may not generalize to performance judgments. Furthermore, retrospective studies cannot distinguish between responses to a given comparison standard, effects arising from biased comparison standard selection (Schlechter et al., 2024), and effects of differential feedback exposure, whereby individuals with depression might encounter more frequent or more strongly negative feedback. False feedback experiments (e.g. Venables & Fairclough, 2009) allow researchers to isolate the response to a given comparison standard: When feedback is experimentally assigned to participants, comparison information becomes independent of actual test scores (van Yperen & Leander, 2014). Given that depression is associated with reduced performance motivation (Grahek et al., 2019), HAPSCI may be absent in individuals with depression because comparisons serving self-assessment and selfimprovement may become less salient. This could selectively reduce the impact of social feedback (Wilson & Ross, 2000). Dysphoric individuals' tendency to identify less with upward, and more with downward standards (Buunk & Dijkstra, 2017) may further contribute to pessimism and behavioral quiescence toward self-improvement (Morina, 2021). Negative self-construal may predispose them to view the mere possibility of outperforming others as unrealistic and expect unfavorable outcomes instead (Disner et al., 2017; Kube et al., 2017) and to experience favorable social feedback as conflicting with a coherent self-construal (Conway et al., 2004). Dysphoria is also associated with negative attitudes toward social comparisons (Schlechter et al., 2023). All these factors may contribute to dysphoric individuals' greater tendency to neglect or dismiss favorable social comparison information.

At the same time, temporal feedback on cognitive performance might be particularly salient in depression, since complaints regarding cognitive deficits are common in depression (Gualtieri & Morgan, 2008; McIntyre et al., 2013), which may lead to preoccupation with changes in one's own performance. Together, these factors may lead dysphoric individuals to deemphasize social comparison information and may even result in a "higher assigned priority of temporal comparison information" (HAPTCI).

The present study

The aim of our study was to investigate if HAPSCI generalizes to individuals with dysphoric symptoms. We tested the effects of concurrent false social and temporal feedback on performance self-assessments for a cognitive task in a group with dysphoric symptoms, adopting the experimental design and feedback of van Yperen and Leander (2014). We applied the n-back task (Klein, 1997), a widely used paradigm requiring participants to attend, maintain, update and match stimulus information across several trials. Developed as a measure of working memory, the n-back task seems to reflect a range of cognitive functions.

Its ecological and face validity (Jaeggi et al., 2010; Kane et al., 2007), as well as the considerable rate of error in performance self-judgments (Conte et al., 2023), make it wellsuited for false feedback. Furthermore, people with depression show worse n-back performance than controls, most pronouncedly at the 2-back level of difficulty (Nikolin et al., 2021). Participants worked through two blocks of a visuospatial 2-back task and then either received simultaneous false temporal and social comparison feedback, or no performance feedback at all, following van Yperen and Leander's (2014) experimental manipulation.

Feedback valence (favorable vs. unfavorable) was varied independently for each comparison standard. Our main dependent variable was subsequent performance self-assessment. We additionally considered achievement goals and objective n-back performance. We predicted feedback effects on performance self-assessments to match feedback valence, with more pronounced effects of negative than of positive feedback.

Method Sample

Participants were recruited from a pool of German-speaking users of the PsyWeb platform (https://psyweb.uni-muenster.de), which maintains a pool of interested volunteers for participation in psychological studies. Using G*Power (Faul et al., 2007), we estimated required sample size based on the smaller in each pair of main effects in van Yperen and Leander's (2014) studies 1 and 2 (i.e. the average effect of temporal comparison information on self-assessment). To detect a main effect equivalent to the weighted average of f=.254 in a 2x2 between-groups ANOVA with a power of 1-β= .80, and an accepted type 1 error risk of α=.05, N=124 (n=31 per group) participants are required. Adding a no-feedback control group raised this target to N=155. Interested candidates (N=407) were considered eligible for the study if they were between 18 and 60 years of age and proficient in the German language, reported at least mild symptoms of depression (i.e. PHQ-8 score of five or higher; Kroenke et al., 2009, see "materials and apparatus"), and reported neither current suicidality or a psychotic disorder. Of N=252 eligible candidates, only those who completed at least two blocks of the n-back task and the performance self-assessment were included in our final sample, which consisted of N=159 participants (115 women, 41 men, 3 non-binary, diverse gender or undisclosed, mean age= 41.77 years, SD=12.24, range =19-60).

Materials and Instruments

Patient Health Questionnaire-8. Dysphoric Symptoms were assessed using the German translation (Gräfe et al., 2004) of the 8-item depression subscale of the Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-8, Kroenke et al., 2009). The PHQ-8 is a self-report scale for symptoms of depression, except for self-harm and suicidality. Participants indicate how often they experienced each symptom during the last two weeks on a four-point-scale (0 = not at all,3 = nearly every day), which are then added up. Only participants with a score ≥ 5, indicating at least mild symptoms, were included in our study. Good internal consistency is reported for the 9-item version's German translation (Cronbach's α = .88) and was similar for the PHQ-8 in our sample (Cronbach's α = .799).

N-back task. For a graphic illustration of a short sequence of n-back trials, see Figure 1.

In the visuospatial n-back task (Kirchner, 1958), participants match a current stimulus display to the one encountered a number n of trials prior. To ensure sensitivity to depression (Nikolin et al., 2021), and a sufficient difficulty level to constrain self-assessment accuracy (Conte et al., 2023), we chose 2-back task, adopting a template provided by Labvanced (also used in Meyer et al., 2026). Stimuli consisted of nine different versions of a visual array wherein eight circles with black outlines (hex code: #000000) were arranged in a ring pattern at the center of a white (#FFFFFF) screen. Of these, eight were target arrays, each containing one green-filled (#00c100) circle in a different position, with the remaining seven circles left blank. The remaining one was a placeholder array consisting of 8 blank circles without color fill. The task was introduced to participants as a "test of concentration and memory". They worked through one block of twelve practice trials before starting the main task, which consisted of three separate blocks of 82 trials. At the start of each trial, a target array containing a green circle was presented for 2000ms. During practice trials, target arrays were followed by accuracy feedback for the participant wherein the word "correct" or "incorrect" appeared in black font at the center of the screen for 1000ms. If no response was registered within 2000ms of target onset, the feedback read "incorrect". There was no accuracy feedback during the main task. Instead, target arrays were followed by the placeholder array for 500ms, before the next trial began with a new target array. Participants' task was to memorize the position of the green circle across trials and decide if its position in the current trial -n -matched its position in the second-to-last-trial -n-2 -for each new target array, by pressing either "C" if it did (repetition), or "M" if it did not (non-repetition). There were four repetitions and eight non-repetitions during the practice block, and 24 repetitions and 58 non-repetitions during each block of the main task. Trial order was identical for all participants.

The green circle appeared in each position between six and 14 times.

Achievement goals. We assessed achievement goals according to Elliot and McGregor's (2001) 2x2 framework using a single forced-choice question. Participants indicated their goal for the following two blocks of the 2-back task by clicking on one of the following four options: "do better than others in both blocks" (performance-approach), "not do worse than others in both blocks" (performance-avoidance), "do better in the second block than in the first block" (mastery-approach), and "not do worse in the second block than in the first block" (mastery-avoidance).

Social and temporal comparison information: Comparison information statements were adopted from van Yperen and Leander (2014). Social feedback referred to participants' performance relative to the sample and could be favorable (indicating better performance than most other participants) or unfavorable (worse performance). Temporal feedback referred to participants' change in performance between the two main blocks and could likewise be favorable (indicating improvement) or unfavorable (indicating deterioration).

Social and temporal feedback appeared on screen simultaneously, with one type presented on top of the other, separated by a paragraph break. To control for primacy effects (Sullivan, 2019), we randomized feedback position (social vs. temporal on top) between participants (see Table S1). Favorable social feedback read: "In Version 1 and 2 of the concentration task, you have achieved a rank of 27%. The lower your rank in percent, the better your performance. Compared to the rest of the sample, this is a good performance." For unfavorable social feedback, this was changed to: "In Version 1 and 2 of the concentration task, you have achieved a rank of 73%. The higher your rank in percent, the worse your performance. Compared to the rest of the sample, this is a bad performance." Favorable temporal feedback read: "Between Version 1 and Version 2 of the concentration task, your performance has improved from 50% to 4%. The lower the rank in percent, the better your performance compared to before." For unfavorable temporal feedback, this was changed to: "Between Version 1 and Version 2 of the concentration task, your performance has declined from 50% to 96%. The higher the rank in percent, the worse your performance compared to before." Feedback was presented in black font on a white background, with percentages and the words "good/bad performance", "improved" and "declined" in boldface, and remained on screen until participants chose to continue.

Performance self-assessment. Following van Yperen and Leander (2014), participants judged their own performance during the n-back task by clicking on the corresponding option on a 10-point rating scale, ranging from 1 ("very poor") to 10 ("excellent").

Procedure

Participants completed the study online, from their own desktop or laptop computer.

Interested candidates were invited to participate via PsyWeb and started their session by clicking a link to Unipark where they were welcomed, briefed and given the opportunity to consent or withdraw. Consenting candidates then entered the screening. They were asked to generate a pseudonymous code for the purpose of linking datasets across platforms, and to give information on their age, German language proficiency, and presence of suicidal ideation or psychosis, and to complete the PHQ-8. If any exclusion criterion was met, candidates were immediately redirected to a final page providing a reason for the exclusion as well as researchers' contact information. For suicidality, we also included information on online and phone support resources, and for suicidality and psychosis, candidates were encouraged to consult with a professional. The remaining participants then provided information on their demographic background and current medication intake, before being redirected to Labvanced for the experiment phase where, after re-entering their pseudonymous code, they received instructions for the n-back task and completed the practice block. They then indicated their achievement goal for the two following n-back blocks. After once more receiving instructions, they started the main n-back task. Participants worked through two blocks of 82 trials (164 trials in total), separated by a self-paced pause, during which they again received reminder instructions and were informed they had completed the first part of the concentration test. Once participants had completed the second block of n-back trials, the false feedback manipulation was administered. Participants in the four experimental groups were told they would receive information about their performance compared to other participants and across the two blocks, and were then shown the social and temporal false feedback according to the study design. For participants in the control group, the feedback was skipped. Next, participants gave their performance self-assessments. To check if they understood the feedback correctly, participants in the four experimental groups answered two additional questions, indicating if their performance in block two, compared to block one, and compared to other participants, respectively, had been "good" or "bad". All groups then worked through a third block of 82 2-back trials. Afterwards, participants were thanked, debriefed and given the opportunity to offer additional remarks as free text. The study took approximately 40-45 minutes to complete. We collected personal and sensitive information via Unipark (Questback GmbH., 2017), and all other data via Labvanced (Finger et al., 2017).

Treatment of data and statistical analysis

All results described as statistically significant are associated with a p-value of <.05 (twotailed, unless otherwise noted). Of the N=159 participants who completed the first two n-back blocks and self-assessments, one participant did not provide responses during blocks 1 and 2, and two further participants dropped out before completing the third n-back block, resulting in a total of N= 156 (98.1%) participants included in analyses of feedback effects on performance. Our primary dependent variable was self-assessment of performance (see Figure 2 & Table S1), while accuracy and response time as measures of objective performance constitute additional outcomes. N-back accuracy was computed as mean sensitivity Pr (Snodgrass & Corwin, 1988), defined as the difference between the proportion of correct responses in one type of trial ("C" in repetition trials) and the proportion of errors resulting from the same response ("C" in non-repetition trials). Trials without responses were excluded from Pr. Mean correct reaction times (RTs) are based on correct n-back responses in the main task given between 200ms to 2000ms after target onset.

Discussion

Drawing on the achievement goal framework (Elliot & McGregor, 2001), we investigated contributions of experimentally manipulated social and temporal comparison information to performance self-assessments on a visuospatial n-back task (Kirchner, 1958) in a sample of dysphoric individuals. Instead of an "Overpowering Effect" (TOESCI; van Yperen & Leander, 2014) or "Higher assigned priority" (HAPSCI) of social comparison information, we found that only temporal comparison information had a clear impact on selfassessments. This remained true even when controlling for potential confounds such as presentation order, symptom severity, objective performance, and age. Temporal feedback accounted for almost 8% of total variance in self-assessments, while social feedback contributed little, if any unique variance (<1%). Thus, the evidence in our study points toward a "higher assigned priority of temporal comparison information" (HAPTCI) in individuals with dysphoria. In line with research on cognitive biases in depression (Kube, 2026; LeMoult & Gotlib, 2019) we found a valence asymmetry in feedback effects on judgments: When compared to a baseline without feedback, the impact of temporal comparison information was strongest when it was unfavorable. Moreover, we replicated the widespread preference for mastery goals over performance goals (van Yperen & Orehek, 2013), although this preference decreased with increasing symptom severity. As expected, participants with more severe dysphoric symptoms judged their own performance as poorer.

While the impact of temporal feedback on self-assessments was not accompanied by corresponding effects on performance, the otherwise observed performance gains were absent following negative social feedback.

Temporal or social comparison information: Which is prioritized?

Our results suggest that social comparison information may not always exert a stronger influence than temporal comparison information, calling into question the generalizability of HAPSCI. Our participants with subclinical symptoms of depression, prioritized temporal comparison information, exhibiting HAPTCI. Thus, the relative impact of either type of comparison information may depend on the population under study.

Feedback effects on self-assessment were not accompanied by corresponding effects on performance itself, such that temporal comparison information affected self-assessments, but not n-back performance. Overall, participants' speed and sensitivity improved across blocks, reflecting genuine performance gains without a shift in response bias, although it seems that this upward trend was curtailed after unfavorable social feedback. We currently do not know why this disruption occurred or if it is indeed a robust phenomenon. We did not predict this disruptive effect of social feedback on performance. Together with the intergroup nature of our design and the lack of a controlled laboratory environment, it may well represent a spurious finding. Conversely, performance self-assessments might have been impacted by temporal feedback only because social comparisons are viewed more negatively, and selfassessment ratings more closely reflects participants' explicitly endorsed values or strategic effort to respond in a goal-consistent manner. Social feedback then might have selectively impacted the n-back task because speeded performance is more prone to disruption and less controllable than explicit judgments. N-back performance may reflect more automatic responses to unfavorable feedback, such as reduced attentional control and diverted cognitive resources. This would fit with evidence that such disruptive effects of negative feedback are typically exacerbated in depression (Mueller et al., 2015; Xie et al., 2022), but raise the question of why temporal feedback did not likewise affect performance.

We obtained partial support for the suggestion that comparison feedback consistent with recipients' personal goals is more effective (Janson et al., 2022). While HAPTCI was largely in accordance with mastery-oriented participants' achievement goals, endorsement of a performance goal did not translate into a corresponding prioritization of social comparison information. In line with previous evidence that the link between unfavorable comparisons and depression is stronger when comparisons relevance is considered social, rather than just personal (Thwaites & Dagnan, 2004), participants with more severe symptoms were more likely to endorse performance goals. On the other hand, performance goals were also more frequently chosen when objective performance was better, perhaps reflecting a well-founded trust in one's ability. While this may seem surprising in depression, it is worth remembering that expecting to outperform a given comparison group need not translate into a positive selfjudgment. Above-average performance itself might not be enough to meet one's personal standards for success, and the significance or difficulty of a successfully completed task might be downplayed (Elliot & Hulleman, 2017; Morina, 2021).

To our knowledge, the methods described here have so far largely been confined to educational, sports, and occupational psychology, despite the prominent roles of social and temporal comparisons in depressive cognition. The present study was conceived as a preliminary step toward expanding this research into the field of psychopathology and, as such, focused on individuals with depressive symptoms. The lack of a direct comparison without dysphoric symptoms precludes any sweeping conclusions about the pattern of HAPTCI versus HAPSCI in our study as either a unique or a universal feature of depressive cognition.

Several study parameters may have contributed to the seeming discrepancy between our and van Yperen and Leander's (2014) findings. For one, our experiment took place in an online setting, which might have rendered social comparisons less relevant and prevented local dominance effects (Zell & Alicke, 2010). Head-to-head comparisons between webbased and laboratory experiments support the validity of online research (Semmelmann & Weigelt, 2017) and our own experience bears this out (e.g. Englert et al., 2023). Web-based comparison studies could find ways to compensate for the absence of a real-life peer group, for instance by providing more information about social comparison standards and highlighting similarities, or by embedding social interactions into their procedures. Second, van Yperen and Leander's samples from narrow professional and educational contexts (i.e.

university students and call center employees) may have emphasized the importance of competition and performance goals (Garcia et al., 2013; Wolff & Möller, 2022). Rather than reflecting a disconnect between their goals and rational feedback utilization, perhaps HAPSCI occurred simply because the prioritization of social comparison is fulfilling the demands of the setting. Our participants did not have any such external incentive and were therefore perhaps freer to ignore information that did not fit their personal mastery orientation. Third, our participants' average age was in their early forties, whereas Vvn Yperen and Leander ( 2014) conducted their research on students and young professionals.

This difference may matter because achievement goals, and by extension the relative importance of different types of comparisons, tend to change across the lifespan: The importance of performance goals, competition and competence-based achievement domains seems to decrease over the course of working age (Buchinger et al., 2022; Caldwell et al., 2004; Heidemeier & Staudinger, 2015; Lange et al., 2010). Therefore, age should correlate with a stronger focus on personal development, mastery goals and temporal comparison information. Indeed, participants who endorsed performance-approach goals in our study were younger on average than those who endorsed mastery goals. For instance, neuroticism, which is closely linked to both social comparison orientation and mental health (Gibbons & Buunk, 1999; Jensen et al., 2020; Kotov et al., 2010) may be a potential mediator, since it tends to decrease with age, with the steepest decline reported roughly between the ages of twenty and forty years (Roberts et al., 2006). The relationship between HAPSCI/HAPTCI and age, as well as potential developmental and personality factors, should be examined in future studies.

Aside from sample characteristics, there are differences in feedback presentation and the cognitive tasks on which assessments were supposed to be based. First, we tried to control for the possibility that primacy effects (Sullivan, 2019) inadvertently contributed to HAPSCI.

Given that Latin script is read from left to right and from top to bottom, concurrent presentation of social and temporal feedback on the same screen (van Yperen & Leander, 2014) is not enough to rule out this potential confound. We varied presentation order through the placement of social and temporal feedback and did not observe any effects of feedback placement, ruling out any account of HAPSCI as an artifact of presentation order. Second, we do not know for certain how much trust participants had in the feedback in either the d2 (Brickenkamp, 1962) used by van Yperen and Leander or the 2-back (Kirchner, 1958) tasks.

A few participants indeed expressed having been suspicious of the feedback as well as difficulty comprehending the task, both of which might have affected the extent to which their judgment relied on the comparison information we gave them. Since objective performance usually informs self-assessment to some extent (Freund & Kasten, 2012), false feedback may be less effective when the task is transparent, and familiar and when performance is easy to track and either very high or very low. This was not the case in our study. Although our participants showed a relatively high ability to discriminate between targets and non-targets, they still made a considerable number of errors across blocks, and several later commented on the task's difficulty. Subjective judgments of one's own accuracy do not seem to reliably track visual working memory or 2-back performance (Adam & Vogel, 2017; Conte et al., 2023). In line with this, we found that objective performance explained only a moderate proportion of the variance in self-assessments when no feedback was given, which was further reduced in the experimental groups despite the prevalence of doubts about feedback validity. Furthermore, high metacognitive accuracy should have selectively weakened trust in temporal feedback. After all, participants could only track their own performance, but no similar insight into the objective performance of others should be possible. Given that the effect of temporal feedback persisted when controlling for objective performance, it seems justified to conclude that the feedback was believable enough to modify participants' prior expectations. Even so, metacognitive beliefs about one's ability and trust in researchers feedback need to be considered in the context of a given cognitive task. Future false feedback studies should compare performance, feedback sensitivity, and participant's metacognitive accuracy and across tasks tapping into different cognitive domains, at varying levels of difficulty and include independent measures of meta-cognitive accuracy.

Clinical implications

While achievement goals and comparison standards are associated with well-being in general (Dickhäuser et al., 2017; Elliot & McGregor, 2001; Rawsthorne & Elliot, 1999; van Yperen & Janssen, 2002), research on their clinical implications remains limited. Achievement goal focus (mastery vs. performance) and valence (approach vs. avoidance) might interact in characteristic ways with effects of comparison type (e.g. social vs. temporal) and direction (upward vs. downward) in depression. Motivational deficits, as well as pessimism and low confidence in one's abilities (Disner et al., 2017; Grahek et al., 2019; Kube et al., 2017) should be reflected in a higher prevalence of avoidance goals (Boissicat et al., 2022; van Yperen & Orehek, 2013). Although symptom severity did not correlate with goal valence in our sample, it was associated with higher rates of performance orientation. As discussed earlier, upward social comparisons should not be considered detrimental across the board (Buunk & Dijkstra, 2017). For instance, in contexts that promote approach rather than avoidance motivation and given an attainable standard, dysphoric individuals may even benefit from upward social comparisons in some cases (Boissicat et al., 2022). However, upward comparisons characteristic of depressive cognition are widely experienced as distressing, and often seem to run counter to the individual's needs (Schlechter et al., 2023 (Schlechter et al., , 2024)). Achievement goals may provide one framework for distinguishing between adaptive and maladaptive comparisons. Future studies should re-examine their relationship to comparisons and clinical symptoms with increased statistical power and finer measures of goal structure. Feedback was more impactful when it was unfavorable than when it was favorable: Unfavorable temporal comparison information disproportionately affected performance self-assessments compared to no feedback, and unfavorable social comparison information appeared to disrupt performance gains. While the causes of these differential effects of feedback type (social vs. temporal) remain to be studied, this valence asymmetry is in line with depressive cognitive biases in judgment, attention and feedback processing ( Disner et al., 2017; Kube et al., 2017; LeMoult & Gotlib, 2019; Mueller et al., 2015; Xie et al., 2022). Participants' self-assessments were more resistant to social comparison information than previously reported in comparable studies that did not select for symptoms of psychopathology (Janson et al., 2022; van Yperen & Leander, 2014; Wolff et al., 2018).

Participants also showed a clear negativity bias when recalling their own performance, as they were more likely to agree with social feedback when it was unfavorable than when it was favorable. While the same pattern did not emerge for temporal feedback, neither was there evidence of self-enhancement bias in the recall of performance across time. It seems plausible that an unresponsiveness to favorable feedback would impede the correction of the kinds of dysfunctional self-cognitions common in depression. Susceptibility to unfavorable temporal, and resistance to favorable social feedback should be further investigated as potential contributors to the maintenance of depressive cognition. Comparisons matter to our well-being and self-concept (Morina, 2021), and insight into motives and mechanisms behind "depressive" responses to social and temporal comparison information may help clinicians target maladaptive cognitions, and provide more effective feedback to dysphoric patients (Hoppen et al., 2025). More research into the clinical role of social and temporal comparisons in dysphoria is clearly warranted.

Limitations and directions for further research

A rigorous test of HAPSCI/HAPTCI and their boundary conditions hinges on strong experimental design. We argue that, for now, effects of concurrent social and temporal feedback should be termed "prioritization effects" (HAPSCI/HAPTCI), rather than "overpowering effects" (van Yperen & Leander, 2014). Simple differences in effect size are insufficient to conclude that one type of information "overpowers" another, especially when temporal feedback retains a demonstrable impact. To evaluate this claim, direct effects and interactions between different types of feedback need to be disentangled in a full-factorial design, to test for potential interactions. If one type of comparison information is truly "overpowering", effects of other types should be reduced or eliminated by competing feedback, as compared to conditions in which the same feedback is provided in isolation.

Single-feedback conditions would also permit a stronger test of valence asymmetries by providing additional, unconfounded controls. Future experiments therefore need to expand their designs to include single-feedback controls to isolate the respective impact of social and temporal comparison information. A dichotomous measure of performance versus mastery orientation may not capture the full reality of achievement goals and overestimate the presumed misalignment between achievement goals and the impact of comparison information: Different achievement goals need not be mutually exclusive, and not everyone has a single dominant goal (Lange et al., 2010; van Yperen, 2006). Relevant information may be obscured when achievement goals are assessed via a single forced-choice question. Future studies should address this, for instance via a number of successive paired choices (van Yperen, 2006) or by obtaining independent ratings of each goal's personal importance. Of course, for feedback to be effective, participants must first understand it in the intended way.

Indeed, a few participants noted that the temporal feedback especially felt arbitrary or unclear to them. However, we aligned our phrasing and score values with van Yperen and Leander's (2014) feedback which we consider a successful precedent, and a majority of our sample recalled their performance in a feedback-congruent way, suggesting that it was effectively conveyed to most participants 4 . Regardless, we suggest probing future participants' interpretation as a precaution. Although we found preliminary evidence for the occurrence of HAPTCI in depression, its generalizability across populations remains to be investigated further. While our sample was diverse in terms of age, education level and occupational background, it only consisted of German-speaking volunteers interested in psychological research who were registered on PsyWeb. Future research could benefit from recruitment from a broader participant pool when studying HAPSCI/HAPTCI in relation to psychopathology. Another factor is comparison dimension. The personal relevance of a given dimension may vary between individuals and demographics. Our results cannot be presumed to generalize beyond the specific kind of cognitive performance task examined here.

Achievement in one domain may be intimately tied to a person's self-concept, while achievement in another may simply not matter all that much (Morina, 2021; Sedikides & Strube, 1997). It is quite possible that the typical goal distributions and effects of comparison information found in one domain do not hold in another (van Yperen et al., 2014). Of course, by no means are only judgments of competence and achievement informed by comparisons.
