Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). Statcheck might still miss one-sided tests that are described in another sentence, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Abstract

Mindfulness of death (MDM) is a contemplative practice centered on death and life's finitude.

We review its concept, practice forms, and empirical findings and propose a three-dimensional framework: death-focused content, attentional regulation, and value guidance. Death-focused content centers contemplation on death and life's finitude. Attentional regulation maintains and redirects attention to death and may include decentering from experiences arising during practice. Value guidance, when present, directs practitioners toward value-related understandings of death and life. We discuss psychological processes associated with these dimensions and potential mechanisms of MDM. We also examine its contributions to death research: introducing death reconstrual as a distinct form of death awareness, extending the paradigm from a one-time induction to repeatable practice, and broadening epistemological and ideological perspectives.

Finally, we outline directions for future research.

The Concept of Mindfulness of Death

The core operation of MDM is repeated recollection of the inevitability of death, uncertainty about the time of death, and the brevity and fragility of life, while continuously maintaining death and the finitude of life as objects of contemplation (Bodhi, 2012; Buddhaghosa, 2011; Moon, 2019). Some MDM practices ask practitioners to repeatedly recite statements such as "death, death" or "death will occur; the life force will be interrupted," whereas more complex forms may ask practitioners to consider death-related questions, such as what they would do if death were imminent or if they could not be reborn as a human in a future life (Buddhaghosa, 2011; Moon, 2019). Beyond MDM itself, systematic Buddhist training concerning mindfulness of death may include a broader range of doctrinal themes, such as rebirth, with details and emphases varying across traditions (Buddhaghosa, 2011). Regardless of the specific form, traditional MDM aims to reduce heedlessness and attachment to life by emphasizing and reflecting on death, deepen recognition of impermanence, and encourage practitioners to value the present and practice more diligently (Bodhi, 2012; Buddhaghosa, 2011). It is important to clarify that MDM can easily be confused with another important Buddhist practice, meditation on foulness (asubha). Meditation on foulness primarily involves contemplating the unattractive or impure nature of the body in order to reduce attachment to the body and sensual desire, and some forms of the practice may involve visualizing a corpse in various stages of decay and decomposition. Although such practices also involve death-related content, they differ substantially from MDM in their primary aims and intended effects (Buddhaghosa, 2011; Moon, 2020). The two practices should therefore be carefully distinguished in research, application, and practice.

Modern psychology retains the core structure of MDM-actively evoking and continuously processing death awareness-while adapting the practice through secularization and standardization according to research aims and target populations. Simpler practices may require participants to repeatedly recite statements concerning their own death while maintaining steady breathing. More complex practices may use guided imagery in which participants visualize dying, death, or other death-related content and then consider how they should live within a finite life (Moon, 2019; Tan et al., 2025; Tang et al., 2026). In addition to contemplative practice itself, modern MDM interventions may include structured psychoeducation on understandings of death, meaning in life, and value-based choices in everyday life. For example, Tang et al. (2026) combined contemplative practice with content concerning the inevitability of death, life priorities, and how to engage with present life, prompting participants to distinguish trivial or unproductive concerns from what they genuinely value and to consider where to direct their attention and energy. Moon (2020) likewise described MDM-based death education that integrates death contemplation with topics such as definitions of death, the dying process, neardeath experiences, religious and philosophical views of death, suicide, and meaning in life. Thus, modern MDM can range from brief repetition of death-related statements to systematic interventions integrating contemplation, death education, and values reflection. Its content can be adapted to practitioners' age, cultural background, psychological state, and intervention goals.

Effects of Mindfulness of Death

Direct empirical research on MDM remains limited, with substantial variation in research design, form of practice, and training intensity. Several studies have evaluated multi-session or structured MDM training. Moon (2016 , as cited in Moon, 2020) implemented a 13-session MDM-based death education program among Korean adolescents; after the course, students showed more positive attitudes toward life and greater death acceptance. Park et al. (2016) used quasi-experimental design to examine an MDM program lasting five nights and six days, totaling 48 hours. Compared with the control group, the intervention group showed lower death anxiety and higher quality of life after training. In a randomized controlled study of patients with cancer, Tan et al. (2025) found that guided MDM reduced fear of death and several indicators of distress related to death and dying. Some participants, however, experienced discomfort with imagery involving bodily decomposition and dying or perceived conflicts between the practice and their religious beliefs. More recently, Tang et al. (2026) conducted a three-arm randomized controlled trial comparing MDM, mindfulness meditation, and death-scenario imagery. Relative to mindful breathing, MDM elicited greater anxiety and fear; relative to death-scenario imagery, however, it produced lower anxiety, fear, fear of death, and death avoidance, together with greater calmness and natural acceptance of death.

Laboratory research has further compared the immediate and subsequent effects of MDM with other death-related or mindfulness practices. Tang et al. (2026) found that, relative to mindful breathing, MDM may increase state anxiety and fear and reduce calmness; relative to death-scenario imagery, however, MDM produced lower anxiety, fear, fear of death, and death avoidance, together with greater calmness and natural acceptance.

Additionally, cross-sectional research provides further evidence on associations between MDM-related practice and psychological adjustment. Among older Thai Buddhist meditation practitioners, death contemplation was associated with lower levels of depression, while more positive attitudes toward death were associated with greater well-being and gratitude and lower depression (Glushich et al., 2025). DeMaranville et al. (2025), studying adolescents in Thai boarding schools, found that MDM practice moderated the association between stress and depressive symptoms: among students who reported practicing MDM, the association between stress and depression was weaker.

Qualitative research further adds practitioners' subjective experiences. In interviews with experienced practitioners, Sadykova (2025) found that participants commonly described MDM as a practice that helped them reinterpret the relationship between life and death, clarify values, strengthen awareness of relationships, and deepen metacognition and emotion regulation. The intensity and acceptability of the practice experience were also affected by practice frequency, practice intensity, and individual emotional readiness.

Overall, existing research has begun to examine a range of psychological outcomes of MDM, including death-related attitudes and emotions such as death anxiety, fear of death, death avoidance, and death acceptance, as well as broader outcomes such as quality of life, and values reflection (Moon, 2020; Park et al., 2016; Tan et al., 2025; Tang et al., 2026). However, the evidence remains limited, particularly with respect to rigorous randomized controlled designs.

Findings concerning death anxiety or fear, death avoidance, and death acceptance are relatively consistent across studies, whereas evidence concerning attitudes toward life and general psychological adjustment remains more inconsistent (Moon, 2020; Park et al., 2016; Tan et al., 2025; Tang et al., 2026). Moreover, studies differ substantially in intervention content, practice format, training dosage, and sample characteristics, making it difficult to draw firm conclusions about the overall effectiveness or stability of MDM effects (Moon, 2020; Tan et al., 2025; Tang et al., 2026). Notably, most research has focused on changes in death-related cognition, emotion, and attitudes, with little attention to the attentional processes inherent in MDM as a meditation practice. Yet attentional regulation is an important process in meditation (Hasenkamp et al., 2012; MacLean et al., 2010). At the same time, MDM practices can also contain explicit valueoriented guidance. For example, the MDM-based death education described by Moon (2020) asks practitioners to consider how they would live if only limited time remained and links this reflection to meaning in life and life choices. Such guidance may involve additional process variables that contribute to MDM outcomes, but its independent contribution has not yet been directly tested. As discussed below, this feature is also present in traditional Buddhist practice and represents a distinctive aspect of MDM. Taken together, understanding MDM merely as a means of keeping death in awareness may overlook other practice-specific processes inherent in meditation training itself that may contribute to its effects.

A Three-Dimensional Framework of Mindfulness of Death

Although the basic concept of MDM has been provisionally defined, the practices used across studies still vary substantially. Existing research has focused more on psychological outcomes and mediating variables than on systematically describing the structure of MDM itself.

Drawing on the specific operations of MDM and the existing empirical literature, we propose three basic dimensions: death-focused content, attentional regulation, and value guidance. This framework can be used to describe and compare different forms of MDM, clarify how MDM may produce its effects, and provide a basis for future research and practice optimization.

Death-Focused Content

First, the most obvious feature of MDM is that its content centers on death. Specific exercises may vary by developer, training goal, and target population, but they share the feature that death or the finitude of life remains the central object of contemplation. In this sense, MDM evokes death awareness in practitioners. Moon (2019) likewise described MDM as a tool for increasing mortality salience.

Death awareness may also account for some effects of MDM. Terror management theory proposes that awareness of one's inevitable death constitutes a fundamental existential threat and initiates corresponding emotional and motivational regulatory processes (Pyszczynski et al., 1999). Consistently, Tang et al. (2026) found that, compared with mindful breathing, a single MDM session produced greater anxiety and fear and less calmness, suggesting that death-related content itself can elicit a pronounced threat response.

However, changes arising from death awareness are not limited to fear. Research suggests that when individuals situate their lives within the context of death and finitude, they may also readjust their goals and values and orient more strongly toward intrinsically meaningful and growth-related goals (Cozzolino et al., 2004; Lykins et al., 2007; Vail et al., 2012). By repeatedly emphasizing that death is inevitable and that its timing cannot be known, MDM places current goals, relationships, and behaviors within the reference frame of a finite life, prompting individuals to reconsider what deserves their investment and what matters most. Moon (2016 , as cited in Moon, 2020) found that adolescents who participated in an MDM-based death education program showed more positive attitudes toward life and greater acceptance of death following the program. Thus, death-focused content in MDM may not only generate threat but also provide a motivational basis for subsequent evaluations of life and changes in attitude.

Attentional Regulation

Beyond death-focused content, another important dimension of MDM is attentional regulation, which is fundamental to its operation as a meditative practice. Traditional Buddhism commonly distinguishes samatha from vipassana meditation; samatha cultivates concentration by stabilizing attention on a specific object, and death is one of the objects used in traditional meditation (Buddhaghosa, 2011). From the perspective of contemporary psychology, the basic operation of MDM resembles focused-attention meditation: attention is maintained on a selected object and redirected to that object after attentional drift (Lutz et al., 2008).

Existing MDM research has focused primarily on death-related aspects of the practice (Shonin & Van Gordon, 2014; Tan et al., 2025; DeMaranville et al., 2025), with less discussion of its specific psychological operations as a meditation practice. The process most often described is that practitioners establish death as the object of attention and sustain attention to it.

When attention drifts, they redirect it to death. This resembles the cycle of "mind wanderingawareness of mind wandering-reorientation-sustained attention" described by Hasenkamp et al. (2012).

Beyond attentional maintenance and reorientation, we propose that MDM may also involve a process of decentering. Decentering refers to a shift from immersion in one's experience to observing the experience itself-that is, noticing internal experiences without overidentifying with or automatically following them (Bernstein et al., 2015). Existing descriptions of MDM practice contain similar features. For example, DeMaranville et al. (2025) describe practitioners as keeping death in awareness while allowing reactive thoughts and feelings to pass and continuing to maintain meditative focus; Shonin and Van Gordon (2014) likewise emphasize contemplating death with equanimity and allowing experience to unfold without clinging to it. These descriptions suggest that MDM may involve not only sustained attention to death but also noticing internal experiences that arise during practice without becoming overly absorbed in them, followed by returning attention to death. This pattern involves a decentering process. Decentering may alter how death-related experiences are noticed, understood, and responded to, which could help explain the lower anxiety, fear, fear of death, and death avoidance and greater calmness and natural acceptance of death observed in MDM research (Tang et al., 2026). However, existing MDM research has not directly measured attentional maintenance or decentering as process variables. Future studies therefore need to measure these processes directly to test whether they actually occur during MDM and whether they help explain its psychological effects.

Value Guidance

Beyond death-focused content and attentional regulation, MDM also includes an important dimension that we term value guidance. Value guidance refers to explicit guidance within the practice that directs practitioners toward particular value-related understandings and encourages them to reconsider meaning and modify their behavior accordingly. In traditional Buddhist practice, MDM not only encourages practitioners to recognize death and impermanence, but may also guide them to reduce heedlessness and unwholesome behavior, weaken attachment, and deepen their understanding of impermanence, suffering, and nonself (Buddhaghosa, 2011). Contemporary MDM may further provide explicit guidance concerning what is genuinely important and how one should live in the face of death and a finite life, linking death awareness to meaning in life, personal values, relationship choices, use of time, and life goals (Moon, 2020; Shonin & Van Gordon, 2014; Tang et al., 2026). It should be clarified, however, that value guidance is not a necessary component of MDM. Some basic forms primarily involve repeatedly recollecting or reciting death-related content without explicit valueoriented prompts (Buddhaghosa, 2011; Moon, 2019). Value guidance is therefore better understood as an important but variable dimension of MDM practice.

From a broader theoretical perspective, value-oriented practices may influence cognitive and affective responses by changing how individuals interpret their experiences. Dahl et al. (2015) classified death contemplation as a value-oriented constructive practice and proposed that such practices may operate through processes such as cognitive reappraisal. More broadly, research on values has shown that reflection on personally important values can facilitate information acceptance and changes in intentions and behavior (Epton et al., 2015). Existing MDM research also provides some indirect evidence that value guidance may contribute to practice outcomes. Tang et al. (2026) found that, compared with death-scenario imagination, MDM reduced anxiety, fear, fear of death, and death avoidance while increasing calmness and natural acceptance of death. These findings suggest that the effects of MDM may not arise from death-related content alone and that additional guidance within the practice may also contribute.

However, the study could not determine whether these differences were specifically attributable to value guidance or distinguish its contribution from those of other practice dimensions. At the same time, death awareness itself may prompt values reflection and changes in attitudes toward life (Cozzolino et al., 2004; Lykins et al., 2007), making it difficult to determine how much of the observed effects can be independently attributed to value guidance. Overall, existing evidence provides theoretical and indirect support for a possible role of value guidance, but its independent contribution has not yet been directly tested and should be examined through targeted comparative designs.

In addition, whereas death-focused content is relatively consistent across MDM practices, value guidance varies considerably. For example, Moon's (2020) intervention emphasizes meaning in life and valuing one's limited lifetime, whereas the intervention described by Tang et al. (2026) places greater emphasis on life priorities and self-regulation. Which forms of guidance are effective, and whether different forms produce distinct outcomes, remain open questions.

Overall, value guidance should be treated as an important practice dimension that can shape MDM outcomes rather than being reduced to death-focused content alone. As discussed below, this dimension also provides an important basis for understanding MDM's distinctive contribution to death research.

How MDM Extends Death Research

A New Form of Death Awareness: Death Reconstrual

Beyond functioning as a meditation technique, MDM also offers death research a distinctive form of death awareness. Previous research on death awareness has focused primarily on death anxiety and death reflection. Research on death anxiety examines responses such as anxiety, avoidance, and defensiveness when individuals confront death, whereas death reflection uses specific death scenarios, life review, and questions about relationships and values to prompt consideration of what death means for one's own life (Greenberg et al., 1990; Pyszczynski et al., 1999; Blackie et al., 2016; Cozzolino et al., 2004). As discussed above, MDM can likewise be understood as a form of death awareness (Moon, 2019), but the form it produces differs from these two. We refer to this form as death reconstrual: death awareness that emerges as individuals sustain attention to death and their related psychological responses while reprocessing and reorganizing their understanding of death and its relationship to life. Such death reconstrual differs from existing forms of death awareness in several ways (Table 1).

First, death reconstrual differs from existing forms of death awareness in how deathrelated content is processed, a difference that arises from MDM as a practice. Prior research has characterized death anxiety as an affective "hot" response in which confrontation with mortality elicits relatively direct and intense emotional reactions, whereas death reflection is more cognitive and "cool," involving deliberate consideration of death and its meaning for life (Grant & Wade-Benzoni, 2009; Yuan et al., 2019). Death reconstrual, by contrast, involves a more integrative mode of processing that includes both emotional experience and cognitive reflection.

It also incorporates decentering: individuals not only experience death-related thoughts and emotions but also notice their current psychological states and changes in those states. Death anxiety and death reflection primarily concern the emotional or cognitive responses elicited by death-related situations and do not, in themselves, emphasize further monitoring of those responses.

Second, death reconstrual differs from existing forms of death awareness in whether death is continuously maintained as the object of attention. Death anxiety and death reflection are typically elicited by death-related stimuli or scenarios, in which death-related content primarily serves as a cue for subsequent emotional responses, cognitive processing, or reflection on meaning; these downstream processes may persist even when death itself is no longer in focal attention (Pyszczynski et al., 1999; Grant & Wade-Benzoni, 2009; Burke et al., 2010). By contrast, in death reconstrual, death is not merely the starting point for subsequent responses but remains an object to which attention is repeatedly maintained and returned throughout the practice. When attention drifts, practitioners redirect it to death, making death awareness itself a process sustained through continued practice.

Third, death reconstrual differs from existing forms of death awareness in the source and controllability of its content. In death anxiety, specific associations, emotions, and defensive responses arise largely spontaneously. Death reflection constrains the scope of reflection through particular scenarios and questions, but the specific content and meaning ultimately generated still arise primarily from the individual (Blackie et al., 2016; Cozzolino et al., 2004). MDM, by contrast, organizes death-related processing through predetermined contemplative themes and guidance, enabling practitioners to engage with death in relatively specified ways. Intervention developers can therefore design the presentation and sequence of death-related content more explicitly. They cannot determine the understanding practitioners ultimately develop, but the processing context is more amenable to intervention design. Fourth, death reconstrual also differs from existing forms of death awareness in terms of systematicity. Here, systematicity primarily refers to the extent to which the content of death awareness is organized according to a certain sequence and structure to form an integrated whole. Death anxiety mainly consists of emotional and defensive responses elicited by deathrelated stimuli. Its content is relatively fragmented and therefore exhibits a relatively low degree of systematicity (Greenberg et al., 1990; Pyszczynski et al., 1999). Death reflection, by contrast, exhibits a certain degree of systematicity. After presenting a death-related scenario, researchers typically use a series of interrelated questions to guide individuals' reflection, thereby giving their thinking a certain organizational structure (Cozzolino et al., 2004; Blackie et al., 2016; Yuan et al., 2019). Death reconstrual is more systematic because the MDM on which it is based is itself organized in a relatively systematic manner. For example, the MDM developed by Tang et al. (2026) presents its practice procedures, core content, and value-oriented guidance to practitioners in a particular sequence, with these components being interconnected and building upon one another to form an organized whole. Rather than functioning as isolated exercises, the different components are arranged so that earlier practices provide a basis for subsequent reflection, reinterpretation, and value-oriented consideration. Thus, compared with death anxiety and death reflection, death reconstrual exhibits a higher degree of systematicity

A New Practice Paradigm

MDM also provides a new practice paradigm for studying death awareness. Research on death anxiety and death reflection has primarily used one-time death stimuli, scenarios, or reflective tasks and then examined psychological and behavioral outcomes after death awareness was induced (Greenberg et al., 1990; Cozzolino et al., 2004). MDM can likewise be used in single-session experiments, but a more important contribution is that it can be developed into repeated practice lasting days or weeks (Park et al., 2016; Tang et al., 2026). Death awareness thus need not remain a state briefly induced in a single experiment; it can become a process that recurs and changes over a longer period.

Such prolonged and repeated practice allows researchers to observe the unfolding process of death awareness rather than merely measuring outcomes after a single induction. Researchers can track how death-related cognitions, emotions, and attitudes change as individuals repeatedly confront death. Death research can therefore expand from asking primarily "what outcomes does death awareness produce?" to examining "how does death awareness unfold and change?" Shortterm states of death awareness and longer-term changes in attitudes toward death can be studied within the same practice process. Compared with one-time experiments, this approach creates new conditions for understanding the dynamic process and longer-term development of death awareness.

It is worth noting that death-education programs lasting several weeks already exist, but these studies generally organize death-related content through courses and didactic activities (Testoni et al., 2021). MDM differs in that its core is the repeated performance of death-related psychological exercises, through which individuals repeatedly enter and process death awareness. MDM therefore extends death research not simply by lengthening the duration of an intervention but by transforming death awareness from a one-time experimental state into an object of inquiry that can be repeatedly practiced, tracked over time, and examined as it changes.

It thus offers a research paradigm centered on sustained practice and repeated training.

Epistemological and Ideological Perspectives in Death Research

Finally, the nature of MDM practice may also shape the epistemological and ideological stance of the research in which it is used. Tang et al. (2026) emphasized that their MDM practice drew on Buddhist traditions while also being inspired by ideal psychology. Ideal psychology is a psychological approach that emphasizes future-oriented perspectives. Its ideological orientation includes exploring new possibilities, critically examining the present and the everyday, and using professional methods to move beyond ordinary psychological functioning; epistemologically, it is closer to critical theory than to logical positivism (Zeng, 2025).

Through intensive and repeated practice, MDM cultivates deliberately designed responses to death, reflecting ideal psychology's emphasis on using professional training to move beyond everyday psychological reactions. In particular, MDM entails redesigning responses to death that are often treated as instinctive: the practice does not merely observe fear, rejection, and avoidance as they arise naturally but attempts to alter these responses through training. This feature reflects ideal psychology's orientation toward critically examining and moving beyond existing reality and its assumption that psychological responses remain open to further change (Tang et al., 2026; Zeng, 2025). By contrast, previous death research has more often adopted a logical-positivist approach aimed at identifying psychological regularities that already exist when people confront death. Terror management theory, for example, proposes that death awareness elicits proximal and distal defenses and treats these responses as relatively entrenched psychological mechanisms in the face of mortality threat (Pyszczynski et al., 1999). This research tradition primarily asks, "How do people respond when they confront death?" MDM shifts the question toward, "Can these responses be changed through training?" The two approaches therefore differ not only in the death-related responses they examine but also in their research paradigms. This does not mean that all research on MDM must adopt a critical ideological or epistemological stance. Researchers can treat MDM as an established traditional practice and study its effects and mechanisms within a logical-positivist framework.

Nevertheless, MDM as a practice inherently contains an intention to modify responses to death through training. In this respect, it differs from research that primarily describes death anxiety or guides death reflection.

Directions for Future Research

Existing research provides preliminary evidence of potentially beneficial effects of MDM, and several directions warrant future study. First, future research should further test MDM's effects and broaden the range of outcome variables. The empirical literature remains limited, and existing findings require replication across samples and contexts. The present analysis also suggests that some outcomes potentially associated with the three dimensions of MDM remain largely unexamined, particularly attention-related outcomes. Future studies can use the present framework to examine a broader range of psychological and behavioral variables and, where appropriate, integrate relevant perspectives from Buddhist theory. For example, Buddhist traditions regard mindfulness of death (maraṇasati) as a means of cultivating diligence and a sense of urgency in practice (Bodhi, 2012), a function that has received little direct Given the sensitivity of death-related material and the potential risks indicated by existing research, future work should clarify appropriate populations, contexts of use, and possible contraindications. At the same time, value guidance is an especially flexible dimension of MDM because it can directly shape how practitioners interpret death and life. More or stronger guidance is not necessarily better; its design should take account of individual needs, acceptability, cultural context, and value congruence (e.g., Zhou et al., 2024). Determining how to individualize MDM and ensure safety while preserving its core features will therefore be an important direction for optimization.

Conclusion

This article reviews the concept, practice forms, and existing research on mindfulness of death (MDM) and proposes a three-dimensional framework of death-focused content, attentional regulation, and value guidance for understanding its structure and potential mechanisms. It further situates MDM within death research by introducing death reconstrual as a distinctive form of death awareness, highlighting a practice paradigm in which death awareness can be repeatedly trained and observed over time, and considering its broader epistemological and ideological implications. Together, these contributions provide a theoretical basis for the empirical study, refinement, and application of MDM.
