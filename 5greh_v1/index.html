Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Marginal Significance works

List all sentences that describe an effect as ‘marginally significant’.

The marginal module searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The list of terms is a subset of those listed in a blog post by Matthew Hankins. The module returns all sentences that match terms describing ‘marginally significant’ results.

Some of the terms identified might not be problematic in some contexts, and there are ways to describe ‘marginal significance’ that are not detected by the module.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. Some differences come from the ground truth: it lacks p-values with a correction name (“p Bonf = .061”), and counts partial eta squared written with its subscript after it (“η 2 p = .05”), “p = .05”, and some table notes and figure legends (“† p < .10, *p < .05”), which this module leaves out, as non-significant p-values. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. Abstract

The electronic Human Relations Area Files (eHRAF) World Cultures database indexes primary ethnographic documents at the paragraph level, allowing researchers to assemble systematic samples of text on a wide range of topics and across diverse societies. Unlike some other cross-cultural databases, eHRAF does not provide precoded society-level variables. It provides source text from which researchers can construct variables, and as such offers researchers greater flexibility in their ability to operationalize variables. We present an eight-step workflow for building a coded cross-cultural dataset from eHRAF: 1) formulating a question suited to the ethnographic record; 2) selecting Outline of Cultural Materials codes and constructing a search; 3) defining the unit of analysis and data structure; 4) addressing research ethics and working within the terms of service; 5) assembling a database; 6) developing and piloting a coding scheme; 7) training coders and assessing reliability; and 8) analyzing nested data. Drawing on our published research and experience supervising student projects, we identify common challenges, offer practical guidelines, and provide a reporting checklist. The workflow is intended to make eHRAF-based research more accessible and prevalent within the fields of behavioral and cultural research, while facilitating a more systematic, transparent, and reproducible approach.

Introduction

Cross-cultural analysis of the ethnographic record is one of the few empirical strategies available for identifying what elements of cultural expression vary across human societies and what may be considered widespread or universal. The ethnographic record contains globally distributed scholarly accounts on aspects of human social life and organization; the methodological challenge in such a monumental corpus is to retrieve and analyze that evidence systematically. This article presents a practical workflow for using the https://ehrafworldcultures.yale.edu/ or eHRAF. It draws on our experience conducting eHRAF-based research as scholars of cultural diversity, anthropology, and psychology, as well as on our experience supervising student projects at both the undergraduate and postgraduate level. Although no single procedure is appropriate for every cross-cultural study, a series of recurring decisions determines whether an eHRAF project produces a defensible dataset.

Our intention here is to facilitate increased awareness and use of the eHRAF database, and speak to multiple, non-exclusive audiences across the full spectrum of seniority and experience. This includes more senior scholars undertaking comprehensive programs of research; graduate students who may be surveying a diverse literature in the service of a review or grant application; and undergraduate students undertaking their first crosscultural project, often for a thesis or dissertation. Our workflow is agnostic to purpose, and applies equally to all. More advanced extensions will be specifically discussed in the sections: Going further.

The article does not provide technical instruction for analysis in R (or any other analytic software), natural language processing, relational database design, programming, or mixedeffects models, although such knowledge will likely be necessary for work with eHRAF. Instead, it takes the reader from a research question to a well-documented coded dataset and identifies appropriate analytic approaches.

Existing documentation describes the contents of eHRAF and the operation of its interface (Human Relations Area Files, n.d.-a). Our contribution is to help shape the decisions that connect a research question to an analyzable dataset: how to define the unit of analysis, how to develop and evaluate a coding scheme, which reliability statistics are appropriate for the prevalence imbalances common in ethnographic coding, and what must be reported for the study to be reproducible.

What eHRAF is, and what it is not

eHRAF is a collection of primary ethnographic documents indexed at the paragraph level. This paragraph-level indexing is the feature that makes systematic sampling of ethnographic text possible.

Each paragraph carries one or more subject codes from the Outline of Cultural Materials (OCM, Murdock et al., 2008), a classification system covering most domains of human social life. The OCM contains approximately 90 major categories (for example, 740 Health and Welfare) and more than 700 subcategories (for example, 743 Hospitals and Clinics and 744 Public Health and Sanitation). Researchers can search the corpus by subject code, keyword, or Boolean combinations of the two and retrieve the paragraphs that match the specified search.

As of October 2026, eHRAF covers more than 300 societies across over 6,300 documents, and material is continually being added.

The Human Relations Area Files project began in 1949. In its early decades, analysts reproduced ethnographic passages using typewriters and Photostat machines, indexed them by OCM category, and distributed 5-by-8-inch paper files to member institutions. The collection moved to CD-ROM in 1993 and to the web in 1997. The project's history also includes military sponsorship. During World War II, George Murdock expanded the files for the Office of Naval Intelligence (Price, 2008). Over a four-year period, HRAF received approximately $4 million in U.S. Army funding and produced 63 unclassified country monographs and 34 classified handbooks (Price, 2016).

Unlike other well-known cross-cultural databases, eHRAF provides ethnographic texts from which researchers can code measures suited to their research questions. The Ethnographic Atlas (EA) contains precoded variables for more than 1,200 societies (Murdock, 1967). The Standard Cross-Cultural Sample (SCCS) provides a common sample of 186 societies for which researchers have coded a wide range of variables (Murdock & White, 1969). Binford's hunter-gatherer dataset covers 339 forager groups and includes numerous socioecological measures (Binford, 2001). D-PLACE brings several of these datasets together with linguistic and environmental information, facilitating comparisons across datasets (Kirby et al., 2016).

Precoded approaches facilitate comparative analysis and allow researchers to build on previous coding efforts (cf. Ember, 2007; Gelfand et al., 2006; Watts et al., 2022). Attempts to validate ethnography-based, quantitative comparative research have provided evidence supporting the use of ethnographic codes (Bahrami-Rad et al., 2021). However, the operationalization of particular society-level measures remains open to debate. For example, the Kinship Intensity Index (KII) combines measures of cousin marriage, polygyny, extended-family coresidence, descent, and community organization (Schulz et al., 2019). Campbell et al. (2024) question the assumption that these components contribute equally to kinship intensity and identify a problem with the cousin-marriage component: societies that practice cousin marriage without preferring a particular type can receive the same score as societies that do not practice it. These concerns illustrate why researchers should examine coding definitions and assess whether available measures capture the variation relevant to their research question.

These databases provide quantitative or qualitative variables for a set of societies on defined variables, which are typically documented by the database itself. eHRAF instead provides a corpus of primary ethnographic text that the researcher must retrieve, evaluate, and code. Compared to precoded databases, this approach requires more labor but gives researchers greater control over what they measure and how they operationalize it. New measures derived from eHRAF can also be combined with variables from precoded databases.

Why eHRAF is a good place to start, especially with students For supervised student research, eHRAF offers several advantages. Students can work directly with ethnographic sources and assemble samples providing evidence of human cultural diversity that would be impossible to collect through primary fieldwork. The work requires only access to eHRAF and can be conducted remotely and on a flexible schedule. Because the database consists of published texts and analysis typically occurs at the level of paragraphs or societies, projects present substantially fewer ethical concerns than primary research with human participants (

see Step 4).

There is also a pedagogical benefit. Many students, especially in psychology, have limited experience examining cultural variation. A cross-cultural project requires sustained engagement with evidence from diverse societies and moves beyond broad contrasts such as East versus West, Global North versus Global South, or collectivist versus individualist.

For more experienced scholars and more senior graduate students, eHRAF has many unique affordances. Most notably, research using eHRAF can constitute a singular, standalone scholarly contribution. Among graduate students, eHRAF can serve as a useful foundation for an analytical overview of a given phenomenon, which can itself (or in concert with other work) constitute an empirical chapter of a dissertation. Meanwhile, a less thorough preliminary review can help determine the utility of a research question for fellowship applications or similar purposes. More senior scholars can use eHRAF to quickly and thoroughly evaluate the scope of a new research question or topic, as a source of pilot data for grant applications, or as the basis for scholarly publications.

Access to eHRAF is generally provided through institutional library subscriptions. Researchers at institutions without access can request a trial directly from https://ehrafworldcultures.yale.edu/ and encourage their libraries to subscribe.

# Step 1: Formulating a question suited to the ethnographic record

Before searching eHRAF, one must formulate a question that the ethnographic record can address. eHRAF is particularly well suited to questions about behavioral or cultural variation: patterns of commonality and diversity across societies, relationships between socioecological conditions and cultural practices, or the distribution of a phenomenon across the ethnographic record. The question should guide the search strategy, society sample, and coding scheme. It is easy, particularly for those inexperienced with the database, to formulate a question which -while sensible in the abstract -is not amenable to the affordances of eHRAF.

Consider carefully what the record can and cannot show. Most eHRAF texts were written by anthropologists, and some date back prior to 1900. The corpus also includes accounts by explorers, government officials, missionaries, and other observers. The documents span many regions, disciplines, and more than a century of scholarship. Questions about readily observed behavior, such as gender differences in a particular activity, may be feasible. Questions requiring consistent accounts of abstract constructs, such as the cognitive consequences of visual aesthetic preferences, may not be. Before committing to a research question, a scholar (and/or their team) must read a substantial sample of search results from several societies and, where possible, several complete ethnographies. This preliminary reading clarifies both the possibilities and the limits of the source material, and should be done without pre-commitment to specific subject codes.

Scale of analysis

The scale of analysis should be explicit from the outset. eHRAF data are commonly analyzed at one of two levels. At the paragraph level, the question concerns what occurs or co-occurs within a paragraph. To examine whether children segregate by gender more in play than in work, for example, researchers might search the relevant OCM codes and code each paragraph for activity and gender composition. At the society level, paragraph-level observations are aggregated into a summary measure and related to society-level predictors. To examine whether community size predicts gendered divisions of labor, researchers might code paragraphs for the participation of women and men in different forms of work, construct society-level measures, and relate those measures to community size drawn from eHRAF culture summaries or another database.

Analyses of individual variation in behavior are challenging. Descriptions of individual behavior appear in ethnographic documents, but are inconsistent and not systematically recorded. Discrete, publicly observable behaviors are likely easier to identify. The availability of individual accounts also varies across topics. For example, Syme et al. (2016) identified 224 individual cases of suicidal behavior among 474 records (47.3%) with the remaining 250 records describing cultural models. In a dataset on leadership (Garfield et al., 2019), 163 of 1,212 paragraphs (13.4%) included descriptions of individual leaders or followers, while the remaining 1,049 described only cultural models. A sufficiently large corpus of ethnographic paragraphs may nevertheless provide enough cases to investigate individual variation. Where cases are too few or unevenly documented for quantitative analysis, qualitative analysis can still provide insights into how the target behavior is expressed.

Going further: when the paragraph is too coarse

The paragraph is the most readily available unit in eHRAF, and most studies, including most of ours, use it. Paragraphs nevertheless vary substantially in length and density. One may contain a single brief observation, whereas another may describe several distinct events (and some may be a single sentence!). If the question requires within-paragraph distinctions among actors, outcomes, causal sequences, or conflicting accounts, the paragraph may be too coarse. In such cases we then code the instance: a discrete occurrence of the focal phenomenon within a paragraph. Instance-level coding preserves additional information but requires a more complex data structure, with careful curation of contiguous paragraphs and additional within-text keyword search (this is described in more detail in Step 3).

Qualitative or quantitative

Both quantitative and qualitative approaches require substantial effort, but at different stages. Quantitative coding concentrates effort in the development and validation of the coding scheme (and requires considerable reading of an anticipated dataset). Once the scheme is stable, coding is more standardized and the resulting data structure is specified in advance. Qualitative and thematic analyses require iterative reading as themes are developed and refined. As a general distinction, quantitative coding is well suited to testing specified hypotheses, whereas qualitative coding is well suited to exploring less-developed questions.

Step 2: Selecting Outline of Cultural Materials codes and constructing a search

Once the question is defined, identify the OCM codes that correspond to it. Code selection largely determines which material is retrieved, so the OCM should be reviewed carefully. This matters not simply because one hopes that one's review is comprehensive, but for the sake of reproducibility, as well as the efficiency of workflow.

The eHRAF interface allows users to search subject categories before selecting them. Enter keywords in the subject search and review the numbered OCM codes returned. Because the codes are nested and cross-referenced, one code often leads to related categories. Read each definition and inspect a sample of indexed paragraphs before constructing the full search to confirm that the codes retrieve the intended material.

Next, construct the search by combining codes with AND and OR, adding keyword strings where appropriate, and nesting the Boolean logic. The distinction between AND and OR substantially affects the retrieved sample. For example, flint AND knapping will produce results that relate to both the substance type (flint) and the practice (knapping) in cooccurrence, while flint OR knapping will produce information on either of these, including when they do not appear together in the same paragraph. As a logical rule, an and search will produce fewer paragraphs than an or search. Keyword searches are also possible, but are more likely to produce false positives: a search for knap*, for example, might retrieve knapsack.

A subsequent inclusion-and-exclusion stage is therefore necessary (Step 6).

Breadth of the first search

The initial search should be deliberately broad and retrieve more text than the final analysis requires. It can then be refined as the research team becomes familiar with the subject codes and terminology. Ethnographers working in different periods and regions may use the same term for different phenomena or different terms for the same phenomenon, and thus, a broad review is necessary to identify relevant terms for Boolean search. A broad initial search reduces the risk of overlooking relevant material.

Volume

There is no universal target; the appropriate number depends on team size and coding capacity. In our projects and those of colleagues who supervise students, an initial search often returns 2,000 to 10,000 paragraphs. More than approximately 5,000 paragraphs is generally too many for a small team to analyze, although this may be a reasonable starting point for curation. A final analytic sample of 2,500 or fewer paragraphs is often manageable, and many eHRAF studies include 700 to 2,000. The sample should be calibrated to the amount of text the team can read and code reliably. It is advisable that the leader of the project reads a sizeable representative sample of the selected paragraphs (even if they do not code all the paragraphs) to ensure that they understand the context and limits of the paragraphs' content. This familiarity is helpful in meaningfully communicating the coding, and in resolving any disagreements in coding.

Sample

eHRAF includes more than 300 societies. Most projects require a defined subset rather than the full society sample. Commonly used sub-samples include: • The Standard Cross-Cultural Sample (SCCS, Murdock & White, 1969): 186 societies selected for global representation, diversity, and relative historical independence, each with a designated time-and-place focus.

We increasingly use the SCCS because its size offers greater statistical power, it covers a diverse range of societies, a cultural phylogeny is available (Minocher et al., 2018), and the many existing society-level variables from the SCCS database can be incorporated. • The Probability Sample Files (PSF, Naroll, 1967) The sample should be selected in advance and justified by the theoretical or methodological goals. For example, Thomson et al. (2025) examined the maintenance of egalitarianism in four African hunter-gatherer societies because the available literature provided sufficient depth for comparative analysis.

Researcher degrees of freedom

Choices about codes, societies, and time periods shape the results and constitute researcher degrees of freedom. Document and justify these choices in the methods and, where possible, preregister them before coding or analysis. Base them on the research question and the team's capacity. Larger samples can increase statistical power and coverage, but only if the material can be coded reliably.

Related databases

For questions with a society-level component, D-PLACE (Kirby et al., 2016) provides access to a wide variety of precoded variables by integrating many of the aforementioned crosscultural databases (e.g., SCCS, EA) alongside linguistic, environmental, and phylogenetic data. Researchers can combine these variables with measures coded from eHRAF. For example, a study could code descriptions of children's learning from eHRAF and test whether the prevalence of teaching varies with dependence on animal husbandry, using subsistence measures available through databases hosted by D-PLACE. Such comparisons require matching the societies, time periods, and locations represented in both sources.

Step 3: Defining the unit of analysis and data structure

Define the unit of analysis before constructing the database. eHRAF data are relational, and the following hierarchy can guide the organization of tables: • Society, with metadata such as subsistence type and region, and links to external databases such as the SCCS.

• Document, with metadata including publication year, ethnographer, document type, and eHRAF quality rating.

• Paragraph, linked to its document, carrying a UUID and OCM codes.

• Instance (optional), linked to a paragraph, with its own unique ID.

For instance-level coding, create a separate table in which each row represents an instance and includes an identifier linking it to the parent paragraph. These identifiers preserve the nesting of instances within paragraphs, documents, and societies for analysis.

Going further: instance-level coding

A paragraph-level table records whether each paragraph is usable, the type of text it contains, and the number of emotions described. A second table contains one row per emotion. If a paragraph describes two emotions, it contributes two rows to the emotionlevel table, each linked to the same paragraph identifier. In another project, we coded instances of social learning because a single paragraph could describe several learning events involving different actors, content, settings, and rationales (for example, fathers teaching boys to climb trees and grandmothers teaching granddaughters through storytelling). This structure requires additional data management but preserves variation that a single paragraph-level code would obscure.

Two distinctions worth coding

Ethnographic paragraphs often combine two kinds of content, and distinguishing them can affect interpretation. Cases describe particular events involving identified or observed people at a specific time: "A man became angry with his neighbor, and they fought." Cultural models describe normative expectations or beliefs about how people generally act: "Men are expected to show restraint in disputes."

Cultural models can be further classified as emic or etic. An emic model is expressed by a member of the community, whereas an etic model is the outside observer's characterization based on fieldwork. When a paragraph contains both, researchers can code both accounts separately or prioritize the account most relevant to the research question. Define this rule in advance and apply it consistently. A paragraph can also contain both a case and a cultural model and may be coded accordingly.

Step 4: Addressing research ethics and terms of service

The review requirements for eHRAF-based studies vary by institution. Such projects commonly involve no interaction with living participants and no collection of identifiable private information because they analyze published texts at the paragraph or society level. These features may qualify a project for exemption or mean that it does not constitute human-participant research under local rules.

Researchers should nevertheless consult their institutional review board or equivalent authority rather than make this determination themselves. Any application or determination request should state that the data consist of published ethnographic texts held in a licensed institutional database, that the unit of analysis is the paragraph or society rather than the individual, and that no new contact with human participants will occur.

The British Psychological Society's Ethics Guidelines for Internet-mediated Research (British Psychological Society, 2021) offer useful guidance by analogy for the secondary analysis of ethnographic material. The guidelines identify four principles: respect for the autonomy, privacy, and dignity of individuals and communities; scientific integrity; social responsibility; and maximizing benefits and minimizing harm. Meanwhile, the American Anthropological Association's Code of Ethics states: "Anthropological researchers must do everything in their power to ensure that their research does not harm the safety, dignity, or privacy of the people with whom they work…" (American Anthropological Association, 1998, p. 2).

It is relatively easy to imagine circumstances in which eHRAF could be used, willfully or otherwise, to produce research that compromises these principles. The principle "Nothing about us without us" deserves careful consideration here. Secondary analysis risks removing accounts from their original contexts and objectifying the people described, even when the authors and the people they wrote about have since died. Thus, even where formal IRB approval is not required, researchers should carefully consider their ethical obligations to the communities included in their analyses and guard against unintentional harm.

Finally, eHRAF has strict terms of service. Researchers should not scrape eHRAF. Automated extraction may violate the database's terms of use, and access typically requires an institutional login. Copyright restrictions also constrain what can be archived and shared. Researchers can generally share coded variables and the paragraph UUIDs that identify the source material, but not the copyrighted paragraph text. The data-management and sharing plan should account for this constraint before coding begins.

Step 5: Assembling the database

Once the search and unit of analysis are fixed, retrieve the records and construct the working database. This stage is time-consuming and often underestimated.

eHRAF does not permit bulk download of paragraph text, but it does allow researchers to download a metadata file for each society. A basic workflow is: 1.

In the eHRAF settings, set the maximum number of paragraphs per page to 150, the current limit.

2. For each society in the sample, open the culture record and export the metadata as a CSV file.

3. Add a column called text.

4.

Copy each paragraph from the eHRAF interface into the corresponding row.

5. Repeat for every society. A society returning more than 150 paragraphs requires more than one sheet.

6. Combine the society files by rows into a single database.

For each paragraph, the metadata includes a universally unique identifier (UUID; eHRAF's internal identifier), primary author, document title, publication year, page number, document type, culture, region, subregion, subsistence strategy, associated OCM codes, and a permalink. The permalink points to the paragraph but does not contain the paragraph text, which must therefore be copied separately.

Going further: semi-automating assembly

Database assembly can be partly automated after the search results have been curated. One of us (Rohan Kapitany) developed a Google Sheets tool that uses eHRAF's standardized page structure. The researcher opens all retrieved paragraphs for a culture and pastes the page as plain text into a culture-specific sheet; a custom script then arranges the source paragraphs in a coding-ready format. In our experience, this procedure can prepare several hundred paragraphs in less than two hours. The tool is available from the authors on request.

Step 6: Developing and piloting a coding scheme

A coding scheme translates theoretical constructs into decisions that can be applied consistently to ethnographic text. The following principles guide our scheme development.

Simplicity in the scheme

Ethnographic paragraphs are context-dependent and were written for purposes unrelated to the research question. Fine-grained ordinal scales are therefore difficult to apply reliably. More tractable schemes use a small set of categories, such as evidence for, no evidence, evidence against, and ambiguous. A confidence rating such as strong, moderate, or weak may be added. In most eHRAF studies, statistical power comes from coding many paragraphs across many societies rather than from applying highly granular codes to individual records.

Operationalization

For each variable, specify what constitutes evidence for, evidence against, no evidence, and ambiguity, as applicable. Provide a concrete example of each category. Examples allow team members to evaluate whether a proposed code matches the intended construct and reduce disagreements based on abstract definitions alone. Some variables require more detailed rules than others. It may also be worth coding a subset of paragraphs for the degree to which they 'fit' a certain category, which can be published alongside any analysis as supplementary material to give the readers a clear idea of the ease/complexity of the coding process.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Before substantive coding, assess every paragraph against explicit inclusion and exclusion criteria. Add an indicator coded 1 when the paragraph meets a criterion and 0 when it does not. A project may require several indicators (for example, inclusion_age and inclusion_play). This screening pass removes unusable records, such as incomplete sentences or duplicate reprints, and familiarizes coders with the range of material before substantive coding begins. Retain excluded records so that exclusion counts and reasons can be reported.

The courtroom test

We ask coders to apply a courtroom test: could they defend the code before an impartial judge by identifying the exact words in the paragraph that meet the written definition? If a coder cannot identify specific supporting text, the code should not be applied. This deliberately conservative rule gives the team a shared evidentiary standard. When a term is ambiguous, coders should consult a dictionary or other agreed reference and document the reasoning behind the decision.

The codebook

Compile definitions, codes, examples, decision rules, and exceptions in a single codebook and update it as the scheme develops. Once definitions stabilize, a concise printed or otherwise continuously visible reference sheet can reduce the need to switch between documents while coding.

Piloting with project leads

Before additional coders begin, project leads should independently code a random sample of approximately 100 to 200 paragraphs drawn from the full corpus. This sample becomes the training dataset. Compare the independent codes, identify and diagnose disagreements, revise the scheme, and repeat the process until the leads agree on both the rules and the benchmark codes. The finalized training set can then be used to evaluate subsequent coders. Errors or ambiguities left unresolved at this stage will propagate through the full corpus and become difficult to diagnose later. Allocate substantial time to this step.

Step 7: Training coders and assessing reliability

Training coders

Once the scheme and benchmark training set are finalized, train coders using the following procedure: 1.

Assign background reading relevant to the research question.

2. Take coders through the scheme and every operationalization.

3. Emphasize conservative, evidence-based coding and require coders to support each decision with specific text.

4. Code the first few training paragraphs together (we recommend doing so until the leader/researcher finds themselves at, or approaching, consensus. Our experience is that this usually requires around 10% of the paragraphs).

5. Assign each coder the same training set, comprising a further 10% of the paragraphs and excluding those reviewed together in step 4. Have each coder code these paragraphs independently.

6. Compare each coder's results with the benchmark set, calculate reliability, and review each discrepancy individually.

7. Finally, to evaluate intercoder reliability, coders are assigned 20% of the sample to be coded independently.

We conduct training in waves. In an initial test wave, all coders evaluate approximately 10 paragraphs to identify problems with the scheme or data-entry system. In a larger training wave, all coders evaluate approximately 20% of paragraphs (10% together, 10% separately) to assess agreement and complete calibration. Problems should be resolved during these waves. Once substantive coding begins, the scheme should remain fixed unless a serious problem requires revision; any such change should be documented and applied consistently to previously coded material.

Two ways to deploy on the full corpus

The consensus matrix is the traditional approach. Two coders independently code every paragraph, reliability is calculated, and each disagreement is resolved through discussion or by a third adjudicator to produce one consensus code per unit. This approach produces a single adjudicated dataset but requires substantial labor.

For large projects, we increasingly use a multi-coder random-effects approach. Each coder receives the corpus in a different random order and codes as many paragraphs as capacity permits, with the aim of obtaining at least two independent codes per paragraph. Rather than adjudicating disagreements, the analysis retains all codes and includes coder identity as a random effect. This design accommodates unequal contributions as coders enter and leave the project, models coder-level variation directly, and fits naturally within a mixedeffects framework (see Garfield et al., 2023).

Rules that apply to both approaches

Randomize the order of paragraphs in each coding sheet and, when the research question permits, mask the society name. A coder who recognizes that a paragraph concerns the ! Kung may draw on prior expectations; masking and randomization reduce this source of bias. Use drop-down menus or constrained input fields to limit data-entry errors, together with an "other" category, a free-text field for cases not covered by the scheme, and a notes field for ambiguous decisions. Keep training records separate from the substantive coding corpus.

Coder workload requires careful calibration. As a rough guideline, we assign each coder three to five variables across approximately 2,000 paragraphs. In one project, asking student coders to evaluate more than three variables across 1,700 paragraphs increased coding variance and reduced reliability. Capacity also varies across coders, and some will complete substantially more material than others. Coding assignments should accommodate this variation while prioritizing reliability over speed.

Project leads should participate in reliability coding so that they remain familiar with the source material and coding decisions. The completed dataset should also be checked before analysis. Manual copy-and-paste procedures can introduce transposition and alignment errors that may otherwise remain undetected.

Intercoder reliability

Following training, intercoder reliability should be reported for all quantitative coding schemes. As described above, coders can first evaluate a practice set totaling approximately 20% of the paragraphs. After comparing codes and revising the scheme, they should independently code a separate reliability sample of approximately 20% and calculate an appropriate agreement statistic.

Two statistics are commonly used. Cohen's kappa corrects observed agreement for chance and is widely recognized, but it can produce counterintuitively low values when category prevalence is highly imbalanced. Such imbalances are common in eHRAF coding because many traits are rare. Gwet's AC1 (Gwet, 2008) is less sensitive to prevalence imbalance because it estimates chance agreement differently. Comparative work on skewed data supports its use in this setting (Wongpakaran et al., 2013), and we recommend it for many eHRAF coding applications.

As a practical threshold, we aim for a kappa or AC1 of at least 0.70. If reliability falls below this level, clarify the scheme, review disagreements, and code a new reliability sample. Kappa can be calculated in JASP under Frequencies → Reliability. In R, the irr and irrCAC packages provide both statistics. With one column per coder and one row per paragraph, irr::kappa2() calculates Cohen's kappa for two coders and irrCAC::gwet.ac1.raw() calculates Gwet's AC1. Both functions take raw codes rather than a pretabulated agreement table.

For qualitative work, the corresponding goal is analytic credibility rather than identical labels. A similar two-stage process can be used: code a practice sample totaling 20% of the data, refine the thematic scheme, and then code a separate 20% validation sample. Report the percentage agreement, the number of paragraphs included, and the procedure used to resolve disagreements. We use 80% agreement as a practical target, while recognizing that percentage agreement does not adjust for chance and that thematic analysis is inherently interpretive.

Step 8: Analyzing nested data using multilevel and penalized models

Analyses can be broadly classified as confirmatory or exploratory, and the distinction should be stated explicitly. Confirmatory analyses test predictions specified in advance, ideally through preregistration. Exploratory analyses identify patterns in the coded records or OCM structure that were not anticipated. Both are valuable, but they should be reported separately so readers can distinguish planned tests from post hoc findings. Our eHRAF research includes both a priori tests of specified theories (Garfield et al., 2019; Syme et al., 2016) and deliberately exploratory analyses (Garfield et al., 2020; Garfield & Lew-Levy, 2025).

Grain of analysis

Even when the primary interest is in society-level patterns, retain paragraph-or instancelevel data whenever possible. This permits simultaneous estimation of within-and betweensociety variation, preserves the nesting of instances within paragraphs, documents, and societies, and allows paragraph-level features to enter the analysis as covariates.

Frequency estimates with uncertainty

An initial question is often how frequently a coded variable occurs. Raw percentages do not adequately represent the nested structure of the data: text records are nested within documents and societies, and observations may also be nested within coders when multiple people code each record. An intercept-only mixed-effects logistic model with random intercepts corresponding to the sampling and coding design can estimate prevalence and its 95% interval. In Garfield et al. (2020), we used this approach to estimate the prevalence of leadership dimensions, with random intercepts for authors nested within cultures in lme4 (Bates et al., 2015), and separately estimated the proportion of cultures exhibiting each dimension using a cluster bootstrap. In Garfield et al. (2023), we extended this approach by including varying intercepts for coder, paragraph, document, and society in Bayesian multilevel models. This design incorporates coder disagreement into parameter uncertainty rather than treating each code as independent.

Variation across societies

To assess whether an outcome varies by subsistence strategy, region, group context, gender, or another predictor, extend the same multilevel framework. Retain random intercepts for the relevant clustering units, add predictors as fixed effects, and compare models. We have used both frequentist logistic mixed models in lme4, compared with intercept-only models using Akaike's information criterion (Garfield et al., 2020), and Bayesian multilevel logistic models that report posterior medians, credible intervals, and the probability of direction (Garfield et al., 2023).

Phylogenetic Autocorrelation

Societies are not statistically independent because they share historical relationships and exchange traits through contact; statistically, this is 'phylogenetic autocorrelation' (but is often informally referred to as "Galton's problem"). Carefully constructed samples such as the SCCS reduce recent dependencies but do not eliminate them. Researchers can address this non-independence by linking hand-coded data to society-level information from the SCCS, the Ethnographic Atlas, or D-PLACE and modeling spatial or phylogenetic dependence. Common approaches include spatial autocorrelation models (Dobson & Gelade, 2012) and phylogenetic comparative methods (see Ringen et al., 2019).

Penalized regression on OCM codes

Each paragraph is tagged with OCM codes in addition to those used in the initial search, and these codes are included in the exported metadata. They therefore provide an established set of potential predictors. When the number of codes is large relative to the number of observations, ordinary regression is prone to overfitting. Penalized regression can reduce this problem by shrinking weak coefficients toward zero, with the penalty selected through cross-validation [for example, the glmnet package; Friedman et al. (2010)]. In Garfield & Lew-Levy (2025), we modeled counts of evidence for teaching using the full set of major OCM codes in an elastic-net Poisson regression. The retained predictors identified cultural domains associated with descriptions of teaching. The same approach can examine associations among researcher-coded variables.

Text analysis

The paragraph text can also be analyzed directly. One approach is to construct a documentterm matrix and fit a penalized model predicting a coded outcome from word frequencies. Retained terms can reveal the semantic content associated with a code and provide a partial check on whether the coding captures the intended construct. In Garfield et al. (2020), terms such as respect, prestige, and honor were strong predictors of high-status leaders. In Garfield & Lew-Levy ( 2025), text analysis distinguished vocabulary associated with skill transmission from vocabulary associated with norm transmission.

Heat maps and cluster analysis

When a dataset contains many coded variables, lower-dimensional structure can be examined before fitting a larger model. Hierarchical cluster analysis identifies variables that tend to co-occur, and a heat map with rows and columns ordered by the clustering makes these groupings visible. We have combined clustering with logistic principal components analysis and minimum-spanning-tree or k-nearest-neighbor graphs to derive features for subsequent analysis (Garfield et al., 2020). Mosaic plots provide a simpler way to display relationships among two or three categorical variables (Garfield & Lew-Levy, 2025).

Bias in the ethnographic record

Like historical and archaeological sources, the ethnographic record reflects systematic biases in observation, documentation, and preservation. eHRAF metadata allow researchers to measure some potential sources of bias, including ethnographer occupation, gender, and nationality; publication year, duration of fieldwork, and temporal span; and the number of ethnographers, documents, and pages available for each society. Sensitivity analyses incorporating these variables can indicate whether results are robust to observable features of the record, although they cannot remove all sources of bias. The corpus changes over time, so the same query may later return a different set of paragraphs.

A note on large language models 599 Large language models (LLMs) may change how researchers code ethnographic text. The 600 manual workflow described above remains the established approach, but recent studies 601 have begun to evaluate LLM-assisted coding. Their results are mixed. 602 Syme et al. (2026) present a transparent human-in-the-loop framework that uses GPT-4 to 603 convert ethnographic text into binary structured variables.

In an application to ritual fasting 604 across 56 HRAF societies, the model matched or exceeded human coders on clearly defined 605 variables and identified systematic omissions in human coding. Two broader guides describe iterative workflows in which researchers use LLMs to develop and apply coding taxonomies. Dubourg et al. (2024) provide a step-by-step procedure for cultural annotation, including descriptions of nonindustrial societies, and Carmona-Díaz et al. (2026) report high human-LLM agreement and an approximately 87% reduction in analysis time.

By contrast, Goodall et al. (2026) evaluated seven LLMs on 121 ritual features across 567 ethnographic excerpts and found performance below the level required for reliable automated annotation. Longer passages, ordinal distinctions, and ambiguous constructs were especially difficult. Features that produced greater human disagreement also tended to be difficult for the models, and model performance remained inadequate even on some features for which human agreement was high. The authors conclude that LLMs cannot yet replace human expertise in ethnographic annotation.

Together, these findings suggest that LLMs may be most useful as additional coders within a human-in-the-loop workflow. They appear more effective for clearly operationalized variables and for identifying inconsistent decisions than for ambiguous classifications that require substantial contextual judgment. The reliability procedures described in Step 7 can be extended to LLMs: model-generated codes should be evaluated against the same benchmark set used for human coders, and model identity can be represented as a coder effect in the analysis.

Any such use must comply with eHRAF's terms and conditions. These prohibit submitting HRAF materials to third-party LLM services "that may store or re-use user input." Subject to approval, the terms permit certain locally hosted or institutionally managed LLM services, provided that these do not retain data or use them for training or other purposes, that institutional services are approved for private and secure data, and that appropriate safeguards are documented (Human Relations Area Files, n.d.-b). Researchers should confirm that their proposed workflow meets these requirements before submitting eHRAF material to an LLM.

We have not used LLMs in our published eHRAF studies, although we are evaluating their use in ongoing work.

Scope and limitations

This article presents a workflow rather than a validation study. The paragraph-count targets in Step 2 and coder-workload estimates in Step 7 are based on our experience and that of colleagues, not on a systematic comparison of research-team designs. They should be treated as starting points for project planning rather than established parameters.

The article also gives more attention to quantitative coding than to qualitative and thematic analysis, reflecting our own research experience. Thomson et al. (2025) provides a more developed example of thematic cross-cultural analysis.

Finally, no workflow eliminates the limitations of the ethnographic record. The corpus reflects what ethnographers documented, the concepts they used, and the places and periods in which they worked. Step 8 describes ways to model several observable sources of bias, but modeling does not remove them. Some questions cannot be answered with the available record.
