Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Marginal Significance works

List all sentences that describe an effect as ‘marginally significant’.

The marginal module searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The list of terms is a subset of those listed in a blog post by Matthew Hankins. The module returns all sentences that match terms describing ‘marginally significant’ results.

Some of the terms identified might not be problematic in some contexts, and there are ways to describe ‘marginal significance’ that are not detected by the module.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. Some differences come from the ground truth: it lacks p-values with a correction name (“p Bonf = .061”), and counts partial eta squared written with its subscript after it (“η 2 p = .05”), “p = .05”, and some table notes and figure legends (“† p < .10, *p < .05”), which this module leaves out, as non-significant p-values. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

Full text

Abstract

Classic opinion dynamics models typically focus on a single opinion or treat them as independent dimensions. Several empirical studies indicate however that attitudes are interdependent and embedded within structured belief systems. These systems exhibit a mix of order and variability that standard opinion dynamics models cannot capture. In this work, we have developed an agent-based model that integrates cognitive consistency pressure with attitude structures to recreate these patterns. Specifically, we test two mechanisms through which agents perceive consistency among opinions: one grounded in issue-level associations, and another based on co-occurrence patterns at the position level. Our simulations show that neither mechanism alone is sufficient to reproduce the empirical complexity of empirical belief systems. However, a hybrid approach combining both mechanisms generates attitude structures that closely resemble empirical data. Our findings highlight the role of social perception in shaping belief systems and suggest a novel modelling pathway for studying ideological polarisation across multiple issue domains.

1 Introduction

Opinion dynamics models have become a central tool in studying how attitudes evolve through social interaction (Flache et al., 2017; Sîrbu et al., 2017; Peralta et al., 2022). These models typically focus on a single issue, with the aim of reproducing well-known macro-level patterns such as consensus, bifurcation, or the emergence of discrete opinion clusters (DiMaggio et al., 1996).

Despite the effort to generate these "high-level" patterns, much less attention is paid to the empirical foundation of how opinions are represented within opinion dynamics models (Flache et al., 2017). Consider, for example, that models usually represents opinions as a single numerical value in a given interval (e.g 0.4 on a scale from 0 to 1). By operationalising opinions in this way, modellers implicitly assume that (1) opinions can be analysed independently of each other (since no other opinion is added to the model) or, similarly, collapsed into a single scale. Moreover, they also implicitly imply that (2) opinions can be measured as a continuum through an interval scale.

However, empirical research has shown that opinions do not form independently, but are embedded within structured belief systems, where positions on different issues are systematically related to each other (Brandt & Sleegers, 2021; Kozlowski & Murphy, 2021). Similarly, research in psychometrics highlights how opinion data are actually "ordinal" (Luce et al., 2007) and converting them into an interval scale generates several theoretical and practical problems in agent-based modelling (ABM) (Carpentras & Quayle, 2023) 1.

In addition, survey data highlight two patterns that are relevant in the study of ideological polarisation. The first is issue alignment, where positions across different issues become increasingly more correlated (e.g. gun control and gay rights in the United States) (Kozlowski & Murphy, 2021). The second is issue asymmetry, where the two sides of an issue do not mirror each other, for example, being neutral on the topic of vaccination is often considered as "closer" to the anti-vaccine side than to the pro-vaccine side, thus breaking the symmetry (Carpentras et al., 2022). Both patterns can only be replicated by a model when opinions are analysed on their original ordinal scale.

In this paper, we explore which micro-behavioral rules are able to replicate patterns of issue alignment and issue asymmetry as observed in election survey data of the Dutch Parlimentary Election Studies (DPES). We specifically use Dutch election data because due to the relatively stable, multi-party system with long history of shifting electoral coalitions sorting alongside political parties is largely absent. We will first discuss how opinions are structured empirically and how they are represented in the current ABM literature. We then develop a discrete multi-dimensional opinion dynamics model that is centralised around the theory of associative diffusion and compare its output directly with four waves of the DPES. We show that the model replicates ordered issue alignment, but not structural issue asymmetry for consecutive time periods. We end by pointing out that adding heterogeneity among agents and calibration and validation across repeated surveys are the next steps to strengthen empirical similarity even more.

2 Theoretical background

2.1 Analysing opinions as a system

On the macro level, capturing multi-dimensional belief dynamics requires that the relevant output not simply restate the distribution of opinions on single issues but be able to map their relationships with 1 This refers to the fact that in Likert-type opinion data, participants endorse positions such as "Strongly agree", "Agree" etc. The "ordinal" nature of these data refers to the fact that we can say that "Strongly agree" is more positive than "Agree" but we cannot say how much more positive. This is different from "interval" and "ratio" measurements used, e.g., in physics, where we can not only claim that a 2-kilo object weighs more than a 1-kilo object, but we can also say that one weighs twice as much as the second each other. Ideological structures and ideological constraints have been quantitatively analysed as a network within a method called Belief Network Analysis (BNA) (Boutyline & Vaisey, 2017). In BNA, issues (i.e., the questions or items of a survey) are represented as nodes, and the Pearson correlations between issues as weights of the edges, allowing researchers to map the structure of co-occurring attitudes within a population. Some readers may find it easier to think of BNA as a (partial) correlation matrix between the survey items; the technique has been productively applied to study issue alignment across political domains (DellaPosta, 2020).

One key feature of "classic" BNA is that it implicitly assumes equal spacing, ordinality and symmetry; an approach that, as we will discuss later, has been somehow integrated also in many opinion dynamics models. Here, we refer to Equal spacing as the fact that responses are equally far apart within the latent variable. For example, the distance between the response "strongly agree" and "mildly agree" is supposed to be identical to the distance between "mildly agree" and "neutral" for a 5-point likert scale. Ordinality instead refers to the order-preserving quality of items. For example, a structure where the issue positions "Agree" and "Disagree" are closer to each other than to the "Neutral" position violates ordinality, as the expected order is Agree-Neutral-Disagree (or in reverse). Finally, symmetry refers to the idea that the two sides of a spectrum mirror each other. Taking the political left-right spectrum as the latent variable, this would means that the neutral response on an issue should be located exactly halfway between the political left and the political right. The empirical data from DPES 2023 (Jacobs et al., 2024) shows that this is not necessarily the case. Respondents who would admit more asylum seekers place themselves on average at 2.2 on the left-right scale (0 = left,10 = right), and respondents who would send them back at 7.0. If both sides would be symmetrical, respondents with a neutral position would on average place themselves halfway, at 4.6. Instead, they place themselves at 4.1 much closer to the left side of the political spectrum (de Bruin, 2026a).

The properties equal spacing, ordinality and symmetry are related to one another. An equally spaced scale is always ordinal and symmetrical, since both halves of the scale have the same spacing. On the contrary, a scale can be unequally spaced while remaining symmetrical, for example when the extreme positions of issues cluster together while the moderate positions are spread out (see for example network c in Figure 2). Both linearity and symmetry (or their lack thereof) can be better observed using Response Item Networks (ResIN); an extension to BNA in which nodes do not represent items but their individual response options (Carpentras et al., 2024). In ResIN, if two responses appear close to each other (i.e. they are strongly correlated), people who select one of the responses tend to also select the other.

ResIN thus enables analysis of the relationship between two response options (e.g. being supportive to both climate policies and European Unification) without assuming that the same relationship holds for the "opposite" responses (e.g. being negative to climate policies and being negative to European Unification).

For example, comparing political issues from the DPES of the years 2006, 2021, 2023 and 2025 between BNA (Figure 1) and ResIN (Figure 2), it becomes clear that BNA is able to visually support our previous argument about the systematic embeddedness of issue positions. Indeed, we can see how different topics reinforce each other within a detectable network structure. However, the ResIN method is able to provide much more additional information about the existing asymmetries, non-ordinal structures and linearities within the belief constraint, see Figure 2). Notice that the asymmetries here are both the displacement of neutral positions from the "center" towards one of the more extreme issue position groups, (e.g. trans_3 in both DPES 2021 (network a) and DPES 2023 (network c), and the asymmetrical distribution of position nodes across the network (such as DPES 2021 (network a) and DPES 2025 (network b). Non-ordinal structures are visible in DPES 2006 (network d), where all the extreme responses are clustered on the left while the more moderate responses are clustered on the right. The empirical data tell us that there is some level of complexity associated within opinion spaces in which people are able to generate relatively ordered structures while retaining significant deviations from it. Since we are exploring this problem from an ABM angle, we will explore which kind of rules can generate similar patterns throughout the remainder of this manuscript.

2.2 Towards new micro-behavioral rules for multi-dimensional opinion dynamics

As stated above, the most common approach within the dynamics of opinion is to treat opinions as isolated variables, studying one issue at a time (Flache et al., 2017). This means that most models study the distribution of opinions based on isolated issues.

Interestingly, the micro-behavioural rules that successfully generate polarisation and clustering in one-dimensional opinion dynamics models do not transfer straightforwardly to multi-dimensional contexts. Schweighofer & Garcia (2024) note that bounded confidence and repulsion mechanisms, when extended to multi-dimensional opinion spaces, fail to produce issue alignment. Bounded confidence increasingly isolates agents from one another as dimensionality grows, while repulsion drives opinions toward extreme responses along arbitrary rather than shared ideological dimensions (Flache et al., 2017; Schweighofer & Garcia, 2024). This suggests that capturing multi-dimensional belief dynamics requires alternative conceptual mechanisms at the micro-behavioural level. Agents may not update their issue position in isolation, but rather consider its coherence with their existing positions on other issues. This opens up the question of how to model such a type of interaction. Goldberg & Stein (2018) propose the concept of associative diffusion as an alternative micro-behavioural mechanism that has the potential to replicate the patterns within issue constraint. While classic opinion models assume that issue responses can be diffused during social interactions, the theory of associative diffusion suggests that what is diffused is the associations between issue positions. Through repeated exposure to these co-occurrence patterns, via social media, public discourse, or direct social interaction (Lorenz-Spreen et al., 2023), individuals gradually internalise a decision-making map of which attitude combinations are coherent and which are not. To provide an example, by observing people in favour of gay rights also expressing concerns for climate change, we learn that the two are associated, even if no direct logical connection is present between the two topics. Experimental evidence from Lüders et al. (2023) supports this view. Their research show that when a person expresses an attitude on one issue, observers systematically predict that person also holds specific positions on other issues, and that these predictions closely mirror the empirically observed co-occurrence structure of issue positions within the larger population. This suggests that the structure of issue constraint is actively perceived and internalised through observation, rather than transmitted through direct encounter. The literature on cognitive dissonance (Festinger, 1957; Matz & Wood, 2005; Izuma, 2013; Heider, 1946; Thagard, 2000) further suggests that once people have internalised this associative structure, they experience pressure to align their own belief system accordingly, providing the motivational force behind attitude updating in our model.

Apart from formally introducing the theory of associative diffusion, Goldberg & Stein (2018) also use a multi-dimensional opinion dynamics model to demonstrate how associative diffusion can generate cultural differentiation without structural segregation. A similar approach has also been implemented by Schweighofer & Garcia (2024), relying on Weighted Balance Theory. In their model, an agent first determines how much it likes or dislikes another agent, based on how much the two agree across all issues. It then adjusts its own issue positions to restore cognitive balance with this interpersonal attitude. It moves towards the positions of the other agent when it likes the other agent, and away from them when it dislikes the other agent. Repeated over many interactions, this generates issue alignment, as all issues collapse onto a single ideological spectrum. Similarly, Edmonds et al. (2026) took a related approach in which agents update their beliefs based on coherence with group-specific correlation structures derived from survey data. All three contributions (Schweighofer & Garcia, 2024; Edmonds et al., 2026; Goldberg & Stein, 2018) see issue constraint as a fundamental building block of their model and operationalise it as the main mechanism driving the opinion change. Unlike Goldberg & Stein (2018), both Schweighofer & Garcia (2024) and Edmonds et al. (2026) also validate their models empirically. Schweighofer & Garcia (2024) validate against aggregated polarisation trends, Edmonds et al. (2026) validate against qualitative patterns of within-and between-group diversity. However, neither of these two contributions tests whether the structure of belief interdependencies that emerges in the simulated population actually resembles the structure observed in empirical survey data. Furthermore, while both models produce some form of issue alignment, it remains an open question whether the generated structural properties within the issue constraint match what we observe empirically.

These models do not account for a key form of issue constraint: the displacement of neutral issue positions. As Figure 2 highlights neutral displacement is a clear case of issue asymmetry where the neutral issue position are dislocated from the center of the network. This limitation exists in part because all three contributions represent opinions on a continuous scale, creating a structural mismatch with survey data and preventing direct quantitative comparison of simulated and empirical belief structures (Carpentras, 2023; Keijzer & Mäs, 2022). Moreover, the decision-making modules within these contributions operationalise issue constraint using correlations at the issue level, which implicitly treats opinion scales as interval-level measurements. Consequently, the emergent belief structures within these models tend to be overly symmetrical, preventing the kind of asymmetric patterns that we observe empirically within (see Figure 2). However, as we have shown in the introduction, empirical surveys follow an non-ordinal scale (e.g. see the asymmetries in figure 2), meaning that the distances between response options cannot be assumed to be equal.

Therefore, our contribution is to address this gap by (1) modelling opinions in ways that match empirical data structures and (2) developing cognitive models that can accommodate asymmetric structures. As we will demonstrate within the results sections, this approach allows us to directly compare our results with empirical data.

3 Model description

3.1 General understanding of the model

In this section, we introduce a new model which we call the Replicating Issue Alignment and Issue Asymmetry model (RIA 2 ). RIA 2 is a discrete multi-dimensional opinion dynamics model that developed to test whether and under which conditions patterns of issue alignment and issue asymmetry emerge. By "discrete" we mean that, unlike most opinion dynamics models that represent opinions as a continuous variable within a bounded interval, our model only allows a finite set of opinions on each topic. This is designed to mimic empirical Likert-type data, for which the number of response options for a given issue are discrete and limited (usually between 3 and 10). Instead of agents persuading each other directly, as typically conceptualised within opinion dynamics models, agents are aware of all other agents' issue positions and use this information to construct a decision-making module of issue constraint which operates as the driving force for updating opinions (Figure 3). Each agent observes the full population's issue positions (arrow A), constructs a decision-making module of how attitudes co-occur across issues (arrow B; as we will explain later, different types of decision-making modules are also possible), and uses that decision-making module to probabilistically update one of their own responses (arrow C; as we will explain later, the general tendency of the agent is to hold a series of beliefs which is more consistent with what observed). The model thus operationalises the theory of associative diffusion (Goldberg & Stein, 2018) in a discrete opinion space, enabling researchers to compare simulation results with empirical survey data.

Within the model, we test two operationalisations of how agents construct and apply their decisionmaking module. The first computes correlations between issues at the issue level, akin to Belief Network Analysis (Boutyline & Vaisey, 2017), and implicitly assumes linear, monotonic relationships between issue positions. The second computes co-occurrence at the response level on a nominal scale, akin to Response Item Networks (Carpentras et al., 2024), and allows for non-ordinal and non-monotonic relationships.

3.2 Entities and State Variables

The model contains 𝑁 agents, each holding one of 𝑅 ordered responses for each of 𝐼 issues. issue positions are discrete, so that the response of a given agent on issue 𝑖 is 𝑠 𝑖 ∈ {1, … , 𝑅}. The full set of an agent's responses is stored in an issue vector 𝐬 ∈ {𝑠 1 , … , 𝑠 𝐼 }, with one entry per issue, and in a dummy-coded response vector 𝐫 ∈ {0, 1} 𝐼 ×𝑅 , where 𝑟 𝑖,𝑙 = 1 if and only if 𝑠 𝑖 = 𝑙, and 0 otherwise. As each agent holds exactly one response per issue, each block of 𝑅 entries within 𝐫 sums to exactly one. During initialisation, each response is drawn independently from a uniform distribution, 𝑠 𝑖 ∼  {1, … , 𝑅}. The population is therefore initialised with no particular attitude structure, so that any emergent alignment or asymmetry arises entirely from the modelled micro-behavioural dynamics.

3.3 Model processes

After initialisation, one model iteration consists of agents going through the following cycle: 1) agents observe the current responses of all other agents, 2) agents use this observation to update their decision-making modules 3) agents use their decision-making module to pick one new response for one of the issues. This model cycle is repeated for 10 random agents at a time, which means semi-asynchronous updating.

Step 1: Observing the Responses of Others

At each time step, agents have access to the complete set of responses held by all other agents in the population. This assumption of global observability is a stylised version of the process by which individuals are exposed to the co-occurrence of attitudes through public discourse, social media, and direct social interaction. The full population's responses are assembled into an 𝑁 × 𝐼 matrix 𝐏, where each row corresponds to an agent's issue vector 𝐬, and into an 𝑁 × (𝐼 ⋅ 𝑅) matrix 𝐐, where each row corresponds to an agent's dummy-coded response vector 𝐫.

Step 2: Constructing the decision-making modules

Agents construct two base decision-making modules of the population's attitude structure, which they use in Step 3 to update their responses: an issue-level decision-making module and a response-level decision-making module. Depending on the specification of the run, the decision-making module employed for step 3 is one of the two base decision-making modules or a combination of them.

For the issue-level decision-making module, the issue vectors are stacked into an 𝑁 × 𝐼 agent-by-issue matrix 𝐏 in which each row is an agent and each column is an issue. Correlating the columns of 𝐏 yields an issue-by-issue correlation matrix, akin to BNA. 4 Measurements

We use five different metrics to numerically compare the model output with empirical data on issue alignment and issue asymmetry. For the issue alignment metric, we use the metrics distortion, linearisation, and link density. For issue asymmetry, we look at neutral displacement and asymmetry.

During every timestep within a simulation run, we store the response-level correlation matrix 𝚽 RL (Equation 1), which enables us to construct a response item network of all the attitudes of the simulated population. By tracking how the shape of the response item network evolves over the duration of the simulation, we can distil whether and how issue alignment and issue asymmetry evolve over time.

Every response item network is constructed as the method prescribes (Warncke et al., 2026) using the positive correlations from 𝚽 RL as weights within the Fruchterman-Reingold force-directed algorithm.

Next, the network is rotated rotated using principal component analysis so that the first principal axis of the layout becomes the x-axis and the second becomes the y-axis (Carpentras et al., 2024). The x-axis is subsequently centred on the midpoint of its own range, so that 𝑥 𝑚𝑎𝑥 = -𝑥 𝑚𝑖𝑛 . This gives every response node (𝑖, 𝑙), denoting response 𝑙 of issue 𝑖, a position (𝑥 𝑖,𝑙 , 𝑦 𝑖,𝑙 ) in a two-dimensional plane. 5 Simulation results

We performed modelling simulations to assess the extent to which item-and response-level decisionmaking can replicate patterns of issue alignment and issue asymmetry. We performed a full factorial analysis for the two input parameters we varied: 1) the population size 𝑁 , 2) 𝜆 which determines how two decision-making modules are balanced when updating responses. Within one time step in the modelling output, the modelling process described above is repeated 𝑁 20 times, which means after every recorded timestep, about half of the populations have updated their response.

5.1 Comparing the two decision-making modules

To explain the difference between issue-level decision-making and response-level decision-making, we first show the response item networks for a model run with 𝜆 = 1 (issue-level decision-making module only) and 𝜆 = 0 (response-level decision-making only) (Figure 5). For issue-level decision-making (𝜆 = 1), the population develops a strongly ordinally structured ResIN network. After the initialisation period, the extreme responses of every issue sit on opposite ends of the x-axis while the neutral responses are located near the centre. In this respect, the simulated networks closely resemble the DPES 2023 network (Figure 2, network c), which also combines high issue alignment with an ordinal structure. In terms of asymmetry, issue-level decision-making shows mixed results. Although asymmetry seems to be relatively high and close to response-level decisionmaking, this is slightly misleading as this is caused by all neutral responses being placed to their right side of the centreline. This comes clear when looking into the neutral displacement metric which shows that the distance of neutral responses is over the whole run much lower compared to response-level decision-making. Networks with item-level decision-making thus fail to capture two empirical features: the displacement of neutral responses in DPES 2021 and 2023, and the one-sided distribution of response nodes in DPES 2021.

We suspect this is caused by how the issue-level module scores a candidate response. Agents form an expected response as a weighted average of their normalised responses on the remaining issues (Equations 6-8). Each candidate response is then scored by its distance from this expected response (Equation 9). As the issue-level correlation matrix contains one value per pair of issues, it can only express to what extent two issues move in the same direction. Using the example where the correlation is 0.1 between issues A and B and -0.1 between issues A and C, and agent Z, who holds extreme issue positions for both issues B and C and is asked to reconsider its position regarding issue A, then the positive correlation between issues A and B is cancelled out by the negative correlation between A and C, meaning that the expected response that agent x adopts for issue A would be closer to the neutral position. Additionally, since selection is probabilistic, agents also adopt responses next to their expected response, as the neutral response is closed to all other responses, it has overall the highest chance of being picked. Both the issue correlation and the probabilistic adoption function, eventually leads to a normal distribution in the response distribution of issue.

For response-level decision-making (𝜆 = 0) on the other hand, every response node has its own correlation with every other response node. The module can therefore represent that a neutral response co-occurs with the extreme responses of one camp only. Figure 5 shows that the responselevel decision-making module produces clear response alignment. We see two separate clusters of responses, and responses within a cluster are strongly co-selected by agents (high link density). The composition of these clusters, however, do not follow the ordinal logic. Extreme, moderate and neutral response nodes are widely scattered across the response item network. The distortion is therefore much higher compared to the DPES data of 2021 and 2023 in Figure 2. High distortion is caused by the fact that the response-level module does not provide any ordinal information. So agents are unaware that "mildly agree" lies between "neutral" and "strongly agree". The response-level score of a candidate is the sum of its correlations with the responses the agent already holds (Equation 3). The module thus reorganises which responses are held together while leaving the frequency of each response roughly similar to the uniform distribution at the start of the simulation.

The value of 0.352 of DPES 2021 is very rare, and the value of 0.450 of DPES 2006 lies above every simulated network. Here not only are the neutral positions all on one side of the network, but also the centre positions taken over by positions right next to the neutral position (Node E_5 and C_3)

To determine how often the model fits the empirical data, we count the networks that are close to a DPES network on asymmetry, neutral displacement and distortion at the same time. We define close as within ±10% of the range of a metric, where the range covers all simulated networks and the four DPES networks. Table 3 shows that the model never reproduces DPES 2021: not one of the 90,900 networks comes close on all three metrics. The same holds for DPES 2006, with the exception of a single network. DPES 2023 is reproduced in 0.4% to 1.4% of the networks and DPES 2025 in 0.8% to 6.3%, with a peak at 𝜆 = 0.15 (19.6% for 𝑁 = 1,000, 13.2% for 𝑁 = 5,000 and 8.8% for 𝑁 = 10,000). The model thus only comes close to the two networks with an asymmetry of 0. No network with low distortion (at most 0.2) reaches an asymmetry of 0.3. Low distortion can be combined with some neutral displacement, as 4% to 6% of the networks with low distortion reach the 0.143 of DPES 2023, but not with the high asymmetry of DPES 2006 and 2021.

The picture is different for the three metrics for issue alignment: distortion, linearization, and link density. Table 3 shows that the model is well able to replicate issue alignment as found in DPES 2023. For 𝜆 = 0.6, 78% of the networks with 𝑁 = 5,000 and 93% of the networks with 𝑁 = 10,000 are close to DPES 2023 on all three metrics. The same holds for DPES 2021, where 98% of the networks with 𝑁 = 5,000 are close at 𝜆 = 0.5. This requires a sufficiently large population, as linearization stays around 3 for 𝑁 = 1,000, compared to 6.5 in DPES 2023. The DPES networks have a comparable size, with 5,640 respondents in 2023.

Other patterns in the survey data remain far out of reach. No network comes close to DPES 2006 for these three metrics at the same time. Within the model, distortion of 0.698 only occurs at 𝜆 = 0 or in runs without any alignment, while in DPES 2006 this distortion goes together with a clear structure in which the extreme responses cluster on one side of the network. The DPES 2025 network has a low distortion (0.159), which on its own lies within the range of the model. However, the model does not combine this with the low linearization: for 𝑁 ≥ 5,000 only 0.01% to 0.06% of the networks have both a distortion below 0.25 and a linearization below 2.5. When we consider all five metrics together, the model barely reproduces any of the DPES networks (Table 3, panel A). Only DPES 2023 is reached, by 0.33% of the networks for 𝑁 = 5,000 and 0.30% for 𝑁 = 10,000, and DPES 2025 by 0.08% for 𝑁 = 1,000.

Even at the best value of 𝜆, this is at most 2.0% of the networks (panel B), since the model is unable to displace the neutral positions while maintaining an ordinal structure.

Ideally, we would thus have a mechanism that provides ordinal structure but which also creates enough variation to generate structural asymmetric patterns. A mixed model combining issue-and responselevel decision-making modules does not provide this for extensive periods of time. An asymmetry of at least 0.2 occurs in 7% to 9% of the networks, but only during short episodes, since the longest episode in all these runs lasts 220 of the 2,000 time steps. The asymmetry only remains for longer at 𝜆 of 0.15. However, at these values of 𝜆 the median distortion is above 0.3, so the networks lack the ordinal structure of the DPES networks, and also they do not reach an asymmetry of 0.3.

5.3 Scaling up the population

For most values of 𝜆, a larger population leaves the ordinal structure and link density of the network largely unchanged, although the fluctuations become smaller and the network becomes more linear.

For example, at 𝜆 = 0.5 the median link density is 0.033 for all three population sizes, while the median neutral displacement decreases from 0.073 for 𝑁 = 1,000 to 0.044 for 𝑁 = 10,000 and the median linearization increases from 3.2 to 6.5. The outlier for higher values of 𝑁 is for 𝜆 = 1. For 𝑁 = 1,000 all runs reach issue alignment, but for 𝑁 = 5,000 and 𝑁 = 10,000 no run does within 4,000 time steps. The median link density then stays at 0.006 and 0.004 and the median linearization at 1.1, compared to 0.026 and 3.4 for 𝑁 = 1,000. The response distribution also remains uniform, with 15% of the responses on the neutral position (14% would be fully uniform), compared to 24% for 𝑁 = 1,000.

The reason for this size-induced difference is the varying strength of the correlations during initialization. As responses are drawn independently, the initial correlations between issues are produced by stochastic processes, and they become smaller when the population grows. In the simulation run with seed = 1, the mean absolute correlation at the start is 0.023 for 𝑁 = 1,000, 0.010 for 𝑁 = 5,000 and 0.005 for 𝑁 = 10,000. The issue-level decion-making module takes the strength of these correlations into account using 𝑤 𝑖,𝑎 in Equation 10. At the start, 𝑤 𝑖,𝑎 is only 0.16 for 𝑁 = 1,000 and 0.04 for 𝑁 = 10,000, meaning that the probability of adopting a preferred opinion of an agent is almost random. A single change in issue position configuration has a marginal effect on the constraint between issues, and with many agents moving in either direction, the driver force to adopt one response of the other gets more marginalized. For 𝑁 = 1,000 the correlations are just strong enough to reinforce themselves, and the mean correlation increases to 0.34 within 200 time steps. For 𝑁 = 5,000 this does not happen and the mean correlation between respons is still 0.018 at time step 1,600.

The response-level decision-making module does not have this problem. In Equation 4the most coherent candidate always receives a score of 1 and the least coherent a score of 0, regardless of how weak the underlying correlations are. Thus, a correlation of 0.01 leads to an equally decisive choice as a correlation of 0.3. The MINMAX function therefore works as a trigger for alignment, as it amplifies even the smallest chance correlations. A small share of the response-level decision-making module is enough for this: at 𝜆 = 0.9 all runs with 𝑁 = 5,000 align and 65% of the runs with 𝑁 = 10,000, whereas no run aligns at 𝜆 = 1 for these population sizes.

6 Discussion

Our results show that the model is able to replicate issue alignment similar to what we observe in the DPES. For sufficiently large populations, the combination of issue-and response-level decision-making modules generates networks with distortion, linearisation, and link density comparable to the DPES 2021 and 2023. A simple rule of associative diffusion in which agents observe how responses co-occur and update their own responses accordingly is thus sufficient to generate this type of ordered issue alignment from a population without any structure.

However, there are patterns in the survey data that this model cannot reproduce. This includes, for example, the asymmetry of DPES 2021, as not a single network comes close on asymmetry, neutral displacement, and distortion. Moreover, it also is not able to reach the non-ordinal structure of DPES 2006, where distortion is high while the extreme responses cluster together. It also does not replicate DPES 2025, where extreme response clusters are still connected while linearisation is increasing. Associative diffusion in this form is thus most likely a mechanism for symmetrical issue alignment, and additional behavioural rules have to be introduced to reproduce the other patterns.

The reason for a lack of structural asymmetry is inherent to how associative diffusion has been operationalised in the model. Neither decision-making module treats one side of an issue differently from the other. If we swap "agree" and "disagree" on an issue, both modules behave exactly the same. Moreover, there is no heterogeneity among the agents, all agents follow the same rule, and all agents observe the same population. Therefore, asymmetry appears only as a temporary break due to stochastic variance, and the issue-level module restores the symmetry only once the model is out of balance. This also explains why the network asymmetry decreases as the population size grows. The noise created by the response-level decision-making module has a significantly smaller impact which reduces the asymmetrical patterns. Among the aligned networks with 𝜆 between 0.35 and 0.8, the share of any asymmetry decreases from 35% for 𝑁 = 1,000 to 13% for 𝑁 = 5,000 and 5% for 𝑁 = 10,000.

Adding response-level decision-making thus adds variation around a symmetrical structure, but does not lead to a permanent asymmetrical network structure.

6.1 What population scaling means for the model

Our results also show that issue alignment in the model depends on how agents deal with weak correlations. The issue-level decision-making module takes the strength of correlations into account, which seems plausible as an agent should not act decisively on an association that is barely present. But exactly because of this, the model fails to reach alignment for larger populations. The response-level decision-making module does reach alignment for every population size, but only because the MINMAX function lets agents act decisively on associations that are almost non-existent. For a population of 10,000 this concerns correlations of around 0.01, which no person could realistically perceive.

This is particularly important for realistic population sizes. Typical electorates consist of millions of voters, for whom the initial chance correlations would be even lower. The robustness of our results in a larger population is due to the MINMAX function within the response-level decision-making module. At the same time, this result points at a limitation of the assumption that agents observe the whole population: people do not observe an electorate of millions, but a limited number of connected others, either through day to day facial interaction or through social media, and the correlations they perceive are therefore based on a smaller sample. The size of the population within the model might thus be better understood as the size of the group that an agent observes, rather than the size of the electorate. This makes the assumption of global observability more than a simplification, and it provides a strong argument for adding a social network to the model in future work.

6.2 Taking opinion dynamics out of its hypothetical paradigm

Beyond the specific results, our present approach holds an important contribution to the study of opinion dynamics more generally. Opinion dynamics models often start from a hypothetical mechanism, such as repulsion, and show which macro patterns this mechanism can generate. Whether the mechanism and its outcome actually correspond to what happens in society is very difficult to validate, because the model is defined on a scale that does not correspond to the scale of survey data (Flache et al., 2017; Carpentras, 2023; Carpentras & Quayle, 2023). Instead, we started from the empirical data by operationalising the model on exactly the same scale as these data, and only then do we apply micro-behavioural rules to test which macro-behavioural patterns they generate.

The advantage of our approach is that we can directly test for which micro-behavioural rules or which specific formalisation might be able to replicate real world patterns. Our model does not reproduce the asymmetry of DPES 2006 and 2021, and because the simulated networks and the DPES networks are computed in exactly the same way, we can say precisely where and how often it fails. We consider this failure to be a meaningful result, as it suggests that this way of operationalising associative diffusion with symmetrical rules as a sufficient explanation on its own is not able to produce structural asymmetry. This statement can only be made when the output of a model is directly comparable to the empirical data. Models in a hypothetical paradigm can show that a mechanism is able to generate a pattern, but they are rarely able to show why it is not.

A second advantage of a discrete and multi-dimensional model is also to provide more reference points for validation than a model focussing on a single issue only. Beyond comparing to response distribution within each issue, our approach also allows for validation at the alignment between issues, and at the shape of the network as a whole. Plausible mechanisms of driving polarisation should be able to generate, in combination with others, at least several of these patterns at the same time. In this specific paper, the issue-level decision-making module generates a realistic ordinal structure, but also creates a centred response distribution within every issue. On the other hand, response-level decision-making generates clear clusters of responses, but leaves the uniform response distribution intact and lacks ordinal structure. Looking at only one of these patterns would have led to a different conclusion about each module. Comparing model output to a variety of empirical data structures makes it more difficult for a mechanism to fit, and therefore provides a stronger test of the validity of a micro-behavioural rule.

6.3 Calibration on repeated election surveys

Using the same scale as the empirical data also makes calibration more straightforward. In this paper, we start with a uniform distribution of issue positions, which does not impose any particular opinion structure at initialisation. As the core mechanism of the model is the matrix with the responses of each agent to each issue, survey data can directly, without any transformations or rescaling, be used to sample the agent be filled with the responses of survey participant. We could, for example, initialise the model with the responses from DPES 2006, run the model, and see to what extent it comes close to the empirical state of DPES 2025. The surveys held in between then function as a validation of whether the process of getting from 2006 to 2025 follows grosso modo the same pattern as the empirical data. This is a much stronger test than comparing a final state, as a micro-behavioural rule then has to reproduce a trajectory rather than only an end point. It would strengthen the falsifiability of micro-behavioural rules as causal explanations for empirical patterns.

There are some requirements for this design. The selected issues have to be available across all waves, which is the case, for instance, for European Unification, Asylum Seekers, and Income Difference in 2006 and 2025, but not for Climate Policy or Transgender Rights. Moreover, the DPES consists of different respondents per wave, therefore results can only be compared at the the aggregate, not the individual level. Moreover, the model has to be initialized with issues with same number of responses available in the empirical data. Finally, a model iteration has no fixed length in calendar time, so that the number of time steps between two surveys has to be calibrated as well. The intermediate surveys can be used for this, by calibrating on a part of the waves and using the other waves for validation.

7 Conclusion

This paper uses a multi-dimensional discrete opinion dynamics model to demonstrate the extent to which two different decision-making modules of associative diffusion are able to replicate patterns of issue alignment and issue asymmetry observed in empirical election survey data. Simulations indicate that the issue-level decision-making module leads to issue alignment with highly symmetrical and ordinal structures, while the response-level decision-making module creates more distorted and asymmetrical patterns. Combining both decision-making modules appears to generate patterns that are most closely aligned with the empirical survey data under certain conditions. The model, however, struggles with generating asymmetrical but highly ordinal patterns that last for longer consecutive time periods. By aligning response scale and analysing techniques between simulated and empirical data, we present an opinion dynamics model with strong explainable replications of or deviations from the empirical observations, and highlights the underexplored potential of agent-based models as explanatory tool for understanding social dynamics.
