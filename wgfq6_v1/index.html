Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. What we found

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Memories for the personal past and imaginings of the future can be examined via the participant's subjective experience or a quantitative assessment of their episodic content. There is ongoing debate as to the relationship between these measures. Here we conducted two independent studies (n = 52 and 82) in healthy young adults who were presented with several cue words to prompt recollection of autobiographical memories and imaginings of possible future events. They subsequently rated each of these experiences for its overall vividness and their sense of (p)reliving it. The episodic content of each narrative was scored by the experimenter according to a standardised system (Autobiographical Interview; Levine et al., 2002). Linear mixed-effects modelling, which can examine relationships on a trial-by-trial basis, revealed a significant association between subjective ratings and experimenterscored episodic content in both studies. Further analysis revealed that within the episodic content category, the subcategories of event details (e.g. actions, people and objects, which are related to the narrative unfolding of events), and emotion/thought details were the primary ones significantly related to both the sense of vividness and (p)reliving. Alignment tended to be stronger when imaging the future than recalling the past. Our findings refute strong previous claims, based on suboptimal analyses, that quantitative measures of content and subjective ratings are distinct aspects of remembering. Thus, episodic content meaningfully aligns with, but does not fully determine, subjective experiences of vividness and (p)reliving. Further research is now required to determine the factors which might moderate this link.

1. Introduction

A remarkable feature of the human mind is the ability to transport ourselves in time; to transcend the present moment, and either to mentally go back in time to remember our personal past or project ourselves into the future and imagine events that might happen (Atance & O'Neill, 2001; Brewer, 1992; Buckner & Carroll, 2007; Tulving, 1985; 2002). This recollective/imaginative experience can evoke rich episodic content details, such as the people and objects present, spatiotemporal and sensory details, as well as remembering or eliciting emotions and thoughts (D'Argembeau & Van der Linden, 2006; Levine, 2004; Vannucci et al., 2020). Early conceptualisations of episodic memory (Tulving, 1972) emphasised the importance of retrieved content, focusing on the personal event ("what"), the place that it happened ("where"), and the timing ("when"). However, Tulving's later writings stressed more the subjective aspects of remembering, introducing the notion of autonoetic consciousness, which is an awareness of oneself as a continuous entity through time (Tulving, 1985; 2002). This awareness gives an individual the ability to engage in mental time travel, to both past events and imagined personal future events, and underpins the rich subjective (re-)experiencing of the event. Tulving regarded autonoetic consciousness as a necessary and defining feature of episodic memory, which is not present in other forms of memory (Tulving, 1985; 2002; Wheeler et al., 1997). A fundamental issue in the literature is the link between the subjective experience of the individual, tapping autonoetic consciousness, and the behavioural retrieval of specific episodic event details in both episodic memory and future thinking.

There are a variety of ways of capturing participants' subjective experiences (see Folville et al., 2022; Sutin & Robins, 2007; Zaman et al., 2024). A longstanding measure used to tap autonoetic consciousness is the Remember/Know paradigm. When participants indicate that they Remember previous events this is presumed to indicate an autonoetic episodic experience, whereas Know responses are noetic and are thought to be more linked to semantic memory information (Tulving, 1985 (Tulving, , 1989)). However, there are several question marks over the extent to which the Remember/Know paradigm can capture, and separate, autonoetic and noetic awareness (see Rubin, 2005; Umanath & Coane, 2020; Zaman et al., 2024 for discussion). An alternative way of examining autonoetic consciousness and the experience of mental time travel is to explicitly ask participants e.g. to what extent did you feel like you were (p)reliving the event? (e.g. Cordonnier et al., 2018; Daselaar et al., 2008; Hassabis et al., 2007; Janssen et al., 2011; Rubin et al., 2003; 2019; Zaman et al., 2024). There are other aspects of the subjective experience which participants can report upon that are argued to index autonoetic consciousness (Rubin & Umanath, 2015; Zaman et al., 2024). The most prevalent measure is asking participants to report on the vividness of the experience (e.g. Armson et al., 2021; Baddeley & Andrade, 2000; Brewer & Pani, 1996; Giusberti et al., 1992; Johnson et al., 1988; Kensinger et al., 2011; Leer et al., 2014; Morton & MacLeod, 2023; Richter et al., 2016; Rubin, 2006; Rubin et al., 2003; Sheldon & Levine, 2013). Memory vividness is generally thought to indicate the richness of the details in the event and clarity of the experience, as compared with the experience of actual perception (Brewer, 1992; D'Angiulli et al., 2013; Rubin et al., 2003). Thus, autonoetic consciousness can be inferred by self-report ratings of memory qualities, including a sense of (p)reliving and contextually rich mental imagery (vividness).

An alternative way in which people's memories can be examined is via a quantitative assessment of specific episodic event details completed by the experimenter or another independent rater. When examining episodic memory this is generally completed by scoring the accuracy of recollection. For example, participants might be asked to retrieve contextual details from a previously presented study phase. Autobiographical memories are more complex and tend to have more detailed and multifaceted representations compared to laboratory-based episodic memory tasks (Roediger & McDermott, 2013).

These can be scored by the experimenter for their accuracy (by corroborating with diaries or friends and family e.g. Barclay & Wellman, 1986) or narrative coherence (Reese at al., 2011), but most research examines the content of the memory, including how many episodic details it contains and perhaps of what type (e.g. Hassabis et al., 2007; Koriat et al., 2000; Schlagman et al., 2006; Vijayarajah et al., 2023).

As autobiographical memories contain episodic and non-episodic details e.g. semantic information (Brewer, 1992; Conway, 2001), the Autobiographical Interview protocol (AI; Levine et al., 2002) was developed to segregate these two memory types and to objectively quantify the amount and type of details that a participant recounts. In the AI, the participant is asked to recall a past event in which they were personally involved, and which is specific to a certain time and place. The details produced are then categorised by the experimenter as 'internal' or 'external'. Internal details describe the episodic content, such as what happened, who was there, and the time and place of the event. External details are non-episodic and comprise semantic details and information that is repeated or tangential to the main event described. An extensive body of research has used this instrument to investigate autobiographical memory (see: https://levinelab.weebly.com/ai-bibliography.html)). More recently it has been adapted to examine episodic future thinking (e.g. Addis et al., 2008; Reed & Evans, 2024; Williams et al., 2020). This is consistent with Tulving's notion of mental time travel being bidirectional (Tulving, 1985 (Tulving, , 2002) ) and the constructive episodic simulation hypothesis (Schacter & Addis, 2007), which proposes that episodic memory supports the construction of imagined future events by the retrieval and flexible recombination of stored information into a new scenario.

According to Levine (2004, page 56) the AI (Levine et al., 2002) "transfers this burden of self-report from the participant to the experimenter" and "assumes that details reflecting specific happenings, perceptual information, or mental states reflect episodic recollection (operationalized as ''internal'' details)". It might therefore be anticipated that participants' phenomenological reports of autobiographical memory and the behavioural retrieval of specific episodic event details would be well correlated dimensions of remembering. This might be particularly so as AI internal (episodic) details are scored using categories adapted from a subjective measure of memory: the Memory Characteristics Questionnaire. This was originally developed by Johnson et al. (1988) to distinguish memories of experienced events, which contain more sensory and contextual details, from memories of imagined events, which contain more elements related to cognitive operations. Some studies have examined the correlation between experimenter scored internal details on the AI and trait questionnaires (e.g. The Survey of Autobiographical Memory, Palombo et al., 2013; The Autobiographical Recollection Test, Berntsen et al., 2019) completed by participants assessing their past memory or future episodic thinking.

These findings paint a mixed picture. While there is some evidence for associations between these measures (e.g. Armson et al., 2021; Matsumoto et al., 2022; Palombo et al., 2013; Setton et al., 2021), there are also numerous instances where significant relationships have not been found (e.g. Clark & Maguire, 2020; Matsumoto et al., 2022; Palombo et al., 2013; Setton et al., 2021). However, there is a mismatch. The AI is an event-based instrument wherein the participant recounts a specific experience, whereas the questionnaires tap trait mnemonics, i.e. general tendencies in how individuals recall memories (Palombo et al., 2018; see Aytürk et al., 2024, for the more general point that trait and event level phenomenology do not correlate strongly in autobiographical memory).

Other studies have looked at both subjective and quantitative episodic measures at the event level by asking participants for their phenomenological ratings after each narration. Even using this approach Clark and Maguire (2020) did not find a significant relationship between subjective vividness and episodic (internal) detail of memories using the AI in over 200 healthy volunteers. Based on this null result they concluded that internal details on the AI might not reflect the participant's subjective experiences of remembering and they may in fact "be measuring different things" (page 21). Similarly, Wardell et al. (2021) assessed the correspondence between clarity/vividness judgements participants gave to memories with different emotional valences from recent and remote time periods and several episodic details from the AI. No significant relationships were observed after correcting for multiple comparisons. However, Lockrow et al. (2024) in a sample of healthy younger and older adults did find a significant, albeit small, association between episodic detail and vividness. Thus, empirical work largely does not support there being a link between vividness, which is thought to index autonoetic consciousness, and episodic recollection on the AI when examining autobiographical memory.

There are very few studies that have examined this link in episodic future thinking. This is surprising as while there are many commonalities in their cognitive processes, phenomenology, and neural basis (e.g. Addis et al., 2007; Cordonnier et al., 2018; Schacter et al., 2007; Szpunar et al., 2007); there are also differences (Suddendorf, 2010). For example, autobiographical memories are typically richer in sensory details, more vivid and spatially coherent than imagined future events (De Brigard & Giovanello, 2012; Rubin, 2014). Future events often display a positivity bias (adults imagine better futures), which is not typically present in memories of past events (Rasmussen & Berntsen, 2013). These differences in phenomenology mean that it is unclear whether associations will also be present for episodic future thinking, and if so, whether they would be equivalent in magnitude. This was examined by Thakral et al. (2020) who placed young healthy volunteers in an MRI scanner, where they were asked to imagine personal future events in response to cues and then to assess the vividness of the construction on each trial. Outside the scanner participants reported what they had thought about in the scanner and these narratives were scored using the AI. On an individual participant basis, correlations were computed between vividness ratings and internal details on the AI for that event. Across participants it was found that these correlations were significantly greater than zero, but small in magnitude (although note that the authors conclude that vividness ratings and internal details "operate via distinct neural mechanisms"; Thakral et al., 2020, page 10). Recently, Zaman et al. (2024) completed an exploratory factor analysis of subjective ratings given by healthy young adults in an autobiographical memory and future thinking task and identified a 'core autonoetic consciousness' factor for each temporal direction, on which vividness and (p)re-experiencing ratings loaded highly. However, there were no significant relationships between this factor and participants' episodic detail scores from the AI for either past or future events. In summary, there has been limited work examining the relationship between participants' subjective ratings and episodic content details in future thinking and the results from this work portray an unclear picture. It is possible that this relationship has been occluded or attenuated by the way in which researchers typically examine it. It is usual for participants to recount several autobiographical or imagined future experiences and to give phenomenological ratings after each of them. The scores for each type of memory measure are totalled/averaged across trials, and these summary statistics are correlated across all participants i.e. this examines the relationship between the average rating on the subjective measure and the average rating on the quantitative content measure. Therefore, this approach does not allow for a direct comparison between participants' memory ratings and the amount of episodic content of specific memories/imagined events. It ignores the nested structure of the memories and the impact of both individual differences and the cues themselves (Aytürk et al., 2024), which reduces statistical power (Wright, 1998). Alternative approaches, such as linear mixed-effects modelling (LMM; Baayen et al., 2008) can assess this relationship on a trial-wise basis and are appropriate for the clustered structure of the data, due to each participant contributing multiple experiences. This approach has been used, for example, to determine the correspondence between memory accuracy and memory confidence (Wong et al., 2012) and the relationship between internal and external scores on the AI (Devitt et al., 2017). In the latter study when the authors reanalysed data using LMM, they found several significant negative relationships between AI internal and external details that were not evident when using averaged correlations, highlighting that this approach can be more sensitive.

Our aim in the current work was to provide a more comprehensive assessment of the relation between subjective ratings, tapping autonoetic consciousness, and quantitative measures of episodic content.

The internal dimension of the AI (Levine et al., 2002) was designed to capture episodic retrieval i.e. autonoetic consciousness and the experience of mental time travel, so it is important for validation to assess this link. Previous studies have made strong claims based on finding no link between quantitative measures of episodic content and participants' report of the phenomenological qualities of the event.

For example, Clark and Maguire (2020; page 21) stated that "AI internal details…might not reflect an individual's subjective experience of memory recall" and Zaman et al. (2024, page 14) concluded that "experimenter derived memory scores may tap into separate aspects of remembering than the selfreport questions used to characterize subjective experience". However, these conclusions are based upon studies which aggregated memories or only used a single memory, respectively. A more appropriate method to address this question is LMM, as it takes into consideration person and memory level effects (Wright, 1998).

We conducted two independent studies in which healthy young adults were presented with cue words and asked to recollect a personal episode from the past or to imagine a plausible personal event in the future involving that word. Study 1 used cue words that were predominantly positive (without any explicit instructions regarding valence), whereas Study 2 employed cue words and instructions designed to prompt both positively and negatively valenced events. Two key phenomenological ratings were gathered from participants after each narrative: vividness and the sense of (p)reliving, both of which appear to be integral to autonoetic consciousness (Rubin, 2005; Rubin & Umanath, 2015; Zaman et al., 2024). Participants' narratives were scored according to the established AI protocol (Levine et al., 2002).

In the AI all the details produced by the participant are categorised into an internal category, which describes the episodic content, or an external category, which contains non-episodic details. The internal score is comprised of several subcategories. Event describes what happened (i.e. the narrative core of the episode) and includes objects and who was there. Time indicates temporal details, while place includes any information involving scene layout and localisation in space. Perceptual details are those from any sensory modality, and the emotion/thought category describes details related to the mental state of the participant at the time of the event. See Table 1 for more details on AI scoring.

We predicted that there would be significant associations between participants' phenomenological ratings (vividness and sense of (p)reliving) and episodic content, as indexed by AI internal details, using a more sensitive analysis (LMM), which can examine this relationship at the trial level. Moreover, we also wanted to examine this in a more nuanced way by examining the subcategories of the AI, which might help us to understand what type of episodic details may be driving subjective ratings of vividness and (p)reliving. While perceptual detail (Cooper & Ritchey, 2022; Dawes et al., 2022; Rubin, 2005; St-Laurent et al., 2009 , 2015), emotion (Kensinger et al., 2011; Phelps & Sharot, 2008; St Jacques & Levine, 2007; Talarico et al., 2004) and narrative quality (Habermas & Diel, 2013; Nelson et al. 2008 Nelson et al. , 2021) ) may all contribute, prominent recent accounts (Hassabis & Maguire, 2007; Robin, 2018; Rubin & Umanath, 2015; see also Gaffan, 1991) consider autobiographical memories, and their defining subjective properties, as fundamentally dependent on mental scene construction. Thus, a further aim was to determine which episodic detail type(s) might demonstrate greater correspondence to people's subjective experiences. All associations will be examined for past memory and future imagining to determine if they hold across both temporal directions and are of similar magnitude. Our findings provide important new evidence to inform the debate around the relationship between episodic memory content and recollective experience.

2. Study 1 -Method

>Study 1 -Method

2.1. Participants

As this study was exploratory, no a priori power calculation was performed (Ditroilo et al., 2025). Eightytwo healthy participants were recruited to take part in Study 1 in exchange for course credits. Of these 82 participants, 30 were excluded due to a systematic error wherein one experimenter did not follow the standard AI procedure. The transcripts for participants interviewed by this experimenter had markedly lower word counts. The mean word count for this experimenter was 816 words (based on 30 transcripts) versus 1552 words (Experimenter 2, based on 30 transcripts) and 1387 words (Experimenter 3, based on 22 transcripts). For this experimenter, when participants gave little narrative or detail they did not consistently use standardised probes to elicit more information, whereas the other experimenters did. Thus, a decision was taken during AI scoring, prior to analysis, to omit all data collected by this experimenter. The remaining sample comprised 52 undergraduate students (46 female) who were aged between 18-39 years (mean age = 20) and were fluent in English. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to testing. This research was reviewed and approved by Cardiff University School of Psychology Research Ethics Committee (EC.07.03.06.1209GA11).

2.2. Materials

>Materials

2.2.1. Cue Word Paradigm

Participants completed an adapted autobiographical cue word paradigm that probed both past and future events (Addis et al., 2008; Crovitz & Schiffman, 1974). A series of ten cue words (Holiday, University, Home, Day Trip, Party, Family, Pets/Animals, Christmas, Shopping, Birthday) were selected by the experimenters. For those words which could be found in The Glasgow Norms (Scott et al., 2019) the mean valence rating was 7.25 (6.21-7.91) on a scale ranging from 1 (very negative) to 9 (very positive), arousal was 6.08 (4.84-7.52) on a scale ranging from 1 (very low arousal) to 9 (very high arousal), and imageability was 5.87 (4.97-6.65) on a scale ranging from 1 (very unimageable) to 7 (very imageable).

Cue words were presented both orally and visually (using cue cards) to solicit production of both past and future events, with half in each temporal direction (past, future). Participants were asked to generate events that were spatiotemporally specific and did not exceed 24 hours (Levine et al., 2002). In addition, future events were required to be novel and plausible given the participant's current plans (Addis et al., 2008). Participants described five events for one temporal direction (past or future) before beginning the next condition, with the order counterbalanced across participants. The allocation of cue words to condition was also counterbalanced, with a fixed order in each condition. Participant responses were audio recorded by the experimenter.

2.2.2. Autobiographical Interview Scoring

The audio recordings were transcribed and scored according to the standardised AI scoring protocol (Levine et al., 2002). Psychometric evaluation of the AI scoring has demonstrated high inter-rater reliability, replicable age effects, robust internal consistency across timepoints, and positive correlations with standard, performance-based episodic memory tasks (Lockrow et al., 2024).

First, responses were segmented into distinct sections to score for internal and external details. Details were typically grammatical clauses referencing a unique occurrence, observation or thought (Levine et al., 2002). Details were then scored as internal if they directly pertained to the main event and external if they were unrelated to the main event (Levine et al., 2002). Although most events given by participants were specific i.e. occurring at a particular time and place, some instances were not e.g. they extended over days or weeks. These were not excluded. For these cases, as outlined in the AI scoring manual, the scorer needed to define the main event (the one described in the most detail) and then categorise segments that were part of this event (internal details) separately from those that were not (external details). Internal responses were subcategorised into event, time, place, perceptual, and emotion/thought details, see Table 1. In a slight adjustment to the original AI scoring, one's own orientation in space e.g. "I was to the right of Carl" was coded under place, rather than as a perceptual detail (see Hodgetts et al., 2017 and Williams et al., 2020, who also coded this way).

Scoring was completed by two independent raters trained on the coding scheme. The two raters blind double-scored the first 8 participant's transcripts i.e. 80 memories/future events (10% of the original sample e.g. see Cole et al., 2012). An intra-class correlation analysis (ICC; two-way random model) was conducted in IBM SPSS Statistics (Version 26) on these data to evaluate the inter-rater consistency across all AI subcategories. Significant ICCs (all ps < .01) were observed for both the broader categories (internal: r = 0.99; external: r = 0.97) and their subcategories (internal: event r = 0.99; time r = 0.99; place r = 0.99; perceptual r = 0.93; emotion/thought r = 0.94). For subsequent transcripts these were scored by one rater and checked by the other to maintain scoring fidelity. Any discrepancies were resolved by discussion between the raters. Experimenters were blind to the hypotheses of the study.

2.2.3. Subjective Ratings

Following each event description participants completed a short self-report questionnaire that was constructed by the experimenters based upon previous measures of memory phenomenology (e.g. Autobiographical Memory Questionnaire, Rubin et al., 2003). All questions were rated on a 7-point Likert scale.

Individual items assessed the overall vividness of each event ('The overall vividness of this event was…';1 = not vivid,7 = very vivid) and the participant's sense of (p)reliving ('To what extent did you feel you were experiencing this memory/future scenario as if you were actually there?' ;1 = not at all,7 = It felt like I was really there). Correlations were modest between vividness and (p)reliving ratings (past: τb = 0.47, future: τb = 0.58, both ps < .001), indicating that these ratings are not redundant. Previous research has suggested that overall vividness and feelings of (p)reliving are core features of autonoetic experience for past and future events (Cordonnier et al., 2018; Habermas & Diel, 2013; Rubin et al., 2003 Rubin et al., , 2019;; Zaman et al., 2024).

Ratings were also given (1 = not at all clear,7 = extremely clear and distinct) for the clarity of episodic details for the following attributes: event/perceptual ('Details including people, objects, and surroundings are...'), time ('The time in which this event takes place is...'), place ('The location where this event takes place is...') and emotions/thoughts ('My own thoughts and feelings when the event takes place are...'). These additional ratings are not examined in the main text but see the Supplementary Results for complementary analyses of the relationship between these ratings and the AI subcategories.

2.3. Procedure

All participants were tested in-person and individually. The experimenter explained that participants would be presented with a series of cue words and that they were to use each word to either remember an event from the past or imagine an event involving themselves in the future. It was clarified whether the past or future condition would take place first and that they would be notified before commencing the next condition. Participants were asked to describe each event in as much detail as possible, refer to a specific place and time, and to choose an event lasting no longer than 24 hours. Before commencing the future condition, participants were instructed to imagine events that were both plausible and had not happened to them previously i.e. it needed to be novel. There were no constraints on the recency of events that were described i.e. participants could describe past and future events within any time frame. In the instance that a participant failed to provide sufficient detail or generated an event that was not very specific, they were probed using a standardised prompt such as 'Are there any other specific details you can think of?'. The experimenter presented each cue word on a cue card, as well as stating it orally as part of the standardised instructions. No limit was implemented on the amount of time participants took to describe each event. Once the participant had confirmed that their description was complete, they were asked to give their subjective ratings for the event. This procedure was repeated for all ten cue words.

2.4. Data Analysis

Linear mixed-effects modelling (LMM) was used to assess the relationship between internal details and participant ratings (vividness and (p)reliving) on a trial-by-trial basis. Linear mixed-effects modelling using the GAMLj package (Gallucci, 2023) was implemented in Jamovi (Version 2.4.8; The jamovi project, 2023). The models treated the participants and cue words as random effects (Wright, 1998). More specifically, the models included random intercepts for both participant and cue words. In all models, the dependent variable was the participant ratings, number of internal details was a continuous predictor, and the temporal condition (past or future) was a categorical predictor. The interaction between internal details and temporal condition was also examined to determine if these relationships differed by temporal condition. Internal details and temporal condition were added as fixed effects.

It should be noted that GAMLj: i) automatically mean-centres all continuous predictors to ensure that the range of any newly created variables (i.e. interactions) are kept around the means of the data, which helps with interpretation of the data and improves the chances of convergence (Enders & Tofighi, 2007) and ii) implements the Satterthwaite method for degrees of freedom, which leads to differences in the denominator degrees of freedom between different analyses. The contrast coding of the categorical predictor was set to 'simple' which means the first group was compared to the subsequent groups whilst centring the contrast to 0. As the first and subsequent groups are defined by alphanumeric order, the future condition was the first group, and the past condition was the subsequent group (futurepast); the mean of the future condition was compared against the mean of the past condition. Two of these models were examined between: i) experimenter scored number of internal details and participant rated vividness of each event and ii) experimenter scored number of internal details and participant rated sense of (p)reliving of each event.

General linear models (GLM) were used to investigate the extent to which the number of details of each of the AI subcategories (event, perceptual, time, place and emotion/thought details) predicted participant ratings (vividness and (p)reliving) on a trial-by-trial basis. Alike the linear mixed-effects models, GLM were implemented in Jamovi (Version 2.4.8; The jamovi project, 2023) using GAMLj (Gallucci, 2023). In both the GLMs, the dependent variable was the participant ratings, the number of details from each of the AI subcategories were continuous predictors, and the temporal condition (past or future) was a categorical predictor. The interaction between each AI subcategory and temporal condition was also examined to determine if these relationships differed by temporal condition.

Therefore, the GLMs were used to perform a moderated regression on a trial-by-trial basis (Lorah, 2022). Two of these models were implemented, wherein the dependent variable was either i) vividness or ii) (p)reliving ratings.

4. Summary of Study 1 findings and rationale for Study 2

Linear mixed-effects modelling (LMM; Wright, 1998) demonstrated that there was a significant and positive relationship between the number of episodic details as scored by the AI protocol (Levine et al., 2002) and both ratings of vividness and (p)reliving provided by the participant. This was found for both autobiographical past and future events. In contrast, correlations conducted on quantitative episodic content and subjective phenomenological ratings that were summed across events revealed significant relationships in the episodic future thinking condition only. Furthermore, GLM demonstrated that, within the internal category, thought to reflect episodic autobiographical content (Levine et al., 2002), event and emotion/thought details were significant predictors of overall vividness and (p)reliving ratings for past and future events.

Given that these findings contradict claims made in previous work (e.g. Clark & Maguire, 2020; Zaman et al., 2024), a second study was run with a larger sample to determine if these results would replicate. As prior work indicates that the majority of variance in self-reported phenomenology (including vividness and reliving) is attributable not to individual differences in autobiographical memory ability, but to individual memories and their cues (Aytürk et al., 2024), in Study 2 we manipulated cue characteristics.

As previous research has indicated that positive and negative events differ in the amount of episodic detail in which they are described (Ford et al., 2012; Holland & Kensinger, 2010; St Jacques & Levine, 2007) as well as how they are rated by participants (D' Argembeau & van der Linden, 2004; Kensinger & Schacter, 2006; Rasmussen & Berntsen, 2013; Simpson & Sheldon, 2020), cue word valence may be an important cue-level factor that moderates the relationship between quantitative episodic content measures and subjective experience. Whereas Study 1 employed predominantly positively valenced cue words, Study 2 included both positive and negative cue words. Thus, Study 2 allowed us to determine the replicability of results from Study 1 as well as whether they would generalise when negatively valenced cue words were used.

5. Study 2 -Method

All methodological details for Study 2 are the same as for Study 1 except as indicted below.

5.1 Participants

To estimate statistical power, we used the Mixedpower R package (as suggested by Kumle et al., 2021), which facilitates simulation-based power analyses for LMMs. The simulations were based on the data from Study 1 using the final sample of n = 52. Because Mixedpower inherits the within-participant structure from the fitted model, these simulations reflected the Study 1 design of 10 autobiographical events per participant. The parameters for these simulations included the fixed effects of internal details and temporal condition, the number of participants was varied across 4 different plausible sample sizes (n = 62, 72, 82, 92), each sample size was subjected to 1000 simulations to ensure robust estimates, and the critical value for significance was set at 2 (Kumle et al., 2021). As Study 2 cued 12 events per participant, using the Study 1 structure with 10 events makes the resulting power estimates slightly conservative, as additional repeated measures increase precision without altering the fixed-effect estimates. The results of these simulations indicated that for both the vividness and (p)reliving models, n = 82 would achieve statistical power of 0.98 in predicting the relationship between number of internal details and subjective ratings (the fixed effect of internal details). Therefore, we recruited 82 participants for Study 2. These 82 healthy undergraduate students (67 female) were aged between 18-23 years (mean age = 19). There was no overlap in participants between studies.

5.2 Materials

In the second study, the AI scoring system (Levine et al., 2002) and participant ratings were consistent with the first study. However, the cue word paradigm for past/future events (Addis et al., 2008; Crovitz & Schiffman, 1974) was adapted to include both normatively positive (Birthday, Achievement, Adventurous, School/University, Christmas, Holiday) and negative (Exam, Falling Over, Getting into Trouble, Failing, Embarrassed, Mistake) cue words that were selected by the experimenters.

Many of the positive cue words overlapped with those given in the first study, but the negative cue words were distinct. For those words that could be found in The Glasgow Norms (Scott et al., 2019) for positive prompts the mean valence rating was 7.28 (5.23-7.94) on a scale ranging from 1 (very negative) to 9 (very positive), arousal was 6.22 (4.33-7.52) on a scale ranging from 1 (very low arousal) to 9 (very high arousal), and imageability was 5.52 (3.88-6.57) on a scale ranging from 1 (very unimageable) to 7 (very imageable) and for negative words the mean valence rating was 2.66 (2.35-3.21), arousal was 5.00 (4.56-6.03), and imageability was 4.23 (3.43-6.21). Participants completed three events for each valence (positive, negative) and temporal condition (past, future), with all events of one valence first and then the other (e.g. past positive, future positive, past negative, future negative). The order of emotional valence was counterbalanced across participants. As in Study 1, the allocation of cue words to temporal condition was also counterbalanced, with a fixed order in each condition. The correlation between vividness and (p)reliving ratings were examined and again revealed that these were related but not redundant ratings (past positive: τb = 0.65, future positive: τb = 0.70, past negative: τb = 0.58, future negative: τb = 0.65; all ps < .001).

5.3 Procedure

All participants for Study 2 were also tested in-person and individually, but two adaptations were made to the procedure. One minute was selected to maximise efficiency and to reduce the cognitive burden on participants. We considered this a reasonable limit to impose due to evidence demonstrating that the ratio of internal-to-total details does not significantly change across one-, three-, and fiveminute limits (Cole et al., 2012). Standardised prompts were used as in Study 1. Figure 4 around here 7. Discussion

According to Tulving (2002), episodic autobiographical memory is the conscious recollection of a specific event from one's personal past. Episodic memory has also been proposed to form part of a broader capacity for mental time travel, enabling conscious awareness of past experiences and the imagination of specific future events (Tulving, 2005). This capacity, often described as autonoetic consciousness, is typically assessed through self-report measures of phenomenological qualities such as vividness and a sense of (p)reliving (Habermas & Diel, 2013; Rubin et al., 2003 Rubin et al., , 2019;; Zaman et al., 2024). Subjective memory experience and the retrieval of episodic details might be expected to correlate, particularly because the Autobiographical Interview (AI) coding protocol was developed from a phenomenological framework (Johnson et al., 1988; Levine et al., 2002). However, evidence for such an association has been inconsistent, leading some researchers to argue that subjective experience and episodic content reflect distinct aspects of remembering (e.g. Clark & Maguire, 2020; Zaman et al., 2024). A limitation of much of this work is that it has overlooked the multilevel structure of autobiographical events, treating memories as independent observations or aggregating across events (Aytürk et al., 2024; Wright, 1998).

To address this issue, we used multilevel modelling to examine trial-by-trial associations between subjective phenomenology and episodic content across both temporal directions.

Across two independent studies, a significant link was found between subjective recollective experience and the quantity of episodic content, supporting our hypothesis. More specifically, we found that the number of episodic details, based on the AI coding scheme, was significantly associated with the ratings provided by the participant for both vividness and sense of (p)reliving across both autobiographical past and future events. Furthermore, analyses of individual AI subcategories indicated that event and emotion/thought details were most consistently associated with phenomenological ratings. These findings suggest that subjective autonoetic experience and quantitative measures of episodic content are related, despite previous claims that they represent largely independent constructs.

Our work demonstrates the utility in using a multilevel modelling approach and the increased sensitivity of this analytic method. In our data the ICCs (which assessed the extent to which scores or ratings were consistent within an individual) for internal details, vividness and (p)reliving were low (0.32 -0.55, see Supplementary Results). This indicates that although the scores given by the experimenter for the episodic content and participants for their subjective experiences tended to be consistent, there was a lot of variability not due to individual differences i.e. due to situational factors, such as the cue words presented. This aligns with the results and conclusions of Aytürk et al. (2024) who found that the amount of trait-like variance for vividness and reliving was 32% to 37% respectively, suggesting that at least 60% of the variance in memory ratings on certain phenomenological ratings is due to the specific memories. This supports the use of a statistical approach which can account for this variability. This issue may help explain inconsistencies in the existing literature. Many previous studies have relied on aggregated scores or examined only a single memory per participant, ignoring any cue-related variance. Consistent with this interpretation, when we aggregated phenomenological ratings across narrative events (e.g. all vividness ratings for past memories) and correlated with aggregates of AI scores for the same events, most of these were not statistically significant. The overall pattern generally echoed that observed in the multilevel analyses, but only the strongest relationships remained detectable using this less sensitive approach. Thus, it is likely that the reason our results diverge from other studies, such as Clark and Maguire (2020) and Zaman et al. (2024) is due to our use of a more rigorous analysis method. Although it should also be noted that Zaman et al. (2024) examined only one event per condition and collected written responses online, whereas our study used verbal narratives collected face-to-face. Given evidence that written and spoken autobiographical recall can differ in important ways (Bunnell et al., 2020; Pearson et al., 2023), methodological factors may also contribute to discrepancies across studies.

Whilst the current evidence indicates a meaningful relationship between subjective autonoetic experience, as indexed by ratings of vividness and (p)reliving, and quantitative measures of episodic content, as defined by the AI protocol, the effect sizes are modest. While Cohen's (1992) rule-of-thumb criteria can be used i.e. small (R² = .02), medium (R² = .13), or large (R² = .26), caution needs to be adopted given they are somewhat arbitrary and depend upon the research context (see Rights & Cole, 2018). There is currently no set of standardised effect size measures which have been widely adopted by researchers using multilevel models, so it is difficult to compare across studies e.g. no R² measures in the work by Devitt et al. (2017) or Aytürk et al. (2024). There are several a priori reasons to think that the magnitude of the relationship we would obtain might be moderate.

First, the way the AI is scored is according to the 'benefit of the doubt' rule, whereby any detail that could reasonably be considered internal is categorised in this way (Levine et al., 2002). The rule is designed to be lenient towards episodic memory, ensuring that rich, specific details (internal details) are not mistakenly disregarded as generic, repeated or semantic information (external details). This avoids a false positive classification of a memory deficit based upon incidental factors yet might dilute associations between participant ratings and experimenter ones using the AI. Second, there are cases where the two aspects of memory diverge. One example is the neuropsychological case M.L., who suffered a traumatic brain injury. He had a profoundly impaired phenomenological experience, as indexed by reduced Remember responses in the classic Remember/Know paradigm (Tulving, 1985 (Tulving, , 1989)), but only marginal reductions in internal details (Levine et al., 2009). Dissociations have also been reported in a syndrome called Severely Deficient Autobiographical Memory by Palombo and colleagues (2015). Their three healthy, high functioning participants demonstrated reduced internal detail generation only for remote time periods, but their rating of vividness was significantly reduced for all events recalled. Finally, Addis et al. (2010) investigated the recall of personal past and future events and found that experimenter scored internal details were lower in the older adult groups compared to the young participants. However, there were no differences in self-report measures, except that older adults reported experiencing greater emotional intensity when imagining the future. This suggests that although the number of episodic details recalled is an important indicator of vividness and (p)reliving judgements, other factors also contribute to subjective ratings. In this regard, modest effect sizes are unsurprising and suggest that episodic detail meaningfully aligns with, but does not fully determine, subjective experiences of vividness and (p)reliving.

Among the AI subcategories, event details showed robust and consistent associations with vividness and (p)reliving, evident across both studies and for both remembering the past and imagining the future.

Event details constitute the largest component of the AI internal category and encompass the happenings, actions, people and objects, i.e. those elements central to narrative coherence (Conway et al., 2009 , Sekeres et al., 2016). A key aspect of the event category is the temporal unfolding of the episode. In the following example, "My dad told me there was a problem with my car, so I phoned the garage to make an appointment and took it in later that day", several event details -the 'happenings' are being relayed but these have a strong temporal structure. It is possible that our findings with the event category might reflect the temporal sequentiality/causal structure of the event details. Specific autobiographical memories have a temporal and causal ordering of events (Shapiro et al., 2006), which is usually evident when people communicate these to others. Indeed, Nelson et al. (2021) found that linguistic measures of narrative temporal structure were positively associated with AI internal details, while Habermas and Diel (2013) reported that narrative structure was related to independently rated vividness. They argued that reliving depends partly on experiencing events in a coherent temporal sequence. Taken together with the present findings, this evidence suggests that event details may contribute to phenomenological experience because both rely on a common feature: the temporal unfolding of an episode. The results therefore provide further support for the AI as an index of episodicity and highlight the importance of narrative structure in subjective remembering.

The emotion/thought subcategory also showed reliable associations with vividness and (p)reliving across both studies and temporal conditions. This finding is consistent with a substantial literature demonstrating close links between emotional experience and memory phenomenology. Emotional intensity has repeatedly been shown to predict ratings of vividness, reliving, and mental time travel more strongly than factors such as valence or memory age (Talarico et al., 2004; see also Habermas & Diel, 2013; Reisberg et al., 1988 and reviewed in Rubin, 2005; Kensinger et al., 2011; Kensinger & Fields, 2024; Phelps & Sharot, 2008).

Importantly, the emotion/thought category extends beyond emotion alone. It includes references to feelings, thoughts, beliefs, expectations, and other aspects of an individual's internal mental state.

Unlike perceptual or spatiotemporal details, these elements represent internally generated experiences rather than properties of the external environment. In this respect, they resemble what Andrews-Hanna and Grilli (2021) describe as the distinction between the "mind's eye" and the "mind's mind." Because these details are closely linked to self-representation, they may contribute particularly strongly to autonoetic experience. Remembering is not merely the retrieval of isolated details. Autobiographical narratives also serve functions related to meaning-making, identity, and self-continuity (Conway et al., 2019; Fivush, 2011; Habermas & Bluck, 2000). Recalling what one thought or felt during an experience may therefore strengthen the sense that an event happened to "me" and facilitate re-experiencing rather than as a detached reconstruction of external circumstances. Consistent with this interpretation, Su et al. (2026) found that memories richer in internal mental states were also judged as more personally important. Thus, emotion/thought details may be particularly influential because they capture those aspects of experience most closely tied to the self.

Intriguingly, St Jacques and Levine (2007) found using the AI that freely recalled emotional memories (both positive and negative) contained both more event and emotion/thought details relative to neutral memories but did not differ on other episodic details e.g. perceptual. Emotional memories were also rated as more vivid than neutral ones. These studies together with our own suggest it is the event and the emotion/thought subcategories that are driving the association with vividness and (p)reliving. This supports the claim that it is the narrative/evaluative features, which reflect their central meaning to the self, which contribute more to autonoetic consciousness i.e. the sense of reliving and vividness. For the most part, the precise perceptual or spatiotemporal details seem to be less important in predicting autonoetic consciousness (see Habermas & Diel, 2013). Perceptual and/or spatiotemporal details might be more important in determining other subjective aspects of memory, such as confidence or veridicality. Furthermore, it is also likely to depend upon the goal of the participant e.g. creating a 'vivid' narrative for a listener versus recalling the details of an event with high fidelity. Thus, future research would need to determine whether manipulating these variables would affect the specific subcategories of the AI found to drive alignment between subjective experience and episodic content.

One exception to the lack of association between specific detail and ratings of autonoetic conscious was observed between the time subdimension of the AI and phenomenological characteristics in Study 2 but not in Study 1. This was evident as an interaction with temporal condition, such that for vividness it was present in the future thinking condition but not past. Time is important when considering episodic memory as it is included in the definition of it -events must occur at a specific time and place (Tulving, 1985). It is notable that we only observed this effect in the future thinking condition. One possibility is that temporal information plays a particularly important role in rendering future scenarios plausible.

Imagined future events are inherently less fixed than memories, so specifying when an event will occur may help transform a vague possibility into a concrete representation. Some support for this interpretation comes from a study by Ernst and D'Argembeau (2017) who found that organising personal events on a mental timeline, including estimated time of occurrence, contributed to the person's sense of 'realness' of future events. Since future events are often connected to personal goals and plans (Ben Malek et al., 2018), temporal information may be especially relevant when constructing prospective simulations. Given that this effect emerged in only one study, however, this interpretation remains tentative.

Across both studies the subjective ratings and the episodic content scores from the AI, were higher when participants recalled events from the past compared to future imaginings, consistent with previous research (Addis et al., 2008; Berntsen & Bohn, 2010; De Brigard & Giovanello, 2012; Thakral et al., 2019; Williams et al., 2020). Despite these differences in overall level, the pattern of associations between quantitative episodic content and subjective ratings was remarkably similar across temporal conditions. This is consistent with neuroimaging work demonstrating common neural substrates across these abilities (Thakral et al., 2017; Williams et al., 2020), similar deficits in psychopathology (Hallford et al., 2018) and that there is a relationship between how well people can recall the past and imagine specific future events (Williams et al., 1996). This provides support for the constructive episodic simulation hypothesis (Schacter & Addis, 2007) which suggests that the episodic memory system plays a fundamental role in creating episodic future imaginings by retrieving and flexibly combining elements of past episodes into a mental simulation of a novel scenario. It is also consistent with Tulving's (2005) notion of episodic memory being part of a more general faculty, that makes mental time travel to the past and future.

Interestingly, we found that some associations between subjective ratings and episodic details were stronger when imagining versus remembering. Future events differ from memories in the way in which they are derived. Participants were explicitly told to generate a novel event which could plausibly happen to them in the future. Therefore, perhaps it is unsurprising that there would be more alignment between a future scenario that the participant has just created and described, and the questions they answer about that event immediately afterwards. However, when recalling a past event, there might have been additional factors that informed subjective judgments, such as how confident the participant is in the accuracy of the memory (Pezdek, 2003), how often it has been rehearsed (Ritchie et al., 2006; Skowronski & Walker, 2005), and the extent to which an event has been important or meaningful to a person's life story (Bluck & Habermas, 2001). Some support for the idea that the subjective contributions to future imaginings are reduced compared to autobiographical memory comes from the study of Zaman et al. (2024) who found that the factor structure, when imaging the future, was simpler compared to remembering the past. It is therefore plausible that the subjective ratings given in the autobiographical memory condition were influenced by a range of other factors besides the episodic content in the narrative, whereas these aspects might have exerted less of an influence in the future thinking condition.

Despite the association between emotion/thought content and phenomenology, we observed no effects of cue word valence. A likely explanation is that the cue words were only mildly arousing and, where ratings were available, negative cues were generally less arousing than positive cues. This is not what is typically found (Lang et al., 1997). Previous studies have shown that cue arousal predicts both the production of internal details and elevated phenomenological ratings (Kensinger et al., 2011; Kensinger & Corkin, 2003; Phelps & Sharot, 2008; Sheldon & Donahue, 2017; Simpson & Sheldon, 2020).

Consequently, stronger manipulations of cue arousal may reveal effects not evident in the present work.

In both studies, autobiographical narratives were collected before phenomenological ratings, consistent with most autobiographical memory research (see Sotgiu et al., 2025). Although this raises the possibility that ratings of vividness and (p)reliving were influenced by the details generated during narration, this order was chosen to minimise metacognitive interference that could bias recall.

Importantly, Sotgiu et al. (2025) found no differences in subjective ratings, including vividness and reliving, between narrative-first and phenomenology-first groups. While narrative-first participants reported fewer details overall, the proportion of cognitive and sensory/perceptual details did not differ, and they reported a higher proportion of affective-process words. The authors argue that administering narrative tasks first may provide a more accurate index of the influence of emotional experiences on memory. While future research could counterbalance task order, current evidence suggests that testing order is unlikely to account for the present findings.

Similar to previous work (e.g. Rubin et al., 2003; Zaman et al., 2024) we chose not to provide precise definitions of the terms vividness and (p)reliving (which we described as "if you were actually there"), allowing us to examine how participants naturally rated these questions. Consequently, we cannot determine precisely which aspects of experience participants considered when making their ratings.

Some theorists regard autonoetic consciousness as a direct, pre-reflective property of recollection rather than as a quality that requires explicit judgment or evaluation of detail (e.g. Klein, 2016; Tulving, 1985 Tulving, , 2005)). Vividness may reflect not just the clarity i.e. whether the memory is clear or vague and dim, but also an emotional/interoceptive dimension. Ford and Kensinger (2016) distinguished "external" vividness, corresponding to the reinstatement of perceptual and sensory details, from "internal" vividness, encompassing pre-experiential bodily or affective states. This suggests that participants may interpret vividness broadly, encompassing not only visual clarity but also sensory, emotional, and interoceptive dimensions (see also Tooming & Miyazono, 2021). Taken together these findings suggest that participants' ratings of vividness and (p)reliving may be shaped by a multitude of factors, such as perceptual, affective and self-referential processes. Further research is required to understand what participants understand by these concepts and how this understanding might differ between individuals.

In conclusion, using multi-level modelling we found that participants' subjective autonoetic experience of autobiographical memories or events involving themselves in the future are tied to the quantity of episodic details in the narration. Episodic content related to event details, as well as one's thoughts and feelings at the time of the episode -those features of episodic content that capture the storyline or narrative structure and have a central meaning to the self -are most tightly coupled with an individual's sense of overall vividness and (p)reliving of freely recalled cued autobiographical events. This connection seems to be stronger for future thinking than in autobiographical memory. Our findings provide a strong counterpoint to studies which have claimed, based on suboptimal analyses, that quantitative measures of content and subjective ratings are distinct constructs. Further research is now required to determine what other factors contribute to participants' subjective memory judgements and how this set of factors might change or be weighted differently according to whether people remember the past or imagine the future, the way in which memories are cued and expressed, as well as any moderating individual differences.

Supplementary Results

In the main paper we detail the relationships that were found when participants were asked to rate the vividness and sense of (p)reliving, which are widely agreed core features of autonoetic experience for past and future events (Cordonnier et al., 2018; Habermas & Diel, 2013; Rubin et al., 2003 Rubin et al., , 2019;; Zaman et al., 2024). Participants were also asked to give ratings for the clarity of their memories/imaginings broadly aligned to the internal detail subcategories of the AI. These were: event/perceptual ('Details including people, objects, and surroundings...), time ('The time in which this event takes place...'), place (The location where this event takes place...'), and emotions/thoughts ('My own thoughts and feelings when the event takes place...'). Participants rated each of these dimensions on a scale of 1-7 (1= not at all clear,7= extremely clear and distinct). Below we report the mixed-effects models we ran using these subjective clarity ratings and the corresponding experimenter scored indices. All of these indicate significant alignment between the ratings given by the participant and the experimenter scored subcategories from the AI.
