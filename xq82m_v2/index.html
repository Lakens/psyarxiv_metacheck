Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. Some differences come from the ground truth: it lacks p-values with a correction name (“p Bonf = .061”), and counts partial eta squared written with its subscript after it (“η 2 p = .05”), “p = .05”, and some table notes and figure legends (“† p < .10, *p < .05”), which this module leaves out, as non-significant p-values. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Full text

Abstract

Vision science has traditionally sought mechanisms shared across observers, treating individual differences as noise. Yet factor-analytic and latent-variable studies show that variation among observers is highly structured, recovering reproducible dimensions in contrast sensitivity, color, stereopsis, motion, and face recognition, among others. What are these latent dimensions? I propose they be treated as candidate computational phenotypes: a term borrowed from computational psychiatry and re-operationalized for vision as a stable, measurable pattern of visual information processing recovered from the covariance structure of individual differences. A computational phenotype is not a latent factor, mechanism, endophenotype, or biomarker; it occupies Marr's algorithmic level, between behavior and biology, and earns the name only by meeting stringent criteria of reliability, replication, convergent validity, and mechanistic relevance. I review candidate phenotypes drawn from many laboratories, recast development, aging, and disease as perturbations of phenotypic structure, and turn the strongest objection (that vision has no common factor) into support, since the relevant phenotypes are narrow and mechanism-specific. The payoff spans audiences: diagnostic profiles for clinicians, a noninvasive way to count and test visual mechanisms for basic scientists, and performancepredicting traits for human-factors work. The framework moves vision science from the average observer toward a precision science of visual computation.

1. The question after the answer

Vision science has been spectacularly successful at characterizing the average observer. Across psychophysics, electrophysiology, neuroimaging, and computational modeling, data are pooled across participants to estimate lawful relationships between stimuli and responses, and this strategy has delivered most of what we know about contrast sensitivity, color vision, stereopsis, motion, attention, and object recognition. The cost of that success is a systematic blind spot. The variation that pooling discards is not error around a true central tendency; in many domains it is the most diagnostic quantity in the data.

A small, stubborn tradition has made this case for four decades. The individual-differences or covariance paradigm exploits a simple logic: if two stimulus conditions are served by the same underlying mechanism, observers sensitive in one will tend to be sensitive in the other, whereas conditions served by independent mechanisms show uncorrelated sensitivities across observers (de-Wit & Wagemans, 2015; Mollon, Bosten, Peterzell, & Webster, 2017; Peterzell, 2016; Peterzell, Werner, & Kaplan, 1991 , 1993 , 1995; Wilmer, 2008). Submitting a battery of measurements to correlational and factor-analytic analysis therefore recovers the number and tuning of the mechanisms that constitute human vision. Applied to the contrast sensitivity function, this method repeatedly recovers a few frequency-tuned factors rather than one global dimension, in infants and adults alike (Min & Reynaud, 2024 , 2026; Peterzell & Elliott, 2026; Peterzell & Teller, 1996 , 2000; Peterzell, Werner, & Kaplan, 1993). Applied to color appearance, it recovers multiple narrowly tuned chromatic factors that overturn the simple two-axis opponent model (Emery, Volbrecht, Peterzell, & Webster, 2017a , 2023). Applied to stereopsis and orientation, it recovers tuned disparity and orientation channels (Llamas-Cornejo, Peterzell, & Serrano-Pedraza, 2025; Peterzell, Enongue, Serrano-Pedraza, & Read, 2026; Peterzell, Serrano-Pedraza, Widdall, & Read, 2017). Applied to luminance contrast across orientation, it recovers a small number of broadly tuned orientation factors rather than the many narrow channels inferred from masking (Peterzell, Bachtoula, Llamas-Cornejo, Martín-García, & Serrano-Pedraza, 2026).

Applied to face recognition, it recovers a specific, highly heritable ability dissociable from general intelligence (Wilmer et al., 2010; Wilmer, 2017).

I take that body of work as established and ask the question that comes after it. Once stable latent dimensions of visual performance have been identified, what kind of thing have we found? The default answer, "a latent factor," is a statement about statistics, not about vision. It tells us that several measures covary; it does not tell us what an individual observer's position on that dimension is. This article proposes a more committal answer. Reproducible dimensions of visual variation should be treated as candidate computational phenotypes: stable, measurable patterns of visual information processing that characterize individual observers. The proposal is not a renaming. It is a claim about explanatory level, an evidentiary standard that most latent factors will fail, and a research program that follows from taking the claim seriously.

It is worth saying at the outset what this buys and for whom, because the proposal is easy to mistake for bookkeeping. The claim is that the pattern of differences among observers is a measuring instrument for the hidden architecture of vision, and that instrument serves three very different audiences. To a clinician, it turns a single number into a profile: because disease rarely lowers every mechanism equally, deviation from the normal pattern can reveal it before any single value becomes abnormal. To a basic scientist, it offers a second, non-invasive line of evidence about mechanism, recovering how many processes underlie a function and how they are tuned, and adjudicating between competing models by which one predicts the observed pattern of correlations. To an applied scientist, it offers stable, measurable traits that predict realworld visual performance and let displays, selection, and training be built for the real range of human vision rather than an average eye. Section 9 works each payoff through in detail; the rest of the paper first builds the case that these dimensions are real and says when one of them earns the name phenotype. None of it requires new instrumentation or new physics, only treating the structure of variation as signal, measuring the right dimensions, and giving each its mechanistic reading.

2. Two disciplines: differential psychology and the study of vision

The proposal has a long lineage, and naming it clarifies what is new. Two traditions in psychology have run in parallel for over a century. One is experimental: it manipulates conditions, averages across people, and seeks general laws. The other is correlational, or differential: it measures how people differ and treats the structure of that variation as data. Cronbach (1957), in his presidential address to the American Psychological Association, called these "the two disciplines of scientific psychology" and lamented that they rarely spoke to one another. Vision science has been overwhelmingly an experimental discipline. The present framework is, at bottom, an attempt to bring the correlational discipline fully into it.

The differential tradition began with Galton, whose anthropometric laboratory at the 1884 International Health Exhibition measured the sensory and physical characteristics of thousands of visitors (vision, hearing, reaction time, grip strength) in the first large-scale collection of individual-differences data (Galton, 1883 (Galton, , 1885). Galton's conviction was that variation is not noise but the very thing worth measuring. Spearman (1904) then gave the tradition its central method and its central idea at once: factor analysis, and the proposal that patterns of covariation among scores reveal an underlying latent dimension: general intelligence, g. Thurstone (1938) showed that a single factor was not forced by the data; multiple-factor analysis could recover several "primary mental abilities," establishing that latent structure could be plural. The debate between one factor and many is the direct ancestor of the question this paper asks about vision.

A fourth figure supplies the methodological conscience. Brunswik (1956) argued that psychology should sample its stimuli and conditions as representatively as it samples its people ("representative design") because a process characterized under one narrow set of conditions need not generalize. The warning bears directly on computational phenotypes: a latent dimension recovered from one task battery is a candidate, not a conclusion, until it recurs across representative variation in stimuli and observers. It is, in effect, an early statement of the replication and convergent-validity criteria developed below.

These ideas reached vision science only intermittently. Factor-analytic and individual-differences studies of contrast sensitivity, color, and stereopsis applied Spearman's and Thurstone's machinery to perception (Mollon, Bosten, Peterzell, & Webster, 2017), yet the field never fully adopted the differential tradition's deeper commitment: that the structure of variation is itself a map of the system's architecture. Computational psychiatry, a far younger field, made exactly that commitment and was transformed by it. The argument of this paper is that vision science is now positioned to complete the union Cronbach called for: to treat the latent structure of visual individual differences not as a statistical afterthought but as a description of how visual systems are built, and how they differ.

3. Importing a construct, re-operationalizing it for vision

The phrase is not mine to coin. "Computational phenotype" originates in computational psychiatry, introduced by Montague, Dolan, Friston, and Dayan (2012) and given its canonical operational form by Patzelt, Hartley, and Gershman (2018), who define it as a set of parameters, derived from behavioral and neural data, that characterize an individual's cognitive mechanisms, a representation of each person as a point in a continuous parameter space. The construct now has a maturing psychometrics: Schurr, Reznik, Hillman, Bhui, and Gershman (2024) examine the temporal stability and reliability of computational phenotypes directly, which is precisely the property a phenotype must have to be worth the name. In that literature, the parameters come from fitting generative process models (reinforcement-learning rates, prior precisions, decision thresholds) to choice behavior (Huys, Maia, & Frank, 2016; Schwartenbeck & Friston, 2016; Wang & Krystal, 2014; Adams, Huys, & Roiser, 2016). The phrase itself traces to Montague's analyses of interactive economic games, where model-based measures were first used to parse individuals into types (Montague, 2017). What computational psychiatry is, and what it added, bears stating plainly, because the analogy carries real weight here. Computational psychiatry fits mathematical models of information processing (how a person learns from reward, how they weigh evidence, where they set a decision threshold) to that person's behavior, turning clinical description into specific, measurable parameters. Its contribution to psychiatry has been threefold. First, it replaced vague descriptors with quantities one can actually measure in an individual. Second, it gave those quantities a mechanistic reading, tying each to an identifiable process rather than to a symptom label. Third, it described patients along continuous dimensions that cut across the categorical diagnoses (the program formalized as the Research Domain Criteria) instead of sorting them into boxes (Insel et al., 2010; Huys, Maia, & Frank, 2016). Candidly, this contribution has so far been felt more in how the field conceives of disorders, and how it does research, than in routine clinical practice; there is as yet no standard computational-psychiatry bedside test. The lasting gift was a language and a set of units. That is precisely the gift vision science can take. The latent dimensions recovered from individual differences are measurable quantities of the same kind; they can be read mechanistically, as channels, suppression, capacity, or holistic processing; and they describe observers dimensionally, without reference to any diagnostic category. If anything, vision is better positioned than psychiatry to cash the program out, because the mechanisms it must connect to (chromatic and contrast channels, disparity tuning, cone ratios) are far better understood and more directly measurable than the circuits behind mood or thought. What computational psychiatry did for the description of mental disorders, computational phenotyping can do, and perhaps more concretely, for the description of visual systems. I borrow the term deliberately and re-operationalize it for vision. Where computational psychiatry derives phenotypes from the parameters of fitted process models, I derive them from the latent dimensions recovered by factor analysis of individual differences in psychophysical performance. The two recovery methods differ (model-fitting versus covariance analysis), but the target is the same: a low-dimensional, mechanistically interpretable characterization of an individual's information processing, sitting between behavior and biology. That the recovery method can differ without abandoning the construct is in keeping with computational psychiatry itself, whose own theorists now emphasize that it spans distinct explanatory sub-cultures (Kind & Dayan, 2024); a covariance-based recovery method extends the field along an axis it already contains. Finding that low-dimensional structure is, in the field's own terms, its central methodological task-what Dayan, Dolan, Friston, and Montague (2015) call taming complexity through low-dimensional structure-and the field's founders cast computational psychiatry's remit as including the sustenance of healthy function, not only its disorders (Dayan & Montague, 2017), which licenses carrying the same tools to normal visual variation. This reoperationalization is the paper's central novelty, and I state it plainly to forestall the obvious objection that the term is already taken. It is taken; I am extending it to a domain it has not reached, with a recovery method suited to that domain. The move is itself an instance of the cross-field unification the framework is meant to enable: a computational phenotype is a common currency in which psychophysics, computational psychiatry, precision medicine, and NeuroAI can trade. The two routes can even be stacked: computational psychiatry has itself begun to factor-analyse fitted parameters, recovering a general "decision acuity" dimension across decision-making tasks that is distinct from IQ and stable over time (Moutoussis et al., 2021). What distinguishes the present proposal is its object-psychophysical performance itself rather than model parameters-and its domain, in which, unlike decision-making, no strong general factor governs the measures. A recent study makes the model-fitting branch concrete in a neurodegenerative setting: Monteverdi et al. (2026) build individual brain "digital twins" of patients with mild cognitive impairment, deriving subject-specific excitation/inhibition parameters from a generative neural-mass model and combining them with electroencephalographic measures to explain more than 90% of the variance in episodic memory -parameter-level computational phenotyping of exactly this kind, recovered from a clinical rather than a perceptual population.

To my knowledge, computational phenotype has not previously been used in this psychophysical, factor-analytic sense, though two cognate usages should be distinguished from it. The term perceptual phenotype has been applied to synesthesia and schizophrenia under a predictive-coding account, in which inter-individual differences are attributed to the precision weighting of prior beliefs against sensory evidence (van Leeuwen et al., 2021). And individual differences in vision have already been proposed as endophenotypes, including in work I coauthored (Mollon, Bosten, Peterzell, & Webster, 2017). The present proposal differs from the first in method (covariance and factor analysis rather than Bayesian model-fitting) and from the second in scope, as the next section makes precise.

A useful gloss from the philosophy of computational psychiatry describes computational phenotypes as "precise, low-dimensional characterizations of individual cognitive dispositions at an intermediate level of description." That intermediate level is the whole point, and the next section makes it precise.

4. One factor, several roles

A single recovered factor is not one kind of thing. Depending on the evidence it has earned, the same dimension of visual variation can be read in several ways: as a latent factor, as an indicator of an underlying mechanism, as a computational phenotype, as an endophenotype, and as a diagnostic biomarker. These are not synonyms, and they are not a single ladder. They are distinct roles, each a separate claim, each licensed by a different kind of evidence, and each carrying its own advantage. One factor may wear several of these hats at once, but it earns each one separately, and a central discipline of the field is knowing which hats a given factor has earned and which it has not. The roles are summarized in Table 1 and distinguished below; Figure 1 places them on a single explanatory ladder, from observed behavior to neural implementation, and previews how a latent factor earns the further status of a phenotype. The point of drawing the distinctions sharply is not to fence the phenotype off from its neighbors but the reverse, to see clearly which description a given factor has earned, because the characteristic error in this literature is to wear a hat one has not earned, treating a bare factor as a proven mechanism or a correlate as a validated biomarker.

As a latent factor. At minimum, every recovered factor is a latent factor, a statistical summary of covariance structure (Spearman, 1904; Thurstone, 1938). It describes how measures relate; it does not, by itself, explain anything. Factors can arise from measurement artifacts, task-specific strategies, or peculiarities of a single dataset. A computational phenotype is an interpreted construct that must clear a higher bar (reliability, replication, predictive validity, mechanistic relevance) before a factor earns the title (Section 6). Not every factor is a phenotype, just as not every gene-expression pattern is biologically informative until it is tied to mechanism and consequence. The relation is one of candidacy: exploratory factor analysis proposes; replication and the criteria dispose.

As a window on a mechanism.

A factor can also serve as evidence for a latent mechanism (chromatic opponency, contrast gain control, binocular integration, facial-identity coding), a theoretical entity, a process the visual system is hypothesized to perform. The mechanism is not the phenotype: the mechanism is the posited process, whereas a computational phenotype is measurable, an individual observer's stable position with respect to that process. The distinction mirrors the difference between a mechanism and a phenotype in biology, or between a gene and a genotype. L-M cone opponency is a mechanism; an individual's location on an L-M chromatic dimension, constrained in part by that person's relative numbers of long-and middle-wavelength cones (Gunther & Dobkins, 2002), is a measurable phenotype. The phenotype is where the mechanism becomes a property of a person. This role is not automatic, since a factor can equally reflect a shared task demand or a method artifact and point to no mechanism at all, which is why mechanistic relevance must be earned by convergence with independent evidence.

As an endophenotype.

A factor rises to an endophenotype, in the sense imported into psychiatry by Gottesman and Gould (2003), only when it additionally satisfies five criteria: association with illness in the population, heritability, state-independence, co-segregation with illness within families, and elevated rates in unaffected relatives. These conditions tie the construct to genetics and disease. The bare computational phenotype carries no such requirements. Some computational phenotypes may turn out to be heritable (face recognition is a strong candidate; Wilmer et al., 2010) or disease-linked, and those that are become candidate visual endophenotypes, a valuable special case. Individual differences in vision have, in fact, already been framed this way, including in work I co-authored (Mollon, Bosten, Peterzell, & Webster, 2017); and the endophenotype is itself carefully distinguished from the intermediate phenotype and the biomarker (Lenzenweger, 2013). But the broader construct is agnostic about heritability and applies equally to healthy variation, development, and artificial systems. Endophenotype is the narrower, more demanding concept; computational phenotype is the genus.

As a biomarker.

Finally, a factor can be pressed into service as a biomarker: in the FDA-NIH framework, an objectively measured indicator of a biological or pathogenic process, typically a molecule, an image, or a physiological signal. A computational phenotype is a model-level or latent construct estimated from behavior, not a substance assayed in tissue. The two can converge (a computational phenotype with a neural correlate begins to function biomarker-like), but they are different kinds of thing. The biomarker role is distinctive in one respect: it can be earned mechanism-agnostically, since a dimension can be a validated diagnostic predictor with no accepted mechanistic reading, just as it can be a clean computational phenotype with no established clinical target. Conflating the two would promise a clinical objectivity the framework has not yet earned.

The unifying frame for all four distinctions is Marr's (1982) levels of analysis. Observed behavior sits at the level of performance; biological implementation sits at the level of neurons and circuits; the computational phenotype sits between them, at the algorithmic and representational level, describing how an individual's visual system encodes, transforms, and interprets information. Crucially, a computational phenotype need not map one-to-one onto a neural structure. The same phenotype could be realized by different implementations, just as similar behavior can arise from different circuits. Its identity is fixed not by anatomical localization but by reproducibility, explanatory value, and relationship to visual computation.

Consistent with this intermediate status, individual differences in visual-test performance can correlate only weakly with electrophysiological measures even when those measures are highly reliable within a person, which suggests the algorithmic-level phenotype is not a simple readout of any single neural signal (Garobbio, Lin, Kunchulia, & Herzog, 2025). These roles are best seen at work in a single case. Contrast sensitivity, the most studied dimension of spatial vision, can wear all five hats, showing how each is earned separately.

Exploratory factor analyses of contrast sensitivity across spatial frequency recover several frequency-tuned factors (Billock & Harding, 1996; Dobkins, Gunther, & Peterzell, 2000; Min & Reynaud, 2024; Peterzell, 2016; Peterzell, Chang, & Teller, 2000; Peterzell, Dougherty, & Mayer, 1997; Peterzell & Kelly, 1997; Peterzell, Schefrin, Tregear, & Werner, 2000; Peterzell & Teller, 1996 , 2000; Peterzell, Werner, & Kaplan, 1991 , 1993 , 1995; Simpson & McFadden, 2005), which are latent factors by construction. They are mechanism-indicative, and unusually so, because they separate into different kinds of mechanism: the low-and middle-frequency factors behave like retinal and neural channels, while the highest-frequency factor behaves like an optical process, the eye's own aberrations and focus. Adaptive optics can test that dissociation directly, by correcting the optics and asking which factor survives (Peterzell & Elliott, 2026).

Read as stable, individual-level, mechanistically interpretable traits, both the neural and the optical factors are computational phenotypes, where computational is meant in the algorithmic, information-processing sense and includes the optical transfer stage rather than implying a cortical locus. The same factors are candidate endophenotypes: behavioral-genetic studies show contrast sensitivity is heritable, with twin estimates between roughly 14% and 38% that vary across spatial frequency, and the authors of that study proposed contrast sensitivity itself as a possible endophenotype for Alzheimer's disease, which erodes the low frequencies, and for optic neuritis, which erodes the middle ones (Cronin-Golomb et al., 2007). A twin study in young adults converges on a similar estimate (h² ≈ 0.27), indicating that the heritability is established by early adulthood rather than being a feature of aging (Haak, 2019). The optical factor has heritable support of its own: macular pigment optical density-one of the pre-receptoral filters that factor indexes-is itself highly heritable in twins (Liew et al., 2005). What is still missing is heritability estimated for the factor-analytic channels specifically and shown in unaffected relatives, so the endophenotype hat fits as a candidate rather than a settled claim. Finally, the factors serve as biomarkers, but always for a named target: the low-frequency factor for Alzheimer's, the middle-frequency factor for optic neuritis and multiple sclerosis, the optical high-frequency factor for refractive and media changes, with further uses in amblyopia and glaucoma. These disease associations are established for overall contrast sensitivity; assigning each loss to a specific factor is a prediction of the framework rather than a settled result. A single global contrast-sensitivity score is a weak and non-specific biomarker precisely because it moves for all of these reasons at once; the factor profile is what lets a biomarker be for something in particular. Contrast sensitivity is, in this sense, the cleanest illustration of the general claim: one factor, several roles, each earned by its own evidence and each carrying its own use.

5. Candidate computational phenotypes across vision science

If the construct is to be more than a definition, the field must already contain plausible instances, and they should come from many laboratories, not one. They do. The examples below span lowlevel sensitivity, mid-level coding, high-level recognition, capacity, and control; most were established by groups other than ours, and the strongest were not built with this framework in mind.

Spatial vision.

Factor analyses of contrast sensitivity across spatial frequency recover several frequency-tuned factors rather than one global dimension, in adults and infants and across laboratories and methods (Min & Reynaud, 2024 , 2026; Peterzell & Teller, 1996 , 2000). The recovered factor-channels fall close to the spatial-frequency channels estimated by separate masking experiments, the cross-method convergence a genuine phenotype should produce, and the same frequency-tuned structure recurs in clinical populations, with comparable channels recovered in amblyopic and fellow eyes (Reynaud & Min, 2023).

Color. Individual differences in hue scaling do not collapse onto two cardinal opponent axes; factor analysis recovers a larger set of narrowly tuned chromatic factors (Emery et al., 2017a), and large, stable differences in color appearance recur in other laboratories' data, most vividly in the population-splitting response to "the dress" (Lafer-Sousa, Hermann, & Conway, 2015;

Webster & MacLeod, 1988). Individual differences have themselves become a recognized tool for dissecting chromatic mechanisms (Bosten, 2022). These chromatic differences are partly heritable: in twin data, monozygotic pairs reconstruct near-identical individual colour spaces while dizygotic pairs and siblings diverge, with the residual "variation beyond common genetic inheritance" traced to peripheral and cognitive rather than photopigment sources (Paramei, Bimler, & Mislavskaia, 2004; Bimler & Kirkland, 2004)-placing colour appearance among the candidate chromatic endophenotypes. As with face recognition, this heritability has so far outrun its molecular basis: genotype-phenotype studies link chromatic and luminance traits to opsingene variation (Lawrance-Owen, Bosten, Hogg, Bargary, Goodbourn, & Mollon, 2014), yet genome-wide association has not pinned robust loci for normal colour individual differences.

Stereopsis.

Factor analysis of disparity-corrugation thresholds recovers spatial-frequency-tuned stereoscopic mechanisms (Llamas-Cornejo, Peterzell, & Serrano-Pedraza, 2025; Peterzell, Serrano-Pedraza, Widdall, & Read, 2017; Reynaud & Hess, 2017), and independent population studies of binocular function recover comparable stereo-acuity dimensions (Bosten et al., 2015).

Mid-level and Gestalt organization. Individual differences extend to mid-level vision, where they can be measured with purpose-built instruments. The Leuven Embedded Figures Test isolates individual differences in perceptual grouping and figure embedding (de-Wit et al., 2017), and intelligence and executive function together account for less than a tenth of its variance, so it indexes a specific perceptual ability rather than general cognition (Huygelier et al., 2018). A companion clinical battery, the Leuven Perceptual Organization Screening Test, shows that these mid-level abilities can be measured reliably and applied in patient populations (Vancleef et al., 2015).

Face recognition.

The most fully characterized candidate comes from outside the spatial-vision tradition. Face-recognition ability, measured with instruments such as the Cambridge Face Memory Test, is reliable, replicates across samples and cultures, and is dissociable from both general intelligence and object recognition (Wilmer et al., 2010; Wilmer, Germine, & Nakayama, 2014; Zhu et al., 2010). Its internal structure is layered rather than monolithic: a general face-processing factor coexists with partly dissociable specific abilities across tests of face memory, matching, and perception (Verhallen et al., 2017a). It is strongly heritable: a twin study of more than two thousand individuals estimated heritability near 61%, with multivariate analysis showing the genetic influence on face recognition to be largely unique, not shared with object recognition or general cognitive ability (Shakeshaft & Plomin, 2015). The heritability is, for now, better established than its molecular basis, since candidate-gene and genome-wide associations remain weak or null (Verhallen et al., 2014 (Verhallen et al., , 2017b)), a reminder that a phenotype can be robustly heritable without yet being tied to specific loci. Its developmental trajectory peaks only after age 30, dissociably from general cognition (Germine, Duchaine, & Nakayama, 2011), and its tails define developmental prosopagnosia and "super-recognizers." Few constructs in vision science satisfy so many of the criteria below.

Object recognition. A domain-general object-recognition ability, denoted o, is recovered as a latent factor by structural equation modeling and is distinct from general intelligence (Richler, Wilmer, & Gauthier, 2017; Richler et al., 2019). It generalizes across object categories and even sensory modalities (the same latent ability predicts auditory object recognition), and a recent review treats it as a candidate ability construct in its own right (Chow, Palmeri, Pluck, & Gauthier, 2023; Smithson & Gauthier, 2026).

Visual working memory.

The number of items an observer can hold in visual working memory is a stable, highly reliable individual-differences trait (Vogel & Machizawa, 2004; Xu, Adam, Fang, & Vogel, 2018) with a direct neural index (the contralateral delay activity) that rises with load and asymptotes at each person's capacity. Capacity (number) and resolution are dissociable parameters (Awh, Barton, & Vogel, 2007), and it is number, not resolution, that drives the relationship between visual working memory and fluid intelligence (Fukuda, Vogel, Mayr, & Awh, 2010).

Motion and attention: the instructive partial cases.

Two further candidates matter precisely because they do not pass cleanly. The efficiency of spatial suppression of large moving stimuli correlates with measured intelligence (Melnick, Harrison, Park, Bennetto, & Tadin, 2013), apparently indexing automatic suppression of irrelevant background motion, but the effect replicates at reduced magnitude, and an analogous contrast-suppression version fails to replicate (Arranz-Paraíso & Serrano-Pedraza, 2018). Visual attention, in turn, does not behave as a single dimension at all: individual-differences batteries find distinct attentional abilities to be largely independent (Huang, Mo, & Li, 2012), fractionating into several weakly correlated functional clusters (Skogsberg et al., 2015). The same fragmentation appears developmentally: factor analysis of infant visual attention recovers separate habituation and sensitization factors rather than one attentional dimension (Peterzell, 1993). These cases preview a point the criteria will make explicit: not every latent dimension earns the title.

Reliance on priors: a higher-level candidate. The candidates so far are sensory, channels for spatial frequency, color, disparity, and motion. The framework does not stop at the sensory periphery, and individual differences can in principle index higher, more computational variables, among them how strongly an observer's percept is shaped by prior expectations when the incoming evidence is ambiguous. Tulver, Aru, Rutiku, and Bachmann (2019) measured this across four tasks in which perception departs from the stimulus, including Mooney face recognition, illusory contours, the recognition of blurred words, and representational momentum.

The individual task scores were reliable, but they did not collapse into a single "reliance on priors" dimension: factor analysis favored two factors over one, together accounting for about half the variance and dividing along the hierarchy of processing rather than expressing one general trait. The lesson cuts two ways. It shows that the method reaches above the sensory periphery to inference-level constructs, where some of the most theoretically interesting phenotypes should live; but it also shows that an inference-level candidate can have reliable parts and still fail to cohere into one dimension. Reliance on priors therefore belongs on the list as a promising candidate rather than a promoted phenotype, pending replication in larger, adequately powered samples.

Population-scale structure. The broadest test to date analyzed twenty-five visual and auditory measures in over a thousand observers and recovered a weak general perceptual factor accounting for only about a fifth of the variance, alongside eight independent, replicable specific factors, among them medium-and low-spatial-frequency sensitivity, stereo acuity, flicker sensitivity, and oculomotor control (Bosten et al., 2017). This "one weak general factor plus several specific factors" architecture, recovered in a single large sample by a laboratory other than ours, is close to what the present framework predicts.

These candidates differ widely in how thoroughly they have been validated. None is automatically a computational phenotype; the next section sets the standard each must meet.

6. Criteria for promoting a factor to a phenotype

The framework's utility depends entirely on the ability to separate meaningful dimensions of visual computation from statistical artifacts. Not every latent factor should be elevated. I propose seven criteria of increasing stringency; a candidate computational phenotype should satisfy as many as the evidence allows.

1. Reliability. Individual differences must be stable across repeated measurement, the precondition for any trait interpretation (cf. Schurr et al., 2024, on the temporal stability of computational phenotypes). The bar can be met in practice: individual differences in visual illusions, for example, are stable across eyes, testing sessions, and measurement methods (Cretenoud, Grzeczkowski, Kunchulia, & Herzog, 2021). The contrast with model-fitting is instructive here: fitted process-model parameters often have poor individual-level testretest reliability (Karvelis, Paulus, & Diaconescu, 2023; Vrizzi et al., 2025), whereas factor-derived phenotypes can inherit the reliability of the psychophysical thresholds they are built from-provided that reliability is demonstrated, not assumed.

2. Replication.

The dimension must recur across independent samples, laboratories, and paradigms, because latent structure is sensitive to task selection and sampling.

3. Convergent validity.

Multiple tasks presumed to engage a common computation should converge on the same dimension.

4. Theoretical coherence. The construct should fit existing models of visual processing and generate testable predictions.

5. External relationship.

It should relate to independent evidence: physiological, neural, genetic, developmental, or clinical.

6. Predictive validity. It should help predict future behavior, learning, adaptation, disease progression, or treatment response.

7. Mechanistic relevance. The strongest phenotypes do not merely describe variation; they contribute to the discovery and revision of theories of visual computation.

This standard is deliberately higher than that for a factor. It is the standard that turns a correlation into a candidate cause. A factor that appears only when a specific mechanism is present, shifts when that mechanism is altered, and disappears when it is removed is not merely correlating; it is behaving as a cause behaves. There is parametric precedent: adding a cone photopigment to transgenic mice produces a correspondingly new factor in the individual-difference structure (reviewed in Peterzell, 2016), and on the human side, optical correction selectively removes an optical factor (Peterzell & Elliott, 2026). Interventions that change a mechanism change the recovered phenotype. That is the behavior of a real thing, not a statistical shadow.

To keep the standard concrete rather than rhetorical, consider a working threshold: a candidate should show test-retest reliability above roughly r = .7, replication in at least two independent samples, convergent validity across at least two tasks or measures, and either established heritability or a validated neural index. The criteria earn their keep only if some candidates fail them. By this standard, face recognition and object-recognition o are promoted; visual working memory capacity is promoted on reliability and neural grounding, with the caveat that it is only partly separable from fluid intelligence; the spatial-frequency, chromatic, and stereoscopic channels are promoted within their domains; the motion-suppression index is borderline, pending stronger replication; and "visual attention," treated as a single ability, is not promoted at all, because it fractionates into weakly correlated components rather than converging on one dimension. That the same procedure both elevates and rejects candidates is what separates a computational phenotype from a latent factor with a new name.

A note on method belongs here, because the two main forms of factor analysis do different jobs, and the difference maps onto this promotion logic directly. Exploratory factor analysis discovers structure: it asks how many factors a battery contains and how each is tuned, with no model specified in advance, and it is how most of the candidate channels surveyed above were first found. A representative case is the scotopic contrast sensitivity function, where exploratory factor analysis of individual differences recovered two channels resembling their photopic counterparts together with a channel tuned to very low spatial frequencies absent from photopic vision, the novel factor offered explicitly as provisional pending confirmation (Peterzell, Schefrin, Tregear, & Werner, 2000; reviewed in Peterzell, 2016). Discovery, though, is fallible.

The number of factors retained, the choice of rotation, and the idiosyncrasies of a single sample all shape the solution, so an exploratory factor can capitalize on chance and may not reappear in the next dataset; it may be a real mechanism or an artifact of one study. This vulnerability is not merely theoretical here: several of the foundational individual-differences studies in vision, including some of my own, rest on modest samples, so scaling these analyses to larger and more diverse cohorts is a precondition of the program rather than an afterthought. Confirmatory factor analysis does the complementary job: it specifies a model in advance, the number of factors and which measures load on each, and tests whether an independent sample fits it. This is how a candidate earns the replication and convergent-validity criteria, because a model of the number and tuning of mechanisms, once exploration has proposed it, becomes a hypothesis a new sample can confirm or reject. The arc from exploratory discovery to confirmatory test is the engine of promotion: exploration proposes a candidate, and confirmation in fresh, ideally pre-registered samples is part of what disposes. Methods that stabilize the exploratory step, such as resampling and visualization for choosing the number and tuning of factors (Min & Reynaud, 2024), and confirmatory logics reached by other routes, such as simulating competing generative mechanisms and asking which reproduces the observed correlation structure (Emery et al., 2023), serve the same end. Confirmation also takes a more basic form: showing that the method recovers structure it was not told to find. Factor analysis of simulated observers assembled from known spatial channels returns those planted channels rather than inventing new ones, and factor analysis of archival color-matching data returns estimates that match values established by wholly independent measurement, among them macular and lens pigment density, the spectral positions of the cone photopigments, and rod intrusion (reviewed in Peterzell, 2016; see also MacLeod & Webster, 1988; Webster & MacLeod, 1988). A procedure that recovers independently known quantities where they exist is the one to trust where they do not. The standing caution, long noted in this literature, is that exploratory and confirmatory analyses, and the related principal-component analysis, can yield different solutions, so the method and its criteria should be stated explicitly rather than left for the reader to infer (Tulver, 2019).

7. Worked examples: color appearance and stereopsis

To show the framework doing work rather than only defining terms, consider one case carried through the criteria of Section 6: individual differences in color appearance, and specifically in hue scaling.

When observers report the perceived proportions of red, green, blue, and yellow in stimuli sampling the hue circle, their settings vary widely but systematically. Emery et al. (2017a) submitted hue-scaling functions to exploratory factor analysis and found that inter-observer variation is governed not by the two cardinal opponent axes a red-green / blue-yellow model predicts, but by a larger set of narrowly tuned factors, each confined to a local region of the hue circle, with the "redness" of orange, red, and purple loading on separate factors rather than a single red process. Consider how this candidate fares against the criteria.

Reliability and replication (criteria 1-2).

The factor structure recurs across new and previously published hue-scaling datasets and across the hue circle, rather than appearing in a single sample.

Convergent validity (criterion 3).

The same narrow-band organization emerges at the cortical level in steady-state visual evoked potentials (Kaneko et al., 2025), so a structure first seen in appearance judgments converges with an independent physiological measure; it also recurs across related chromatic tasks, linking hue scaling to color naming (Emery et al., 2017b) and appearing again in the factor structure of simultaneous color and brightness contrast (Kaneko, Murakami, Kuriki, & Peterzell, 2018).

Theoretical coherence and external relationship (criteria 4-5).

The recovered factors are coherent with a population code of multiple narrowly tuned chromatic mechanisms, and chromatic individual differences connect to known biological variation, including the relative numbers of long-and middle-wavelength cones across observers (Gunther & Dobkins, 2002).

Mechanistic relevance and discrimination (criteria 6-7).

Here the example earns its keep, and the logic turns confirmatory. Emery et al. (2023) submitted both hue and motion-direction judgments to the same task and the same analysis, then asked which generative model reproduces each one's inter-observer correlation structure. For hue, factor analysis recovered nine narrow, locally tuned factors accounting for 76% of the variance, each confined to a small arc of the hue circle, and matching a simulated narrow-band code rather than the cardinal-opponent or broadband alternatives, which predict the broad, circle-spanning correlations the data reject. For motion direction, the identical method recovered just three broad, periodic factors organized around the cardinal axes of visual space. The contrast is itself the result: nine narrow factors for hue, three global ones for motion. The latent structure therefore not only adjudicates between competing mechanistic theories of color but, read across the two attributes, reveals that hue and motion are represented by different logic: hue locally, with no privileged axes; motion globally, anchored to a spatial reference frame, plausibly because motion direction has an external spatial ground truth that vision must preserve to guide action while hue has none (Emery et al., 2023).

The same individual-differences method thus reads out not merely different numbers of mechanisms but different representational principles, which is the strongest sense in which a computational phenotype can be mechanistically informative.

A latent factor, by itself, would have told us only that hue judgments covary in a structured way.

Treated as a candidate computational phenotype (reliable, replicated, physiologically convergent, theoretically coherent, and able to discriminate among generative models), the same structure becomes a measurable, individual-level signature that overturns a textbook model of color appearance. That is the difference the framework is meant to make. The case could be sharpened further by intervention, the strongest evidence that a recovered factor behaves like a cause: correcting the eye's optics with adaptive optics selectively removes an optical factor from the contrast-sensitivity structure while leaving the retinal-neural factors intact (Peterzell & Elliott, 2026).

Box 1. Stereopsis as a second computational phenotype.

Binocular depth perception offers a second, independent demonstration. When observers detect sinusoidal depth corrugations defined by binocular disparity, their disparity-sensitivity functions vary across people, and exploratory factor analysis of that variation recovers two stereoscopic mechanisms tuned for spatial frequency: one loading on low (below ~0.4 c/deg) and one on high (~0.8 c/deg) corrugation frequencies, each with nearly identical tuning for horizontal and vertical patterns (Peterzell, Serrano-Pedraza, Widdall, & Read, 2017). This two-component structure has since been independently replicated in a new sample, and shown to be organized by retinal rather than relative, object-based spatial frequency (Peterzell, Enongue, Serrano-Pedraza, & Read, 2026), the cross-sample replication (criterion 2) a stable phenotype requires. A complementary analysis across orientations recovers orientation-selective disparity mechanisms within the same individual-differences framework (Llamas-Cornejo, Peterzell, & Serrano-Pedraza, 2025).

This candidate is instructive precisely because its value differs from that of the color case. Its first strength is convergence: the factor-analytic disparity channels recovered from individual differences correspond to channels independently identified by masking and adaptation, so two methods that share no assumptions agree on the same latent structure, exactly the cross-method coherence (criterion 3) a genuine computational phenotype should show. Its second strength is dissociation: the factors governing stereoscopic sensitivity are statistically independent of those governing luminance contrast (Serrano-Pedraza, Boegaerts, Read, & Peterzell, 2018 , 2026), so depth and contrast sensitivity are separable computational phenotypes measured in the same observers rather than facets of one general visual ability, the discriminant validity the framework predicts and the weak-general-factor literature requires.

Where color overturns a model, stereopsis shows that distinct phenotypes can be cleanly factored apart within a single visual system. Two phenotypes, recovered by the same method and behaving as independent dimensions, are exactly the structured, multi-factor architecture that a computational atlas of vision would aim to map.

8. The hardest objection: vision may have no common factor

The framework must confront its strongest empirical challenge directly, because evasion would be fatal. A respected line of work argues that vision has no common factor at all: across batteries of qualitatively different visual tasks, performance is reliable within each task yet only weakly correlated between tasks, and stable across years rather than reducible to noise (Cappe, Clarke, Mohr, & Herzog, 2014; Garobbio, Kunchulia, & Herzog, 2024; Herzog, Garobbio, Pfau, & Scholl, 2025). The pattern is robust across populations, holding in healthy older adults (Shaqiri et al., 2019) and in people with mild cognitive impairment (Garobbio, Pilz, Kunchulia, & Herzog, 2022); it appears even within a single putative mechanism, since different tasks all labeled "magnocellular" share little variance across observers, so that folk category fractionates too (Goodbourn et al., 2012). Reviews reach the same verdict: the search for a general visual ability analogous to g has largely failed, and perception fractionates into many narrow factors (de-Wit & Wagemans, 2015; Tulver, 2019). Surveying this literature from an independent vantage, Tulver (2019) observes that whatever general factor these studies extract accounts for only about 20% to 34% of the variance, far short of the roughly 50% a genuine g-like factor commands in the study of intelligence, so the monolithic reading of vision is difficult to sustain.

A sharper version of the objection has since emerged from hierarchical modelling. When triallevel measurement error is modelled explicitly rather than left in the scores, weak common factors can be recovered where raw correlations show almost none: a general susceptibility-toillusions factor accounting for only about a fifth of the variance (Mehrvarz, Popat, & Rouder, 2026), and, in magnitude-discrimination batteries that shade toward general intelligence, a larger sensory-discrimination factor (Rouder & Mehrvarz, 2026). This refines the picture rather than overturning it. The recovered general variance stays weak wherever the measures are mechanism-specific and grows only as tasks approach g; the frequency-, chromatic-, and disparity-tuned channels this paper concerns are precisely the mechanism-specific regime in which it stays small. Converging neural evidence points the same way: steady-state visual evoked potential and psychophysical estimates of contrast sensitivity, acuity, and orientation discrimination are each reliable yet uncorrelated with one another, so the fractionation is not an artifact of attention or motivation (Morea, Garobbio, Kunchulia, & Herzog, 2026). The honest statement is therefore not that vision has no general factor, but that it has no strong one: at most a weak common overlay on a structured population of narrow, mechanism-specific factors.

This is sometimes read as bad news for any latent-variable program in vision. In fact it is the program's premise. The framework does not require or predict a single dominant visual dimension; it predicts the opposite-a structured, multi-factor architecture in which functions vary partly independently. The weak between-task correlations that defeat a general factor are entirely consistent with the robust within-function covariation the individual-differences method exploits to recover frequency-tuned, chromatic, and disparity-tuned channels. The large-sample evidence fits: Bosten et al. (2017), analyzing 25 measures from over a thousand participants, found one weak general factor (about a fifth of the variance) alongside roughly eight independent, replicable, task-specific factors-many narrow phenotypes, no governing g. So the no-common-factor literature does not refute the framework; it calibrates it, showing that computational phenotypes in vision will be narrow and mechanism-specific, not broad and general. A framework promising a single visual phenotype would be refuted by these data; one predicting a structured population of narrow phenotypes is corroborated by them.

9. What computational phenotyping is for

A framework should pay rent. What does treating latent visual dimensions as computational phenotypes let us do that we could not do before? Four things.

It tells us how many mechanisms vision has, and how they are tuned. Averaging across observers gives the typical sensitivity at each spatial frequency, color, or disparity, but it cannot reveal how many independent processes produced that sensitivity, because averaging discards the very covariation that carries that information. The individual-differences method recovers it.

If two conditions are served by the same mechanism, observers who are sensitive in one tend to be sensitive in the other; if they are served by different mechanisms, their sensitivities vary independently across people. The pattern of correlations is therefore a readout of the system's parts. This is not a promissory note: the method has already shown that the contrast sensitivity function is built from several spatial-frequency-tuned channels rather than one, that color appearance rests on nine narrow chromatic mechanisms rather than two opponent axes, and that stereopsis has at least two spatial-frequency-tuned disparity channels. In each case a count and a tuning, facts about the architecture of vision, were obtained non-invasively, from behavior, in a way no amount of averaging could provide.

It turns clinical vision testing from a single number into a profile.

Most clinical tests reduce a person's vision to one value (an acuity, a threshold) and compare it to a cutoff, collapsing a multidimensional system to a point and discarding its shape. A phenotypic approach instead locates a patient on each underlying dimension, producing a profile, or fingerprint, across mechanisms. This matters because disease rarely lowers everything uniformly; it changes the shape of the profile, and often a characteristic shape. A patient can fall within the normal range on every individual test yet sit well outside the normal pattern, so deviation from the normative phenotype can flag disease earlier than any single threshold crossing. This is the same move that normative modeling has formalized in precision psychiatry, where an individual's deviation from a population reference model becomes a personalized index of risk (Rutherford et al., 2022).

Two conditions that both reduce acuity may have different phenotypic signatures, aiding differential diagnosis. And because the relevant dimension can be followed over time, progression and treatment response can be measured on the specific mechanism a disease attacks, a more sensitive endpoint than a global score. The reorganization of specific spatial channels with age is a worked instance of exactly this kind of profile change (Min & Reynaud, 2024 , 2026).

It tells us which tests to give, and what they mean.

If the real dimensions of visual variation are known, a battery can be designed to measure them efficiently, a compact "phenotype panel" that pins down a person's position on every important dimension without the redundancy of overlapping tests, and that can be made adaptive, measuring only the dimensions a given question requires. Just as important, knowing which mechanism a test loads on tells a clinician or researcher what an abnormal score means mechanistically, rather than only that it is below normal. The framework thus offers tests that are both more efficient and more interpretable.

It makes treatment and training specific to the individual. If a person's profile shows which mechanism is weak, intervention can target that mechanism: perceptual learning or rehabilitation aimed at the deficient channel, correction tailored to the individual's optics, or a therapy chosen because it moves the relevant dimension. The same logic supplies the test of whether a treatment works: whether it shifts the targeted phenotype while leaving the others intact-achievable in principle, as the adaptive-optics case above shows, where correction removes a specific optical dimension from the contrast-sensitivity profile while leaving the neural dimensions unchanged (Peterzell & Elliott, 2026).

These payoffs are not hypothetical, and the applied turn is already underway in the very research program that produced one of the worked examples above. Building directly on the hue-scaling phenotypes recovered by Emery et al. (2023), Simoncelli and Webster (2025) developed a method for correcting images to an individual's own color appearance: each pixel is mapped through the average observer's hue-scaling function and then through the inverse of the individual's function, so that physically adjusted images elicit matching hue percepts across observers who differ in how they see color. The intended uses are squarely practical, including data visualization, color communication, display calibration, and embedding an artist's or designer's appearance profile so that others see colors as the creator does; and because the correction requires only measured hue percepts rather than spectral-sensitivity assessment, it can be implemented on ordinary displays. The authors are careful to present this as an in-principle demonstration rather than a finished product, but it is exactly the payoff the phenotype framing anticipates: a stable, individual-level signature of color appearance, measured once and put to work. The same group has carried individual-difference color science into other applied questions, from how warm and cool colors are organized in appearance (Manalansan, Whitehead, & Webster, 2025) and their consequences for visual search (Manalansan, Simoncelli, & Webster, 2025) to the efficacy of color-enhancing filters for normal trichromats (Simoncelli, McPherson, Knoblauch, & Webster, 2025). A phenotype, once measured, becomes something a designer or an engineer can use.

Taken together, these are the practical content of the framework: a way to count and characterize the mechanisms of vision, to describe an individual's vision as a structured profile rather than a single number, to build tests that measure what matters, and to aim treatment at the part of the system that is actually affected. The next section develops the clinical and developmental case in detail; the section after places the whole enterprise in its largest frame.

10. Development, aging, and disorders as perturbations of phenotypic structure

The framework's most attractive feature is that it supplies a common language across populations that are usually studied in isolation. Instead of cataloging deficits, it asks one question everywhere: how is the structure of visual computation organized, and how does it differ?

Development becomes the emergence and stabilization of computational phenotypes. Some may be present early, reflecting genetic and developmental constraints; others emerge through experience. The balance between genetic constraint and experiential plasticity is itself organized across the visual hierarchy: the heritability of resting-state functional connectivity falls from early to mid-level visual cortex and then rises again, so different levels of the system remain open to experience to different degrees (Haak & Beckmann, 2019). Longitudinal designs can track how latent visual mechanisms differentiate over time. Two developmental models, one holding each spatial channel fixed in scale as it gains sensitivity and one in which the channels also shift toward higher spatial frequencies with age, fit the averaged infant contrast sensitivity data about equally well; the covariance structure of individual differences breaks the tie, reproducing the infant factor structure only under the shifting-channel account (Peterzell, Werner, & Kaplan, 1991 , 1993; reviewed in Peterzell, 2016).

Aging becomes a reorganization rather than a simple decline. Traditional studies document agerelated losses in contrast sensitivity, color, motion, and stereopsis (Owsley, 2011; Owsley, Sekuler, & Siemsen, 1983), but a factor-analytic treatment of the same domain reframes the question. Applying factor analysis of individual differences to spatial vision, Min and Reynaud (2024, 2026) recover distinct age-related reorganizations in the spatial channels for luminance and texture processing; that is, aging alters the weighting and interaction of latent visual mechanisms rather than uniformly lowering sensitivity. The relevant question therefore shifts from which abilities decline to how the architecture of visual computation is reorganized across the lifespan, which is also the natural way to separate optical, retinal, and cortical contributions to age-related change.

Schizophrenia presents well-documented visual anomalies: altered contrast gain control, reduced surround suppression, abnormal motion processing and perceptual organization, and oculomotor differences (Butler, Silverstein, & Dakin, 2008; Silverstein & Keane, 2011; Yang et al., 2013). A phenotypic approach asks whether the disorder alters the latent architecture of visual processing itself, and computational modeling of contrast sensitivity in schizophrenia already moves in this direction (Silverstein, Demmin, & Bednar, 2017). One visual measure has been carried furthest down this path. Performance on visual backward masking is reliable, separates patients from controls, and shows intermediate deficits in unaffected first-degree relatives, the signature of a heritable endophenotype (Chkonia et al., 2010; Herzog, Roinishvili, Chkonia, & Brand, 2013); a genome-wide analysis found no single locus of large effect but did find that polygenic risk for schizophrenia tracks masking performance, so the genetic grounding is real but diffuse (Shaqiri et al., 2022). A masking deficit thus behaves as a clinical computational phenotype that meets several criteria at once: reliability, replication, a clinical relationship, and partial, polygenic heritability.

Autism involves differences in local-global processing, motion, face processing, and sensory integration, with marked heterogeneity across individuals (Happé & Frith, 2006; Mottron, Dawson, Soulières, Hubert, & Burack, 2006; Robertson & Baron-Cohen, 2017), and Bayesian accounts frame these as altered priors (Pellicano & Burr, 2012). Heterogeneity, a nuisance under the deficit model, becomes information under the phenotypic one: autism may comprise multiple computational profiles rather than a single pattern.

Neurodegeneration, including Parkinson's, Alzheimer's, and posterior cortical atrophy (Crutch et al., 2012), can be conceptualized as perturbation of computational phenotypes, with longitudinal factor analysis, but the move is the framework's own: an individual is located on a small set of mechanistically interpretable dimensions, and disease is read as a shift in that profile. That the excitation/inhibition disruption is not an artifact of one modeling pipeline is reassuring: an independent multiscale inversion of resting-state fMRI recovers the same progressive E/I imbalance from cognitively normal through mild cognitive impairment to Alzheimer's disease, concentrated-as in the digital-twin analysis-in limbic and cingulate regions (Li et al., 2025).

The payoff is a reduced reliance on diagnostic labels, which group together individuals who differ in underlying biology and behavior. Characterizing observers by the structure of their visual computation, rather than their diagnostic status, aligns the framework with dimensional models of brain function and precision medicine (Robinson, 2012; Houle, Govindaraju, & Omholt, 2010).

11. Toward a Computational Atlas of Human Vision

If computational phenotypes are the units, the long-term goal is the map. I propose that vision science work toward a Computational Atlas of Human Vision: an organized description of the major dimensions of visual computation, their relationships, their developmental trajectories, their neural substrates, and their variability across individuals and populations. Its fundamental units would not be psychophysical measures but computational phenotypes, each supported by converging evidence from psychophysics, neuroscience, development, genetics, modeling, or clinical research. The analogy is to the genome or connectome: progress depended on moving from isolated findings to systematic characterization of an entire domain.

Such an enterprise needs datasets larger and more diverse than vision science has traditionally used: broad psychophysical batteries administered to the same individuals, analyzed with correlation, factor analysis, structural equation modeling, network methods, and machine learning, and extended longitudinally and across clinical populations. It would complement, not replace, experimental work: experiments establish causal mechanisms; the atlas reveals the largescale organization of variation across observers.

The atlas also provides a natural bridge to artificial intelligence, and here the present paper meets its companion. A parallel proposal argues that models of vision should be judged not only by their fit to the average human but by whether populations of artificial observers reproduce the covariance structure and latent factors of human individual differences (Peterzell, 2026).

Networks differing only in random initialization develop measurably different internal representations (Mehrer, Spoerer, Kriegeskorte, & Kietzmann, 2020; Storrs et al., 2021), so a trained network is one sample from a distribution of artificial observers, and recent work shows such artificial differences can align with specific humans (Fung, Murty, & Rahnev, 2025). The precedent for turning tools built for human assessment onto artificial agents is explicit in computational psychiatry itself (Schulz & Dayan, 2020). The two papers are complementary: the companion supplies the benchmark and method for comparing human and artificial latent structures; the present paper supplies the ontology: what those latent structures are.

Computational phenotypes are the shared units in which a human population and a population of networks can be compared, and the atlas is the framework within which the comparison is organized. Whether large models converge toward a single shared representation (Huh, Cheung, Wang, & Isola, 2024) is then an empirical question the joint program is well placed to answer.

Seen this way, the atlas is one instance of a larger pattern in the modern life sciences. Biology has its genome and neuroscience its connectome; each became a coordinated enterprise once a field agreed on the units to be mapped and committed to mapping them comprehensively.

Vision science has mechanisms, channels, and pathways, but no comparable map of computational variation, no systematic account of how visual processing differs across individuals, development, disease, and even artificial systems. Computational phenotypes are the natural units of such a map. One might call the enterprise a human visual phenome: not a catalog of average function, but a description of the dimensions along which visual computation varies, and of how those dimensions emerge, change, and break. Whether or not the name endures, the ambition is the point. Vision science has mapped the typical visual system far more thoroughly than it has mapped the differences between visual systems, and the second map may now be within reach.

12. Conclusion: variation as architecture

Vision science learned, through a small and persistent tradition, that the differences among observers are a window onto the mechanisms of sight. This paper takes the next step. Having used individual differences to discover latent visual mechanisms, we should ask what we have found, and answer that stable, reproducible dimensions of visual variation are candidate computational phenotypes: measurable patterns of visual information processing, intermediate between behavior and biology, that characterize individual observers.

The proposal is not psychometrics by another name. Psychometrics is the method; computational phenotyping is the goal, exactly as sequencing DNA is the method and genomics the goal. Not every factor qualifies; the criteria are stringent and most candidates will fail them. But the factors that survive are not statistical curiosities. They are the fault lines of perception, the seams along which the visual system comes apart, where its mechanisms break cleanly enough to be read, and where, increasingly, they can be measured in a person, perturbed by development and disease, and reproduced or not reproduced by an artificial observer. If the history of vision science has been the study of what observers share, its next chapter may be a precision science of how, and why, they differ.
