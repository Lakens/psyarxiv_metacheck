To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 8 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Purpose: This study evaluated the feasibility of an online adaptation of the Fidler diagnostic protocol for identifying developmental language disorder (DLD) in adults. Specifically, we examined (1) comparability of computerized versus face-to-face administration, (2) test-retest reliability, (3) agreement between two-and three-task scoring equations, (4) effects of demographic and cognitive factors on scores, and (5) the relationship between self-reported diagnoses and screener performance.

Method: Over 5,000 adults completed a language and medical history survey, and 526 subsequently completed an assessment over videoconference involving following auditory directions, spelling irregular words, and defining words (modified from Fidler et al, 2011). A subset (N = 53; 25 DLD, 28 with typical language development) then completed an in-person session comparing face-to-face and computerized administration of the following directions task, along with nonverbal IQ testing. Participants were recruited between 2022 and 2025 via online postings and flyers.

Results: No significant effect of administration modality on following directions task performance or Fidler scores was observed. Test-retest reliability was high (r = .91). Scores derived from the two-and three-task weighted equations from Fidler et al (2011) were highly correlated (r = .98), though the three-task equation produced lower (i.e., more typical) scores for participants with DLD-range profiles. Age, gender, and early bilingualism did not significantly influence scores. Although several self-reported diagnoses and concerns were higher among the group identified as DLD than the TD group, overall discriminative accuracy of self-report screening items was low (AUC range: 0.45-0.65).

Conclusions: Online administration of the Fidler protocol is comparable to the original face-to-face version and demonstrates adequate test-retest reliability, supporting its use as a scalable tool for identifying adults with DLD. Practical recommendations for recruiting and screening adults with DLD are discussed to facilitate future research with this population.

Introduction

An estimated 7.6% of the population struggles to learn their first language (Norbury et al., 2016). Although research on developmental language disorder (DLD) predominantly focuses on children, most individuals with DLD are affected into adulthood (Aram et al., 1984; Clegg et al., 2005; Poll et al., 2010; Tomblin et al., 1992a), facing persistent challenges in literacy, employment, and mental health (Dubois et al., 2020; Law et al., 2009). Adults with DLD are often underserved due to difficulties in identification, given that many children with DLD do not receive formal diagnoses and there is not a gold-standard assessment for DLD in adulthood. In this study, we converted an in-person screening for DLD to an online assessment, and we report on the profiles of individuals identified as DLD with this screener.

Developmental Language Disorder in Childhood and Adulthood

In childhood, DLD manifests as difficulties in comprehension and/or production of spoken and written language (Abbott & Love, 2023). This may include challenges in following multi-step directions, using correct grammar, or combining words into sentences (Sansavini et al., 2021). These language difficulties can contribute to increased social challenges, and children with DLD may become more withdrawn or experience higher levels of anxiety (Maggio et al., 2014). In adulthood, DLD continues to manifest through communication difficulties. Adults with DLD may struggle with word-finding, following complex directions, comprehending abstract language and expressing or organizing ideas (Blake et al., 2025). Adults with DLD describe continued challenges in both comprehension and expression that affect daily communication, educational and professional attainment, and overall well-being (Botting, 2020). They frequently report needing additional time to complete language-related tasks, relying on tools such as recorders or written reminders to manage verbal information, and exerting greater cognitive effort to meet communication demands (Hirn, 2023; Orrego et al., 2023). While some specific manifestations of DLD may change over age, DLD continues to affect language, social interaction, and mental health across the lifespan (Clegg et al., 2005).

Identification and Assessment of DLD in adulthood

Identification and assessment of DLD continues to be a barrier to treatment and research across the lifespan (McGregor, 2020). Adults with DLD are typically identified for research studies through one of several approaches: (1) participation in a longitudinal cohort that has tracked individuals with a history of language impairment since childhood, such as the Manchester Language Study, the Ottawa Language Study, or the Iowa Longitudinal Cohort (Dubois et al., 2020); (2) administration of a combination of language assessments validated with younger participant groups and a background screener, with participants identified holistically (Larson et al., 2025). In this case, individuals in the language-impaired group typically perform significantly below the unimpaired group on standardized measures but may not meet formal diagnostic cutoffs, as most available assessments are normed only through adolescence or early adulthood. For example, the Test of Integrated Language and Literacy Skills (TILLS) provides age norms up to 18 years but has not been well validated for adults beyond that age range. Similarly, while the Clinical Evaluation of Language Fundamentals-Fifth Edition (Wiig et al., 2013) and the Comprehensive Assessment of Spoken Language-Second Edition (Carrow-Woolfolk, 2017) extend norms to approximately 21 years, these norms are limited by their derivation from younger clinical samples (CELF-5) or inclusion of participants with other clinical conditions, such as autism spectrum disorder (CASL-2) (Choi-Tucci et al., 2024). The final method of identifying adults with DLD is by (3) completion of the standardized diagnostic protocol developed by Fidler et al. (2011), which was based off of a protocol established by Tomblin et al. (1992) (Fidler et al., 2011; Tomblin et al., 1992b) The Fidler diagnostic protocol offers one of the few empirically validated approaches for identifying DLD in adults and thus helps address many of the methodological challenges associated with assessment in this population. The protocol derives a weighted composite score from up to three tasks: an irregular word spelling test; a following directions task; and a word definition task. Participant performance on these measures is combined using regression-based equations to yield a continuous score reflecting the likelihood of language impairment. Four equations are offered to differentiate between (1) participants with and without a learning disability, (2) participants with and without a history of receiving speech and language services, (3) participants who have children with and without DLD, or (4) combining all groups together (without any history vs adults with a learning disability OR a history of receiving speech and language services OR with a child with DLD) versus controls. The authors recommend using the fourth equation (combined groups) when samples vary in age and educational background. This equation is 6.7110 -0.1698 × spelling -0.1151× word definition (scaled) -0.1107 × following directions, which has reported sensitivity of 75% and specificity of 81% (Fidler et al., 2011). Some groups instead use the second equation (with and without a history of receiving speech and language services), which uses only two of the tasks: 6.5727 -0.2184 × spelling -0.1298 × following directions, with a higher sensitivity (80%) and specificity (87%) (Abbott et al., 2026; Cler et al., 2024; McGregor et al., 2020). In both of these equations, a score above zero indicates a language profile consistent with DLD, whereas scores below zero indicate typical language development.

Research Aims

Building on this validated work, the present study extends the Fidler protocol to an online format to evaluate its feasibility and comparability to the original in-person version. By assessing different Fidler protocol scoring equations and examining how demographic, diagnostic, and task-related factors relate to performance, we aim to refine current approaches for identifying DLD in adults and contribute to the growing body of literature on adult language disorder assessment. Specifically, we address the following research questions: 1.

How does performance on the original face-to-face screener compare to a new computerized, online version of the screener? 2. What is test-retest reliability of the computerized screener? 3. How do the two Fidler protocol equations, identifying adults with a history of speech and language services ("two-task" equation) or identifying adults with any learning disability, history of services, or family with DLD ("three-task" equation) compare in our sample? 4. How do other factors (age, gender, bilingualism, tested non-verbal IQ) relate to performance on the online Fidler protocol? 5. How do self-reported diagnoses and concerns relate to performance on the online Fidler protocol? In particular, how does a self-reported dyslexia diagnosis relate to performance?

Methods Participants

Participants were recruited via online postings and physical flyers. Around 5000 potential participants completed a prescreening survey on REDCap to determine eligibility. Inclusion criteria included ages 18-45; any gender; acquiring English as a first language. Exclusion criteria included any known hearing difference; the acquisition of English at age 3 or above; and the diagnosis of ADHD, ASD, or any other neurodevelopmental or neurogenic (e.g., epilepsy) disorder.

After completing the prescreening survey, 526 participants completed an online language screening over Zoom. A subset of these participants completed in-laboratory testing (N=53) comparing performance on the original instructions to the virtual instructions, as well as nonverbal IQ testing (block design and matrix reasoning). Participants were recruited between 2022 and 2025.

Recruitment strategy.

Our recruitment strategies changed over time. Initially, we generated flyers and postings specifically asking for participants who had a diagnosis of DLD or specific language impairment. We then moved to a pseudo "community sampling" procedure, in which we simply said "we are studying speech and the brain" and ran each respondent through the online screener (the majority of the data we present here). After some time, we did also attempt to recruit a higher percentage of participants with DLD by posting flyers (online and in-person) targeting participants with phrases including "have you ever received help for a learning disability?" ; "do you have trouble keeping up with conversations or lectures?" ; "Do you read the same sentence over and over, trying to understand it?". In all cases, our respondents were largely university students, despite flyering in the community. We attempted to recruit using online social media for students with dyslexia, and we posted flyers in community colleges and other spaces. We targeted individuals with dyslexia because DLD and dyslexia frequently co-occur (Snowling et al., 2020), and because public and clinical awareness of DLD lags well behind awareness of dyslexia (Kim et al., 2023), meaning individuals with unrecognized DLD may carry a dyslexia diagnosis instead. None of these strategies was particularly successful, as most respondents came from posting on a student job board. Many participants responding to the learning disability-focused flyer self-reported ADHD and were excluded from this study 1.

Prescreening

Potential participants filled out a prescreening questionnaire, which research assistants used to determine eligibility and prescreening cohort ("likely TD", "likely DLD"). Potential participants identified as likely DLD were nearly always contacted for follow-up; potential participants identified as likely TD were contacted in phases depending on current recruitment needs. Potential participants were contacted and asked to schedule an online appointment at their convenience for the online language screening.

Online Session

526 participants completed an online testing session. They completed the Fidler protocol, modified as follows for online administration. For the following directions section, we created a website-based assessment on Pavlovia.org in which participants heard modified instructions (e.g., instead of "touch the red circle" they heard "click the red circle") and completed them with their mouse manipulating virtual shapes. These were scored in real time, with video for offline re-checking if needed, by the research assistant. For the spelling test, participants were instructed to have paper and pen available. They heard the word, it used in a sentence, and the word again (pre-recorded). Then they wrote the words and held them to the camera over Zoom for live scoring by a research assistant. The word definition task was completed as in the CELF manual, with the research assistant stating the word and scoring the participant's response according to the manual. Of the 526 participants completing this session, 347 completed all three tasks and 179 completed only two tasks due to protocol changes.

In-person Session

A subset of participants (N=53; 25 DLD, 28 TD) then completed a session in the lab. Participants with online Fidler scores greater than 0.5 (DLD) or less than -0.5 (TD) were prioritized for further recruitment over participants with more borderline scores nearer 0, but no stringent limits were set. The spelling test and word definition tasks were not repeated due to anticipated practice effects and because the online implementations were not predicted to change task performance substantively.

There were two crossed counterbalancing factors in the in-person following directions task: administration modality order (face-to-face or computerized first) and item set assignment (whether odd-or even-numbered items were presented in each modality). Participants were pseudorandomly assigned to one of the four resulting sequences (e.g., odd items face-to-face first, then even items computerized), such that each participant completed the full set of items across the two modalities without repeating any item. The face-to-face items were identical to the items from the original following directions test and recorded by a female American English speaker, with audio presented from computer speakers while participants physically manipulated the colored shapes. They were scored in real-time by the research assistant. The computerized items were the same as those presented in the online session on Pavlovia.org. They completed half of the items in each modality (i.e., 22 items computerized; 22 face-to-face). The computerized version was presented over headphones using a computer and browser in a room adjacent to the research assistant, who observed on a mirrored computer monitor and scored responses live. These participants were also assessed on non-verbal IQ via the block design and matrix reasoning tasks from the Wechsler Abbreviated Scale of Intelligence (WASI-II; Wechsler, 2018).

Statistical analyses

All data cleaning and analyses were completed in R. Survey data and task scores were stored and exported from REDCap.

Research question 1.

The similarity between face-to-face and computerized presentations of the following directions task during the second, in-person session were evaluated with a linear mixed effects model of the form score~modality+cohort+modality×cohort + (1|subjID), where modality refers to completing the following directions task face-to-face or on a computer in the laboratory and cohort refers to TD or DLD using the designation assigned by the score derived from the first online session using the Fidler protocol. A significant effect of modality or interaction between modality × cohort would be interpreted as evidence that there was a systematic difference between accuracy based on presentation modality.

To ensure there were no systematic effects of administration order (computerized first; face-to-face first) or which items were in each modality (evens face-to-face; odds face-to-face), a linear mixed effect model of the form score~modality+cohort+modality×cohort+administration order+odds_modality+(1|subjID) was evaluated.

Research question 2.

The test-retest reliability of the online screener was estimated by comparing performance on the computerized version of the following directions task during the first, online session and second, in-person session. A Pearson's correlation compared task performance directly. To determine the possible effect on group categorization, we also calculated approximated Fidler scores from the in-person session and compared those to the scores from the online session with Pearson's correlations. The online Fidler scores were calculated as above, using the online presentation of the spelling task and following directions task during the first session. The approximated Fidler scores (representing the re-test version, as the participants saw the identical items for a second time) were calculated using the online spelling task and then the performance on the computerized following direction items completed during the second, in-person session. Before entering the performance into the weighted equation, the count of correct items was multiplied by two as only half of the items were given in each modality in that session. This became the approximated, re-test Fidler score to compare to the score derived from performance during the first online session.

Research Question 3.

To compare Fidler scores derived by different weighted equations (with and without the word definition task), results on tasks from the first, online session were entered into the appropriate weighted equations. A Pearson's correlation was used to determine the relationship between the two weighted equations.

Research Question 4.

The effects of age and gender on Fidler scores derived from the online, two-task version of the screening were evaluated with linear models of the form score~age*gender. The effects of bilingualism were evaluated with a t-test comparing Fidler scores between monolinguals versus bilinguals (reporting that they were spoken to in English as a baby "always" or "mostly", but were also exposed to at least one other language).

The relationship between Fidler scores and measures of non-verbal IQ were evaluated with Pearson's correlations between and within groups, as well as between the two measures of non-verbal IQ.

Research Question 5.

In the prescreening questionnaire, participants answered yes/no questions about various concerns (e.g., "Are some of these true for you? □ I have trouble following directions; □ I hate writing; □ I have trouble with grammar") and diagnoses ("Have you ever been diagnosed with a condition that would affect your ability to learn at school, like..." □ Dyslexia or specific reading disorder, □ Expressive/receptive language disorder □ Autism spectrum disorder", etc).

The proportion of participants checking each box who were subsequently identified as TD and DLD were evaluated with chi-square tests. The predictive value of each diagnosis and heuristic question was evaluated with the area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves. To identify the best-performing combination of predictors, we applied LASSO regularization (L1 penalty) via 10-fold cross-validation, selecting the lambda that minimized cross-validated deviance. The AUC was computed from the combined model to determine classification accuracy compared to each predictor individually.

To specifically evaluate the relationship between a self-reported dyslexia diagnosis and Fidler protocol performance, we compared following-directions and spelling scores between participants who did and did not report a dyslexia diagnosis. Group differences in raw and standardized-discrepancy scores were evaluated with Welch's two-sample t-tests and one-way ANOVA.

All analyses were exploratory and so were not corrected for multiple comparisons. Research Question 5: Self-Reported Diagnoses and Concerns

As expected, a higher proportion of adults with DLD reported diagnoses or concerns about various conditions; however, adults with typical language also reported these concerns (see Table 4; Figure 7). There was a statistically significant difference in Chi-squared tests between groups in the proportion of "yes" responses to whether they had been diagnosed with dyslexia or specific reading disorder: χ² = 24.3, p < 0.0001 (DLD > TD); or no diagnoses (TD > DLD): χ² = 16.3, p < 0.0001. More participants with DLD responded that someone had had concerns about their speech, language, or reading: χ² = 27.9, p < 0.0001, and reported that they had concerns about other conditions (χ² = 5.4, p < 0.05). There were no group differences in diagnoses for speech difficulties, diagnoses for any other condition, or being seen by a speech therapist (see Table 4). None of these questions were particularly discriminative as indicated by the area under the curve of the ROC (AUC range: 0.45-0.62; highest for "Has anyone had concerns about your speech, language, or reading?").

A significantly higher proportion of adults with DLD also responded to some of our heuristic statements (Chi-sq tests on yes/no responses to "I have trouble with grammar": χ² = 33.1, p < 0.0001; "I hate writing": χ² = 14.6, p < 0.001). There was no difference between cohorts in the response to "I have trouble following directions" (χ² = 0.58, p = 0.45) or "My friends think I don't listen" (χ² = 0.30, p = 0.59). The likelihood of the DLD cohort responding to any one of the heuristic statements was also significantly higher (χ² = 12.4, p < 0.001), as was the likelihood of responding yes to more than one statement (χ² = 18.0, p < 0.0001). None of the heuristic statements were particularly discriminative as indicated by the area under the curve of the ROC (AUC range: 0.52-0.65; highest for "I have trouble with grammar").

The LASSO model retained five predictors: a diagnosis of dyslexia or specific reading disorder (β = 1.71), self-reported trouble with grammar (β = 1.13), self-reported hatred of writing (β = 0.35), whether anyone had ever had concerns about the participant's speech, language, or reading (β = 0.25), and a diagnosis of any other condition (β = -0.38). At the optimal classification threshold derived from the ROC curve, the LASSO model correctly identified 46 of 89 DLD participants (sensitivity = 52%) and 219 of 256 TD participants (specificity = 86%), yielding an overall accuracy of 77%. However, given the class imbalance in the sample (256 TD, 89 DLD), a naïve classifier predicting TD for all participants would achieve 74% accuracy.

A self-reported dyslexia diagnosis was rarer than expected overall (19 reports total), but was significantly more common among participants eventually meeting criteria for DLD (14/125, 11.2%) than TD participants (5/401, 1.2%; χ² = 24.3, p < 0.0001). To evaluate whether a diagnosis was associated with a distinct performance profile, we compared followingdirections and spelling scores between participants with and without a co-occurring dyslexia diagnosis (Figure 8). Participants who met criteria for DLD with a dyslexia diagnosis did not differ from those without on the following-directions task (mean 29.6/44 correct vs. 30.6, t = 0.90, p = 0.38; Figure 8B), but scored significantly lower on spelling (mean 4.8/15 correct vs. 8.4, t = 3.79, p = 0.002; Figure 8C). The equivalent TD comparisons (TD with vs. without a dyslexia diagnosis) showed the same pattern (following-directions: 38.4 vs. 38.4, t = 0.04, p = 0.97; spelling: 9.8 vs. 12.2, t = 2.76, p = 0.049; Figure 8B,C).

Discussion The present study adapted the Fidler diagnostic protocol for online administration and evaluated its performance relative to the original face-to-face version, as well as its relationship to demographic, cognitive, and task-related factors. Overall, our results suggest that the online implementation is a viable and scalable approach for identifying adults with DLD, while also highlighting the limitations of the protocol and current approaches to classification in adulthood.

Modality and Repetition

There was no evidence that administration modality (computerized vs. face-to-face) significantly affected performance on the following directions task or downstream Fidler scores. There were also no effects of administration order or item assignment. This indicates that the following directions task is fairly robust to online delivery, even with changes in the wording of instructions (e.g. "click" instead of "pick up"). The high test-retest correlation supports the stability of following directions task performance over time. The tendency for participants in the DLD range to score somewhat better during the second session could reflect practice effects, regression to the mean, or both. Cohort designations were generally consistent across sessions and modalities, but the small number of participants who shifted categories is a reminder that the Fidler score is continuous and that any binary cutpoint will be most fragile for borderline cases.

Different Weighted Equations

The two-and three-task scoring equations were highly correlated, indicating that they capture largely overlapping variance in our sample, despite being designed for different grouping assumptions in the original validation work (Fidler et al., 2011). At the same time, the three-task equation systematically shifted scores toward the TD range, particularly for individuals with higher (more DLD-like) scores. One possibility is that adding the word definition task changes the balance of the skills the composite score is testing, either because it taps partially distinct aspects of language or because it introduces additional variability tied to expressive vocabulary and scoring. The word definition task also allows for more open-ended responses compared to the spelling and following directions tasks which are more constrained. For someone with DLD, this can be beneficial as they work through finding the precise words they want to say, which could account for the slight shift toward the TD range.

From a practical perspective, these results support the use of the two-task equation in contexts where efficiency is critical, particularly for large-scale or online screening. This is particularly important given that the additional task (CELF word definition) takes the most time to administer and requires the most training to administer and score correctly.

Demographic and experiential factors

We found no evidence that age, gender, or early bilingualism influenced performance on the online Fidler protocol. Given our inclusion criteria (early English exposure), the lack of bilingualism effects suggests that the online protocol is not overly sensitive to variation in early language experience within this restricted range. These null effects should be interpreted cautiously, because our sample was not demographically broad and details about language exposure were minimal was incomplete, but they are at least consistent with the idea that the screener is relatively stable across common demographic variables in adult samples.

Nonverbal reasoning showed a modest but somewhat task-specific relationship with Fidler scores. Across participants, matrix reasoning was significantly correlated with Fidler scores, whereas block design was not. Within-group patterns differed, such that block design was marginally related to Fidler scores within the TD group but not the DLD group, whereas matrix reasoning showed the opposite pattern, with a marginal relationship in the DLD group but not in the TD group. Although these within-group effects should be interpreted cautiously given the smaller sample sizes, they suggest that the relationship between nonverbal reasoning and language performance may differ across groups and across types of nonverbal tasks.

Importantly, very few participants showed evidence of broadly low nonverbal ability, with only two individuals falling below the standard score cutoff of 70 on matrix reasoning and none on block design. This supports the interpretation that low Fidler scores in this sample are not generally attributable to global cognitive impairment. At the same time, the moderate correlation between matrix reasoning and language performance, particularly within the DLD group, suggests that aspects of fluid reasoning may contribute to variability in language task performance in adulthood -or in fact that language skills may contribute to performance on "non-verbal" reasoning tasks. In contrast, the lack of a consistent relationship with block design suggests that visuospatial construction abilities are less directly related to performance on this screener, or not as helped by language skills. Together, these findings are consistent with the view that DLD is not explained by nonverbal cognitive deficits, while still allowing for meaningful, task-specific relationships between language and nonverbal reasoning.

Prescreening and classification

A practical goal of this project was to determine whether a brief, written prescreen could efficiently enrich the sample for adults likely to meet objective screening criteria for DLD. In practice, prescreening helped with triage but was not a strong predictor of eventual cohort. Participants flagged as "likely TD" were usually classified as TD on the online Fidler protocol, but many fell into the borderline range and a small proportion met DLD criteria. Conversely, among participants flagged as "likely DLD," only about one-quarter ultimately met DLD criteria, with many instead falling into the TD or borderline ranges.

Self-reported diagnoses and concerns showed group differences in the expected direction, but they were not very discriminative. Individual items had AUC values near chance, and the multivariable model improved classification only modestly over a naïve majority-class rule. In other words, the prescreen captured "risk signals," but those signals were also common in adults who screened as TD.

There are a few likely reasons for this mismatch. First, DLD is under-identified and under-labeled across development, so many adults who meet behavioral criteria may not have a clear diagnostic history to report (McGregor, 2020). Second, a written prescreen may be a poor format for capturing developmental history in a population defined by language and literacy vulnerability; in our experience, more detailed (and sometimes discrepant) information often arose in conversation after initial recruitment. Third, because most participants were recruited through a university job board, our sample is skewed toward university students, which may select for people with compensated language weaknesses and/or uneven academic profiles that do not map cleanly onto self-reported diagnoses.

Diagnosis of dyslexia

In our sample, self-reported dyslexia was rarer than population estimates (only reported by 3.5% of respondents), but was more common among participants who screened as DLD, and it was retained in the multivariable prescreen model. One interpretation is true co-occurrence: DLD and dyslexia are thought to overlap frequently, with the best estimates around 17%-36% (Catts et al., 2005), though varying definitions of dyslexia and DLD can make the estimates range up to 71% (Adlof & Hogan, 2018). A second possibility is that "dyslexia" may be the label that some individuals with primarily oral language-based difficulties receive once reading comprehension becomes a salient academic problem. This is plausible because DLD is often missed, whereas reading difficulty (or speech difficulty) is visible and more likely to trigger evaluation and services in school. In this framing, a history of dyslexia diagnosis in adulthood may index service contact and literacy difficulty rather than a clean, separable decoding-specific disorder. Methodologically, this matters because the Fidler protocol includes an irregular word spelling task, which is sensitive to literacy experience and reading-related impairment as well as to language, though is theoretically less dyslexia-specific than regular word spelling. A third explanation is that participants with only dyslexia (without co-occurring DLD) are being identified in the DLD cohort with this screener. The Fidler (2011) paper stated that none of their participants "demonstrated a frank reading impairment". Due to the weighting of the equation, different proportions of scores on following directions and spelling can lead to equivalent scores. A pure dyslexia (decoding) profile could theoretically include TD-like performance on the following directions task but poor enough spelling to result in a DLD designation. Figure 8A indicates at least one such participant with a dyslexia diagnosis: their following directions score was near the median of the TD group (example participant: 36/44; TD median: 38.4, TD SD: 3.5) and their spelling score was 1/15, leading to a Fidler score of 1.7.

Although only a small proportion of our participants reported a dyslexia diagnosis, their performance profiles (Figure 8) may help differentiate between these competing interpretations. Among participants who screened as DLD, those with a reported dyslexia diagnosis did not differ from the rest of the DLD group on the following directions task, but scored significantly lower than the mean on spelling (Figure 8B, C). This pattern of a literacy-specific deficit layered on an otherwise similar oral-language profile is not what would be expected if "dyslexia" were simply a relabeling of broader, undifferentiated language difficulty (the second interpretation above); that account would predict a diagnosis tracking with weaker performance across both tasks, not a deficit isolated to spelling. Instead, the dissociation is more consistent with dyslexia contributing a distinct, decoding/literacy-related component on top of DLD, aligning with both the true-co-occurrence interpretation and the theoretical "pure dyslexia" profile described above. The equivalent comparison within the TD group showed the same pattern, even with a very small number of participants (N=5 who scored as TD and reported a dyslexia diagnosis). It does appear as those with the worst spelling performance across cohorts were those that reported a dyslexia diagnosis (Figure 8C), which may indicate that someone took note of their spelling impairments and referred them for testing, rather than that these participants were the only participants in the cohort with dyslexia.

If these patterns were to hold in larger cohorts, we would suggest that the Fidler protocol seemed to mostly identify oral language impairments (following directions task) separately from dyslexia (spelling task), although future studies may want to focus on separate screening tasks that should not be impacted by dyslexia alone (i.e., no spelling or vocabulary tasks). Future work can test these competing explanations directly by adding brief measures that separate decoding/word reading from broader language (e.g., timed word and pseudoword reading plus a short oral language composite) and by collecting more detailed service/IEP history. This would allow adult participants who report a dyslexia diagnosis to be subtyped into profiles more consistent with (i) primarily reading-specific impairment, (ii) primarily language impairment with secondary literacy impacts, or (iii) combined impairment, thereby improving both recruitment efficiency and interpretability of screened DLD cohorts.

Limitations and future directions

Several limitations should be noted. First, because we were recruiting for neuroimaging studies with specific brain structure hypotheses, we excluded participants who reported diagnoses that may affect those regions, including ADHD and stuttering. Both diagnoses are common in individuals with DLD and, anecdotally, participants with these diagnoses often reported language concerns similar to those seen in our DLD group. Excluding them likely narrowed our sample to a less representative subset of the broader DLD population, one that may not fully reflect the co-occurring difficulties, such as attention or fluency concerns, that many individuals with DLD contend with. Future work should reconsider these exclusion criteria, or at minimum characterize how excluded individuals differ from those retained.

Second, recruitment relied heavily on a written prescreening survey. Although we offered participants the option to complete the screening via phone, no potential participants chose this option. This raises the possibility that individuals with more severe language difficulties may have been less likely to engage with the study at all, potentially biasing the sample. Similarly, the sample was heavily skewed toward university students, which may limit generalizability.

The prescreening survey evolved over the course of the study, which limits the precision and consistency of some self-report measures. In particular, questions about early language exposure were revised over time to improve clarity, but still do not capture much detail about language experience. Instead, these measures were intended primarily to ensure that scores in the DLD range were not attributable to limited English exposure, rather than to provide a detailed characterization of bilingual experience.

All participants completed the online version of the task first, followed by the in-person version, which was split into face-to-face and computerized. The subtests for the online version were always presented in the same order (following directions, spelling, word definitions), which may have contributed to fatigue effects on the later subtests, especially for those who met criteria for DLD, but this was mitigated by breaks and the short length of the sessions (30 mins to 1 hour at most).

Finally, during the in-person session, participants were exposed to half of the same items they had previously encountered online and half in a different modality. Although we did not observe systematic effects of order or item assignment, this design introduces the possibility of repetition or familiarity effects that cannot be fully ruled out, particularly as the participants with DLD appeared to show improved performance.

Recommendations for inclusion of adults with DLD in future research

Future studies may benefit from more targeted recruitment strategies, such as recruiting through speech-language pathology clinics or specialized schools that serve children with academic difficulties in order to reach parents of children with current language concerns, some of whom are likely to have their own history of unrecognized DLD. As previously mentioned, our sample was heavily skewed toward college students and college-educated adults, who likely represent a specific subset of the broader DLD population. We know from our own neuroimaging cohort that these adults with DLD recruit domain-general systems differently than neurotypical peers (Abbott et al., 2026). Evidence from autism suggests that individuals who demonstrate relatively typical behavioral functioning in adulthood may constitute a distinct subgroup, potentially relying on compensatory neural processes that differentiate them from others with the same condition and neurotypical peers (Eigsti et al., 2016). Although our participants met behavioral criteria for DLD, recruiting predominantly college-educated adults may have preferentially sampled individuals who were better able to compensate for their language difficulties or who otherwise differ from the broader population of adults with DLD. Future studies in DLD will need to recruit adults across a broader range of language abilities and educational attainment to determine the extent to which neural and behavioral profiles generalize across the broader DLD population.

Adults with DLD are, as a group, less likely to pursue higher education and more likely to enter less-skilled or non-professional occupations than their peers (Conti-Ramsden et al., 2018; Dubois et al., 2020), which in practice means they may have less flexibility to take time away from work for multiple in-person testing sessions. We also observed practical challenges specific to this population that future studies should anticipate: in-person sessions with participants who screened as DLD tended to run longer than sessions with TD participants, and these participants were also more likely to have difficulty locating our lab space, to cancel sessions, or to arrive on an incorrect day or time. Building in additional session time, providing more redundant scheduling reminders, and offering clearer wayfinding support may reduce burden and missed sessions for this population. Developing assessment approaches that can be completed remotely, like the one discussed here, may further help capture a more representative range of adult DLD presentations by reducing the demands of in-person participation.

Relatedly, because scores near the classification threshold are inherently more ambiguous, we prioritized participants whose online Fidler scores fell clearly within the TD (< -0.5) or DLD (> 0.5) range for the present analyses. Borderline performance may reflect a genuine but milder language weakness, but it can just as easily reflect factors unrelated to DLD, including co-occurring attentional difficulties (e.g., ADHD), situational fatigue or distraction, or technical issues such as an unstable internet connection during online testing. Future studies using the Fidler protocol, or other remote screening tools, for group assignment may want to consider a similar approach of prioritizing participants with clearer score profiles, particularly when resources for in-person confirmatory testing are limited.

Screening instrument design is also worth strongly considering. In our experience, freeresponse questions yielded far less information than checkbox-style items; participants often disclosed diagnoses or relevant history verbally once in the lab that they had not mentioned in the written prescreen, despite having had the opportunity to do so. We also found that participants were often unable to recall specific details about the services or diagnostic labels they received as children, but were more reliably able to recall general impressions, such as finding school difficult or comparing their reading to that of siblings. Screening items that ask about broad constructs (e.g., whether a participant received any outside help in school) rather than specific services or diagnoses (e.g., whether a participant received speech therapy) may better capture this population, particularly given that formal diagnostic practices for this age cohort were inconsistent at best when they were children. Taken together, these considerations suggest that a broad-then-narrow screening approach, casting a wide net for general language, academic, and occupational struggle before using in-person testing to determine diagnostic status, is an appropriate strategy for identifying adults with DLD. This may become less necessary as DLD gains wider clinical and public recognition, but for now, capturing this broader sentiment of struggle is likely necessary to reach the full population of adults affected by the disorder.

Finally, future protocols should consider building in a mechanism for sharing classification information with participants where appropriate. Because our study was not designed with this in mind, and the online Fidler protocol alone was not intended to support individual diagnostic conclusions, we were unable to disclose group assignment to participants, even though many expressed interest in learning this information after completing testing. Future IRB protocols could build in language allowing researchers to share general findings or refer interested participants to appropriate clinical resources (which may also need to be developed in concert with the research efforts), without necessarily providing a formal diagnosis, so that participation can offer something back to the individuals whose experiences make this research possible.

Conclusions Taken together, the present findings support the feasibility and utility of an online implementation of the Fidler protocol for identifying adults with DLD. At the same time, they underscore the complexity of adult DLD classification, including weak correspondence with selfreport measures, modest associations with nonverbal cognition, and meaningful variability near diagnostic thresholds. Moving forward, combining standardized performance measures with richer behavioral data and participant background information may provide a more nuanced and scalable approach to understanding language impairment across the lifespan.
