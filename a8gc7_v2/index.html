Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. Abstract

Live performance provides a paradigmatic setting for collective aesthetic experiences, where the social dynamics shape how audiences perceive and evaluate performances. Building on prior research demonstrating that collective effervescence mediates the relationship between spectators' affective responses and enjoyment in live theatre, the present paper tests whether this same pathway generalises to two contemporary dance choreographies. In Study 1 (Detective Work, N=121), we examined both live (N=65) and screened (N=56) performances, distinguishing between connectedness to audience members and connectedness to performers. Unlike findings in live theatre, valence rather than arousal predicted enjoyment indirectly through connectedness, with performers-connectedness emerging as the stronger mediator in the screened modality, but not formally in the live one. Study 2 (Readings of what was never written, N=73) employed a reduced collective effervescence scale including both connectedness and sacredness facets; sacredness emerged as the dominant mediator of enjoyment, despite substantial covariance between facets. Across both studies, valence reliably predicted enjoyment through collective effervescence, whereas arousal effects were not detected; a sensitivity analysis indicates limited power to detect arousal effects in this sample, so this asymmetry is best read as a candidate dance-specific signature rather than a confirmed dissociation from narrative theatre. These findings extend theoretical models of aesthetic appreciation, indicating that while the core mediation pathway might generalise across performing arts, the most salient facet of collective effervescence and the COLLECTIVE EFFERVESCENCE IN DANCE 3 dominant social tie can vary with artistic form, scale, and dramaturgical design.

Live performance is a paradigmatic context for collective aesthetic experiences. Beyond their intrinsic artistic qualities, performances in theatre and dance are distinctive in that they are typically experienced together, with others who are physically and socially co-present (Lee et al., 2025 ; Simonot-Bérenger et al., 2025). This resonates with longstanding philosophical claims that aesthetic experience is intrinsically social, not only because it is often shared with others, but because our judgments of artworks are embedded in practices of mutual recognition and cultural participation (Carroll, 2001; Walton, 1990). Empirical research in psychology, neuroscience, and audience studies further suggests that this collective dimension plays a crucial role in shaping how performances are experienced and evaluated. Concepts such as "collective effervescence" (Durkheim, 1912 (Durkheim, /1995;; Gabriel et al., 2020), "shared attention" (Rennung & Göritz, 2015), and "co-presence" (Hanich, 2014) have been invoked to capture the heightened sense of connection that can emerge in these contexts. Studies indicate that shared attention to emotionally stimulating material fosters social bonding (Dunbar et al., 2016), and that aesthetic enjoyment often correlates with a subjective feeling of togetherness (Koefler et al., 2024). Moreover, audience research increasingly treats spectators not as isolated individuals but as participants in collective events whose social dynamics shape appreciation (Lee & Orgs, 2022; Walmsley, 2019).

In our previous work, we studied the collective dimension of the theatre experience through the concept of collective effervescence, originally described as a "heightened emotional state that emerges when individuals gather together and participate in shared activities, creating a sense of unity and transcendence [...]" (Durkheim, 1912 (Durkheim, /1995)). We operationalized this concept using a standardized questionnaire that assessed participants' feelings of emotional connection to co-present individuals and their sense of the event's sacredness (Gabriel et al., 2020). Across two large-scale studies, we demonstrated that collective effervescence mediates the relationship between spectators' affective responses and their enjoyment of the play (Simonot-Bérenger et al., 2025). Emotional arousal predicted feelings of collective effervescence, which in turn strongly predicted enjoyment. Moreover, collective effervescence was amplified when spectators felt attuned to the actors' emotions or were given special roles as "accomplices," highlighting the importance of performer-audience permeability.

These findings support the claim that aesthetic experience in theatre is intrinsically social, even in the absence of overt audience interaction.

Recent work in contemporary dance provides a complementary perspective. Lee et al. (2025) combined quantitative and qualitative data to show that audiences' experience of engagement and confusion after watching live and screened contemporary dance performances are distinct but coexisting dimensions of dance appreciation. This focus on confusion was motivated by aesthetic cognitivism accounts, which often assume that ambiguity is aversive unless resolved. Contrary to this assumption, Lee et al. demonstrated that confusion can itself be a productive form of engagement, especially in art forms such as contemporary dance where ambiguity and non-linear meaning-making are central features (see also Pepperell, 2023). Crucially, expertise in dance predicted greater engagement, but ambiguity was not necessarily aversive.

In a companion study using mobile electroencephalogram (EEG), Rai et al. (2025) demonstrated that inter-brain synchrony in the delta band tracked audience engagement during Detective Work. Neural synchrony peaked when performers broke the "fourth wall" and directly addressed the audience, and was higher in live than recorded settings. Importantly, by disentangling physical liveness (live versus recorded) from social liveness (watching together versus alone), the study showed that both dimensions shape spectators' engagement, with summative reports and neural synchrony highest when dance was experienced live and collectively, and lowest when recordings were watched alone. Together, these studies underscore that both the content of dance (ambiguity, performer address) and its context (physical and social liveness) critically shape audience engagement.

In Detective Work (Study 1), we operationalised collective effervescence only as its sub-facet of connectedness, but distinguishing between connectedness to the audience and connectedness to the performers.

By aligning our analyses with those of the theatre study while adapting to the available measures, we seek to refine our understanding of the social mechanisms through which affective responses are translated into the enjoyment of live performance. Data source

This study is a secondary analysis of audience survey data collected by Lee et al. (2025) in the context of Detective Work, a contemporary dance duet performed live and presented as a cinema screening (see https://neurolive.info/Performance-1 for full credits and photo and video documentation). Participants

The provided dataset includes 207 participants across two modalities (Live vs. Screening) and a pilot experiment. The pilot was excluded from the analyses. In the Live modality, the final analysed sample consists of N = 65 (raw N = 127). In the Screening modality, the final analysed sample consists of N = 56 (raw N = 59).

Participants with missing data on the variables of interest were excluded from the analyses; one participant was excluded for being younger than 18 years old. Most exclusions in the Live modality (62 of 127) were due to missing pre-performance SAM data, as participants who did not complete the pre-questionnaire could not be included in change-score analyses. Demographic details of the full dataset (age range, gender balance, recruitment context) are reported in Lee et al (2025).

In our samples, participants in the Live modality had an age range of 19 to 82 years old (M = 36.7, SD = 14.4), and participants in the Screening modality had an age range of 21 to 77 years old (M = 32.0, SD = 11.9).

All participants provided informed consent; the protocol was approved by the Goldsmiths, University of London ethics board (Lee et al., 2025).

Measures

The original questionnaire comprised 28 items covering affective, social, and engagement-related dimensions of the performance experience, alongside demographic information. Below, we describe the measures relevant to the present analyses.

Pre-and Post-performance Affect (SAM).

To assess the affective impact of the performance, participants completed the Self-Assessment Manikin (SAM; Bradley & Lang, 1994) both before and after the performance, reporting their current feelings on 9-point scales for valence and arousal (dominance was not measured). This pre-post design differs from our previous study (Simonot-Bérenger et al., 2025), in which affective experience was assessed via a single post-performance SAM item asking spectators to report how they felt during the play. In the present study, participants instead reported how they felt "right now" at each time of completion, which made it possible to operationalise affect as a change score (post -pre); this operationalisation is detailed in the Data Processing section below.

Post-performance questionnaire. Following the performance, participants completed a questionnaire assessing their experience across several dimensions.

For the present analyses, we focus on three sets of items: enjoyment, connectedness, and being moved.

Enjoyment was measured with one item, "I enjoyed the performance", on a 7-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (Disagree) to 7 (Agree).

Connectedness was assessed with five questions capturing participants' sense of connection to the audience or the performers, rated on 7-point Likert scales, ranging either from "Not at all" to "A great deal" or from "Disagree" to "Agree".

Audience connectedness (2 items): 1) "To what extent did you feel a sense of belonging or connectedness with the rest of the audience?" (Social Bonding; Brown & Novak, 2007). The questionnaire also included additional items measuring emotional and intellectual engagement with the performance on 7-point Likert scales, among which "The performance moved me" from the Aesthetic Emotions Scale (AESTHEMOS; Schindler et al., 2017) was retained for supplementary analyses.

Demographic and Control Variables.

Beyond the 28 questionnaire items, participants reported their age (in years) and their prior relationship to the performers on a 7-point scale ranging from 1 ("I do not know the performers at all") to 7 ("I know the performers very well").

Mobile EEG was collected from a subset of participants in both modalities as part of the broader NEUROLIVE protocol. Because EEG analyses are not the focus of the present paper, we collapse across EEG and non-EEG participants in all analyses and include EEG-group membership as a binary covariate in robustness checks only (1 = EEG group,0 = non-EEG group).

Data analysis

Data were analysed using R, Version 4.2.1 (R Core Team, 2022) on the open-source version of RStudio (V.2021.09.0; RStudio Team, 2020) using the tidyverse package (V2.0.0, Wickham et al., 2019); structural equation modelling (SEM) used lavaan (V.0.6.19; Rosseel, 2012). Analyses were run separately by modality (Live vs. Screening). No outliers were removed, and no post-hoc score filtering was applied.

Our previous study (Simonot-Bérenger et al., 2025) used data-driven model selection. In that study, iterative stepwise addition and backwards stepwise elimination based on the Likelihood-Ratio Test (Matuschek et al., 2017; Stram & Lee, 1994) via the "buildmer" package (Voeten, 2020) was applied to identify a parsimonious predictive model across a wide set of candidate predictors.

By contrast, the present work constitutes a theory-led replication on an external dataset, aimed at testing a specific, a priori pathway: that basic affects relate to Enjoyment both directly and indirectly via Connectedness. Accordingly, we did not use stepwise model selection, which can be unstable and inflates degrees of freedom. Instead, the theoretically motivated paths were fixed and estimated consistently across modalities to maximise comparability and minimise analytical flexibility.

The present paper will concentrate on the replication of mediation analyses, therefore, preliminary linear regressions as well as robustness checks are presented as supplementary data. Similarly, analyses that were not directly comparable to our previous study (such as covariate adjustments and models including "Being moved") are reported as supplementary rather than confirmatory tests, so as to avoid blurring the replication inference.

Data processing.

All variables retained their original response scales (SAM Valence/Arousal on 1-9 point scales; all other items on Likert 1-7 scales). In our previous study, predictors were z-scored prior to modelling because analyses relied on OLS models with unstandardised slopes. In the present analyses, SEMs were estimated on the original response scales and standardised coefficients were obtained from the model's standardised solution. Only covariates (Age and relationship to performers) were z-scored within modality to keep nuisance predictors on a comparable scale; EEG status was coded as a binary (0/1). Because standardised estimates are invariant to linear rescaling, this approach preserves comparability of effect sizes without requiring global z-scoring.

Affect change was computed as the difference between post-and pre-scores on the Valence and Arousal items of the SAM. For Arousal, we evaluated the possibility of using the absolute value of the post-pre difference to reflect intensity change independently of change direction, but these analyses yielded null results (see Supplemental Materials n°8), therefore, raw differences were kept. Connectedness was operationalised as two observed mean composites computed within modality: Connectedness to the audience (2 items) and Connectedness to the performers (3 items), as detailed below.

Connectedness. This study did not administer a dedicated Collective Effervescence scale. However, Collective Effervescence is generally theorised as comprising two sub-facets, a communion/connectedness component and a sacredness/transcendence component (Gabriel et al., 2020). Because the sacredness component had not been collected in Study 1, we focused only on the connectedness sub-facet and operationalised it directly from available items.

Accordingly, we adopt the neutral label "Connectedness" rather than "Collective Effervescence," to avoid construct creep, while retaining the theoretically relevant social-bonding aspect. We formed two observed composites (mean scores) that distinguish the target of connection: • Connectedness to the audience: mean of "I felt I experienced the same emotions as the other spectators" and "Sense of belonging/connectedness with the rest of the audience" (2 items).

• Connectedness to the performers: mean of "I felt I experienced the same emotions as the performers", "I felt bonded with one or both of the performers", and "Sense of belonging/connectedness with the performers" (3 items).

All items were 7-point Likert-type scales; composites preserve the original range. Composites were computed within modality (Live vs. Screening), and internal consistency was evaluated separately per modality.

Performer-connectedness showed good-excellent reliability (Live α = .82; Screening α = .89). The two-item audience composite was good in Live (α = .71) and borderline but usable in Screening (α = .57). As such, we retained both composites for primary analyses, but note that effects involving audience-connectedness in the Screening modality are likely attenuated by measurement error.

Statistical analyses

Modality comparison.

As context, we summarised means and standard deviations for Connectedness (audience/performers), Enjoyment, Valence, and Arousal by modality, and compared Live vs. Screening using two-sided Welch's t-tests (robust to unequal variances and unbalanced N) with Holm correction for multiplicity. We report Hedges' g as the standardised effect size.

Mediation analyses.

We used structural equation modelling ("lavaan" R package V.0.6.19; Rosseel, 2012) to examine whether Connectedness (to the audience and/or performers) mediated the effect of emotional measures on enjoyment of the performance.

As described above, a single a priori path model was specified and estimated consistently across modalities, following a theory-led rather than data-driven approach, and core SEMs were estimated on the original response scales; we report standardised coefficients (β_std) from the model's standardised solution.

For each modality, we estimated a saturated (df = 0) SEM with the following structural equations: • Connectedness to the audience regressed on Valence and Arousal • Connectedness to the performers regressed on Valence and Arousal.

• Enjoyment regressed on Connectedness to the audience, Connectedness to the performers, Valence, and Arousal.

• Residuals of Audience-and Performer-connectedness were allowed to covary; Valence and Arousal were also allowed to covary.

Estimation used Maximum-Likelihood (ML) with bootstrap standard errors and 95% Confidence Intervals (CI) (1,000 draws); bootstrap inference is preferred over asymptotic standard errors as it does not rely on the assumption of multivariate normality. For indirect and total (defined) effects specifically, we report p-values computed from the empirical bootstrap distribution (two-sided, percentile-based) rather than the delta-method Wald p-value lavaan reports by default; the two can diverge for effects with skewed sampling distributions, such as products of coefficients, and we prioritise the method consistent with the percentile confidence intervals reported alongside them. Because the models are saturated, global fit indices are trivially perfect; inference is based on path estimates and bootstrap CIs. Because the dataset does not support stable higher-level identifiers (e.g., date or venue clusters), models were estimated without random effects. For interpretability, we report standardised β for direct paths to Enjoyment and unstandardised indirects with bootstrap CIs.

Simple linear regressions as well as robustness checks are presented in Supplemental Materials n°2.

Participants

The current dataset includes 80 participants to the pre-questionnaire and 134 participants to the post-questionnaire. The final analysed sample, consisting of people over 18 who completed both pre and post-questionnaires, with no missing data on our variables of interest, was N = 73. Measures

The questionnaires used in this study were similar to those used in the dataset from Study 1, with a few modifications.

To measure affect, participants reported their experience of valence and arousal on the 9-point scales of the Self-Assessment Manikin (SAM; Bradley & Lang, 1994) both before and after the performance. Dominance was not measured. Enjoyment of the performance was measured with one overall item on a 7-point Likert scale.

Because of time constraints, it was not possible to administer the full Collective Effervescence Scale (CES; Gabriel et al., 2020) after the performances.

Therefore, four of the eight original items were retained, each rated on a 7-point Likert Scale. For the "Connectedness" sub-facet, items 1 and 2 ("I felt connected to other people present at the event" and "I felt shared emotions") were selected because they showed the highest correlations with the Connectedness construct in our previous theatre study (Simonot-Bérenger et al., 2025). For the "Sacredness"

sub-facet, only items 1 and 2 ("I felt as if there was something special about the event" and "I felt as if the event changed me in some way") were retained following the same logic.

Items relating to connectedness to the audience and performers used in Study 1 were removed (replaced by the CES items), except for "To what extent did you feel a sense of belonging or connectedness with the performers?" (Social Bonding; Brown & Novak, 2007), and one performance-focused item "I feel like I could relate to the performance" (Neurolive).

As in Study 1, other items measured aspects of emotional and intellectual engagement with the performance, including "The performance moved me" from the Aesthetic Emotions Scale (AESTHEMOS, Schindler et al., 2017).

Finally, the questionnaire recorded the participants' perceived relationship to the performers and to other audience members (7-point Likert scale), as well as whether the participant belonged to the EEG-wearing group.

Accordingly, Collective Effervescence was modelled as two theoretically motivated sub-facets (Connectedness, Sacredness), each represented by its original 2-item measure and treated as an observed composite rather than a validated latent factor (see Limitations).

Discussion

The present study aimed to conceptually replicate and extend prior findings on the social foundations of aesthetic enjoyment of the performing arts Below, we first discuss the consistent role that valence plays across both choreographies, as opposed to arousal in theatre, and then examine the distinct contributions of felt social connectedness and sacredness across the two dance performances.

Emotion and Enjoyment

Our theatre studies showed that emotional arousal, regardless of valence, predicted collective effervescence and, ultimately, enjoyment (Simonot-Bérenger et al., 2025). By contrast, in the present dance studies, valence, not arousal, was reliably associated with enjoyment. Importantly, this refers to post-pre SAM change scores: participants who became more positive across the performance also reported greater enjoyment and connectedness. This divergence suggests that the affective dimension supporting appreciation may be context-sensitive: narrative theatre often thrives on high-arousal dynamics that can be collectively amplified in a packed auditorium, and rely on both intensely positive and negatively valenced emotions for storytelling (Huron & Vuoskoski, 2020; Menninghaus et al., 2017) In this way both choreographies promote more self-reflective, low-arousal or dream-like states characterised by attentional absorption, or flow experiences (Csikszentmihalyi, 1990; Freedberg & Gallese, 2007; Lee et al., 2025).

Neural data from Detective Work (Rai et al., 2025) confirm that slow-wave inter-brain synchrony tracked audience engagement, while individual arousal did not (i.e., alpha power). Thus, valence, rather than arousal, may be the affective dimension most relevant to enjoyment in this genre, though collective effervescence mediates enjoyment across both theatre and dance, highlighting a common social mechanism despite context-dependent affective signatures. We note, however, that sensitivity varied substantially across arousal-related paths (Supplemental Materials n°3 and n°6). The MDESs for the direct and total arousal effects ranged from .23 to .37 and generally exceeded the observed estimates.

Lower MDES values obtained for some near-zero indirect effects should be interpreted cautiously because delta-method standard errors can become artificially small when both component paths approach zero. The divergence from the theatre findings is therefore better regarded as an open question than as a confirmed dissociation.

Collective effervescence and enjoyment

In our earlier work, we argued that, even in the highly codified setting of theatre -where spectators refrain from overt interaction -audiences can experience collective effervescence, because their attention is jointly oriented to a common stimulus. Shared attention to emotionally arousing material fosters feelings of closeness and social bonding (Dunbar et al., 2016; Rennung & Göritz, 2015 : Chung et al., 2024), and theatre audiences provide a prime example of such collective emotional niches (Nagatsu & Salmela, 2022). Our results are consistent with the claim that aesthetic experiences in the performing arts are intrinsically social (Barnes, 2000; Walmsley, 2019; Orgs, Caspersen & Haggard, 2016; Orlandi, Cross & Orgs, 2021), echoing recent evidence that enjoyment correlates with subjective connection to co-present others (Koefler et al., 2024). The present studies extend this reasoning in two complementary ways.

In Readings of what was never written, sacredness significantly outperformed connectedness as the proximal mediator between affect and enjoyment (bootstrap contrast: diff = .19, 95% CI [.07, .36], p = .009), reflecting the artistic intentions behind the choreography, which was explicitly conceived to evoke a ritual atmosphere, drawing on the logic of an oracle -an encounter with something that exceeds full comprehension but demands interpretation (Neurolive, Performance 4). Audiences may genuinely reinterpret confusing, indeterminate moments as "larger than the self". Sacredness may thus index a phenomenology of aesthetic awe, resonance, or being moved, translating affect into appreciation even in secular performance contexts.

This interpretation resonates with prior evidence that confusion is not antithetical to engagement but can be a constitutive part of it (Lee et al., 2025). In the aesthetics literature, ambiguity and indeterminacy are known to elicit interest rather than disengagement, provided the spectator feels supported in the encounter (Silvia, 2010 (Silvia, , 2013)). Similarly, models of aesthetic emotions describe how experiences of "being moved" emerge from mixed emotional states that are valued for their intensity and depth rather than for cognitive resolution (Menninghaus et al., 2017). This dynamic may be particularly pronounced in dance, where artistic intentions are often primarily communicated nonverbally, making their uptake dependent on inference and prior experience with the art form (Lee et al., 2025). From this perspective, the sacredness facet may capture the phenomenology of encountering something not fully comprehensible, a pleasurable encounter valued as experience rather than understanding.

In Detective Work, where sacredness items were absent from audience questionnaires, connectedness nonetheless mediated the affect-enjoyment link.

Importantly, the target of connectedness matters: connectedness to performers reliably predicted enjoyment in both live and screened formats, suggesting effective communication between performers and spectators across different presentation formats of the same performance. In contrast, connectedness to other audience members was significant in the Live condition but not in Screening -a contrast we treat cautiously because the corresponding path coefficients were not formally compared across modalities (see Liveness effect section below). These results highlight that connectedness is not unitary: its relative weight depends on performance context, dramaturgical design, and the direction of social attention. We now turn to examine how these facets of connectedness play out across performers-audience coupling and the effects of liveness.

Performers-audience coupling

In Screening, connectedness to performers significantly outperformed connectedness to other audience members as a mediator of the affect-enjoyment pathway (bootstrap contrast: diff = .40, 95% CI [.20, .60], p < .001); in Live, performer-connectedness showed a numerically larger indirect effect, but the difference between the two pathways was not statistically significant (diff = .07, 95% CI [-.06, .21], p = .308).

This supports the claim that when spectators relate to the performers, or experience themselves as permeable to performers' emotions, the sense of collective effervescence is heightened. In Detective Work, Rai and colleagues (2025) demonstrated that inter-brain synchrony in the delta-band peaked precisely when dancers broke the "fourth wall" with direct eye contact, aligning with subjective reports of engagement. This echoes our earlier theatre findings, where spectators who were assigned "accomplice" roles in a partially participative performance reported higher collective effervescence, and the ability to feel the actors' emotions strongly predicted their own affective states, which in turn predicted collective effervescence (Simonot-Bérenger et al., 2025). Together, these converging strands of evidence support the idea that dramaturgical devices which reduce performer-audience distance (e.g., accomplice roles, direct gaze, explicit address) reliably enhance the subjective and neural signatures of collective engagement.

Liveness effect

Our analyses show descriptively higher connectedness and enjoyment in the live modality than in the screening modality -though only the Enjoyment difference survived correction for multiple comparisons (see Modality comparison, above) -paralleling Rai et al.'s (2025) findings of stronger neural synchrony and engagement for live versus recorded performances.

At the same time, a similar statistical mediation pattern (affect → social mediator → enjoyment) appeared in both modalities. We did not formally test whether individual path coefficients differed between modalities, which would require a multigroup SEM with equality constraints; the safest conclusion is therefore that the mediation pattern appeared in both modalities, while Enjoyment was descriptively higher live.

Whether the pathway's magnitude is genuinely amplified by liveness remains untested. We also note both modalities involved social co-presence (the screening was itself a shared cinema event), so this comparison speaks to physical/performance liveness specifically, not to social co-presence.

However, this apparent live/screen contrast was not uniform across social ties. Audience-connectedness emerged as a significant predictor of enjoyment in the live modality but dropped to near zero in the screening modality, despite both being collective experiences (i.e., the screening was a shared cinema event).

Because the path coefficients were not formally compared between modalities, we cannot determine whether this apparent difference represents a genuine effect of liveness. It should therefore be treated as descriptive rather than as a confirmed dissociation. With that caveat, one plausible (non-exclusive) explanation lies in the specific dramaturgical design of Detective Work: dancers repeatedly disrupted the conventional boundary between performers and audience: dancers entering audience seating areas, making prolonged eye contact, or performing absurd facial expressions directed at individual spectators (Rai et al., 2025). These moments of rupture heightened spectators' awareness of one another, fostering a shared attentional field that cannot be reproduced by simply watching the same screen in the same room.

Accordingly, if replicated in an adequately powered sample, the liveness effect on audience-connectedness may not be a generic property of physical co-presence, but a function of dramaturgical devices that direct spectators' attention to both the performance and fellow audience members. These results tentatively nuance the long-standing debate about liveness (Auslander, 2008; Fischer-Lichte, 2008): engagement mechanisms can operate in mediated contexts, but any conclusion that they are strongest when the performance is both physically live and socially shared awaits confirmation with a better-powered design.

Limitations

Several limits should be noted. Fourth, instruments and anchors differed between theatre and dance, precluding direct comparison of mean levels of collective effervescence or enjoyment. For instance, enjoyment in the present studies was captured with a single item, versus a five-item construct in the theatre studies. Similarly, affect was measured as post-pre difference scores rather than as retrospective ratings of in-performance feelings, as in the theatre study. These differences may capture partially distinct processes, complicating comparisons. Future work should adopt harmonised batteries and scale-equating techniques. Relatedly, we conducted sensitivity analyses for the direct, indirect, and total effects in both SEMs, calculating the minimum standardized effect size detectable with 80% power at α = .05 from the corresponding delta-method standard errors (full results in Supplemental Materials n°3 and n°6).

In Study 1, the standardized MDESs were .19-.20 for the Valence indirect effects via Performers-connectedness and .22-.27 for the total Valence effects, below the corresponding observed standardized estimates of .20-.36 and .58-.61, respectively. Sensitivity was lower for the direct and total Arousal effects, whose MDESs ranged from .23 to .37 and exceeded the observed estimates. The non-significant Arousal results therefore should not be interpreted as evidence that arousal plays no role in dance audiences. The bivariate Audience-connectedness → Enjoyment association had an MDES of .30 in Live and .38 in Screening. Because these values do not constitute a formal cross-modality comparison of the adjusted SEM paths, the apparent live-screen difference should be treated as descriptive rather than confirmed.

In Study 2, the standardized MDES was .19 for the Valence indirect effect via Sacredness, compared with an observed standardized indirect effect of .21. For the total Valence effect, the MDES was .33 and the observed estimate was β = .35. The direct Valence → Enjoyment path was estimated less precisely: its MDES was .29, compared with an observed estimate of β = .14. Finally, the indirect effect via Connectedness was close to zero (ab ≈ .00, 95% CI [-.03, .04]), suggesting that this effect was small in the reported metric. A formal bootstrap contrast confirms Sacredness's indirect effect was significantly larger than Connectedness's (diff = .19, 95% CI [.07, .36], p = .009), supporting the dominance of Sacredness as a mediator; the absolute size of the direct Valence effect remains comparatively uncertain.

Finally, our mediation models assume a causal direction, yet all variables were measured concurrently, so reverse or reciprocal pathways cannot be ruled out. Future designs should address this via time-resolved measures or longitudinal measurements. Additionally, our SEMs were saturated and just-identified, precluding model fit indices or tests of parsimony. This strategy was chosen deliberately, as theory-driven replication with small samples is more transparent in a saturated framework than in stepwise pruning. Nevertheless, larger samples will allow nested model comparisons to test whether simpler structures capture the data equally well.

Future directions

Future research should build on the convergences and divergences observed here to clarify when and how collective effervescence underpins enjoyment across the performing arts.

First, systematic designs should measure both facets of collective effervescence (connectedness and sacredness) within the same performances, to determine conditions under which each facet dominates.

Second, time-resolved indices of collective synchrony, such as heart-rate coupling, neural alignment, or acoustic convergence, could be combined with retrospective questionnaires to approximate fluctuations of state effervescence in real time. While effervescence itself cannot be measured continuously via self-report, synchrony-based methods may provide indirect markers of moments of heightened "togetherness", allowing us to test whether peaks of shared affective intensity mediate the translation of emotion into enjoyment.

Third, the performers-audience relationship should be placed at the centre of future designs. Beyond manipulating permeability through dramaturgical devices, performers themselves should be systematically integrated into data collection. Do they also feel collective effervescence? Is their sense of connection correlated with the audience's? And when spectators report "feeling the emotions of performers", is this genuine resonance or projection? Addressing such questions will clarify whether collective effervescence is a phenomenon among spectators only or a genuinely bidirectional coupling between audiences and performers.

Fourth, larger samples would enable model parsimony testing: nested SEMs could establish whether simpler models (excluding non-significant paths) fit as well as saturated ones.

Fifth, future work should formally test measurement invariance across art forms and cultural contexts, to establish whether CE facets are comparable.

Harmonising measurement batteries (full CES, multi-item enjoyment scales) will enable cross-study comparisons with greater validity.

Finally, cross-cultural replications with harmonised tools are needed to disentangle the contributions of art form, audience scale, and institutional context to collective effervescence and performance enjoyment.

In sum, our current data supports the claim that the enjoyment of live performances emerges not only from individual affect but also from the social routing of those feelings, and future work should determine whether this routing is confined to the audience collective or truly circulates bidirectionally across the performer-audience boundary.

Conclusion

We conducted two independent studies in the context of contemporary dance to examine the relationships between emotion, collective effervescence, and enjoyment of live performance. In Detective Work, we found that spectators' affective valence predicted their enjoyment indirectly through feelings of connectedness, with performer-audience connectedness emerging as a stronger mediator than audience-connectedness in Screening, though not formally distinguishable from it in Live. In Readings of what was never written, the inclusion of sacredness items revealed that sacredness dominated connectedness as the proximal mediator of enjoyment, despite their substantial residual covariance (r = .42, 95% CI [.22, .62]).

Although enjoyment is influenced by multiple contextual and aesthetic factors, these findings extend our previous theatre work by demonstrating that collective effervescence remains a robust explanatory mechanism for dance, while the salient facet and dominant social ties vary across contexts.

Performers-audience permeability, whether via direct address or subtler forms of interaction, appears central, suggesting that performers should be actively integrated into the experimental protocols in future research.

Together, these results provide exploratory evidence for the relevance of collective effervescence in understanding audience experience in both dance and theatre, though replication with larger samples and harmonised measures is needed to confirm these patterns. They align with current trends in aesthetics and audience studies that emphasise the social foundations of art appreciation, highlighting models that integrate both spectatorship and performers-audience coupling. Ultimately, these findings underscore live performance as a cultural practice in which emotions are not only shared among audiences but actively circulate between performers and spectators, reinforcing its role as a unique collective and relational form of human expression.

Constraints on generality

The present findings are based on adult audiences of contemporary dance in the United Kingdom, drawn from urban, educated, arts-engaged populations (WEIRD samples). Results may not generalise to mainstream or large-scale performing arts (e.g., opera, rock concerts, musical theatre), to non-Western cultural contexts, or to audiences without prior engagement with contemporary dance. The two choreographies studied are non-narrative. Additionally, findings rely on self-report measures administered immediately post-performance, and the affective predictors are change scores rather than in-performance ratings, which may capture partially distinct constructs from those used in other aesthetic experience research.

First, sample sizes

in our dance studies were modest (Detective Work: N=65 live, N=56 screening ; Readings of what was never written: N=73), much smaller than in our theatre studies (N≈200-400). Bootstrap confidence intervals quantify sampling uncertainty, although estimates may remain unstable with modest samples. Small effects may have gone undetected for lack of statistical power, and power for mediated (indirect) effects is likely lower. Second, covariate information was limited. Unlike in our theatre studies, we could not adjust for empathic traits, prior expertise, or detailed attendance frequency, all factors that significantly explained variance in collective effervescence and enjoyment. Variables such as dance expertise and gender were collected in the original Neurolive project, but were not included in the dataset available for the present analyses. Third, construct coverage differed. Detective Work lacked sacredness items, while Readings of what was never written included only half of the full collective effervescence scale. In addition, reliability was borderline for some subscales (α=.58 for audience-connectedness, α=.66 for connectedness in Study 2), which likely attenuates observed associations. The two collective-effervescence facets were represented by only two items each; Connectedness's latent measurement model could not be estimated without an inadmissible solution (see Measures), so the distinction between Connectedness and Sacredness should be regarded as theoretically motivated but only provisionally supported psychometrically.
