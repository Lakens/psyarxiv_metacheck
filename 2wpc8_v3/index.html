How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Decoding Affect: A Computational Model of Affective Adaptation and Construction

Consider a familiar winter experience: entering a warm room after being out in the cold can feel pleasant, yet that pleasure often fades within minutes. Likewise, the same sweet drink can taste more pleasant after something bitter but less pleasant after something sweeter. These patterns illustrate a common feature of affective adaptation: the affective impact of an experience is not fixed, but changes with continued exposure and prior context.

The concept of affective adaptation, also known as hedonic adaptation, was introduced by Brickman and Campbell (1971) to describe the tendency for affective responses to gradually diminish in the face of sustained or repeated stimuli, often moving toward a baseline level. As a pervasive psychological phenomenon, affective adaptation serves an important evolutionary function: by reducing the cognitive demands associated with processing repetitive stimuli, it frees up limited attentional resources, allowing individuals to more effectively detect and respond to novel threats or opportunities, thereby enhancing their chances of survival in dynamic environments (Bijlsma & Loeschcke, 2005; Lazarus, 1991; Walker et al., 2013). Beyond its evolutionary significance, affective adaptation is also relevant to behavioral economics and decision research because the experienced value of outcomes can change over time and as a function of prior experience, with important implications for valuation, choice, and welfare (Frederick & Loewenstein, 1999; Kahneman & Thaler, 2006). More broadly, such changes in affective experience are relevant to questions of well-being and mental health (Becker et al., 2019; Lucas et al., 2003).

Psychological constructionist approaches provide a distinct perspective for understanding affective adaptation. Broadly, these approaches differ from simple stimulus-response accounts by viewing affective responses not as direct readouts of stimulus properties, but as context-sensitive outcomes of multiple interacting psychological processes (Gendron & Barrett, 2009; Lindquist, 2013; Russell, 2003). This context sensitivity helps explain why affective value can vary across situations and over time. Within this broader tradition, one prominent contemporary formulation is the Theory of Constructed Emotion (TCE), which situates emotion construction within predictive processing and emphasizes the integration of current exteroceptive and interoceptive inputs with prior experience and conceptual knowledge (Barrett, 2017; Barrett et al., 2025). In the present article, we focus specifically on how momentary affective value along the pleasant-unpleasant dimension is constructed and changes over time, rather than on the full construction of discrete or complex emotions or broader, temporally extended affective outcomes such as mood and well-being.

Existing research suggests that affective adaptation is jointly influenced by multiple factors, including reference points, attention, stimulus inherent value, adaptation rate, negativity bias, and time (Frederick & Loewenstein, 1999; Luhmann & Intelisano, 2018; Wilson & Gilbert, 2008). Reference points-typically derived from individuals' prior experiences-serve as comparative anchors for evaluating the affective value of current stimuli (Kahneman, 2000). Early theories formalized this comparative principle, proposing that affective value depends on the discrepancy between current stimuli and reference points (Helson, 1947 (Helson, , 1948 (Helson, , 1964;; Parducci, 1968; Ryder & Heal, 1973). Subsequent research showed that stimulus-reference discrepancy alone does not fully determine affective responses; stimulus inherent value, referring to the perceived inherent goodness or badness associated with a stimulus, also contributes (Frederick & Loewenstein, 1999). Here, inherent value is operationally defined as the baseline affective valuation anchored solely to the adaptation level.

The inherent values of both the target stimulus and reference points influence attention allocation, with greater salience of target inherent value drawing more attention to the target (Cui et al., 2023). Sustained attention, in turn, is associated with slower affective adaptation (Lyubomirsky, 2011). While these findings suggest that attention mediates the link between inherent value and adaptation rate, the precise computational nature of this interplay remains unspecified.

Negativity bias (Baumeister et al., 2001; Kanouse & Hanson, 1972) serves as another critical determinant of affective adaptation. Negative stimuli and environmental deteriorations tend to command greater attentional resources (Carretié et al., 2001; Kätsyri et al., 2016) and exhibit slower adaptation rates than their positive counterparts (Cacioppo et al., 2014). This dual asymmetry suggests that both attention allocation and adaptation rate depend on target inherent value (positive vs. negative) and target-reference discrepancy (improvement vs. deterioration), yet how these two dimensions jointly shape attention allocation and adaptation rate remains quantitatively unspecified.

Taken together, affective construction and adaptation are shaped by multiple interacting determinants. Although prior research has identified many of these key factors and quantified the role of reference points, a quantitatively explicit account of how these determinants interact to generate affective dynamics is still lacking. Crucially, without capturing these interactions, many conventional accounts remain constrained in two respects: (a) explanatory depth: they tend to prioritize the description of phenomenological regularities over the explication of how underlying mechanisms interact to drive them; and (b) explanatory scope: they do not readily account for anomalous phenomena, such as cases in which inherently positive stimuli adapt more slowly than their negative counterparts or environmental improvements fail to elicit pleasure.

Subsequently, we demonstrate the model's generative capacity by simulating both classic affective phenomena (e.g., exponential decay, affective after-reaction, and alliesthesia) and anomalous nonlinearities. Next, we provide a proof-of-concept empirical evaluation of its thermal-affective predictions and examine its broader relevance through a deliberately limited illustrative extension to selected behavioral-economic phenomena. Finally, we benchmark ARIA against nine major theoretical frameworks, spanning from classical descriptive accounts and behavioral economic models to normative paradigms such as active inference.

Theoretical Background

In this section, we review representative theoretical models of affective adaptation, organized into four broad approaches: reference-based frameworks, attention-based accounts, autoregressive and generative approaches to temporal dynamics, and prediction-error-based paradigms. For each category, we examine key contributions and limitations, with particular emphasis on explanatory scope, mechanistic specificity, and formal tractability. Our analysis reveals that, although these approaches explain distinct aspects of affective adaptation, they do not provide a computationally tractable account of how its key determinants interact to generate continuous affective trajectories.

The modified model provides no formal principle for determining how relative change (T -R) and inherent value ( T -0) should jointly shape adaptation rate. This indeterminacy prevents the model from specifying when inherently negative stimuli should exhibit the commonly observed pattern of adapting more slowly than inherently positive stimuli, and when this pattern should reverse, such that negative stimuli adapt more rapidly under particular reference conditions.

Thus, the modified model does not provide a unified theoretical account of both the commonly observed asymmetry of adaptation and its anomalous reversal.

Attention-Based Frameworks

Attention is a key mediator of affective adaptation (Rezec et al., 2004). As a cognitive control mechanism, attention regulates the extent to which stimuli are mentally rehearsed and affectively reinforced (Lyubomirsky, 2011). Salient affective stimuli trigger automatic attentional capture, and sustained attention decelerates affective adaptation (Dolcos et al., 2020; Raymond, 2009; Taylor & Fragopanagos, 2005; Todd et al., 2012; Yiend, 2010).

Although the regulatory role of attention in affective adaptation has largely evaded formal mathematical specification, several descriptive frameworks have established important conceptual groundwork. A particularly influential example is the AREA (attend, react, explain, and adapt) model proposed by Wilson and Gilbert (2008), which posits that affective adaptation unfolds through this sequence. According to this model, individuals attend and react to affective events until they construct a cognitive explanation for them. This explanation process functions to reduce the attention-holding power of the event, thereby facilitating affective adaptation. Conversely, events that evade adequate explanation continue to command cognitive resources, resulting in prolonged affective responses. Autoregressive and Generative Frameworks

Research on affective dynamics has generally adopted two main analytical strategies to characterize temporal patterns in affect. The first relies on summary statistics to describe individuals' affective time series (Dejonckheere et al., 2019; Houben et al., 2021; Sperry et al., 2020; Wendt et al., 2020). Although widely used, this method provides limited insight into the generative processes that shape affective trajectories (Vanhasbroeck et al., 2022). The second strategy centers on computational modeling frameworks designed to capture the underlying dynamics of affect with greater precision and theoretical coherence. Within this literature, autoregressive models represent a foundational class of computational models (Hamaker et al., 2015; Waugh & Kuppens, 2021). Operating under the assumption that current affective states can be predicted by a linear combination of past values, these models leverage their mathematical simplicity to capture three core features of affective time series: fluctuations around a baseline (adaptation level), variability over time, and regulation toward the baseline following significant deviations (Loossens et al., 2021; Loossens et al., 2020).

These capabilities have established their prominence in both empirical research and the statistical literature (Bringmann et al., 2018; Wichers et al., 2020).

Beyond direct autoregressive formulations of affective time series, probabilistic generative models introduce latent situational structures to explain temporal dependencies in affective experience. Ryan et al. (2025) introduced a generative model in which discrete situational states evolve over time according to a Markov process, with each state governing the mean of continuous affective responses. This structure allows the model to capture how affective experiences unfold dynamically as a function of situational changes.

While autoregressive and generative models provide valuable insights into affective dynamics, conventional autoregressive models do not by themselves specify the cognitive mechanisms underlying reference dependence and evaluative asymmetry. The generative model proposed by Ryan et al. (2025) goes beyond purely statistical autoregressive approaches by explicitly linking affective responses to situational states and thereby introducing a more psychologically interpretable structure. Nevertheless, it still encounters theoretical limits regarding the adaptation process itself. Because affective intensity in this framework is tethered to the current environmental state, the model implies that if a situation remains constant, the predicted affective response will persistently fluctuate around a fixed mean. Without an endogenous mechanism for within-state adaptation, such architectures have limited ability to account for the attenuation of affect under sustained and stable stimulation.

Reinforcement Learning-and Active Inference-Based Frameworks

A growing body of work has adopted reinforcement learning (RL) frameworks to model affective fluctuations, often relating affect to expectations and learning-related signals, including reward prediction errors (Bennett et al., 2022; Blain & Rutledge, 2020; Eldar & Niv, 2015; Eldar et al., 2016; Rutledge et al., 2014).

In many of these models, reward is operationalized as the outcome delivered in the experimental task. This approach is well suited to task-based settings, but typically leaves the contribution of current physiological state to affective valuation unspecified. Allostatic accounts, however, emphasize the important role of internal physiological states in shaping valuation and motivated behavior (Schulkin & Sterling, 2019; Sterling, 2012). In particular, the brain continuously weighs the metabolic demands of visceromotor actions, such that the metabolic costs and benefits of a stimulus-situation shape its predictive value. Computational work further shows that the value of learned cues can be dynamically recalibrated by current physiological state (Zhang et al., 2009). Notably, this form of state dependence is compatible with RL frameworks; homeostatic RL, for example, formalizes it by defining reward as reductions in homeostatic drive relative to an internal set point (Hulme et al., 2019; Keramati & Gutkin, 2014).

The RL-based affective models reviewed above have advanced computational accounts of affective dynamics, particularly by elucidating how reward outcomes, expectations, prediction errors, and action-related advantages shape subjective well-being and mood over time. However, relatively less is known about how attention dynamically weights external-and internal-reference valuation discrepancies in constructing affective responses, and how this attentional weighting relates to the rate of affective adaptation.

A different computational perspective on affective and emotional processes is provided by active-inference approaches. Hesp et al. (2021) processes, the broader active inference framework also provides a formal treatment of attention (Feldman & Friston, 2010; Veissière et al., 2020). Attention is typically instantiated through precision weighting of prediction-error signals and, at the policy level, through active sampling, where expected free energy integrates epistemic and pragmatic value to guide information seeking and goal-directed behavior (Veissière et al., 2020; Vossel et al., 2014).

However, the active-inference-based affective models reviewed above do not explicitly formalize how value salience, negativity bias, and attentional weighting jointly shape momentary affective valence. Consequently, they provide limited explanation of how the magnitude of stimulus change shapes the resulting affective valence. For example, a modest reduction in a stimulus that initially elicits pleasure may preserve positive affect, whereas a sufficiently large reduction may induce displeasure. The threshold at which this transition occurs, and how it varies with attentional and reference conditions, remains insufficiently characterized. These explanatory limitations motivate complementary, algebraically tractable frameworks that explicitly link value salience, attentional weighting, and negativity bias to affective construction and adaptation.

Theoretical Limitations and Challenges

The frameworks reviewed above have each illuminated important aspects of affective adaptation: reference-based models highlight contextual evaluation, attention-based accounts emphasize attentional regulation, autoregressive and generative approaches capture temporal structure, reinforcement-learning-based affective models relate affective responses to reward prediction errors, and active-inference-based affective models extend normative inference principles to affective processes. Despite these advances, existing frameworks lack a computationally tractable account with sufficient explanatory scope to encompass the diverse phenomena associated with affective adaptation.

This limitation stems largely from two interrelated challenges. First, in conventional formulations, most frameworks address only a subset of the core determinants of affective adaptation-such as reference points, inherent value, attention, adaptation rate, negativity bias, and temporal dynamics-without jointly formalizing them within a coherent computational framework. Second, and more fundamentally, they generally provide limited mechanistic specification of how these underlying factors dynamically interact to shape affective experience over time.

To address these theoretical gaps, we propose ARIA. By treating attention as a valuedriven computational gate that regulates dual-reference integration, ARIA provides a parsimonious computational framework that incorporates the core determinants of affective adaptation outlined in Figure 1A and explicitly specifies their dynamic interactions. In doing so, the model provides a process-level account of both classic affective phenomena and anomalous nonlinear patterns, while integrating affective construction and adaptation within a single computational framework.

Construction of the ARIA Model

ARIA builds on a prior computational model of experienced-utility evaluation (Cui et al., 2023). The concept of experienced utility, introduced by Daniel Kahneman, refers to the pleasure or pain actually experienced at the time of an outcome (Kahneman & Thaler, 2006; Kahneman et al., 1997). Transient Model: Accounting for Attention, Reference Points, and Inherent Value

Operational Definition of Inherent Value

Within the ARIA framework, inherent value is defined as a time-indexed latent computational state representing the affective evaluation of a stimulus under a minimal reference condition, where evaluation relies solely on the current adaptation level.

Formally, consider an individual who is affectively neutral at time t, and let A L t denote the individual's current adaptation level. Here, AL t serves as an internal allostatic reference.

When the individual subsequently encounters a stimulus S, the affective evaluation computed under this minimal reference condition is defined as the inherent value of stimulus S relative to AL t . Thus, the term "inherent" does not imply that affective value is an intrinsic or fixed physical property of the stimulus. Rather, it denotes the value assigned to the stimulus when no additional external reference is introduced beyond the current adaptation level. Because this value depends on both stimulus characteristics (primarily intensity) and the individual's evolving adaptation level, inherent value is time-dependent: the same stimulus may possess different inherent values at different moments. By definition, the inherent value of the current adaptation level is zero.

Limitations of the Transient Model

While the transient model captures key roles of attention, reference points, and stimulus inherent value in shaping affective responses, three limitations remain.

Limitation 1 (Temporal dynamics): The model does not specify how affective values and reference states evolve recursively over time.

Limitation 2 (Negativity bias): The model does not incorporate asymmetries arising from negativity bias, which manifests in two main respects:

Attention allocation: The model assumes that attention depends only on the magnitude of inherent value and is insensitive to its sign, as reflected in Equation ( 6). That is, stimuli with equal magnitude but opposite valences (e.g., T = 1, R = 1 vs. T = -1, R = 1) receive equivalent attentional weights. This assumption overlooks substantial evidence that negative stimuli disproportionately capture and sustain attention compared to positive stimuli (Delplanque et al., 2004; Fiske, 1980; Hansen & Hansen, 1988; Wentura et al., 2000).

Adaptation rate: The model assumes a constant adaptation coefficient (ε), implying identical adaptation rates for environmental improvement and deterioration. However, empirical findings consistently indicate asymmetric adaptation dynamics across these conditions (Frederick & Loewenstein, 1999; Lyubomirsky, 2011; Morina et al., 2024).

Accounting for Negativity Bias

To formalize the role of negativity bias in affective valuation, we first clarify the theoretical role of the adaptation coefficient ε. In the transient model, ε already regulates the effective contribution of the historical reference to current valuation. ARIA retains this role but gives ε a process-level interpretation motivated by two related observations: sustained attention to affective events has been proposed to prolong affective responses and impede adaptation (Lyubomirsky, 2011; Wilson & Gilbert, 2008), and greater attention to a stimulus can increase the intensity of the affective response it elicits (Mrkva et al., 2019). Specifically, we conceptualize ε as representing the attention-capturing capacity of the historical reference The adaptation coefficient ε therefore has a dual computational role, governing both attention allocation and adaptation rate. As ε increases, attention weight α rises while β declines, shifting attention toward the historical reference and away from the current target.

At the same time, the influence of prior experiences on current affective valuation strengthens, resulting in faster adaptation. Thus, ARIA formalizes the proposed attention-adaptation coupling in which greater reference capture is associated with faster adaptation, whereas sustained attention to the current target is associated with slower adaptation.

Behavioral-Economic Relevance and Illustrative Extension

Beyond the thermal proof-of-concept evaluation, we consider selected findings from behavioral economics as illustrative points of convergence with ARIA's computational principles. One example is the duration-of-current-ownership effect: longer ownership tends to increase the affective impact of subsequent loss (Strahilevitz & Loewenstein, 1998), a pattern qualitatively consistent with ARIA's prediction that longer exposure produces deeper reference updating and a larger subsequent contrast response (Figure 5B). A second example concerns asymmetric reference-point adaptation in securities trading, where reference points have been found to adjust more rapidly following gains than losses (Arkes et al., 2008), broadly corresponding to ARIA's asymmetric adaptation dynamics (Figure 9A). These correspondences do not establish that identical psychological mechanisms operate across thermal-affective and behavioral-economic phenomena, but they suggest that ARIA's computational principles may have broader relevance in domains where valuation depends on prior states and asymmetric adaptation.

We next examine the certainty and reflection effects to illustrate a possible extension of ARIA's valuation principles. Because ARIA is formulated to capture momentary affective valuation of realized changes and its temporal adaptation, rather than decision-making under uncertainty, this analysis addresses a narrower question: whether its reference-dependent and asymmetric valuation principles can yield preference orderings consistent with the certainty and reflection effects. For this purpose, the analysis treats monetary outcomes as externally specified, schematic valuation inputs.

The certainty effect, as postulated by prospect theory (Kahneman & Tversky, 1979), describes the tendency to overweight certain gains relative to probabilistic alternatives. In the classic demonstration involving N = 95 participants, when presented with a choice between a certain gain of 3,000 (Option A) and a gamble offering an 80% chance of gaining 4,000 and a 20% chance of gaining nothing (Option B), 80% of subjects preferred the certain option.

Under the ARIA framework, evaluating the probabilistic outcomes of Option B (4,000 vs. 0) against the reference point defined by the certain outcome of Option A (R =3,000), with an adaptation coefficient set to ε = 0.5, yields psychological values of 3,591 and -1,500, respectively. 2 Consequently, the derived expected psychological value of Option B ( E [ V B ] = 2,573) falls below the certain value of Option A (3,000).

Further parameter sensitivity analysis reveals a critical threshold mechanism: risk aversion (i.e., a preference for the certain option) emerges only when ε > 0.214. This finding of a "tipping point" in preferences resonates with the salience theory proposed by Bordalo et al. (2012). That study introduced a "local thinking" parameter, δ, to capture deviations from the rational benchmark of expected utility, with δ =1 corresponding to that benchmark and δ → 0 representing extreme local thinking. The local thinking coefficient reflects how much attention an individual allocates to highly salient outcomes compared to less salient ones.

Specifically, in their analysis of the Allais Paradox, they showed that risk aversion emerges only when δ falls below a critical value (e.g., δ < 0.73), causing decision-makers to disproportionately focus on the highly salient "downside". From a different computational perspective, the ARIA model offers a complementary interpretation of these dynamics: the "local thinking" (low δ) described by Bordalo et al. (2012) bears a functional resemblance to "rapid adaptation" (high ε) within the ARIA framework. A higher adaptation coefficient ε shifts the reference point toward the high-reward prospect. This shift makes the zero-outcome state more salient by contrast, thereby biasing attention toward the perceived relative loss.

Consequently, risk aversion might be interpreted as a computational consequence of strong reference-dependent adaptation reshaping the psychological value space. The above example focuses on the gain domain, but the same valuation principles can also generate analogous patterns relevant to the reflection effect in the loss domain.

In summary, the empirical evaluation provides initial proof-of-concept support for ARIA's account of dynamic and reference-dependent affective valuation in the thermal domain. The behavioral-economic analyses are included as illustrative extensions of the model's computational principles. Direct tests of the proposed attentional mechanism and applications beyond the thermal domain remain for future research.

Discussion

In this article, we introduced ARIA as a computational framework for explaining how momentary affect is constructed and changes over time. The central contribution of ARIA is to formalize the dynamic interplay among dual-reference valuation, value-driven attention, negativity bias, and recursive reference updating, specifying how these established determinants jointly shape momentary affect and its adaptation over time. This formalization provides a process-level explanation for both classic affective phenomena and anomalous nonlinear patterns, while generating further testable predictions. In what follows, we discuss the theoretical implications of this architecture, its relation to adjacent frameworks, and its boundary conditions and future directions.

Theoretical Implications

From Descriptive Generalization to Algorithmic Explanation

ARIA advances affective adaptation research by moving beyond descriptive generalization toward algorithmic explanation. While extant theories have elucidated key determinants and properties of affective adaptation, there remains a need for an integrative quantitative framework capable of explaining how these factors interact to generate diverse affective phenomena. Consequently, despite incorporating specific psychological constructs, many existing models remain better suited to characterizing affective patterns than to specifying the underlying computational processes that give rise to these patterns.

Consider the most fundamental and paradigmatic phenomenon in affective dynamics: the exponential decay of affective experience under constant stimulation. To reproduce this pattern, classical adaptation theories often adopt single exponential smoothing (Equation 3) from time-series forecasting, whereas many reinforcement-learning-based affective models generally rely on leaky integration of recent prediction errors. Although such methodological choices facilitate data description, they often treat exponential decay as an a priori functional form, offering limited insight into how such trajectories may arise from underlying computational operations. ARIA complements these accounts by formalizing the underlying computational logic that generates these trajectories.

ARIA suggests that macroscopic affective phenomena, such as exponential decay and pleasure-generation thresholds, can emerge algorithmically from the step-by-step interaction of modeled processes, specifically reference-point updating and value-driven attention allocation, rather than being mathematically hardcoded into the system's global output. This interaction allows the system to progressively reduce valuation discrepancies while preserving cognitive resources by withdrawing attention from well-adapted stimuli. At a functional level, this interaction can be understood as supporting a balance between stability and sensitivity, with stability supported by reference-point updating and sensitivity preserved through selective attention to salient deviations.

While ARIA provides an account of affective adaptation, it remains an intermediate-level theory rather than a first-principles account. Unlike more foundational frameworks such as active inference, ARIA does not derive its attentional gating function from biophysical constraints, but treats it as a first-order approximation. By treating attentional gating at a functional level, ARIA captures its role in affective adaptation while maintaining computational tractability.

Reconceptualizing Relativity and Asymmetry: Attention as a Computational Gate

ARIA reconceptualizes two core properties of subjective valuation: relativity and asymmetry. In classical behavioral economic frameworks, most notably prospect theory (Kahneman & Tversky, 1979; Tversky & Kahneman, 1992), relativity (reference dependence) and asymmetry (loss aversion) are typically treated as fixed, independent, and irreducible axioms of subjective valuation. ARIA challenges this view by suggesting that these properties can instead be understood as contingent, dynamically coupled, and conditionally reducible.

Conditional reducibility arises from the fact that, under specific conditions, the contributions of reference dependence and evaluative asymmetry can vanish. As established in the model construction, both relativity and asymmetry are governed by a single parameter, the adaptation coefficient (ε), which modulates attention allocation between the reference point and the target stimulus. As ε approaches zero-e.g., when the stimulus signals a threat to survival-attention is concentrated on the target, leading affective evaluation to collapse from a relative mode to an absolute mode. Similarly, as the divergence in ε between environmental improvement and deterioration approaches zero-e.g., when environmental changes are small relative to the reference-evaluation collapses from an asymmetric mode to a symmetric mode. These boundary relations yield a testable prediction of ARIA: the observable strength of reference dependence and evaluative asymmetry should systematically diminish under the corresponding attentional conditions, approaching zero when attention is concentrated on the target and when attentional gating no longer differs between improving and deteriorating conditions, respectively.

The dynamic coupling and contingency of relativity and asymmetry arise from the same underlying mechanism by which ε modulates attention allocation and adaptation rate. During environmental deterioration, the system tends to reduce ε, thereby slowing adaptation and allocating more attention to the target stimulus; as a result, relativity is weakened.

Conversely, during environmental improvement, increased ε accelerates adaptation and enhances the influence of the reference point, thereby strengthening relativity. This suggests that relativity and asymmetry are intrinsically coupled, and that the strength of relativity is contingent on reference conditions. The expression of asymmetry is likewise contingent on these conditions. As a simple behavioral-economic illustration, consider an individual who has already amassed $1 million: the subsequent gain or loss of $100 exerts a negligible impact on their psychological state, leading to nearly identical adaptation coefficients and thus dissipating evaluative asymmetry. In contrast, for an individual starting with only $10, the same $100 fluctuation represents a profound shift relative to their current state, eliciting a marked divergence in adaptation coefficients and a corresponding intensification of asymmetry.

Within the ARIA architecture, relativity and asymmetry are jointly governed by the attentional gating mechanism formalized in Equations 5, 6, and 10. They therefore constitute two manifestations of the same attentional mechanism operating along different dimensions.

By regulating the relative influence of target and reference values, attention functions as a computational gate through which affective experience is constructed and dynamically updated.

But why does the cognitive architecture require such a dynamic gating mechanism? We suggest that this mechanism can be interpreted as an adaptive response to the trade-off between cognitive efficiency and survival security. Under routine conditions, the system prioritizes cognitive efficiency: by maintaining a "relative mode" (large ε), it filters out predictable information to minimize computational cost. This efficiency, however, comes at the expense of evaluative objectivity: high adaptive efficiency shifts valuation away from the "absolute mode"-a state in which valuation is less dominated by contextual comparison. In contrast, when the system confronts high-intensity negative stimuli or rapid environmental deterioration, it activates the "absolute mode" (small ε) via attentional locking. By suspending adaptation in the face of high-stakes negative stimuli, the system preserves the veridical magnitude of warning signals, helping prevent the organism from fully adapting to conditions (e.g., acute pain or starvation) that require immediate behavioral resolution. Thus, ARIA's dynamic gating mechanism offers a possible account of how the cognitive system flexibly negotiates the trade-off between efficiency and survival under changing environmental demands.

Dual-Reference Valuation: Linking Affective Construction and Adaptation

ARIA provides a computational basis for understanding affective construction and adaptation within a single framework. Within this architecture, current sensory input is evaluated relative to two distinct reference standards: an external reference derived from prior experience and an internal reference associated with allostatic regulation.

These comparisons generate external and internal valuation discrepancies, which constitute the immediate computational inputs to momentary affect. The same discrepancies evolve over time through reference-point updating, producing affective adaptation. At any given moment, external and internal valuation discrepancies can differ in magnitude and may even diverge in sign. The system therefore faces a computational arbitration problem: the relative contribution of each discrepancy to momentary affect must be dynamically weighted rather than determined by either discrepancy alone. ARIA formalizes this arbitration through attentional weighting, such that momentary affect reflects the weighted combination of external and internal valuation discrepancies. The corresponding weights are determined by value salience (governed by | R /T |) and negativity bias (modulated by ε).

On a longer timescale, affective adaptation can be understood as the progressive reduction of valuation discrepancies relative to external and internal reference standards.

When environmental conditions change, the resulting shift in experience generates discrepancies relative to both external and internal reference standards. With continued exposure to the new environmental condition, reference-point updating progressively reduces both discrepancies. This progressive reduction in valuation discrepancies, in turn, decreases the magnitude of momentary affect, bringing affective experience progressively closer to neutrality. In this sense, adaptation reflects the temporal evolution of the same computational quantities that contribute to momentary affective construction.

The attention-weighted dual-reference architecture formalized in ARIA yields a modelderived prediction: equivalent momentary affect need not imply equivalent subsequent adaptation. This is because the same affective value can arise from different combinations of external and internal valuation discrepancies, potentially giving rise to different subsequent adaptation trajectories.

At a broader theoretical level, ARIA's dual-reference valuation account connects with computational accounts of learning and regulation. As discussed in the formal correspondence analysis, the external and internal valuation discrepancies can be formally related to reward and interoceptive prediction errors, respectively, under the simplified Markovian approximation (Figure 3). This correspondence situates ARIA's two valuation discrepancies in relation to familiar constructs in computational neuroscience, allowing their formal roles to be compared across frameworks. Such comparability is important given that related ideas about valuation and adaptation have often developed separately across psychology, computational neuroscience, and behavioral economics. ARIA therefore provides a computational perspective for identifying points of correspondence among these traditions.

Comparison with Related Theories

The following comparisons situate ARIA among representative frameworks by highlighting differences in their explanatory scope and in how they conceptualize and formalize affective adaptation.

Descriptive Foundations of Affect (Opponent-Process, Alliesthesia, and AREA)

Classical descriptive frameworks-including opponent-process theory, alliesthesia theory, and the AREA model-have highlighted important mechanisms of affective adaptation, including opponent processes, homeostatic dependence, and attention. Drawing on insights from these models, we develop ARIA as a quantitative framework that enables the continuous simulation of dynamic affective trajectories.

Opponent-Process Theory (OPT) explains affective and physiological responses as a dynamic balance between two opposing processes (Solomon, 1980; Solomon & Corbit, 1974). When a stimulus is introduced, it triggers an initial, intense reaction, followed by a slower, opposing reaction that counteracts it and restores equilibrium. When the stimulus ceases, the opponent process persists for a short time, producing the affective after-reaction.

Both OPT and ARIA capture affective after-reactions (Figure 5A) and their amplification with increasing stimulus intensity or duration (Figures 5B and 5C). While OPT attributes these phenomena to the structural interaction between primary and opponent processes, ARIA explains them computationally through the dynamic interplay among reference points, stimulus inherent value, and attention. This formulation allows ARIA to characterize affective changes elicited by a broader range of stimulus transitions. Specifically, ARIA can predict whether a decrease in a rewarding stimulus induces displeasure or whether a reduction in an aversive stimulus produces pleasure, whereas OPT is primarily formulated to explain responses to stimulus onset and full stimulus removal.

Alliesthesia theory (Cabanac, 1971; Cabanac et al., 2020) posits that a given stimulus can produce either pleasure or displeasure depending on the individual's internal state. Stimuli that restore homeostasis are experienced as pleasant, whereas those that increase deviation are experienced as unpleasant. Both alliesthesia theory and ARIA argue that affective responses to environmental stimuli depend on the interplay between external conditions and internal states. ARIA highlights the role of attention in modulating affective experiences, which is not explicitly formalized in alliesthesia theory. According to alliesthesia theory, pleasure is derived from useful environmental change. In contrast, ARIA contends that useful environmental change does not necessarily lead to pleasure, depending on both the magnitude of the change and the prechange environment. By formally deriving threshold conditions under which environmental change generates pleasure, ARIA provides a computationally explicit complement to alliesthesia theory.

The AREA model (Wilson & Gilbert, 2008) seeks to explain why individuals' affective responses to events tend to diminish over time. Both AREA and ARIA incorporate attention -a key cognitive factor overlooked by conventional adaptation-level theories. However, they differ in two important respects. First, AREA highlights three principles in affective adaptation (antagonism, reference point, and attention) but does not explicitly specify the structural relationships among them. ARIA delineates these relationships mathematically, specifying that antagonism arises from the interaction between the reference point and attention, with the former determining the latter. Second, AREA places particular emphasis on the role of "explanation" in affective adaptation, a higher-order process that lies beyond ARIA's current scope.

Reference Dependence and Evaluative Asymmetry (Weighted-Average Adaptation-Level Theory, Prospect Theory, and Salience Theory)

Behavioral economic models, such as Prospect Theory (PT; Kahneman & Tversky, 1979; Tversky & Kahneman, 1992) and Salience Theory (ST; Bordalo et al., 2012), primarily conceptualize reference dependence within discrete contexts, whereas psychological models such as Weighted-Average Adaptation-Level Theory (WALT; Ryder & Heal, 1973) conceptualize it within continuous affective dynamics. Despite their shared reliance on reference-dependent valuation, these frameworks differ in how they formalize evaluative asymmetry and the role of attention.

PT formalizes evaluative asymmetry within a reference-dependent value function, but does not specify how this asymmetry dynamically interacts with attention and reference dependence. ST, in turn, places context-dependent attentional weighting at the center of valuation, but does not explicitly model negativity bias as a dynamic mechanism governing both attention allocation and adaptation rate. WALT likewise does not provide a formal account of evaluative asymmetry. As discussed earlier, extensions of WALT that modify the updating coefficient (θ in Equation 3) to allow faster adaptation to improvements and slower adaptation to deteriorations (Strahilevitz & Loewenstein, 1998) capture asymmetries driven by relative change but do not jointly account for asymmetries arising from inherent value.

Taken together, these frameworks capture different influences on valuation, including reference dependence, evaluative asymmetry, and attention, but do not fully specify how these influences interact dynamically. ARIA addresses this gap by specifying how inherent value and direction of change jointly shape asymmetries in attention allocation and adaptation rate, and how changes in attention allocation are linked to changes in adaptation rate. This joint dependence yields a model-derived prediction: under particular target-reference configurations, the typical tendency for negative stimuli to adapt more slowly can reverse, such that an inherently negative target may adapt faster than an inherently positive target when the former represents an improvement relative to its reference and the latter represents a deterioration.

Affective Dynamics in Reinforcement-Learning Models

Several influential reinforcement-learning-based affective models formalize affective fluctuations in relation to recent reward prediction errors (Blain & Rutledge, 2020; Eldar et al., 2016; Rutledge et al., 2014; Villano et al., 2020). Within this line of work, Bennett et al. (2022) proposed the Integrated Advantage Model (IAM), which extends prediction-error accounts by modeling mood (a sustained form of affect) as a temporally integrated estimate of action advantage relative to a state-value baseline. IAM and ARIA share a broad premise that affective dynamics involve temporal integration and reference-based comparison. The core distinction between the two models lies in two aspects. First, ARIA focuses on the construction of momentary affect, whereas IAM explicitly models temporally extended affective states (mood). Second, ARIA primarily captures affective fluctuations arising from the evaluation of current stimuli relative to external experiential and internal allostatic references, whereas IAM treats actions and their feedback as the core drivers of affective evolution. In this sense, IAM and ARIA offer complementary accounts of affective dynamics, with IAM emphasizing feedback-based mood integration and ARIA emphasizing attentiongated affective construction and adaptation.

As noted earlier, the Theory of Constructed Emotion (TCE; Barrett, 2017; Barrett et al., 2025) situates affective construction within predictive-processing and interoceptive-inference frameworks (Barrett & Simmons, 2015; Seth, 2013; Seth & Friston, 2016). While ARIA exhibits structural correspondences with active inference, three important distinctions characterize the two frameworks. First, regarding theoretical objectives and analytical levels, active inference functions as a broad normative and biophysically grounded framework that seeks to explain perception and action through free-energy minimization within hierarchical generative models. Affective models developed within this framework extend these principles to account for aspects of affective experience. By contrast, ARIA is a process-level computational formalism designed to capture the unfolding dynamics of affective experience through a tractable mathematical framework.

Second, the two frameworks differ in how they formalize attention allocation. In active inference, attention is typically formalized as the context-dependent modulation of the precision assigned to prediction errors (Friston, 2010; Vilas et al., 2022). ARIA, by contrast, specifies attentional allocation through an explicit algebraic mapping from value salience to attentional weights.

Third, active-inference-based affective models and ARIA make different predictions about the conditions under which valence reversals occur. For example, Joffily and Coricelli (2013) proposed that valence polarity depends on the direction of free-energy change, with decreases in free energy producing positive valence and increases producing negative valence. Hesp et al. (2021) proposed that positive valence reflects inferences of higher expected precision in action models, corresponding to greater subjective fitness. In contrast, ARIA predicts that valence reversal occurs when the attention-weighted combination of internal and external value discrepancies crosses zero.

In summary, ARIA complements the broader inferential perspective of active inference by offering a process-level computational account of affective dynamics, with an explicit algebraic formulation of attention allocation and valence reversal.

Limitations and Future Directions

Four limitations warrant attention in the current framework: (a) the absence of higherorder cognitive processing, (b) the absence of mood-to-valuation feedback, (c) the absence of prospective forecasting, and (d) reference category indeterminacy in naturalistic settings.

Absence of Higher-Order Explanation

ARIA does not currently model how higher-order explanatory processes dynamically modify affective valuation. This limitation makes the current framework best suited to relatively simple and well-defined affective stimuli, particularly those with direct sensory or homeostatic relevance (e.g., thermal stimuli), where affective value can be systematically related to stimulus intensity.

One possible direction for future development is to integrate ARIA with the AREA model by allowing inherent value (X t ), currently specified as a model input, to be dynamically modulated by factors such as explanatory gap and self-relevance. This would provide a route for extending ARIA to contexts in which affective value is shaped by interpretation, while retaining its current formulation for domains in which value can be more directly specified.

Absence of Mood-to-Valuation Feedback

ARIA does not currently include a sustained mood state that feeds back onto momentary affective valuation or updating. Mood-congruency effects, whereby background affective states alter subsequent affective responses and learning (Eldar & Niv, 2015; Eldar et al., 2016), therefore fall outside the present framework. Accordingly, ARIA's association between sustained target-directed attention and slower adaptation should be understood as a property of its current stimulus-driven architecture, rather than as a general claim that stronger affective responses necessarily imply slower learning or updating.

Absence of a Prospective Forecasting Mechanism

The current ARIA framework is reference-based: momentary affective evaluation depends on current input relative to reference states derived from prior experience and internal regulation, rather than on explicit forecasts of future states. This architecture is well suited to situations in which current and past states provide the primary basis for affective evaluation. However, in structured environments, individuals may also use temporal regularities such as trends or cycles to form expectations about future states that differ from both the current state and previously experienced states. Capturing such prospective expectations would require an additional forecasting mechanism capable of representing temporal structure and generating anticipated future states. Incorporating such a mechanism therefore represents a natural extension of ARIA, allowing prospective expectations to contribute to affective evaluation alongside past-derived reference states.

Indeterminacy of Reference Categories in Naturalistic Contexts

In laboratory settings, reference points can be precisely defined, but real-world experiences often afford multiple plausible standards of comparison. For example, when playing an action video game, an individual may evaluate the experience relative to previous installments in the same series, other action games, games from different genres, or other forms of entertainment. Each comparison standard defines a different reference category and may yield a distinct affective evaluation, thereby limiting the precision of model predictions when reference categories are not explicitly specified. Future research could examine the principles governing reference-category selection when multiple standards are available.

Conclusion

We developed ARIA, a computational model of affective adaptation and construction.

The model characterizes the dynamic interplay among dual-reference valuation, value-driven attention, negativity bias, and recursive reference updating, specifying how these established determinants jointly shape momentary affective valuation and its adaptation over time. On this basis, ARIA offers a process-level account of a range of affective phenomena, including counterintuitive patterns that remain insufficiently explained by conventional frameworks.

More broadly, ARIA suggests that reference dependence and evaluative asymmetry need not be treated as fixed, independent, and irreducible axioms of subjective valuation. Instead, they can be modeled as arising from a shared attentional mechanism, rendering them contingent, dynamically coupled, and conditionally reducible.

Ultimately, ARIA reconceptualizes affective adaptation as the temporal unfolding of affective construction, in which attentional gating shapes the contribution of valuation discrepancies to momentary affect, while reference-point updating progressively reduces both discrepancies over time. Although substantial work remains to fully examine the model's predictions, we hope that ARIA provides a foundation for future empirical and theoretical advances in the study of affective dynamics.

Design and Procedure.Thirty-two graduate students (mean age = 23.59 years, SD = 2.05) participated in the study. A power analysis conducted a priori indicated that this sample size provided sufficient power (> 0.80) to detect the predicted referencedependent effects, based on large effect sizes (Cohen's dz = 1.0) derived from prior thermal comfort research(Cui et al., 2024). All participants provided written informed consent, and the study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board. Detailed participant demographics, recruitment criteria, technical specifications of the environmental chambers, and comprehensive calibration and anchoring protocols are documented in Supplemental Material S2. To capture the dynamic evolution of affect, the experiment utilized a step-change paradigm within a suite of three physically adjacent climate-controlled chambers maintained at 29°C, 25°C, and 33°C, respectively. The experimental protocol consisted of four continuous 10-minute phases designed to impose varying degrees of homeostatic deviation and relief: Phase 1 (29°C), Phase 2 (25°C), Phase 3 (33°C), and Phase 4 (25°C). This sequence allowed for the observation of affective responses to both the onset of stress (deviations from the 25°C neutral baseline) and the subsequent recovery (relief). During the session, participants continuously reported their real-time thermal pleasure using a digital visual analog scale (VAS) ranging from -5 (Extremely Unpleasant) to +5 (Extremely Pleasant), with 0 representing neutrality. Data were sampled at 60 Hz to capture fine-grained temporal dynamics. Prior to the main data collection, all participants underwent a standardized anchoring and calibration procedure to align their subjective neutral baseline and response dynamics with the scale.

Test Strategy. To assess ARIA's predictive performance, we separated the data into calibration and prospective test intervals. The calibration set (0-21 min), comprising Phase 1, Phase 2, and the onset of Phase 3, was used to estimate the relevant adaptation coefficients (ε). The remainder of the experiment (21-40 min) served as the prospective test set for evaluating the model's ability to predict affective responses without further reparameterization. A key component of the calibration process was the use of a peak-alignment strategy to accommodate the initial response-stabilization interval (15-66 s) observed following the onset of each phase. This interval may reflect multiple processes not explicitly modeled by ARIA. By aligning the model's initial predicted affective value for each calibrated phase with the corresponding observed peak response, we excluded the initial ramp-up period from the modeled trajectory and focused the empirical evaluation on the post-peak adaptation. Under this strategy, model coefficients were identified by anchoring the model to key functional landmarks: for Phases 1 and 2, the model was parameterized to align both predicted peak and terminal values with their respective experimental observations; for the Phase 3 onset, the coefficient was adjusted to match the initial observed peak.
