To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 16 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power Analysis Check

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Lifelong bilingualism has been proposed as a cognitively enriching experience that mitigates agerelated declines in cognitive abilities; however, the evidence remains mixed due to issues such as small samples, absence of effects in younger samples, and not accounting for confounding factors (e.g. education). Here we address these issues by examining adults aged 18-88 from the Cam-CAN dataset in two ways: by comparing monolinguals to bilinguals (N=390) and by examining bilingual experience as a continuous factor with a newly devised metric (N=460). Generalised additive models assessed fluid reasoning, ecological multitasking, and verbal abstraction. Increased age was associated with declined fluid reasoning and multitasking, but better verbal abstraction. Compared to monolinguals, bilinguals showed better preserved fluid reasoning in older age, suggesting decelerated cognitive decline, and higher verbal abstraction scores. Our results help refine theories on the effects of bilingualism on cognition by showcasing that are likely to emerge in older age only.

1 Introduction

Global population ageing is one of the most significant demographic trends in the twenty-first century, with the proportion of older adults increasing rapidly worldwide. Ageing is accompanied by widespread changes in brain structure and function, including reductions in grey and white matter volume (e.g., Giorgio et al., 2010), declines in white matter integrity (e.g., Voineskos et al., 2012), and alterations in large-scale functional networks (e.g., Vidal-Piñeiro et al., 2014). These neural changes are often associated with age-related declines across multiple cognitive domains, such as attentional control, working memory, and the management of complex tasks (Zanto & Gazzaley, 2017). Accordingly, considerable research has focused on identifying factors that may help maintain cognitive functioning and promote healthy ageing.

One influential framework for explaining individual differences in resilience to age-related cognitive decline is cognitive reserve (Stern, 2002 (Stern, , 2009)). Cognitive reserve refers to the capacity to cope with age-related brain changes or pathology through the flexible recruitment of neural networks and cognitive strategies that are relatively resistant to disruption. Rather than directly preventing neural deterioration, cognitive reserve is thought to influence how effectively individuals adapt to such challenges, thereby reducing their impact on observable cognitive performance. Individuals with greater cognitive reserve may therefore maintain relatively stable cognitive functioning despite agerelated changes in the brain. A growing body of research suggests that cognitive reserve may be shaped by a range of lifelong experiences, including educational attainment (Meng & D'Arcy, 2012), occupational complexity (Foubert-Samier et al., 2012; Lee et al., 2020), and engagement in cognitively stimulating activities (Cheng, 2016). These experiences are thought to accumulate across the lifespan and contribute to the preservation of cognitive functioning in later life.

Bilingualism has been proposed as another potential contributor to cognitive reserve because of the cognitive demands involved with managing more than one language. Broadly, bilingualism can be defined as the regular use of two or more languages in everyday life (Grosjean & Li, 2013).

Evidence suggests that a bilingual individual's languages remain simultaneously active even when only one language is currently in use, often without conscious awareness (Marian & Spivey, 2003; Schwartz & Kroll, 2006; Wu & Thierry, 2010). Gallo and Abutalebi (2024) further argued that bilingualism may occupy a distinctive position among cognitive reserve-enhancing experiences because the management of multiple languages is a pervasive activity that can be maintained in everyday life across the lifespan. Managing this cross-language co-activation requires bilingual speakers to recruit cognitive-control processes to select the target language, resolve interference from the non-target language, and monitor the linguistic context. Repeated engagement of these processes has been associated with adaptations in brain structure and function (Pliatsikas, DeLuca, et al., 2020) and has been proposed to support executive functioning (Ware et al., 2020), including inhibitory control, attentional switching and working memory (Ware et al., 2021) across the lifespan.

Over the past few decades, a substantial body of research has investigated whether bilingualism is associated with cognitive performance during adulthood and ageing. However, much of this work has examined younger and older adults as separate age groups, and relatively few studies have investigated how bilingualism may influence cognitive trajectories across the adult lifespan. Among younger adults, evidence for bilingual-monolingual differences is mixed, with some studies reporting benefits associated with bilingualism and others finding no reliable group differences.

Among studies reporting positive findings, Bialystok et al. (2004) compared Simon-task performance in monolingual and bilingual adults and found evidence of more efficient executive control among young bilingual adults. Better behavioural performance, including faster responses, has also been reported in bilingual young adults across several cognitive-control paradigms, including the Flanker task (Costa et al., 2009), the Simon task, and the Attention Network Test (Woumans et al., 2015). More recently, Manica et al. (2026) found that young bilingual adults performed significantly better than monolingual adults on measures of working memory, inhibitory control, and cognitive flexibility.

In contrast, several studies have found little or no evidence of bilinguals outperforming monolinguals in young adults. Kousaie and Phillips (2012) observed no significant group differences across the Stroop, Simon, or Flanker tasks. Similarly, Paap & Greenberg (2013) reported comparable performance between bilingual and monolingual participants on the Simon, Flanker, and colourshape switching tasks. Likewise, Naeem et al. (2018) found no reliable bilingual benefits on either the Simon task or the Tower of London task after controlling for socioeconomic status, despite initially observing faster responses among bilingual participants. Consistent with these findings, a large-scale investigation involving 11,041 participants by Nichols et al. (2020) found little evidence of general benefits of bilingualism across a broad battery of executive-function tasks. More recently, Planckaert et al. (2026) focused on the concept of language dominance, which could be defined as the relative balance or imbalance between languages in daily use. Asymmetrical language use is proposed to place greater demands on cognitive control than balanced bilingual engagement. They found no reliable associations between language dominance and either executive functioning or fluid intelligence in their younger adult group. This inconsistency is also reflected in meta-analytic evidence, which has generally found limited support for robust and generalisable bilingual benefits in executive functioning among adults (Donnelly et al., 2019; Lehtonen et al., 2018).

In older adulthood, positive cognitive findings associated with bilingualism have been reported somewhat more frequently than in younger adulthood, although the overall evidence remains mixed.

For example, Bialystok and colleagues found that bilingual older adults outperformed their monolingual peers on executive-control tasks, including the Simon task and a modified antisaccade task (Bialystok et al., 2004 (Bialystok et al., , 2006)). Similarly, Schroeder & Marian (2012) reported better performance among bilingual older adults on both a Simon task and a picture-scene recall task assessing episodic memory. Using the Flanker task, Abutalebi et al. (2015) also found that bilingual participants responded faster than monolingual participants on both congruent and incongruent trials.

More recently, Achaa-Amankwaa et al. (2023) reported that multilingual older adults outperformed monolingual adults on measures of working memory, interference suppression, and phonemic fluency after controlling for educational attainment and engagement in daily activities. The multilingual advantage was particularly pronounced on the backward digit-span task, which places relatively high demands on attentional control. Further evidence that the cognitive correlates of bilingualism may depend on specific dimensions of language experience was provided by Grueso et al. (2026). In a large sample of cognitively unimpaired adults spanning mid-to-late adulthood, individuals with high second-language proficiency performed better than low-proficiency bilinguals on semantic verbal fluency and processing speed, irrespective of whether the second language had been acquired early or later in life. Early high-proficiency bilinguals additionally showed better verbal short-term memory. These findings suggest that bilingualism-related associations may be domain-specific and more strongly related to sustained proficiency and active language use than to age of acquisition alone.

Nevertheless, null and inconsistent findings have also been reported in healthy ageing. Several studies have failed to identify significant behavioural differences between bilingual and monolingual older adults on executive-function tasks (e.g., Kievit et al., 2014; Kousaie et al., 2014), indicating that bilingual cognitive effects are not consistently observed even in later adulthood. Planckaert et al. (2026) similarly found only minimal and inconsistent associations between language dominance and cognition in their healthy ageing group, and these associations were no longer reliable after age and educational attainment were taken into account. Thus, although bilingualism-related cognitive differences may become more detectable during periods of increased cognitive vulnerability, they are not consistently observed even in later adulthood and may be smaller than the effects of age, education, and other background characteristics.

A major limitation of the existing literature is that relatively few studies have examined cognitive ageing continuously across the adult lifespan. Most research has instead compared discrete groups of younger or older adults, with limited attention to the intervening stages of adulthood. This distinction may be important for understanding the apparent differences between findings in younger and older populations. Executive functions are typically close to their peak during early adulthood, which may limit the sensitivity of behavioural tasks to bilingualism-related differences because of ceiling effects (van den Noort et al., 2019; Ware et al., 2020). In contrast, any contribution of bilingualism to cognitive reserve may become more apparent later in life, when age-related cognitive changes create greater demands for compensatory cognitive processes. If this account is correct, the trajectories of bilingual and monolingual adults would be expected to begin diverging at some point between early and later adulthood, potentially during midlife, when age-related cognitive decline starts to become detectable (Dohm-Hansen et al., 2024). Identifying this potential point of divergence, or inflection in the relationship between bilingualism and cognitive performance, is therefore important for explaining why bilingual-monolingual differences may be absent in younger adults but more apparent in older adults. Modelling age continuously across the lifespan provides a means of examining whether and when such differences emerge and can therefore contribute to theories of bilingualism and cognitive reserve.

A further source of inconsistency concerns how bilingualism has been defined and measured. Many studies have relied on binary comparisons between bilingual and monolingual groups, despite substantial variation in the nature and extent of bilingual experience within bilingual populations (Luk & Pliatsikas, 2016). Luk and Bialystok (2013) argued that bilingualism cannot be adequately represented as a simple categorical variable because the distinction between monolingual and bilingual speakers is inherently imprecise and depends, at a minimum, on the interaction between language proficiency and patterns of language use. Bilingualism is now increasingly understood as a multidimensional and dynamic experience shaped by factors such as age of acquisition, language proficiency, duration of immersion, patterns of language use, and language-switching practices (Pliatsikas, DeLuca, et al., 2020). Accordingly, de Bruin (2019) emphasised that bilingual participants should be characterised in considerably greater detail, including not only their proficiency and age of acquisition but also their patterns of language use and switching and the interactional contexts in which their languages are used. Nevertheless, these dimensions have not always been measured or reported systematically, and the criteria used to assign participants to bilingual and monolingual groups have varied considerably across studies (Surrain & Luk, 2019).

Such variability may obscure associations that are present only under particular configurations of bilingual experience and may contribute to inconsistent findings across studies. These limitations do not necessarily mean that categorical comparisons should be abandoned. Categorical and continuous approaches can address different research questions, and incorporating both categorical distinctions and continuous variation may provide a fuller account of bilingualism (Kremin & Byers-Heinlein, 2021). In the present study, we therefore used these approaches to address complementary questions: whether cognitive performance differed between bilingual and monolingual participants, and whether it was associated with variation in cumulative bilingual experience.

Other established contributors to cognitive reserve may also account for some of the inconsistent findings. Educational attainment and engagement in cognitively stimulating leisure activities have both been associated with greater cognitive reserve and better-preserved cognitive functioning in later life (Cheng, 2016; Foubert-Samier et al., 2012; Lee et al., 2020; Meng & D'Arcy, 2012). These factors may differ between language groups but have not always been adequately controlled.

Consequently, an observed bilingual-monolingual difference may reflect bilingual experience, broader demographic and lifestyle characteristics, or some combination of these factors. Studies examining bilingualism and cognitive ageing should therefore account for these potential confounding influences when estimating the independent contribution of language experience. A small number of recent studies have begun to address these methodological limitations by treating age and bilingual experience as continuous variables. For example, Incera and McLennan (2018) examined adults across the lifespan and found that younger age and greater bilingual experience were independently associated with reduced Stroop interference, although the interaction between age and bilingual experience was not significant. Neither Age nor bilingual experience was significantly associated with Flanker interference after controlling for baseline performance. More recently, Grundy and Pujols-Beltran (2026) examined 312 adults aged 18-80 years using continuous and multivariate measures of bilingual experience and executive functioning. Greater bilingual experience, characterised by earlier second-language acquisition and higher second-language proficiency, was positively associated with executive-function performance, with the most consistent association observed for task switching. Earlier second-language acquisition was also associated with better non-verbal task-switching performance specifically among older adults who reported a history of COVID-19, suggesting that bilingual experience may contribute to executive-function resilience following COVID-19.

Together, these recent studies demonstrate the value of moving beyond simple bilingualmonolingual comparisons by considering individual dimensions of bilingual experience and examining cognition across broader periods of adulthood. However, few studies model age continuously across the adult lifespan while also incorporating detailed, dimensional measures of bilingualism and established contributors to cognitive reserve. Existing studies also vary considerably in the cognitive abilities assessed and in how bilingual experience is operationalised. Further research using large lifespan cohorts, complementary categorical and dimensional measures of bilingualism, a broad range of cognitive outcomes, and measures of education and cognitively stimulating activities is therefore needed to establish how bilingualism-related associations vary across adulthood and across cognitive functions.

The present study addresses these issues by investigating the relationship between bilingualism and cognitive performance across the adult lifespan using data from the Cambridge Centre for Ageing and Neuroscience (Cam-CAN) cohort. Three cognitive domains were examined: fluid intelligence, assessed using the Cattell Culture Fair Intelligence Test (Cattell & Cattell, 1960); complex multitasking, assessed using task completion and total time deviation in the Hotel Task (Manly et al., 2002); and verbal abstraction, assessed using the Proverb Comprehension task (Wachholz & Yassuda, 2011). Bilingualism was examined both categorically, by comparing bilingual and monolingual participants, and dimensionally, using a continuous measure of cumulative language experience. Education and engagement in cognitively stimulating leisure activities were included to account for other established contributors to cognitive reserve, while sex and handedness were included as additional covariates. Together, these analyses tested whether bilingualism was associated with task performance and whether this association varied with age and across cognitive domains. We also examined whether these associations remained after accounting for other contributors to cognitive reserve and relevant individual characteristics.

We hypothesised that: (a) cognitive task performance would decline with increasing age; (b) bilingual participants would demonstrate better cognitive performance than monolingual participants, and greater bilingual experience would be associated with better performance within the dimensional measurement; and (c) bilingualism would moderate age-related cognitive trajectories, such that bilingual status and greater bilingual experience would be associated with slower age-related declines in cognitive performance.

2 Method

2.1 Participants

Participants were drawn from Stage 2 of the Cambridge Centre for Ageing and Neuroscience (Cam-CAN) project (Shafto et al., 2014; Taylor et al., 2017), a population-based lifespan cohort recruited from the general population of the United Kingdom, through the Dementias Platform UK (DPUK) Data Portal. The Cam-CAN sample spans a wide adult age range (18-88 years) and comprises healthy individuals screened for neurological and psychiatric conditions. The present study focused on participants who completed the behavioural measures of interest, including the Cattell Test, the Hotel Task, and the Proverb Comprehension task (see section 2.5 for descriptions of these tasks), as well as questionnaires assessing participants' second-language experience, highest education reached, and engagement with leisure activities. Sex and handedness information of participants were also included in the analysis. Handedness was treated as a continuous variable ranging from -100 to 100, with lower values indicating stronger left-handedness and higher values indicating stronger right-handedness. Only participants with complete data across these measures were included in the current analyses, and therefore sample sizes varied amongst different tasks and analyses (see respective tables in the Results section). Exclusion procedures and data preprocessing steps for each task are described in the following sections.

2.2 Language

Second language experience was assessed using the Lifetime of Experience Questionnaire (LEQ; Valenzuela & Sachdev, 2007) administered in the Cam-CAN protocol. The LEQ evaluates selfreported foreign language use across three life stages: young adulthood (18-30 years), mid-life (30-65 years), and late life (65 years and above). For each life stage, participants reported the frequency of their foreign language use using a seven-point ordinal scale. To facilitate the quantitative analysis, we rescaled such that the original value of 0 was treated as NA, and the remaining values range from 0 to 5 (i.e., original 1→0, 2→1, …, 6→5), with higher scores indicating greater foreign language use. The scoring scheme is presented in Table 1.

Participants were excluded from the analyses if they had missing responses or values outside the predefined valid response range at any life stage. For example, responses coded as 888 or 999 were treated as invalid for questionnaire items with valid response options ranging from 0 to 6. After rescaling and applying criteria, we first grouped the participants according to their language use. Participants with a rescaled answer of 0 in all their life stages were categorised as "monolingual" (N=152), and participants with a rescaled answer of 1-5 in all life stages they have reached were categorised as "bilingual" (N=238). This classification allowed us to have a group of "pure" monolinguals and a group of long-term bilinguals with varying but uninterrupted bilingual experiences.

2.5 Behavioural tasks

Of the variety of tasks included in Cam-CAN, the present study focused on three tasks assessing partially distinct cognitive domains relevant to executive functioning: Cattell Test, Hotel Task, and Proverb Comprehension.

Fluid intelligence was assessed using the standard form of the Cattell Culture Fair Test (Scale 2, Form A; Cattell, 1971). The test comprises four non-verbal subtests involving series completion, classification, matrices, and topological reasoning. Across these subtests, participants were required to identify missing elements, detect abstract patterns, and infer logical relationships among visually presented stimuli. The test is designed to assess reasoning and problem-solving abilities in novel situations while avoiding the influence of language, cultural background, and acquired knowledge.

Each correct response was awarded one point, yielding a maximum total score of 46.

The Hotel Task was used to assess executive functions related to complex planning and multitasking (Manly et al., 2002; Shallice & Burgess, 1991). It has been suggested that compared with other labbased multitasking paradigms, this task more closely reflects assessment of real-world multitasking demands and therefore has greater ecological validity (Torralva et al., 2012 (Torralva et al., , 2013)). Participants were instructed to imagine that they were managing a hotel and were required to allocate their time across five different hotel-related tasks in a paper-and-pen format. Participants were given 10 minutes to work on the tasks, which was insufficient to complete tasks in full. Thus, participants were expected to organise their time to ensure they attempted all tasks. The outcome measurements of this task were the number of tasks attempted (range 0-5) and the total deviation, which was calculated as the sum of the absolute differences between the time spent on each of the five tasks and the target duration of 120 seconds per task. For tasks that were not attempted, the time spent was coded as 0 seconds, corresponding to an absolute deviation of 120 seconds. Higher values indicated less effective time allocation.

Verbal abstraction ability was assessed using a Proverb Comprehension task, which has been used as a measure of abstract reasoning, a subcomponent of executive function (Wachholz & Yassuda, 2011).

Abstract reasoning involves the ability to extract generalised meaning from symbolic or figurative information, such as interpreting proverbs. Participants were presented with three commonly used English proverbs and were asked to explain their meanings in their own words. The three proverbs were "One swallow does not make a summer", "Still water run deep", and "A bird in the hand is worth two in the bush". Responses were scored using a three-point scale. An incorrect or "don't know" response was scored as 0, a partially correct but literal interpretation was scored as 1, and a fully correct abstract interpretation was scored as 2. Total scores ranged from 0 to 6.

2.6 Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using R (version 4.5.2). To examine the effects of age and bilingualism on performance across the three experiments, while controlling for the effects of education and engagement in leisure activities, we employed Generalised Additive Models (GAMs) using the bam() function from the mgcv package. This approach was selected for its computational efficiency in handling complex model structure and its ability to model potential non-linear developmental trajectories. Such non-linear patterns are consistent with evidence from large-scale behavioural and neuroimaging studies showing that age-related changes follow nonlinear trajectories across adulthood (Antal et al., 2025). Separate GAMs were run for each task.

Data was analysed in two ways. First, following an analytical approach previously used to model nonlinear age-related patterns in a developmental sample (Pliatsikas, Meteyard, et al., 2020), a between-group analysis was conducted using bilingualism as a categorical variable (Monolingual vs.

Bilingual). The primary between-group analysis focused on the effects of bilingualism, age, and their interaction. Bilingualism was included as a categorical predictor, while age was modelled as a smooth term to capture non-linear lifespan effects. An interaction between age and bilingual status was incorporated to examine whether age-related cognitive trajectories differed significantly between monolingual and bilingual participants. Sex, handedness, education level and engagement in leisure activities were included as covariates to control for potential confounding influences. To account for the unequal sample size between the two language groups, case weights were applied and calculated as the inverse of the total sample size of each group, thereby ensuring that monolingual and bilingual participants contributed equally during model fitting. To facilitate the interpretation of group-specific effects, bilingualism was treated as an ordered factor. The Monolingual group was initially specified as the reference level (baseline) to estimate the smooth difference between groups. The analysis was subsequently repeated with the Bilingual group specified as the reference level to assess the reliability of the observed interaction effects. Statistical outputs from both model parameterisations are reported, and an age-by-language interaction was considered reliable only when it was statistically significant under both reference-level specifications. For tasks showing a reliable age-bylanguage interaction, a follow-up model was fitted to further unpack the interaction. This model included a main effect of bilingual status and separate smooth terms for age within the Monolingual and Bilingual groups. This allowed the age-related trajectory of each language group to be estimated independently and enabled the direction and shape of the between-group developmental differences to be characterised. Significant age-by-language interactions were visualised using the plot_smooth() function from the itsadug package, with 95% confidence intervals displayed around the estimated trajectories.

Second, a complementary within-group analysis employed the continuous BEI, allowing language experience to be modelled as a graded variable across the sample. Age and BEI were modelled as separate smooth terms, and their joint effect was modelled using an interaction smooth. Sex, handedness, education level and leisure activity engagement were again included as linear parametric covariates. To facilitate interpretation of the continuous models, fitted interaction surfaces were visualised using the vis.gam() function from the mgcv package. Age-by-BEI interaction effects were displayed as three-dimensional perspective plots (plot.type = "persp"). All models were fitted using

Restricted Maximum Likelihood (REML) to obtain unbiased estimates of the smoothing parameters.

For all statistical tests, the significance threshold was set at p < .05.

4 Discussion

The present study used the Cam-CAN lifespan dataset to examine whether bilingualism moderates age-related cognitive changes across different domains, including fluid intelligence, complex multitasking, and verbal abstraction. Importantly, the analyses accounted for other potential contributors to cognitive ageing and cognitive reserve, including educational attainment, engagement in leisure activities, handedness, and sex. Overall, our findings suggest that the relationship between bilingualism and cognitive ageing is domain specific and may depend on how bilingualism is operationalised. Compared with monolingual participants, bilingual participants showed a more favourable age-related trajectory in fluid intelligence, characterised by a less pronounced decline across adulthood, as well as higher overall performance in proverb comprehension. By contrast, bilingualism was not significantly associated with either measure of complex multitasking performance, as measured by the Hotel Task. Moreover, performance in our tasks was not significantly predicted by a newly developed continuous measure of cumulative bilingual experience across the lifespan. Thus, categorical bilingual status was associated with selected aspects of cognitive performance and ageing, whereas individual variation in bilingual experience, as quantified by our BEI, did not reliably account for either overall cognitive performance or interact with agerelated cognitive trajectories. The following sections discuss the findings for each cognitive domain in greater detail.

The first outcome examined was fluid intelligence, measured using the Cattell Culture Fair Intelligence Test and closely associated with executive functioning (Aken et al., 2016) and has previously been used in research involving older bilingual adults (Luo et al., 2013). Three principal findings emerged. First, fluid intelligence declined significantly with age, demonstrating a robust age-related reduction in non-verbal reasoning ability across the adult lifespan. Second, bilingual participants showed higher overall performance than monolingual participants. Third, the significant age-by-bilingualism interaction indicated that the age-related trajectories of fluid intelligence differed between the two language groups. Follow-up models estimating separate age smooths confirmed significant age-related changes in both monolingual and bilingual participants. In other words, although Cattell performance declined with age in both groups, the estimated decline was less pronounced among bilingual participants. Importantly, the estimated trajectories suggested that the difference between bilingual and monolingual participants was relatively small at younger ages but became increasingly pronounced in later adulthood. Although the present analyses did not identify a specific age at which the group difference first becomes statistically reliable, this pattern may help reconcile the apparently inconsistent findings reported in studies of younger and older adults. Indeed, studies restricted to younger adults have been less likely to detect bilingual-monolingual differences, possibly because performance is still relatively well preserved and potentially at ceiling. By contrast, group differences have been reported more often in older adults as age-related decline places increasing demands on cognitive resources. This is broadly compatible with previous studies reporting better preserved cognitive performance among bilingual older adults (e.g., Abutalebi et al., 2015; Gold et al., 2013).

Our finding helps reconcile both types of evidence, suggesting that the benefits of bilingualism on cognition are likely to emerge later in life, when some cognitive abilities are no longer at their peak.

This pattern is consistent with cognitive-reserve accounts proposing that lifelong cognitively demanding experiences may become particularly relevant when age-related neural and cognitive changes begin to challenge existing cognitive resources (Stern, 2002 (Stern, , 2009)). With specific reference to bilingualism, the sustained management of two language systems may repeatedly recruit monitoring, selection, and interference-control processes (Bialystok & Craik, 2022; Liu et al., 2016), potentially supporting the maintenance of higher-order reasoning abilities as people age.

However, this pattern was not reproduced when bilingual experience was modelled dimensionally using the continuous BEI across the sample. Cattell performance again declined significantly with age, but neither the main effect of the BEI nor the age-by-BEI interaction was statistically significant. This discrepancy may reflect limitations in the dimensions of bilingual experience captured by the BEI. Alternatively, the BEI may not fully capture the specific dimensions of language experience most relevant to cognitive control and reasoning, such as the age at which active bilingual language use began (Luk et al., 2011), the frequency and type of language switching (Luk et al., 2012), or the interactional contexts in which the two languages are used (Luk & Bialystok, 2013).

The second task was the Hotel Task, which assesses complex planning and multitasking (Kievit et al., 2014; Manly et al., 2002). Performance showed significant age-related changes on both outcome measures. Specifically, participants attempted fewer tasks and showed greater deviation from the optimal time allocation as age increased, indicating age-related declines in multitasking efficiency and strategic time management across adulthood. However, bilingualism did not emerge as a significant predictor of either measure of Hotel Task performance, or did it significantly moderate the corresponding age-related trajectories. These findings provide no evidence that bilingualism was associated with the maintenance of complex multitasking abilities across the adult lifespan. One possible explanation for the absence of bilingualism-related effects concerns the sensitivity of the Hotel Task. The task has primarily been used to identify relatively pronounced executive difficulties in clinical populations, including individuals with frontotemporal dementia, multiple sclerosis, ADHD, and bipolar disorder (Cardoso et al., 2015; Torralva et al., 2012). It may therefore be better suited to detecting clinically meaningful impairments than the potentially subtler cognitive effects associated with bilingual experience in a neurologically healthy lifespan sample such as Cam-CAN.

In addition, successful Hotel Task performance depends on the coordinated operation of multiple processes, including planning, prospective memory, self-monitoring, task initiation, switching, and time management. Any bilingualism-induced effects on more specific executive-control processes may consequently have been diluted within this complex, multidimensional measure of performance.

The third task was the Proverb Comprehension task, which assesses verbal abstraction and the ability to derive general meanings from non-literal language (Wachholz & Yassuda, 2011). In this task, we observed a significant age-related pattern, with performance generally improving across adulthood.

Bilingual participants also showed significantly better performance than monolingual participants.

However, the age-by-bilingualism interaction did not reach statistical significance, providing insufficient evidence that the age-related trajectory of proverb comprehension differed between the two language groups. When bilingual experience was represented continuously using the BEI, age remained a significant predictor of proverb comprehension. However, similar to the Cattell Task, neither the main effect of BEI nor its interaction with age reached statistical significance. The agerelated improvement observed in the present study is consistent with previous research reporting preserved or enhanced proverb comprehension in older adults (e.g., Kljajevic, 2022), although previous findings remain mixed, with some studies reporting declines in later life (e.g., Uekermann et al., 2008). One possible explanation is that proverb comprehension depends not only on abstract verbal reasoning but also on accumulated semantic knowledge and life experience, both of which continue to develop throughout adulthood. Because proverbs are culturally transmitted expressions, older adults may also have had greater lifetime exposure to the specific expressions included in the task, so the proverbs may have been more familiar or culturally relevant to them. The higher overall performance of bilingual participants may similarly reflect differences in linguistic, educational, or cultural experience; for example, bilingual participants may have received formal education in English or may have developed greater experience interpreting meaning across languages and cultural contexts. Such experiences may be associated with greater flexibility in interpreting nonliteral meanings. For example, Horvat et al. (2022) found that multilingual speakers were more likely than monolingual speakers to find meaning in novel metaphors. Although this finding provides a possible basis for interpreting the present group difference, its relevance to proverb comprehension remains to be established. However, the Cam-CAN data available for the present study did not provide sufficiently detailed information about participants' language of education, country of origin, migration history, cultural background, or exposure to English-language proverbs. It is therefore not possible to determine whether the observed group difference was attributable specifically to cognitive benefits linked to bilingual language use, or to associated educational and cultural experiences.

Overall, the present findings do not support uniform effects of bilingualism across different cognitive domains. Instead, bilingualism-related associations varied across the three cognitive outcomes examined. The clearest evidence for benefits of bilingualism emerged for fluid intelligence, for which bilingual and monolingual participants showed significantly different age-related trajectories.

Bilingual status was also associated with higher overall proverb comprehension performance, although it did not significantly moderate the corresponding age-related trajectory. By contrast, neither measure of complex multitasking was significantly associated with bilingualism. Considered together, these contrasting patterns suggest that any cognitive benefits of bilingualism may be selective and depend on the cognitive processes and task demands involved, rather than reflecting a general enhancement across cognition. This pattern is consistent with cognitive-reserve accounts, which propose that reserve accumulates through lifelong experience and becomes increasingly evident when age-related decline begins to challenge cognitive functioning (Stern, 2002 (Stern, , 2009)). The Cattell findings are compatible with this interpretation, as the bilingual and monolingual trajectories were relatively similar at younger ages but became more clearly separated in middle and later adulthood, a pattern that aligns with suggestions that middle adulthood might represent a critical window for early identification and preventative intervention, potentially offering greater efficacy than interventions initiated in later life (Dohm-Hansen et al., 2024). However, this pattern did not generalise to the other outcomes: Hotel Task performance declined with age without evidence of bilingual moderation, whereas proverb comprehension generally improved across adulthood. The findings therefore highlight the importance of considering the distinct lifespan profile and processing demands of each cognitive ability when evaluating bilingualism within a cognitive-reserve framework.

The contrasting results obtained from categorical bilingualism measurement and the continuous BEI raise a separate methodological issue concerning how bilingualism is operationalised. The two approaches should be viewed as complementary rather than interchangeable, because they may capture different aspects of bilingual experience and cannot be assumed to produce the same pattern of results. In the present study, categorical bilingual status was associated with Cattell and Proverb Comprehension scores, whereas the BEI showed no significant associations with any cognitive outcome or age-related trajectory. Neither approach provides a complete or inherently superior measure of bilingualism: categorical comparisons obscure variation within language groups, while the BEI is constrained by the language-experience information available in Cam-CAN. Using both approaches therefore makes these differences visible and prevents conclusions from depending on a single operationalisation of bilingualism.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. Most importantly, Cam-CAN was not originally designed to provide a comprehensive characterisation of bilingual experiences. The available data did not include detailed information about participants' first and additional languages, language of formal education, objective language proficiency, age of acquisition, frequency and context of language use, language-switching practices, migration history, or broader cultural background. It was therefore not possible to determine whether bilingual participants were native English speakers who had acquired an additional language or speakers who had acquired English as an additional language.

Such constraints are not uncommon in secondary datasets used to investigate bilingualism (Pliatsikas, Meteyard, et al., 2020). Nevertheless, findings from these datasets remain valuable because they provide large samples and broad age coverage that are difficult to obtain in purposebuilt bilingualism studies. At the same time, the resulting measurement noise reinforces the need for more detailed and systematic assessment of bilingual experience in future research. The available language-background information also constrained the construction of the BEI. The index could only incorporate the dimensions of bilingual experience recorded in Cam-CAN and should therefore not be regarded as an exhaustive measure of bilingualism. Thus, the absence of significant effects of bilingualism when viewed as a continuous measure may reflect limitations in the underlying language-background data and/ or the construction of the BEI rather than demonstrating that individual variation in bilingual experience is unrelated to cognition.

A further limitation is that the present analyses were based on cross-sectional lifespan data. The estimated age-related functions describe differences between individuals of different ages rather than cognitive changes occurring within the same individuals over time. Accordingly, the terms "agerelated decline" and "trajectory" refer to cross-sectional age patterns and should not be interpreted as direct evidence of longitudinal change. Cohort differences in language exposure, cultural experience, and other unmeasured characteristics may also have contributed to the observed age patterns.

Longitudinal research is therefore needed to establish whether bilingual experience predicts withinperson rates of cognitive change and to determine more precisely when any divergence between bilingual and monolingual participants develops.

Large secondary lifespan datasets such as Cam-CAN nevertheless have considerable value for research on bilingualism and cognitive ageing. They provide broad age coverage, relatively large samples, and multiple cognitive measures, allowing questions to be addressed that may be difficult to In conclusion, the present study demonstrates the value of examining bilingualism and cognitive ageing across the adult lifespan, across cognitive abilities with different processing demands, and through complementary measures of bilingualism. A key finding was that the effects of bilingualism on cognition may be observable in specific domains and at certain ages. The contrasting findings obtained using categorical bilingual status and the continuous BEI indicate that different operationalisations may capture partly distinct aspects of bilingual experience and may therefore lead to different conclusions regarding bilingualism-cognition associations. Large secondary lifespan datasets can make an important contribution to this area, but their findings must be interpreted in light of the scope and quality of the available language-background information. Future progress will require the combination of lifespan modelling, comprehensive cognitive assessment, detailed characterisation of multilingual experience, and longitudinal research designs.

Such studies should include comprehensive language histories, objective assessments of proficiency, detailed measures of language use and switching across interactional contexts, language of education, migration and cultural background, and established contributors to cognitive reserve.
