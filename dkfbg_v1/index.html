Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

LGBTQIA+ people and couples increasingly pursue parenthood through assisted reproductive technologies (ART), yet the psychological, relational, and institutional processes involved remain dispersed across reproductive health, family studies, psychology, sociology, and LGBTQIA+ scholarship.

Objective: This structured narrative review synthesizes recent literature on how LGBTQIA+ individuals and couples construct parental projects through ART, with attention to reproductive aspirations, route selection, relational negotiation, minority stress, institutional encounters, and family narrative construction. Methods: PubMed and Scopus were searched for publications from 2016 to 2026. After deduplication, 260 unique records were screened, 126 underwent refined relevance appraisal, and 49 articles were retained as the mapped corpus. From these, 17 index studies were purposively selected for in-depth discussion as they offered particularly rich evidence on psychosocial, relational, and institutional processes. The review followed a structured narrative synthesis approach guided by the Scale for the Assessment of Narrative Review Articles (SANRA). Results: Three family-making domains structured the synthesis: lesbian couples' donor conception, IVF, and reciprocal IVF pathways; gay fatherhood through surrogacy and thirdparty reproduction; and transgender, gender-diverse reproductive planning, including fertility preservation and institutional barriers. Across domains, LGBTQIA+ ART family-making involved six recurring psychosocial tasks: aspiration, feasibility appraisal, route selection, relational negotiation, institutional navigation, and narrative integration. Conclusion: ART is not only a set of reproductive techniques but a psychosocial and institutional context in which LGBTQIA+ parental aspirations are translated into reproductive pathways, negotiated through relationships, shaped by clinical and legal conditions, and incorporated into family narratives. These findings support cautious, pathway-sensitive, LGBTQIA+-affirmative fertility counselling.

Introduction

Parenting among LGBTQIA+ people has increasingly become visible not only as a matter of family diversity, but also as a matter of reproductive decision-making. This review uses LGBTQIA+ as an umbrella term for lesbian, gay, bisexual, transgender, queer, intersex, asexual and other sexual and gender minority people, and retains sexual and gender minority (SGM) where it reflects the terminology of the studies cited or refers to broader research populations whose identities are not always specified (Ammann, 2025). For many of these individuals and couples, becoming a parent is not a linear transition from desire to conception. It frequently involves the active construction of a parental project: choosing a reproductive route, negotiating genetic and gestational connections, interacting with fertility clinics and legal systems, deciding how to involve donors or surrogates, and anticipating how family stories will be narrated to children, relatives, schools and communities (Carneiro et al., 2017; Weidenbaum et al., 2025).

In this review, the concept of a parental project refers to the psychosocial, relational, and institutional process through which individuals and couples imagine, plan, negotiate, and organize pathways toward parenthood. Unlike narrower concepts such as reproductive intention or fertility desire, which model the movement from disposition to intention to behaviour (Ajzen, 1991; Miller, 2011), parental projects encompass not only the wish to have children, but also the practical, relational, ethical, and symbolic work involved in constructing a viable and meaningful family trajectory (Fantus & Newman, 2022; Thompson, 2005). In LGBTQIA+ contexts, parental projects frequently involve decisions regarding reproductive routes, genetic and gestational involvement, donor or surrogate relationships, legal recognition, disclosure practices, and anticipated family narratives (Kazyak & Woodell, 2016). The concept therefore allows ART decision-making to be understood not as a single reproductive choice, but as an ongoing process of family-making shaped by identity, relationships, institutions, and social recognition.

Assisted reproductive technologies (ART), including donor insemination, in vitro fertilization (IVF), intracytoplasmic sperm injection (ICSI), reciprocal IVF, gamete donation, fertility preservation and surrogacy, are therefore not merely technical procedures in LGBTQIA+ family-making. They are embedded in identity work, relational negotiation and social recognition. Studies of lesbian couples, for example, describe decisions about known versus unknown sperm donors, reciprocal IVF, racialized donor matching and the meaning of the nongestational mother's role (Cai, 2026; Côté & Lavoie, 2019; Getrajdman et al., 2017; Newman, 2019; Ryan & Moras, 2017; Shaw et al., 2023; Somers et al., 2017; Costa et al., 2020). Research with gay and bisexual men has similarly shown that surrogacy is experienced as a pathway to fatherhood that requires decisions about cross-border care, genetic connection, egg donors, surrogates, disclosure, social legitimacy and financial feasibility (Blake et al., 2017; Fantus & Newman, 2019; Hanson et al., 2022; Nebeling Petersen, 2018 , Weidenbaum et al. 2025).

At the same time, ART decision-making takes place within a wider field of minority stress.

LGBTQIA+ intended parents may encounter heteronormative forms, professionals who presume heterosexual family models, legal uncertainty around filiation, moral scrutiny of surrogacy, limited insurance coverage, or geographic inequality in access (Jacobson, 2018). These conditions do not simply limit access; they shape how people imagine and evaluate possible routes to parenthood. In this sense, decision-making is both psychological and structural. It involves desire, identity, hope, loss, ambivalence and coping, but also the practical assisted family-building; focused only on adoption or foster care without ART relevance; addressed legal or political issues without psychosocial, relational, or institutional relevance to parental projects; or focused exclusively on child outcomes without connecting these outcomes to parental decision-making, reproductive pathways, or family-making processes.

Study Selection and Corpus Refinement

The PubMed search identified 89 records and the Scopus search identified 253 records, resulting in 342 records before deduplication. After 82 duplicates were removed, 260 unique records remained for title and abstract screening. During initial screening, 114 records were classified as potentially relevant, 12 as unclear, and 134 as not aligned with the eligibility criteria. The 114 potentially relevant records and the 12 unclear records were retained for refined relevance appraisal, resulting in 126 records undergoing further assessment.

Following refined appraisal, 49 articles were retained as the broader mapped corpus. These 49 studies constituted the main interpretive base of the review and were used to map the range of populations, reproductive pathways, psychosocial processes, relational issues, and institutional contexts represented in the literature. From this broader mapped corpus, 17 index studies were purposively selected for in-depth thematic discussion. The term "index studies" is used to indicate studies that were especially informative for conceptual synthesis, not studies that were statistically representative of the literature or selected through a reproducible systematic-review weighting procedure.

The 17 index studies were selected because they met four interpretive criteria: (1) direct relevance to LGBTQIA+ ART, donor conception, fertility preservation, surrogacy, or assisted family-building; (2) substantive attention to parental aspirations, reproductive decision-making, route selection, family-making, or reproductive planning; (3) rich discussion of psychological, relational, or institutional processes; and (4) contribution to one of the three family-making domains used to organize the Results section: lesbian couples' donor conception, IVF, and reciprocal IVF pathways; gay fatherhood through surrogacy and third-party reproduction; and transgender and gender-diverse (TGD) reproductive planning, including fertility preservation and institutional barriers.

The number of index studies discussed per domain -six in each of the first two domains and five in the third -was an analytic sampling strategy intended to support balanced cross-domain comparison in a narrative synthesis. It should not be interpreted as evidence that the three domains are equally represented, equally developed, or equally weighted in the broader literature. On the contrary, the broader mapped corpus is markedly uneven across the three domains (15, 24, and 5 articles respectively, with 5 further articles addressing cross-cutting populations; see Results and Supplementary Table S3). The near-symmetrical structure of the in-depth discussion was adopted in response to this imbalance rather than in disregard of it.

Title and abstract screening and refined relevance appraisal were conducted by the first author and subsequently reviewed with the second author through consensus discussion. Records classified as unclear were discussed until a decision was reached regarding inclusion in the mapped corpus, contextual use, or exclusion. Because this was a structured narrative review rather than a systematic review, no inter-rater reliability statistic was calculated. Uncertainties were resolved by returning to the eligibility criteria, the review question, and the intended role of each article within the broader mapped corpus or the in-depth index-study synthesis.

Data extraction and synthesis

For the 49 mapped articles, a structured extraction table was created to document authorship, year, country or context, study design, population or family configuration, ART or familybuilding pathway, decision-making relevance, psychosocial components, relational components, institutional or structural factors, and contribution to the review. Studies were also classified according to their role in the review as either index studies for in-depth thematic discussion or contextual mapped studies used to refine, qualify, or support the interpretation. In addition, a small number of sources not retrieved by the search are cited in the Introduction and Discussion as background literature. These are used only to situate the review within wider theoretical and clinical debates and are never treated as evidence for the synthesis.

The thematic narrative synthesis proceeded in three stages. First, the mapped studies were organized by population focus, reproductive pathway, and family-making configuration. Second, the 17 index studies were examined in depth to identify recurring psychosocial, relational, and institutional patterns within and across the three domains. Initial descriptive codes included reproductive aspirations, access constraints, route comparison, donor and surrogate meanings, genetic and gestational involvement, couple negotiation, institutional encounters, minority stress, disclosure, and family narrative construction. Third, these descriptive codes were grouped into six recurring psychosocial tasks: aspiration, feasibility appraisal, route selection, relational negotiation, institutional navigation, and narrative integration.

The six-task model was therefore not used as a priori coding framework. It was developed through iterative comparison of recurring patterns across the index studies and checked against the broader mapped corpus. The model should be read as a heuristic and interpretive framework for organizing recurring concerns in LGBTQIA+ ART family-making, not as a validated decision-making scale, causal model, longitudinal sequence, or empirically tested pathway.

Results

This review retained 49 articles as the broader mapped corpus and selected 17 index studies for in-depth thematic discussion. The synthesis is organized into three domains: (1) lesbian couples' donor conception, IVF, and reciprocal IVF pathways; (2) gay fatherhood through surrogacy and third-party reproduction; and (3) TGD reproductive planning, including fertility preservation and institutional barriers.

These domains are used as analytic groupings rather than as mutually exclusive or homogeneous categories, and one index study sits at their boundary. Tordoff et al. (2026) recruited sexual and gender minority people assigned male at birth, a sample that includes cisgender gay and bisexual men alongside non-binary people and transgender women. It is placed in the third domain because its analytic frame is assigned sex at birth and structural access to family-building rather than partnership configuration, and because the majority of its participants are transgender or gender-diverse. Studies of predominantly cisgender sexual minority populations were not included in this domain and are drawn on as contextual literature where relevant.

Distribution of the mapped corpus across domains

The 49 mapped articles were unevenly distributed across the three family-making domains. Twenty-four addressed gay fatherhood through surrogacy and third-party reproduction, 15 addressed lesbian couples' donor conception, IVF, and reciprocal IVF, and 5 addressed TGD reproductive planning. A further 5 concerned mixed or cross-cutting LGBTQIA+ populations and were not assigned to a single domain. The distribution of each mapped study is reported in Supplementary Table S3.

This asymmetry is a characteristic of the field rather than an artefact of the search. It also explains the analytic decision described in the Method. Discussing index studies in proportion to the mapped corpus would have given the surrogacy literature roughly five times the space of the TGD literature, reproducing in the synthesis the imbalance that the review seeks to make visible. Discussing a comparable number of index studies in each domain instead allowed each configuration to be examined in similar depth. The trade-off should be stated plainly: in the second domain, six studies were selected from 24, whereas in the third domain the five index studies constitute the entire mapped literature, so that no selection was in fact exercised there. Findings reported for the third domain therefore rest on a narrower and less developed evidence base than those reported for the other two.

A separate integrative model, presented after the thematic synthesis, organizes the findings into six recurring psychosocial tasks: aspiration, feasibility appraisal, route selection, relational negotiation, institutional navigation, and narrative integration. The three domains represent bodies of literature, whereas the six tasks represent an interpretive framework derived from cross-domain comparison. Note. ART = assisted reproductive technologies; IVF = in vitro fertilization; TGD = transgender and gender-diverse. Tordoff et al. (2026) is placed in the third domain because its analytic frame is assigned sex at birth and structural access to family-building, and because 34 of its 56 participants are transgender or gender-diverse; the study is nonetheless a boundary case spanning the second and third domains. Study designs are reported in Supplementary Table S3.

Theme 1. Lesbian couples, donor conception, IVF, and reciprocal IVF

The first theme concerns lesbian couples and families composed of two mothers who face conception through donation, IVF, reciprocal IVF, agreements with known donors, and crossborder reproductive care. Reproductive choices in these studies were carefully shaped by converting desires for motherhood into feasible, relationally important, and institutionally valid pathways. Gouya et al. (2025) directly explored the parental aspirations of lesbian couples and single women seeking motherhood through sperm donation, focusing on intentional motherhood as a central reproductive project. Among lesbian couples who use donor sperm, donor choice is only one of several decisions to be made, as reported by Somers et al. (2017), who offered one of the most pertinent analyses on the subject. The formation of a family, in this context, includes the choice of donor type, the treatment to be followed, the roles that each partner will play, and the family narratives that are already planned. Côté and Lavoie (2019) deepened this theme by investigating families with lesbian mothers who resort to known donors, revealing that procreation can involve several participants and that the donor's participation requires careful negotiation about kinship ties, limits, and the legitimacy of the parental role.

IVF and reciprocal IVF have added additional layers of psychological and relational meaning. According to Shaw et al. (2023), reciprocal IVF can have an important meaning, as it allows both mothers to be physically involved in reproduction, one through genetic contribution and the other through gestation. Cai (2026), however, warned against reading the use of IVF only as a matter of personal preference, highlighting how the acceptance of IVF by lesbian women can also illustrate stratified reproduction and pragmatic overmedicalization. Chautems et al. (2025) situated the ART trajectories of lesbian couples within normative cross-border reproductive health and care systems, highlighting that the choice of route is shaped by unequal access, legal structures, and institutional assumptions. Together, these studies demonstrate that the choice of ART among lesbian couples is not limited to the selection of a medical technique. It involves the negotiation of maternal desires, reproductive corporeality, the meaning of donation, couple involvement, institutional recognition, and structural limitations.

Theme 2. Gay fatherhood, surrogacy, and third-party reproduction

The second thematic domain deals with the paths that gay men take in pursuit of fatherhood through surrogacy and assisted reproduction. This research indicates that surrogacy goes beyond a simple method of conception; it is configured as a family formation process where fatherhood, genetic connection, relational involvement, and family legitimacy are discussed and negotiated. Blake et al. (2017) investigated the emotions and motives that lead gay fathers to opt for surrogacy, highlighting the emotional and motivational dimensions of this practice as a means of becoming fathers. As Fantus and Newman (2019)indicated, the motivations for gay fathers to seek surrogacy include intentional fatherhood, reproductive participation, and genetic connection. Fantus and Newman (2022) expanded this discussion by exploring the procreative identities of men in same-sex relationships, highlighting how gay men can attribute meaning to fatherhood through the concepts of reproduction, legitimacy, masculinity, and relational continuity.

Relational mediation with third parties was crucial for this area. According to Carone et al. (2018), family dynamics between gay fathers, surrogate mothers, and egg donors were investigated, as well as the fathers' choices about when and how to disclose their identity and the children's perceptions of their surrogate origins. Jadva et al. (2019) analyzed the relationships between surrogate fathers and mothers in national and international arrangements, highlighting how the quality of the relationship and the level of contact vary according to the type of arrangement and the context in which they are embedded. Nebeling Petersen (2018) positioned gay fatherhood in the context of transnational commercial surrogacy, showing that fatherhood is formed within global reproductive systems that are shaped by legislation, mobility, financial resources, and recognition.

In all these studies, the decision about surrogacy appeared as relationally dense and institutionally situated. Gay men do not choose surrogacy as a simple option; they construct fatherhood from choices related to genetic connection, their bonds with others, the legal recognition of paternity, how they choose to reveal this information, and the narrative they create around their family.

Theme 3. Transgender and gender-diverse reproductive planning and fertility preservation

The third domain addresses family-building among transgender and gender-diverse people, focusing on fertility preservation, reproductive planning, and structural barriers. Although this field of study is less consolidated compared to the literature on lesbian couples and gay parents, it is essential for understanding how gender identity, corporeality, medical context, and institutional competence influence the formation of parental projects. According to Tordoff et al. (2026), when it comes to forming a family, individuals from sexual and gender minorities who were assigned male at birth face both open paths and barriers, and reproductive planning is influenced by both desire and structural limitations. Maiorano et al. (2025), in a systematic review and thematic metasynthesis of seven qualitative and mixed-methods studies, gathered evidence on fertility preservation in trans and non-binary people and identified clinical, psychosocial, and structural obstacles. Mezzalira et al. (2025), in a systematic review of 39 empirical studies, found that the desire and intention to become a parent can change over the course of gender-affirming care and identity formation. Both are syntheses of multiple primary studies and therefore rest on a wider evidential base than the single-sample studies discussed alongside them. Stuyver et al. (2021) highlighted, based on ten years of experience in fertility treatments and reproductive options for trans men, how reproductive choices are intertwined with bodily, medical, and prospective issues. Payne and Erbenius (2018) investigated how conceptions of transgender parenthood manifest in fertility and family planning services in Sweden, showing that official reproductive rights do not necessarily translate into clinical practices that affirm and guarantee access to these rights. A mixed-methods study of cisgender gay, bisexual, and lesbian military service members (Boedeker et al., 2025) documents institutional barriers to family planning in a highly regulated healthcare setting. It is discussed here as contextual literature rather than as an index study, since its sample is cisgender and its concerns are institutional rather than specific to gender-diverse embodiment. They are not limited to technical aspects related to gametes or the stage of treatment, but encompass themes such as identity, corporeality, uncertainty, access to health, stigma, and institutional recognition.

Integrative synthesis

In all three themes, a recurring pattern of interpretation was identified. The choice regarding LGBTQIA+ ART can be seen as a path that goes from aspiration to feasibility, through feasibility to route selection, from route selection to interpersonal negotiation, from interpersonal negotiation to navigating within institutions, and finally, from institutional navigation to the construction of the family narrative. The specific nature of these processes varied among the groups, and the sequence should not be interpreted as fixed or applicable to all. Instead, the pattern identifies recurring psychosocial tasks that emerged across the index studies and were checked against the broader mapped corpus. Prospective parents questioned, in various ways: Can I visualize myself as a father/mother? What reproductive options are available to me? How will biology, gestation, donation, surrogacy, gender identity, and partnership influence the constitution of the family? How will institutions validate or limit this project? How will this family's story be told to others and, when applicable, to the future child? This integrative pattern indicates that parental projects in LGBTQIA+ parental projects pursued through ART can be understood as psychologically meaningful processes, constructed through relational negotiation and situated within institutional contexts.

Discussion

This review mapped 49 articles on LGBTQIA+ parental projects and ART and examined 17 index studies in depth. Across this literature, ART emerges as more than a set of resources for conception. Throughout the reviewed literature, ARTs are described as a formative family infrastructure through which parental expectations are imagined, converted into viable reproductive options, negotiated within family and kinship relationships, institutionally mediated, and incorporated into family narratives. The index studies differ in kind as well as in focus. Two are systematic reviews synthesizing multiple primary studies; the remainder are single empirical studies, most of them qualitative and based on small purposive samples, together with one theoretical contribution. Conclusions drawn from the syntheses therefore rest on a broader evidential base than those drawn from any individual study, and the difference is noted where it bears on the interpretation. (Blake et al., 2017; Cai, 2026; Chautems et al., 2025; Fantus & Newman, 2019; Gouya et al., 2025; Mezzalira et al., 2025; Somers et al., 2017; Tordoff et al., 2026).

This perspective is important because a significant amount of literature on reproductive decision-making continues to characterize these decisions as individual or couple choices among the alternatives presented. This synthesis indicates that, with regard to reproduction in LGBTQIA+ contexts, choices are rarely limited to preference. The availability of donors, the recognition of non-gestational or non-genetic parents, the legal status of surrogacy, access to fertility preservation, the competence of clinical services, and the symbolic meanings attributed to biology, gestation, and kinship influence family configurations (Carone et al., 2018; Côté & Lavoie, 2019; Jadva et al., 2019; Payne & Erbenius, 2018; Shaw et al., 2023). The decision regarding ART should be understood, in this sense, as a stage in a broader parental project: a psychosocial and relational process in which individuals outline a family trajectory that is viable, meaningful, and socially acceptable.

Therefore, the conceptual model presented in Figure 2 should be understood as an interpretive framework and not as a validated trajectory. The six tasks-aspiration, feasibility appraisal, route selection, relational negotiation, institutional navigation, and narrative integration-do not necessarily follow a fixed sequence (Duff & Goedeke, 2024). Instead, they are recurring psychosocial tasks that appear across the index studies. Instead, they are recurring psychosocial tasks that appear across the index studies. The task of aspiration is supported by research on the desire to be a parent, the motivations for becoming a parent, and the reproductive intentions of lesbian couples, gay men, and transgender or gender-diverse individuals, as well as other sexual and gender minorities (Blake et al., 2017; Gouya et al., 2025; Mezzalira et al., 2025; Tordoff et al., 2026). Feasibility appraisal and route selection are particularly evident in research on gamete donation, IVF, reciprocal IVF, surrogacy, and fertility preservation (Cai, 2026; Chautems et al., 2025; Shaw et al., 2023; Stuyver et al., 2021). Research on known donors, relationships with surrogates and egg donors, disclosure, and procreative identity (Carone et al., 2018; Côté & Lavoie, 2019; Fantus & Newman, 2022; Jadva et al., 2019; Somers et al., 2017) informs the tasks of relational negotiation and narrative integration. Institutional navigation, in turn, is a recurring theme in research addressing heteronormative fertility care, surrogacy in transnational contexts, barriers to fertility preservation, and disparities in access to reproduction (Chautems et al., 2025; Maiorano et al., 2025; Nebeling Petersen, 2018; Payne & Erbenius, 2018; Tordoff et al., 2026).

Positioning the model in relation to existing frameworks

The six tasks described above overlap with several established literatures, and the model's contribution depends on making that overlap explicit rather than obscuring it. Minority stress theory (Meyer, 2003; Frost & Meyer, 2023) provides the most direct account of how distal stigma, expectations of rejection, concealment, and internalized stigma translate into psychological burden among sexual and gender minority people, and extensions of the framework specify the mediating processes involved (Hatzenbuehler, 2009). That account is presupposed rather than replaced here. What minority stress theory was not designed to do is describe the sequence of practical and relational work through which a family is assembled: it identifies stressors and their consequences, treating reproduction as one domain in which stigma operates. In the present model, is not a task but a condition running across all six tasks, most visibly in institutional navigation, where heteronormative documentation, unequal access, and uncertain legal parenthood convert stigma into concrete constraints on what can be attempted. The model also makes room for tasks that are not stress responses at all -aspiration and narrative integration involve meaning-making, anticipation, and identity work that a stress framework does not aim to capture.

Decision-oriented models of reproduction supply the second point of comparison. The theory of planned behaviour (Ajzen, 1991) and fertility-intention frameworks such as the traits-desires-intentionsbehaviour sequence (Miller, 2011) and cognitive-social accounts of fertility intentions (Bachrach & Morgan, 2013) model the movement from disposition to intention to behaviour, while reproductive life planning translates a similar logic into clinical practice (Johnson et al., 2006). These frameworks assume, largely reasonably for the populations they were built on, that the central question is whether and when to have a child, and that the means of conception is either given or a downstream detail. The literature reviewed here suggests that this assumption does not transfer. For LGBTQIA+ people pursuing ART, feasibility appraisal and route selection are not preliminaries to the reproductive decision but constitutive parts of it: donor conception, reciprocal IVF, known-donor arrangements, domestic or transnational surrogacy, and fertility preservation carry different relational, legal, embodied, and financial implications, and the route chosen shapes who counts as a parent and how the family will later be described. Similarly, intention-behaviour models treat access as a moderator, whereas in this corpus institutional recognition frequently determines whether an intention can be enacted at all (Cai, 2026; Chautems et al., 2025; Payne & Erbenius, 2018). Finally, these frameworks close at the behavioural outcome, while the reviewed studies indicate that the work continues into disclosure and family narrative long after conception (Carone et al., 2018; Somers et al., 2017).

A third literature, drawn from kinship studies and the social study of reproductive technologies, has already documented much of what the model organizes: the negotiation of biological and social relatedness in families formed outside heterosexual reproduction (Weston, 1991), the coordination of technical, legal, and relational elements through which parenthood is achieved (Thompson, 2005), the reworking of kinship through assisted conception (Franklin, 2013; Mamo, 2007), and the stratification of reproductive access along lines of class, race, and legal status (Colen, 1995). These accounts are richer than the present model in ethnographic detail and in their theorization of kinship. They are not, however, organized as psychological processes, and they were not written to be used by clinicians. The model's intended contribution is translational: it renders these insights as a small set of recurring tasks that can be recognized in a counselling encounter, while retaining the structural conditions that purely intentionbased models omit.

Stated plainly, the model does not claim to identify processes that were previously unknown. It claims that a framework adequate to LGBTQIA+ ART family-making must treat feasibility, route, institutional recognition, and narrative as constitutive tasks rather than as context, moderators, or aftermath -and that organizing them as recurring rather than sequential tasks better fits a literature in which people revisit earlier questions when a route closes, a law changes, or a relationship shifts. Whether the six tasks are the right number, whether they are distinguishable in practice, and whether they hold for populations underrepresented in this corpus are open empirical questions that this review cannot settle.

The first consequence of this synthesis concerns the importance of reproductive pathways. Conception through egg donation, IVF, reciprocal IVF, surrogacy, and fertility preservation should not be seen as synonymous. Each route raises different psychological and relationship issues. In lesbian couples, conception through egg donation and IVF often involves decisions about the type of donor, genetic and gestational participation, the role of the non-gestational mother, and the creation of a family narrative with two mothers (Côté & Lavoie, 2019; Gouya et al., 2025; Somers et al., 2017). Reciprocal IVF can have particular value as it allows both partners to share the reproductive experience, but its choice may be the result of clinical, legal, or structural pressures rather than personal preference (Cai, 2026; Shaw et al., 2023). Research on donor selection reveals that this selection can take into account factors such as race, similarity, social belonging, and can even reproduce heteronormative or racialized assumptions in the context of fertility care (Newman, 2019; Ryan & Moras, 2017).

In the context of gay men, surrogacy is commonly linked to reproductive identity, genetic connection, relationships with surrogate mothers and egg donors, the disclosure of sexual orientation, and the pursuit of legal recognition of paternity (Blake et al., 2017; Carone et al., 2018; Fantus & Newman, 2019 , 2022). Surrogacy, both in national and international contexts, is widely documented and reveals that the process of becoming a father through this practice is influenced by transnational laws, costs, clinical systems, and the relational management of third-party assisted reproduction (Jadva et al., 2019; Nebeling Petersen, 2018; Smietana, 2017; Sydsjö et al., 2019). For transgender and gender-diverse individuals, fertility preservation and reproductive planning are often intertwined with gender affirmation concerns, body image issues, timing, dysphoria, clinical competence, and uncertainties about future parenthood (Maiorano et al., 2025; Mezzalira et al., 2025; Payne & Erbenius, 2018; Stuyver et al., 2021). Therefore, the broad category of ART can have quite distinct psychological connotations, depending on family configuration, physical capabilities, and institutional environment.

The second contribution refers to LGBTQIA+ family theory. The reviewed literature challenges the idea that family formation can be understood, for the most part, through biology, family structure, or legal status. In these works, the family is constituted through practices of anticipation, negotiation, recognition, and narrative. Genetic, gestational, and social parenthood do not, by themselves, define roles within the family; they gain meaning from the interpretations that prospective parents make, the boundaries they establish, and how they incorporate them into family dynamics (Carone et al., 2018; Côté & Lavoie, 2019; Fantus & Newman, 2022; Lingiardi et al., 2016; Shaw et al., 2023). Donors and surrogate mothers, in turn, can be constructed as absent contributors, important relational figures, or part of a broader origin story, depending on the family configuration and parental interpretations (Carone et al., 2018; Côté & Lavoie, 2019; Jadva et al., 2019; Surtees, 2023). The relevant question, then, is not only whether there is a biological or gestational bond, but how this bond becomes psychologically, relationally, and narratively comprehensible within the parental project. The third implication relates to the clinical setting. The findings indicate that affirmative fertility counseling for LGBTQIA+ individuals should not be a standard approach. It must take into account the specific reproductive pathway being considered. A lesbian couple deciding between anonymous and known donor sperm may need guidance on what donation entails, nongestational motherhood, legal recognition, and disclosure (Côté & Lavoie, 2019; Schrijvers et al., 2020; Somers et al., 2017). A gay couple seeking surrogacy may need space to discuss issues such as genetic connection, the role of the surrogate and egg donor, financial vulnerability, agreements that transcend boundaries, and origin narratives (Carone et al., 2018; Fantus & Newman, 2019; Jadva et al., 2019; Nebeling Petersen, 2018). For a transgender or gender-diverse person considering fertility preservation, counseling may be necessary that combines reproductive possibilities with gender affirmation, autonomy over one's own body, dysphoria, future identity, and uncertainty regarding future family formation (Maiorano et al., 2025; Mezzalira et al., 2025; Payne & Erbenius, 2018; Stuyver et al., 2021). In all situations, counseling should promote not only informed consent but also meaning-making, relationship negotiation, and defense against minority-related stress.

The review also emphasizes the importance of situating reproductive agency within the limits imposed by structures.

LGBTQIA+ individuals and couples are not simply consumers of reproductive technologies; they actively engage in imagining, planning, comparing, negotiating, and constructing their own family pathways (Blake et al., 2017; Fantus & Newman, 2019; Gouya et al., 2025; Somers et al., 2017). Their decisions, in turn, are influenced by legal systems, healthcare policies, costs, geographic mobility, race, gender identity, marital status, and the professional ability to navigate these complexities (Cai, 2026; Chautems et al., 2025; Maiorano et al., 2025; Nebeling Petersen, 2018; Payne & Erbenius, 2018; Tordoff et al., 2026). Therefore, for a psychologically appropriate analysis of the decision regarding ART by

LGBTQIA+ individuals, it is crucial to avoid two simplifications: not pathologizing the formation of LGBTQIA+ families as an exceptional problem and not romanticizing ART as a free and equitable reproductive choice. The reviewed literature, on the contrary, suggests a more intricate scenario, where autonomy and limitation are constantly intertwined.

This is especially important when it comes to research on the family. If the formation of LGBTQIA+ families through ART is understood only from the perspective of the outcomes after the child's arrival, all the psychological and relational work that precedes parenthood ends up being overlooked. Parental planning begins before conception, and in many cases, even before access to treatment. This encompasses the idealized child, the idealized family, prior disclosure, expected institutional barriers, and negotiation over who will be recognized as the father, donor, surrogate, relative, or an external third party (Carone et al., 2018; Côté & Lavoie, 2019; Fantus & Newman, 2022; Shaw et al., 2023; Somers et al., 2017). Future research, therefore, should conduct a longitudinal follow-up of parental projects, from the conception of the idea and the decision-making process regarding the method to the treatment, the birth, the disclosure, and the family histories that follow.

Finally, the synthesis points to important gaps in the field. The literature is still unevenly distributed among different populations and contexts. Lesbian couples and gay parents via surrogacy are more evident than bisexual parents, trans women, non-binary people, intersex people, low-income prospective parents, racialized families, migrants, and families outside the West (Bowling et al., 2019; Maiorano et al., 2025; Mezzalira et al., 2025; Tordoff et al., 2026). More intersectional and longitudinal studies are needed to elucidate how class, race, migratory status, legislation, religion, geography, and health systems influence LGBTQIA+ parenting projects through ART.

Implications for Psychological Practice and Fertility Services

Our findings indicate that affirmative reproductive counseling for the LGBTQIA+ community should be tailored to the psychosocial particularities that arise when forming a family through ART. Counseling should help prospective parents define their desires as parents, assess available reproductive options, discuss the meanings of genetically, gestationally, donation, and surrogacy, and prepare to create consistent family narratives. It should also address minority stress, institutional invalidation, legal ambiguity, and grief over unsuccessful cycles, pregnancy loss, or unavailable options (Mahat-Shamir, 2025).

In fertility services, the review underscores the need for inclusive documentation, clinical language that does not presume heterosexuality, explicit recognition of non-gestational and non-genetic parenting, and staff competence regarding all the diverse LGBTQIA+ family configurations. Despite differences in population and reproductive trajectory, the index studies across all three domains indicated that clinical encounters can support or restrict parental projects. Fertility counseling should therefore be understood as psychosocial care and not merely as procedural guidance, nurturing and validating different paths to becoming a parent.

Limitations

This review has five main limitations. First, it was designed as a structured narrative review with thematic narrative synthesis, guided by SANRA, rather than as a systematic review, scoping review, or metaanalysis. It therefore does not offer exhaustive coverage of the field, quantitative synthesis, or reproducible study weighting, and its conclusions should be read as interpretive rather than conclusive.

Second, only PubMed and Scopus were searched. Because a substantial part of the literature on

LGBTQIA+ family-making is published in sociology, anthropology, gender studies, and LGBTQIA+specialized outlets, which these databases index unevenly, relevant studies indexed in PsycINFO, Web of Science, CINAHL, or Sociological Abstracts may have been missed. This constraint plausibly shaped which studies entered the mapped corpus and not only the completeness of the review.

Third, the selection of index studies from the broader mapped corpus rested on interpretive judgment rather than on a reproducible selection algorithm, and a different pair of readers might have selected a partly different set. The mapped corpus is itself unevenly distributed across the three family-making domains, and this unevenness bears directly on how the domains should be compared: in the domain covering gay fatherhood through surrogacy, six index studies were selected from 24 mapped articles, whereas in the domain covering transgender and gender-diverse reproductive planning the five index studies constitute the whole of the mapped literature, so that no selection was exercised there. The depth of that discussion is constrained by the size of the available evidence base rather than by analytic choice.

Fourth, the methodological quality of the included studies was not formally appraised. SANRA assesses the reporting quality of the review article, not the risk of bias of the studies reviewed, and the corpus mixes single qualitative studies with systematic reviews. Findings drawn from single qualitative studies should therefore not be read as carrying the same evidential weight as those drawn from syntheses of multiple studies.

Fifth, the literature itself remains uneven across populations and contexts. Lesbian couples and gay fathers through surrogacy are better represented than bisexual parents, transgender women, non-binary and intersex people, low-income prospective parents, racialized families, migrants, and families outside Europe and North America. Only English-language, peer-reviewed publications were eligible, which further narrows geographical and linguistic coverage. Most included studies are qualitative and crosssectional, which supports the analysis of meaning-making and reproductive planning but restricts causal inference and generalization.

Conclusion

This narrative review suggests that, rather than constituting a single reproductive decision, LGBTQIA+ ART decision-making can be understood as a family-making process through which parental desire is translated into feasible, relationally meaningful, and socially recognizable routes. Across lesbian couples' donor conception and IVF pathways, gay men's surrogacy arrangements, and transgender/gender-diverse reproductive planning, parental projects are shaped by desire, identity, embodiment, partnership, donor and surrogate meanings, minority stress, and access to affirming institutions. The review contributes to the literature by reframing LGBTQIA+ ART family-making as a relational and institutionally situated process rather than a purely technical reproductive choice. These findings support LGBTQIA+affirmative reproductive counselling and fertility services that recognize diverse family forms, validate parental aspirations, address minority stress, and support complex decision-making. Future research should expand beyond the most studied groups and include more diverse sexual and gender minority populations, intersectional contexts, and longitudinal designs capable of following parental projects across the full ART pathway.
