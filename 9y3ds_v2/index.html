Power Analysis Check

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Marginal Significance works

List all sentences that describe an effect as ‘marginally significant’.

The marginal module searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The list of terms is a subset of those listed in a blog post by Matthew Hankins. The module returns all sentences that match terms describing ‘marginally significant’ results.

Some of the terms identified might not be problematic in some contexts, and there are ways to describe ‘marginal significance’ that are not detected by the module.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. Some differences come from the ground truth: it lacks p-values with a correction name (“p Bonf = .061”), and counts partial eta squared written with its subscript after it (“η 2 p = .05”), “p = .05”, and some table notes and figure legends (“† p < .10, *p < .05”), which this module leaves out, as non-significant p-values. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

The Effect Size module checks if effect sizes are correctly reported in t-tests and F-tests.

The Effect Size check searches for regular expressions that match typical ways in which effect sizes are reported. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Abstract

Better treatment can ease an employee's anger without changing what they expect from the next encounter. When, then, does a new workplace encounter revise a learned expectation of harm? A theory of expectancy updating grounded in cue-appraisal learning explains why positive experiences can leave anticipated mistreatment intact, establish a narrow exception, or support broader change. Confirming encounters can strengthen expected harm and consolidate confidence even as immediate emotion subsides. Favorable encounters support revision when they provide credible evidence about the anticipated consequence and the conditions that made it likely. Safety attributed to exceptional monitoring can be learned as an exception; protection that extends across ordinary conditions offers grounds for broader revision. Harm expectancies can also spread to similar workplace cues and to actors judged responsible for producing or enabling harm. Credible differences in consequences, authority, or responsibility can limit that spread.

Earlier and corrective expectations may nevertheless coexist, allowing anticipated harm to return with changes in context or the passage of time. Such return must be distinguished from responses to renewed mistreatment or withdrawn safeguards. Six propositions connect these processes with differentiated emotion and choices among protective distancing, accountability-oriented engagement, and punishment. The central prediction is that equally safe encounters testing the same anticipated harm can support different scopes of revision: protection attributed to exceptional conditions should produce narrower safety expectations than protection attributed to safeguards expected to operate across ordinary encounters.

When Better Treatment Is Not Enough: Relearning Expectations of Harm at Work

Consider an employee who was punished for reporting misconduct. Months later, the organization announces a new complaint procedure, and the manager begins treating the employee more respectfully. The employee appreciates the change but still expects retaliation if another serious concern is raised. A complaint subsequently handled without punishment offers stronger reassurance. Yet even that experience may seem exceptional if an external auditor was present. Although each encounter improves on the original mistreatment, they offer different grounds for expecting protection the next time a serious concern is raised.

These differences raise a broader question: When does a new workplace encounter revise an employee's learned expectation of harm? Feeling calmer after a pleasant exchange, trusting one protected reporting route, and expecting ordinary reporting to be safe are different outcomes.

The distinction turns on what the encounter reveals about the harm the employee anticipates.

Revision is expected to depend on whether the prediction was tested, why the outcome changed, and whether the conditions responsible for protection are likely to hold again.

Research on organizational fairness provides evidence that earlier judgments and information matter for later reactions. For example, Ambrose and Cropanzano (2003) followed faculty members' fairness perceptions and work attitudes around tenure and promotion decisions over two years. They found that the additional predictive contributions of procedural and distributive justice differed across follow-up measurements after earlier attitudes were taken into account. Similarly, Bell et al. (2006) found associations between applicants' prior justice expectations, pretest attitudes and intentions, and later justice perceptions during selection. Jones and Skarlicki (2005) supplied experimental evidence of an informational effect, showing that fairness-reputation information influenced subsequent interactional justice judgments and retaliation. At the theoretical level, Oh and Farh (2017) connect appraisals, emotions, coping responses, and feedback across abusive-supervision episodes. Together, the studies link earlier judgments and information with later reactions, while the episode model proposes how experience can carry forward. For harm expectancies, the more specific task is to explain how confirming, non-testing, and contradictory encounters change the strength and reach of the learned prediction.

Umbra's (2026a) cue-appraisal theory offers a learning framework for examining how such histories shape later interpretation. It proposes that recurring workplace cues can acquire conditioned appraisal meanings: learned relations that make particular appraisals more likely when those cues recur. Its propositions concern acquisition, appraisal specificity, generalization, discrimination, and revision through credible counter-experience. A harm expectancy applies that framework to the learned expectation that a recognizable workplace cue predicts harmful treatment or wrongdoing. In the proposed updating process, favorable encounters differ in corrective value according to the harm anticipated and the cause to which its absence is attributed. A friendly exchange can therefore leave an expectation of retaliation untested, whereas protected reporting can support either an exception or broader revision, depending on why protection occurred.

The anticipated consequence matters because cue-appraisal theory distinguishes appraising a complaint channel as risky from appraising it as useless (Umbra, 2026a). Umbra's (2026b) demoralization account develops the latter concern by explaining how recurring cues signal whether a blocked or unwanted work state can still improve. A complaint procedure can therefore signal futility without predicting retaliation, or predict retaliation even when an external intervention could eventually correct the problem. In these examples, either expectation can make participation seem unattractive, but harm anticipation directs the explanation toward injury, responsibility, and protection. Anger, contempt, and disgust toward organizational actors or practices are the focal hostility outcomes. Fear and vigilance are included as possible accompanying responses to anticipated personal danger. Anticipating harm, experiencing these emotions, and acting against another person remain distinct parts of the explanation.

Keeping these elements distinct places the employee's expectation about a recognizable workplace cue under specified conditions at the center of the analysis. Acquisition, transfer, correction, and return then concern what the available evidence gives the employee grounds to expect in a later cue-linked encounter. Behavior remains conditional: the umbrella theory treats enactment as dependent on situational constraints, regulation, resources, and competing goals (Umbra, 2026a). An employee may therefore remain angry while using a newly protected complaint route, or stay silent because the anticipated danger remains credible. Neither participation nor immediate relief, by itself, establishes what the employee now expects from the next encounter.

Learning to Expect Harm

A harm expectancy applies Umbra's (2026a) cue-appraisal framework to a specified anticipated consequence: a recognizable workplace cue predicts harmful treatment of the employee or others, or wrongdoing that organizational actors produce, enable, or fail to correct. Such consequences may include degradation, unfairness, exclusion, and retaliation. The expectation therefore links a cue with a consequence under specified conditions. It need not be expressed as a conscious probability estimate. What makes it an expectancy is its availability before the relevant outcome is known. A judgment formed only in response to the outcome describes an appraisal of the treatment received, rather than anticipation of that outcome.

The organizational cue in this relation is a recurring, recognizable workplace feature that an employee encounters across episodes and regards as relevant to appraisal (Umbra, 2026a). A meeting invitation, complaint form, supervisory phrase, or sender's identity can serve this function. For a performance review, the invitation supplies the cue, while the review itself supplies the treatment and consequences. Similarly, a manager's name, presence, or characteristic expression can call up expectations associated with earlier conduct. Because an expectancy concerns specified conditions, it need not make every interaction with that manager threatening. An employee may expect humiliation in public reviews while finding private technical discussions helpful. Another employee with a different history may recognize the same invitation and expect something else.

Within the encounter, a current appraisal evaluates its significance for the person's goals, values, and well-being (Moors et al., 2013). Cue-appraisal theory proposes that learned cue meanings make particular interpretations more accessible within this evaluation (Umbra, 2026a). An employee can therefore expect retaliation before reporting a concern, yet appraise its protected handling as fair treatment. The expectation anticipates the outcome; the appraisal evaluates the encounter as it unfolds; subsequent learning shapes what the employee brings to the next encounter. Keeping these moments distinct allows revision to be assessed without treating a temporary change in feeling as evidence that anticipated harm has changed.

A harm expectancy concerns anticipated consequences, which distinguishes it from both liking and perceived mistreatment. Evaluative conditioning concerns changes in liking following pairings with positive or negative stimuli (Hofmann et al., 2010). Liking a manager more does not, by itself, show that retaliation is expected less often. Abusive supervision, in turn, concerns subordinates' perceptions of sustained hostile verbal and nonverbal supervisory conduct, excluding physical contact (Tepper, 2000). Perceived abuse can supply the history from which a harm expectancy develops. The expectancy nevertheless concerns treatment anticipated in a later encounter, rather than the perceived conduct itself.

A new encounter can add to that history without changing the expectation derived from it. Within the proposed account, its contribution to updating depends on the evidence the employee takes from it. Evidence is diagnostic for an expectancy when it helps distinguish the existing prediction from a plausible alternative. Retaliation after another comparable complaint supports the prediction that reporting is dangerous. By contrast, friendly contact without a complaint may leave that prediction untested. A protected complaint challenges the prediction, but the extent of revision still depends on why protection occurred. Figure 1 depicts the proposed alternatives: confirmation can strengthen or stabilize expected harm, non-testing evidence can leave it intact, and credible contradiction can support either conditional safety or broader revision. In each case, the resulting expectation informs appraisal when the cue reappears.

In Rescorla and Wagner's (1972) model, changes in associative strength depend on the discrepancy between the reinforcement predicted by available cues and the reinforcement that occurs. This principle supports examining how confirming and contradictory experiences contribute to learning, but it does not establish which workplace outcomes employees regard as evidence about a particular expectancy. Such judgments may depend on how employees interpret responsibility, decision authority, and whether the response reflects a practice likely to recur.

Evidence may also come from personal experience, observing others, or credible information.

The present account therefore draws on prediction-dependent learning without equating associative strength with employees' reported likelihood or confidence, or assuming that all expectancy revisions operate through a single associative mechanism.

Extinction research provides a separate foundation for considering why harm expectancies may persist despite corrective experiences. Earlier learning can remain available after extinction, and conditioned responding can return when contextual conditions change (Bouton, 2004). Applied to organizational harm expectancies, this raises the possibility that experiences of safe treatment may support new expectations without necessarily eliminating earlier expectations of harm. Whether such expectations persist and reemerge across workplace contexts remains an empirical question.

Confirmation Can Strengthen Expectation Without Intensifying Emotion

A first harmful encounter can establish an expectancy in this account when the employee connects its consequence to a cue likely to recur. Punishment after a complaint becomes evidence about reporting when the employee attributes it to raising the concern or to the authority controlling its treatment. Severity is predicted to produce a larger initial shift when it makes this cue-consequence connection more persuasive. Subsequent encounters then supply evidence about whether the incident was isolated and which conditions make its recurrence likely. A severe first incident and a repeated pattern can thus establish the same kind of expectation while supplying different amounts of evidence about its reliability.

As those encounters accumulate, Umbra's (2026a) acquisition proposition predicts stronger cue-appraisal learning when recurrence, appraisal consistency, salience, and concern relevance converge. Within this account, expected likelihood concerns how probable an employee judges a specified harmful consequence to be under specified conditions. Confidence concerns the employee's certainty in that likelihood judgment. Confirming experience can change these judgments at different rates. A second comparable retaliation may make punishment seem more likely while also increasing confidence in that judgment. Once expected likelihood is already high, further confirmation has less room to raise it but may still consolidate confidence. The evidentiary value of repetition nevertheless depends on what is repeated: several versions of the same report may add little information about recurrence and should not automatically count as independent confirmation. This consolidation of expectancy need not be accompanied by greater emotional intensity. Craske et al.'s (2014) review of extinction and exposure research notes that fear reduction during exposure does not reliably predict fear at follow-up. The corresponding workplace prediction is that another degrading review can provoke less immediate anger while strengthening confidence that the next review will be degrading too. A quieter emotional reaction would therefore establish neither weakened anticipation nor why emotional intensity changed.

Conversely, an intensely upsetting encounter may add little evidence if the employee already expects its outcome with high confidence. Tests of this prediction therefore need repeated assessments of expected harm and confidence before outcomes are known, alongside separate assessments of emotional intensity.

Assessing the actual prediction is especially important when mistreatment is intermittent. Yoon et al. (2023) examined past and current abusive supervision in two experience-sampling studies of anxiety and turnover intentions. Within this account, respectful encounters in an alternating pattern can reduce the estimated frequency of abuse while leaving its anticipated possibility intact. If the employee expects intermittent danger, a safe encounter is compatible with that prediction. Whether an encounter confirms or contradicts expected harm therefore depends on the expectation's specified frequency and conditions, rather than an assumed belief that every encounter will be harmful.

Proposition 1 (Acquisition and updating): Confirming encounters attributed to a recognizable organizational cue will strengthen or stabilize the expectancy of harmful treatment or wrongdoing and can consolidate confidence in that expectancy. Confirming evidence will produce smaller changes in anticipated likelihood as the consequence becomes well established, whereas credible contradictory evidence will reduce expected harm or restrict the conditions under which it is anticipated.

Different Expected Harms Favor Different Emotions

Expected harm has content as well as likelihood, and that content should matter for emotion. Kuppens et al. (2003) associated anger with seeing another person as responsible and perceiving unfairness, while finding that none of the examined appraisal or action-tendency components was individually necessary or sufficient for anger. Fischer and Roseman (2007), in turn, distinguished anger's tendency toward confrontation from contempt's association with negative character judgments, rejection, and social exclusion. Chapman and Anderson (2013)reviewed evidence that moral violations can elicit disgust, while considering competing accounts of its relationship to physical disgust. Together, these sources support differentiated but conditional relationships between appraisal and emotion.

Responsibility gives these appraisal distinctions a concrete workplace foundation. In Study 1 of Umbra and Fasbender's (2026) research, which repeatedly assessed workplace interactions, appraisals of colleague-perpetrated immorality were associated with colleaguedirected anger and punitive regulation. Appraisals of self-perpetrated immorality, in turn, were associated with guilt and penitent regulation, including apologizing and making amends. These associations concern appraisal, emotion, and response within workplace interactions. They do not, by themselves, establish persistence, generalization, or return of a learned harm expectancy, or demonstrate the proposed contempt and disgust pathways. Umbra's (2026a Proposition 2 (Appraisal and emotion): Learned harm expectancies will differentiate later emotion and action readiness through the appraisals they activate, conditional on current evidence. Anticipated blameworthy injury or injustice will favor anger and confrontation readiness; target devaluation will favor contempt and dismissal; moral corruption will favor disgust and distancing; anticipated danger will favor fear and protective vigilance, allowing these responses to coexist.

How Expectations of Harm Spread and Become Bounded

A learned expectation can also shape encounters with unfamiliar procedures or people. In Umbra's (2026a) cue-appraisal theory, generalization occurs when employees perceive similarities between cues in features relevant to their prior appraisal experience. A new reporting channel, for example, may resemble one previously associated with retaliation or place complainants under the same kind of decision authority. The relevant similarity is whatever the employee understands to predict the anticipated harm. The prediction is that surface resemblance can bring an expectation to mind, while shared control or equivalent exposure can give the employee stronger grounds for expecting the same harm.

Similarity, however, is not required for an expectation to extend to another actor: attributed responsibility can connect otherwise dissimilar people to the same harm. Tomlinson and Mayer's (2009) dyadic trust-repair model proposes that causal attributions, including controllability and stability, shape judgments about damaged trustworthiness and its repair.

Extending that attributional logic to harm expectancy, leaders who know about abuse, control intervention, and repeatedly protect the supervisor may be expected to permit its continuation.

Here, the expected consequence changes with the actor's perceived role: the supervisor is expected to punish, while leadership is expected to tolerate wrongdoing. Organizational affiliation alone provides no comparable basis unless the employee regards it as relevant to producing, enabling, or leaving the harm uncorrected. This distinction also clarifies how an expectancy might carry from one employer to another. A new procedure may evoke an earlier expectancy when it resembles features the employee associates with harm. Common ownership, implicated decision-makers, or another continuing accountability connection may also sustain expectations that organizational actors will produce or tolerate the earlier harm. Reputation information offers a related but more limited basis for anticipating treatment. Jones and Skarlicki (2005) experimentally showed that overhearing peers discuss an authority's fairness reputation influenced subsequent judgments of interactional justice. Whether such information changes a specific harm expectancy across employers remains a theoretical prediction. Transfer should therefore depend on perceived continuity relevant to the expected harm, rather than organizational membership alone. Conversely, Umbra's (2026a) discrimination proposition predicts that meaningful differences in authority, procedure, or accountability can limit transfer.

Proposition 3a (Generalization and responsibility): Harm expectancies will transfer to new organizational cues when employees perceive similarities relevant to the anticipated consequence. Expectations of harmful conduct or non-response will extend to additional actors or institutions when employees attribute responsibility for producing, enabling, or failing to correct the harm; transfer across employers will depend on perceived similarity or a continuing accountability connection rather than organizational membership alone.

Learning Where the Expectation No Longer Applies

Where perceived continuity supports transfer, a meaningful difference can mark its limits. In Umbra's (2026a) cue-appraisal theory, discrimination limits generalization when employees recognize differences between current and prior cues in features relevant to their prior appraisal experience. For a harm expectancy, that difference must bear on the consequence anticipated. A reporting channel under independent authority, for example, can become distinguishable from one controlled by the implicated manager if its safeguards change the treatment expected. By contrast, renaming the channel offers weaker grounds for differentiation when decision control and exposure remain the same.

The same principle can qualify expectations about who will permit or correct harm. Tomlinson and Mayer's (2009) trust-repair model treats perceived control over a negative outcome as relevant to judgments of blameworthiness. Applied to harm expectancy, effective independent intervention can show that one part of an institution opposes conduct tolerated elsewhere. Likewise, credible evidence that an actor lacked knowledge of the wrongdoing may weaken the inference that the actor knowingly allowed it. Evidence of limited authority bears on a different judgment: whether the actor could have prevented or corrected the harm. Such evidence can change who is expected to produce, permit, or correct the consequence. It need not improve the employee's view of the original perpetrator or undermine an expectation still supported by that person's conduct.

Proposition 3b (Discrimination): Recognized, credible differences in consequences will limit expectancy generalization across cues, while differences in involvement, authority, or corrective responsibility will limit extension across actors and institutions. Encounters that provide credible, diagnostic evidence against the original harmful prediction will additionally qualify that prediction, with revision confined to the conditions supported by the evidence.

Learning where an expectancy does not apply need not change the prediction attached to the original cue. An employee may learn that an independent reporting channel is safe and still have good reason to expect retaliation through the ordinary managerial procedure. Revising that earlier expectancy requires evidence about the conditions that made the original procedure harmful. The corrective value of safety therefore depends on whether the evidence addresses the prediction it is meant to change.

What Makes Better Treatment Corrective

Corrective evidence must address the anticipated harm, but even a relevant favorable outcome can admit more than one explanation. If an employee expects punishment after dissent, a friendly exchange in which no concern is raised leaves that expectation largely untested. By comparison, voicing a serious concern and receiving protection offers more relevant evidence.

Yet the employee must still judge why punishment did not follow. A visiting observer, temporary enforcement, or an exceptional officer can explain safety while leaving the expectation of ordinary treatment intact. Such an outcome can therefore support either a narrow exception or a broader change, depending on the explanation the evidence warrants.

The content of the expected harm determines which changes would provide relevant evidence. Anticipated humiliation in public reviews calls for changed review conduct or protection against public degradation. For expected retaliation, the relevant test is whether serious concerns can be reported with effective protection. If leadership is expected to tolerate wrongdoing, corrective evidence concerns an authority's ability and willingness to intervene. Gillespie and Dietz's (2009) organizational trust-repair model likewise connects diagnosis of a failure with reforms directed at the arrangements implicated in it. At the interpersonal level, Kim et al. (2004) found that the relative effectiveness of apology and denial for trust repair varied with whether an alleged violation concerned competence or integrity. These trust-repair contributions do not establish how a particular cue comes to predict less harm.

From Revised Expectation to Response

A revised expectation of safer treatment can change an employee's choices while anger about earlier misconduct remains. An employee may report through a protected channel while continuing to condemn the misconduct, without forgiving the perpetrator or fully restoring trust.

An employee who still expects retaliation may instead avoid reporting. Action readiness refers to motivational tendencies to respond, which componential appraisal theories distinguish from enacted behavior (Moors et al., 2013). In two studies using recalled emotional experiences, Frijda et al. (1989) found differentiated relationships among appraisal patterns, emotion categories, and forms of action readiness.

Whether a response seems preferable depends on what the employee expects it to achieve

and what taking it might cost. Morrison (2014) distinguishes voice efficacy, the belief that speaking up can achieve a desired result, from safety or risk, the perceived possibility of negative consequences for the employee or their relationships. In the harm-expectancy account, correction efficacy is the employee's judgment of whether a particular response can prevent or correct the anticipated harm; risk concerns its possible costs. An employee may doubt that persuading a supervisor will work yet expect a complaints office outside that supervisor's control to intervene effectively. Componential appraisal theories include coping potential or control, which concerns, among other things, whether a person can change the situation (Moors et al., 2013). The Interaction Discrepancy Model similarly proposes appraising who is responsible for a discrepancy between how an interaction is and how it should be, and whether an undesirable discrepancy can change (Umbra & Fasbender, 2025). Neither general changeability nor personal control, however, establishes whether a particular route will work. Leaving the organization may reduce exposure without changing the harmful procedure itself.

The purpose of a response matters as well as its expected effectiveness and cost.

Complaints, documentation, and escalation through a protected channel may seek accountability or correction; silence, avoidance, transfer, and exit may reduce exposure. Humiliation, sabotage, or exclusion used to punish someone by causing harm serves a punitive purpose. These purposes may overlap, and neither the form of the behavior nor organizational disapproval settles its psychological function. Anderson and Bushman (2002) define aggression as behavior directed at another person with an immediate intention to harm, with the actor believing that the behavior will cause harm and that the target wants to avoid it. Their General Aggression Model connects person and situation inputs with internal states and appraisal and decision processes that shape behavior. Expecting harm, judging correction ineffective, or finding participation unsafe therefore does not, by itself, establish aggression.

Recognized changes in correction efficacy or risk are therefore predicted to alter the relative priority of responses. A route employees judge both effective and safe may favor engagement; protection they do not find credible may leave avoidance preferable. Punitive readiness is additionally predicted to depend on blame and perceived capacity to impose harm.

Umbra's (2026a) cue-appraisal theory treats actual behavior as additionally dependent on situational constraints, regulation, resources, and competing goals. Tests must therefore establish whether revised expectations change which responses employees consider necessary or feasible.

Observing silence or escalation alone cannot reveal whether learned anticipation shaped that response.

Proposition 5 (Response selection):

Learned harm expectancies will give greater priority to protective distancing when employees judge engagement unsafe and available correction routes ineffective, whereas recognized correction efficacy with manageable risk will favor accountability-oriented engagement. Punitive readiness will additionally depend on blame and perceived capacity to impose harm, and actual behavior will remain conditional on regulation and situational constraints.

Discussion

The Corrective Value of an Encounter

An encounter has corrective value to the extent that it provides credible evidence about the harm an employee expects. A friendly exchange in which no concern is raised, for example, does not by itself show that reporting a serious concern is safe. When protection is attributed to an exceptional monitor, safety may be learned as an exception; when protection is supported across relevant ordinary conditions, revision may extend more broadly. Umbra's (2026a) revision proposition links changes in conditioned appraisal meanings to repeated, credible counter-experiences. Specifying what harm was expected and why it did not occur sharpens this learning account: equally positive encounters may support different changes in the strength of an expectation and the conditions under which it applies. The theoretical extension specifies how employees' causal explanations for protection shape whether safety is learned as a contextspecific exception or generalized across relevant future encounters. It further proposes when earlier harm expectancies may return despite continuing protection, distinguishing this possibility from renewed anticipation following actual mistreatment or withdrawn safeguards. This relationship between evidence and prediction also connects correction with the spread and return of harm expectancies. If expected retaliation is tied to continuing managerial control, renaming a complaint procedure supplies little reason to expect safer treatment. A channel outside the implicated manager's control may support a different expectation if its safeguards credibly change the consequences of reporting. This can limit the spread of expected harm to the new channel while leaving the expectation about the original procedure intact. If protection is instead attributed to an exceptional setting, a safety expectation may coexist with the earlier expectation of harm. The earlier expectation may then become more accessible when the employee returns to a setting associated with mistreatment, even if protection remains effective. Persistence, limits on transfer, and return are therefore predicted to depend on which conditions employees understand the evidence to cover.

These predictions apply to an employee's expectation about a recognizable cue with a relevant history and an anticipated consequence that subsequent evidence can address. The influence of that history remains conditional: Umbra's (2026a) scope conditions allow clear current information to outweigh prior cue learning. Tests of updating must track expectations within employees across encounters and distinguish the effects of new evidence from differences in actual exposure, access to correction, and stable interpretive tendencies. An association between dispositional negativity and hostility would not establish how a cue-specific expectation was acquired or revised. Collective climates and diffuse distrust may form part of the setting, but their emergence is outside the explanatory scope of this employee-level account. Within that scope, continued anticipation during ongoing abuse must also be distinguished from persistence after credible correction. This distinction prevents anticipation that remains warranted by current treatment from being mistaken for a failure to learn from improved conditions.

Making Organizational Correction Informative

Correction is predicted to weaken an expectation of harm when it provides credible evidence that the conduct or conditions responsible for that harm have changed. A more welcoming invitation offers little evidence of protection from retaliation if the manager implicated in earlier mistreatment still controls complainants' career outcomes. Review outside that manager's control, enforceable protection, and observable handling of serious reports can provide more relevant evidence when they address the particular harm anticipated. This focus on the causes of harm is consistent with Gillespie and Dietz's (2009) organizational trust-repair model, which links diagnosis of a failure to reforms targeting the relevant organizational components. Whether such reforms revise a particular harm expectancy nevertheless remains an empirical question. Verified examples of protected reporting may supply relevant evidence without requiring every employee to risk another harmful encounter.

Evaluating such correction requires distinguishing changes in conduct, anticipated harm, emotion, and participation. Reduced anger does not by itself show that an employee expects retaliation to be less likely, just as continued caution does not by itself show resistance to reform.

If harm continues or safeguards remain uncertain, silence, distance, or exit may serve a protective purpose. If protection is demonstrably effective across relevant conditions and employees recognize this evidence, they have stronger grounds for expecting safer treatment and considering engagement. The practical objective is safer treatment supported by accountable conduct, together with expectations that accurately reflect the conditions employees are likely to encounter.

Conclusion

Better treatment is predicted to revise a learned expectation of harm when it provides credible evidence that the feared consequence is less likely under conditions the employee expects to encounter again. Confirming encounters can strengthen the expectation or consolidate confidence in it, whereas contradictory evidence can weaken it or restrict the conditions under which it applies. The extent of revision depends on why the outcome changed and which future encounters the evidence covers. The resulting expectation informs later appraisal, emotion, and response without determining them. Hostility may therefore persist after a pleasant encounter when the relevant danger remains untested or protection appears confined to an exception.
