To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 3 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

Text Sentence
ps = .058 Associations for Qwen, Terra, and Grok did not survive correction, adjusted ps = .058-.224.
p = .792 The ensemble correlation was .343, adjusted p = p = .792.
ps = .058 Associations for Claude, Qwen, Grok, and Terra did not survive correction, adjusted ps = .058-.311.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). Statcheck might still miss one-sided tests that are described in another sentence, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

If you want to extend the package to be able to download files from additional data repositories reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Abstract

Large language models (LLMs) offer a potential approach to inferring standardized questionnaire responses from qualitative interviews, but their utility for assessing parenting remains unclear. We evaluated six generative LLMs using zero-shot prompting to infer responses to the Multidimensional Assessment of Parenting Scale (MAPS) from interviews with 43 parents. Models inferred responses to 35 MAPS items from two introductory questions about family, parenting roles, and the meaning of caregiving. Performance was evaluated through exact item-response agreement, correlations between inferred and self-reported parenting scores, systematic score differences, and internal consistency. Across models, Fisher-averaged correlations between inferred and self-reported MAPS subscale scores ranged from .320 to .434, with no consistently superior model. Correlations were strongest and most consistent for Warmth, whereas Physical Control showed little correspondence and substantial floor restriction. Across parent-item pairs, 35.5%-44.7% of inferred responses exactly matched parents' selected response categories. All models assigned systematically lower response categories than parents and rarely selected the highest category; score underestimation was particularly evident for Positive Parenting. Model-derived subscales generally showed internal consistency, although higher internal consistency did not consistently correspond to stronger correlations with parentreported scores. Exploratory analyses indicated modest similarity in the associations of inferred and self-reported parenting scores with other psychological constructs. Preliminary comparisons also suggested that shorter interview segments substantially reduced computational requirements without clear evidence of poorer performance. These findings support further evaluation of LLM-based parenting assessment while highlighting limitations in reproducing individual responses, matching score levels, and capturing some parenting dimensions.

1. Introduction

Parenting is a central context for children's emotional, behavioral, and social development. Parents' warmth, support, responses to misbehavior, and consistency in setting limits shape children's everyday experiences and are associated with behavioral adjustment across development (Pinquart, 2017). Parenting behaviors also occur within family circumstances that differ in children's developmental needs, caregivers' resources, cultural expectations, and exposure to stress (Belsky, 1984; Rodriguez et al., 2026). These differences present a challenge for assessment: measures must capture meaningful dimensions of parenting while accounting for variation in how behaviors are understood and enacted across families. Standardized questionnaires are widely used to assess parenting because they offer an efficient approach to assessing multiple domains of parenting using a common set of items and scoring procedures (Parent & Forehand, 2017; Rodriguez et al., 2025). For example, the Multidimensional Assessment of Parenting Scale (MAPS) organizes parents' reports into interpretable dimensions that can be examined in relation to child and family outcomes (Parent & Forehand, 2017). Meaningful comparisons across families depend on whether parents interpret items similarly and whether the scores function comparably across groups. Research on the MAPS has identified measurement nonequivalence across ethnoracial groups and variation in parents' interpretations of MAPS items (Rodriguez, Cadet, et al., 2023; Rodriguez et al., 2025; Rodriguez, La Barrie, et al., 2023; Rodriguez & Shaffer, 2021). These findings underscore the need to examine how parents interpret standardized items and how the circumstances surrounding parenting behaviors inform their responses.

Qualitative interviews allow parents to describe parenting in their own terms, including the challenges they encounter, how they interpret their children's behavior, and the considerations that guide their responses (Rodriguez, Cadet, et al., 2023; Rodriguez et al., 2025). These accounts can illuminate connections among parenting behaviors, emotional experiences, and social circumstances, while allowing parents to emphasize experiences that researchers may not have anticipated (Daly, 2003; Gilgun, 2012). Alongside their value for understanding these experiences, interviews raise a complementary measurement question: to what extent do parents' narratives convey information about the parenting dimensions assessed by standardized questionnaires? Computational text analysis offers tools for examining this question (Janasik et al., 2009) with prior applications identifying common parenting concerns in online discussions (Westrupp et al., 2022). Extending this approach to estimate questionnaire responses from individual interviews requires evaluating how closely those estimates correspond to parents' questionnaire reports. This comparison can identify which parenting dimensions are reflected in parents' narrative accounts.

Generative large language models (LLMs) offer a potential approach to translating narrative information into standardized item responses. Recent work has used zero-shot prompting to infer personality questionnaire responses from open-ended narratives without taskspecific model training (Peters & Matz, 2024; Wright et al., 2026). A model can be given an interview transcript, the wording of a questionnaire item, and a defined set of response options, then asked to infer the response best supported by the narrative. This approach can be evaluated using existing interview datasets without first assembling a labeled sample for task-specific training. Its usefulness, however, depends on whether the resulting responses agree with relevant comparison measures and whether errors reveal systematic distortions (Fan et al., 2023; Speer et al., 2026).

Recent work on personality assessment provides examples of this approach. Wright et al. (2026) used seven generative LLMs to assess Big Five personality traits from spontaneous streams of thought and daily video diaries across two samples (Wright et al., 2026). Modelderived scores converged with self-reported personality and showed associations with daily behaviors and mental health outcomes, although performance differed across models. These findings suggest that narrative data can contain information relevant to standardized psychological assessment. Extending this framework to parenting requires a separate empirical test. Parenting practices are relational and context dependent (Sameroff, 2010). In addition, interviews and questionnaires both rely on parental disclosure. Comparing interviews and questionnaires evaluates correspondence between two formats of parental report.

Evaluating several model families is particularly useful when adapting narrative scoring to a new assessment domain. Wright et al. (2026) found that personality-assessment performance varied across models and that averaging model scores produced the strongest agreement with self-report in their study (Wright et al., 2026). A comparison using common transcripts and item targets can establish how dependent parenting-score convergence is on the selected model. Agreement among models may identify consistently inferred responses, while disagreement may reveal ambiguity or sensitivity to model-specific assumptions. However, consensus alone does not establish accuracy, because models may share errors (Kim et al., 2025). Performance must therefore be evaluated against parents' questionnaire responses for each model, and any combined scoring approach requires its own evaluation.

An additional question concerns how much narrative information is needed to infer parenting scores. A longer-term goal is to reduce assessment burden by using brief spoken accounts to obtain information about multiple parenting dimensions. In daily assessment, parents could describe their experiences in an audio recording that is transcribed and scored, potentially reducing the number of separate questionnaire responses required. Developing this approach requires determining how much narrative material is sufficient and whether particular dimensions benefit from targeted prompts. Additional interview content may provide useful behavioral examples and context, but it also increases collection burden and the amount of text processed by a model. Additional content may be redundant or introduce information less relevant to the assessment target (Liu et al., 2024; Shi et al., 2023). Comparing brief segments with full interviews provides an initial test of how narrative length and content relate to scoring performance and computational requirements.

The role of interviewer speech also warrants examination. Interviewer questions can clarify what a parent is responding to and help resolve brief or ambiguous answers. Conversely, those questions may introduce language about parenting practices that the parent does not endorse (Holstein & Gubrium, 1995). Retaining interviewer turns could thus improve interpretation or influence predictions through content supplied by the interviewer rather than the parent. Comparing full dialogue with versions that omit interviewer turns offers a way to examine whether this contextual information improves agreement and whether its contribution justifies the additional text.

The present study uses qualitative interviews to evaluate whether multiple LLMs can infer parents' responses to MAPS items from their narrative accounts. Our first objective is to characterize agreement with parents' questionnaire responses and examine whether performance differs across models and parenting dimensions. We evaluate exact item matches, prediction error, correlations between inferred and self-reported scores, systematic score differences, and internal consistency. Our second objective is to examine how transcript length and inclusion of interviewer speech relate to prediction performance alongside text and computational requirements, informing future development of brief narrative assessments.

2. Methods

>Methods

2.1 Study design and data source

This study is a mixed methods study. The parent study included an initial caregiver survey and a remotely conducted qualitative interview. The analysis comprises preliminary transcript-selection analyses and a main comparison of six LLMs using a common transcript representation. The main analysis includes n = 43 parents, yielding 1,505 parent-item predictions per model and 9,030 predictions across six models.

2.2 Participants and recruitment

Eligible participants were at least 18 years old, resided in the United States, had a child between 0 and 18 years of age, and completed an interview in English. Participants were recruited through in-person and social media advertisements and screened for eligibility using Qualtrics. Study personnel reviewed eligibility information and monitored demographic recruitment targets. Eligible participants were invited to review the consent materials and complete the initial survey before scheduling their interview.

The main analytic sample comprises 43 parents with interview transcripts and responses to the 35 MAPS items included in this analysis. Parents' mean age was 37.7 years (SD = 6.9; range = 27-54), and children's ages ranged from 0 to 17 years. Detailed demographic characteristics are included in Table 1.

2.3 Ethics and consent

Study procedures were approved by the Institutional Review Board at the authors' institution. Participants reviewed consent materials before completing study activities. Participants were compensated $40 for the initial qualitative interview.

2.4 Survey and parenting measure

The initial caregiver survey was administered through Qualtrics. Parenting was assessed using 35 items from the MAPS (Rodriguez et al., 2025), covering seven dimensions: Proactive Parenting, Positive Reinforcement, Warmth, Supportiveness, Hostility, Lax Control, and Physical Control. Parents rated the frequency of each behavior during the past two months using five response categories: Never, Rarely, Sometimes, Often, and Always. Responses were coded from 1 (Never) to 5 (Always) for analysis, and subscale scores were calculated as the mean of their constituent items. Higher scores indicated more frequent endorsement of the behaviors represented by each subscale. Internal consistency in the present sample ranged from α = .544 for Supportiveness to α = .884 for Proactive Parenting (Table S3). Parents' item responses and subscale scores served as comparison measures for evaluating LLM-inferred responses and scores.

2.5 Qualitative interviews, transcription, and deidentification

We conducted 60-90-minute parenting interviews remotely through the laboratory's HIPAA-compliant Zoom account. Study staff reviewed automated transcripts against the recordings to correct transcription errors and speaker labels while preserving participants' wording. Study staff also prepared separate versions excluding interviewer speech. Study staff labeled transcripts with assigned study identifiers and manually removed information that could identify participants, their children, or others mentioned in the interviews while preserving the substantive parenting content wherever possible. Staff checked the transcripts for remaining identifying information before submitting them to the LLMs for scoring.

2.6 Transcript representations

Prior to the main analysis, we conducted preliminary comparisons using interviews from 15 parents to select an efficient transcript format for the main analysis. Using Qwen and Gemini, we compared four formats: full interviews with both speakers (interviewers and parents), full interviews with parent speech only, introductory (e.g., rapport-building warm-up) segments with both speakers, and introductory segments with parent speech only. Introductory segments comprised responses to two questions: "Can you tell me a little about your family and your role as a parent?" and "What does being a parent/caregiver mean to you?" These comparisons examined prediction performance, computational requirements, and the amount of transcript material submitted for scoring. We selected parent-only introductory segments and supplied the same text to all six models in the main analysis.

Across these 15 interviews, full dialogue contained 176,358 words (M = 11,757 per parent), and introductory dialogue contained 32,774 words (M = 2,185). Removing interviewer speech reduced these totals to 130,375 and 26,084 words, respectively. Based on preliminary comparisons in a subset of 15 parents, we selected parent-only introductory segments for the main analysis of all 43 parents. This format reduced scoring costs and computational demands while limiting the amount of interview text shared with the models.

2.7 Language model scoring

Six LLMs were accessed through OpenRouter, representing OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, xAI, Meta, and Alibaba. The model set included both proprietary and open-weight models. The model set included four proprietary models: GPT-5.6 Terra (openai/gpt-5.6-terra), Claude Sonnet 5 (anthropic/claude-sonnet-5), Gemini 3.8 Flash (google/gemini-3.8-flash), and Grok 4.7 (x-ai/grok-4.7), and two open-weight models: Llama 4 Maverick (meta-llama/llama-4-maverick) and Qwen3 235B A22B 2507 (qwen/qwen3-235b-a22b-2507). We conducted all primary inference on October 11, 2026. Model-specific inference settings, including temperature, output limits, reasoning settings, and random seeds where supported, are reported in Table 2, along with provider routing and software versions. Requests were submitted through OpenRouter with zerodata-retention routing required (zdr=True) and provider data collection disabled (data_collection="deny"); no specific upstream inference provider was manually pinned.

For each parent, each model inferred responses to the 35 MAPS items using the parentonly introductory transcript. Parents' self-reported MAPS responses were withheld from all scoring inputs. Each participant-item pair was scored in a separate request with a fresh context. Models received the interview text, target child age, target MAPS item, and the five permitted response options. They were instructed to respond as though they were the interviewed parent, base their response on parenting practices and behaviors inferable from the interview, answer with respect to the target child and the previous two months, and focus on the target child when multiple children were discussed. Responses were constrained to exactly one of Never, Rarely, Sometimes, Often, or Always, with no explanation, punctuation, or additional text. Responses were subsequently mapped to numerical scores from 1 to 5, respectively. The same prompt wording was used across all six models; only model-specific inference settings differed where supported. No separate system prompt was used. The complete scoring prompt is provided in the Supplementary Materials.

2.8 Prediction agreement, score correlations, and internal consistency

Performance was evaluated at the item, subscale, and broader parenting dimension levels (i.e., negative parenting and positive parenting). At the item level, mean absolute error (MAE) was calculated as the mean absolute difference between inferred and self-reported responses. Exact agreement was the percentage of inferred responses matching the corresponding selfreported response, and agreement within one category was the percentage differing by no more than one response category. Signed error was calculated as the inferred response minus the selfreported response; negative values indicated numerically lower inferred responses. Because MAPS items assess both positive and negative parenting behaviors, the direction of numerical error was interpreted separately from the favorability of the behavior assessed.

Item-level metrics were calculated using all usable predictions for each model. There were 1,505 possible participant-item pairs per model in the main sample and 525 pairs per condition in the 15-parent Qwen transcript-selection analysis. Missing or unusable responses were excluded from the corresponding metric denominators. When one or more constituent item responses were missing, subscale scores were calculated as the mean of the remaining usable items, consistent with the MAPS scoring procedure; a subscale score was treated as missing only if none of its constituent items had a usable response.

MAPS subscale scores were calculated as the mean of their constituent items on the 1-5 Likert scale. Positive Parenting was calculated as the mean of Proactive Parenting, Positive Reinforcement, Warmth, and Supportiveness; Negative Parenting was calculated as the mean of Hostility, Lax Control, and Physical Control. Pearson correlations quantified convergence between inferred and self-reported scores across parents. Spearman correlations were also calculated to assess rank-order convergence. Absolute agreement was evaluated using Lin's concordance correlation coefficient, MAE, and signed error at the subscale level. Broadband analyses included Pearson and Spearman correlations and signed error.

Overall construct convergence was summarized by Fisher z transforming the Pearson correlations for the six subscales with estimable correlations across all models, averaging the transformed coefficients, and transforming the mean back to the correlation scale. Physical Control was excluded from this summary because several models produced invariant scores.

Correlations were not estimated for model-subscale combinations with no between-participant variation.

For the main construct-level analyses, we calculated each parent's MAPS subscale scores separately for each of the six models, applying the missing-item scoring rule within each model. We then averaged the six model-specific scores for each subscale to obtain the parent's ensemble subscale score. We evaluated the ensemble scores using the same construct-level analyses as the individual-model scores. Two-sided Pearson correlation tests were adjusted using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure, separately across the 46 estimable model-by-subscale associations and the 14 model-by-broadband associations, including the ensemble. Lin's concordance correlation coefficient was used to assess how closely model-inferred and selfreported scores matched in both their between-parent variation and absolute values. Confidence intervals were estimated using 10,000 participant-level bootstrap resamples. Each resample retained all item responses from each selected parent, preserving the dependence among observations within parents. Conventional Pearson tests and bootstrap confidence intervals were reported alongside Spearman correlations to characterize associations that differed across these approaches.

Internal consistency was assessed using Cronbach's alpha for each MAPS subscale based on parent-reported responses and each model's item predictions. For the ensemble, we averaged the available predictions across models for each participant-item pair, retained the resulting values without rounding, and calculated alpha from these item-level averages. Alpha was not reported when insufficient response variance prevented estimation.

Exploratory nomological validity analyses examined whether LLM-inferred MAPS scores preserved associations with theoretically relevant external psychological constructs. For the ensemble nomological analysis, we first averaged the available model predictions for each participant-item pair and then calculated MAPS subscale scores from those item-level averages. Correspondence between the resulting nomological profiles was evaluated using profile correlations and mean absolute differences between corresponding associations, with 95% confidence intervals estimated from 10,000 participant-level bootstrap resamples.

We also conducted an exploratory analysis to examine whether disagreement across LLMs was associated with prediction error. Cross-model disagreement was quantified as the standard deviation of the available model predictions for each participant-item combination and examined in relation to ensemble absolute error. The association between cross-model disagreement and ensemble absolute error was evaluated using a linear mixed-effects model with crossed random intercepts for participant and MAPS item. For each participant-item pair, ensemble absolute error was the absolute difference between the mean of the available model predictions and the corresponding parent-reported MAPS response. Additional details on the cross-model disagreement and nomological validity analyses are provided in Supplementary Tables S8-S10.

2.9 Preliminary transcript selection analyses

Preliminary analyses examined two features of transcript representation: interview length and inclusion of interviewer speech. Four representations were compared: full dialogue, full parentonly text, introductory dialogue, and introductory parent-only text. Qwen was used for the initial comparisons, and Gemini was used to examine whether the findings were consistent across a second model. Both models were evaluated on the same 15 parents and all 35 MAPS items, yielding 525 participant-item predictions per transcript condition. Within each model, the same parents, MAPS items, scoring prompt, response options, and inference settings were used across all four conditions, such that transcript representation was the only manipulated input. The same scoring prompt was also used across Qwen and Gemini, although model-specific inference settings differed where applicable; specifically, Gemini used medium reasoning effort whereas Qwen used the non-thinking model variant.

MAE was calculated for each parent in each condition. Paired differences were defined as MAE for the reduced representation minus MAE for the more complete representation; positive values therefore indicated lower error when additional content was retained. Mean paired differences and 95% confidence intervals were estimated using the participant-level bootstrap procedure described above, retaining each parent's paired condition scores within all 10,000 resamples. Confidence intervals were calculated using the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the bootstrap distribution. Confidence intervals containing zero were not interpreted as demonstrating equivalence or noninferiority.

At the item level, inferred responses were classified as unchanged or changed across conditions. Changed responses were further classified according to whether their absolute error decreased, increased, or remained equal relative to the corresponding self-reported response. These descriptive comparisons were considered alongside paired MAE differences and computational requirements when selecting the introductory, parent-only representation for the main analysis. Transcript selection was therefore informed by the preliminary results.

2.10 Textual Input and computational efficiency

Textual inputs and computational requirements were summarized using prompt-token counts and monetary costs for the available scoring runs. For each model and transcript condition, summaries were based on the complete set of 525 participant-item scoring records from the 15-parent preliminary sample. Prompt-token counts and monetary costs were taken from the usage metadata returned by OpenRouter for each successful request and summed across requests within each condition. Percentage reductions in prompt tokens and cost were calculated separately relative to the full-dialogue condition within each model because tokenization, model pricing, and completion usage differed across models. Reported costs therefore represent the recorded billed cost associated with each returned request. Costs from any preceding failed or retried requests were not separately accumulated in these summaries.

All data preprocessing and statistical analyses were conducted in Python (version 3.11.15) using NumPy (version 2.4.6; Harris et al., 2020), pandas (version 3.0.2; McKinney, 2010), SciPy (version 1.17.1; Virtanen et al., 2020), and statsmodels (version 0.15.0; Seabold & Perktold, 2010). Data visualization was performed using seaborn (version 0.13.2; Waskom, 2021) and Matplotlib (version 3.10.8; Hunter, 2007).

4. Discussion

This study evaluated whether six large language models could infer parents' MAPS questionnaire responses from the parent-only introductory segments of qualitative interviews. All models showed positive overall convergence with parent self-report, although performance varied across parenting dimensions and evaluation metrics. Warmth showed the most consistent convergence, whereas associations for Hostility, Supportiveness, and Lax Control depended more on the model. Proactive Parenting and Positive Reinforcement showed stronger Pearson than Spearman correlations, with bootstrap intervals indicating considerable uncertainty. Physical Control showed little convergence and substantial restriction in both self-reported and inferred scores. Across models, positive construct-level associations coexisted with limited exact item agreement and systematically lower inferred response categories. These findings extend research examining LLM-based inference of psychological characteristics from narrative data to the assessment of parenting (Fan et al., 2023; Peters & Matz, 2024; Wright et al., 2026).

4.1 Differences across parenting dimensions

The clearest evidence of convergence emerged for Warmth, for which every model showed positive associations supported by conventional tests, bootstrap confidence intervals, and rank correlations. One possible explanation is that parents' descriptions of their families and the meaning of caregiving convey relational information relevant to warmth even without direct questions about specific behaviors. This interpretation is consistent with approaches that assess representations of caregiving and parent-child relationships through parental narratives (Rodriguez, Cadet, et al., 2023; Rodriguez et al., 2025; Rodriguez, La Barrie, et al., 2023). Future work could examine whether inferred warmth reflects explicit descriptions of affectionate behavior, broader expressions of connection, or other features of parents' narratives.

Findings for the remaining dimensions were less consistent. Hostility and Supportiveness showed convergence for several models, whereas Lax Control was more dependent on the model selected. Supportiveness also had comparatively low internal consistency in parent self-report, introducing uncertainty into the comparison measure. Measurement error in a comparison measure can constrain observed convergence (Schmidt & Hunter, 1996). Differences in convergence therefore may reflect a combination of narrative coverage, model inference, and the measurement properties of the self-reported subscales. The current design cannot isolate the contribution of each.

These findings also build on prior work demonstrating the importance of examining how parents understand assessment items. Cognitive interviews used to revise the MAPS identified difficulties involving item wording, interpretations of affection, and descriptions of disciplinary practices (Rodriguez et al., 2025). Although the present study examined model inference rather than questionnaire revision, both approaches highlight the importance of the information available when translating parenting experiences into standardized responses. Future research could examine whether items that parents interpret inconsistently are also more difficult for models to infer from narrative accounts.

Proactive Parenting and Positive Reinforcement warrant particular attention because their Pearson correlations suggested stronger convergence than their rank correlations and bootstrap intervals. These discrepancies indicate that conclusions depended on how convergence was evaluated. Positive linear associations did not consistently translate into stable ordering of parents across the score distribution. Pearson correlations can be sensitive to influential observations and distributional characteristics, making complementary analyses useful when evaluating convergence (Bishara & Hittner, 2012; Pernet et al., 2013; Wilcox, 2012). With only 43 participants, individual observations may have considerable influence on correlation estimates. Larger samples would allow more precise evaluation of these dimensions, including the influence of individual observations and performance across different score ranges.

Physical Control illustrates a different measurement problem. Nearly three quarters of parents reported scores at the scale minimum, and three models assigned the same subscale score to every parent. Under these conditions, small absolute errors can arise from predictions concentrated near the floor without successful differentiation among parents. Restricted score variation can also attenuate correlations and complicate the interpretation of predictive performance (Branco et al., 2016; Sackett & Yang, 2000; Terwee et al., 2007). Evaluating this sample and transcript segment provided a limited test. Evaluating this dimension will require samples with greater behavioral variation and interview content that provides opportunities to discuss disciplinary practices.

4.2 Correlations, exact response matches, and score differences

Models sometimes captured between-parent differences in broader parenting scores while exactly matching fewer than half of parents' individual item responses. Model rankings also changed across these measures. Llama, for example, showed one of the largest average subscale correlations but the lowest percentage of exact item matches. A positive correlation indicates that parents with higher self-reported scores generally also received higher model-inferred scores. Those inferred scores can still be systematically higher or lower than parents' reports. Correlations therefore provide information about relative differences among parents, while exact item matches and score differences indicate how closely models reproduce parents' responses (Bland & Altman, 1986; Lin, 1989).

Exploratory analyses examined whether model-inferred parenting scores showed similar associations with other psychological measures as parent-reported parenting scores. These associations were only partly preserved, with several correlations differing in magnitude or direction. Thus, models' correlations with parent-reported MAPS scores provided an incomplete picture of how the inferred scores related to other constructs (Cronbach & Meehl, 1955). The small sample and shared reliance on parent reports across the questionnaire measures may have contributed to these differences. Systematic score differences also helped explain the results. Every model assigned numerically lower item responses on average than parents, and selected the highest response category substantially less often. Every model underestimated Positive Parenting, whereas differences in Negative Parenting varied across models, with some showing average differences close to zero. Lower responses indicated less frequent endorsement of the behavior being assessed, so their substantive meaning depended on whether the items described positive or negative parenting practices.

Several explanations may account for these differences. Parental questionnaire responses can be influenced by recall, response formats, and socially desirable responding in parental selfreport (Morsbach & Prinz, 2006). Models may also interpret response categories differently when working from narrative accounts, particularly when high-frequency ratings require explicit evidence about how often a behavior occurs. The introductory interview segment may provide limited examples of such behaviors. Future comparisons with repeated reports and behavioral observations could help distinguish these explanations.

The combined Positive Parenting score correlated more consistently with parent reports than the combined Negative Parenting score. Averaging across positive parenting dimensions may have reduced some variation specific to individual items or subscales, consistent with measurement principles concerning aggregation (Rushton et al., 1983). Results nevertheless varied among the dimensions contributing to each combined score. Positive Parenting included some positive subscales with less consistent correlations, while Negative Parenting combined dimensions with substantially different score distributions and correlations with parent reports.

4.3 Model selection and internal consistency

Positive overall convergence across six model families suggests that the observed associations were not confined to one model. Nevertheless, no model had the largest point estimates across all constructs and metrics, and the ensemble did not exceed the strongest individual models in overall convergence. Model selection should therefore reflect the intended parenting dimension and whether the priority is stronger correlations with parent reports, smaller prediction errors, or closer matching of score levels. Greater disagreement across models was associated with higher ensemble prediction error, suggesting that cross-model variability may provide a limited indicator of prediction uncertainty (Table S8). Shared systematic errors remain a consideration when using agreement among models to estimate prediction uncertainty ( (Kim et al., 2025).

Most model-generated subscales showed coherent internal consistency, but this coherence did not consistently correspond to stronger convergence with parent self-report. For Physical Control, the item-level ensemble showed high internal consistency (α = .865), while the ensemble formed by averaging model-specific subscale scores showed negligible convergence with parent self-report (r = .045). These estimates address distinct questions about the coherence of the averaged item predictions and the correspondence of the aggregated subscale scores with parent reports. Evaluating both provides a fuller account of score performance (Clark & Watson, 1995; Messick, 1995; Sijtsma, 2009). Internally consistent predictions can therefore organize item responses coherently while failing to capture the corresponding differences among parents. Reliability estimates should be considered alongside external agreement rather than treated as sufficient evidence of successful assessment.

4.4 Implications and future directions

The findings support further investigation of LLM scoring as a complementary way to quantify information from parenting interviews. Previous research has used computational text analysis to identify common parenting concerns in online discussions (Westrupp et al., 2022), and methodological work has examined how text mining can complement qualitative analysis (Janasik et al., 2009). The present study addressed a different task: inferring standardized parenting questionnaire responses from individual parents' narratives. This application offers a way to examine connections between narrative accounts and established measures, particularly when qualitative data have already been collected. Linking inferred scores to the original narratives could help researchers examine how parents' descriptions of specific experiences relate to broader parenting dimensions. Future studies should evaluate whether these scores help explain child and family outcomes and how they can complement qualitative analyses of the same interviews.

These findings motivate testing brief daily audio accounts as a potentially lower-burden approach to repeated parenting assessment. A central question is how much audio is needed to infer each parenting dimension. Studies could vary recording duration, the questions parents answer, and the number of days sampled to identify efficient combinations of narrative length and behavioral coverage. Comparisons with daily questionnaires and behavioral observations could examine both differences among parents and changes within the same parent across days.

Completion time, adherence, and parents' perceived burden would provide direct evidence of the practical benefits of this approach.

Further development should distinguish explicit behavioral evidence from inference based on limited information. Allowing models to indicate insufficient evidence, identify supporting passages, or express uncertainty may help clarify when a questionnaire response is supported by the transcript. Evaluation could test whether supporting passages and uncertainty estimates help identify predictions with larger errors (Farquhar et al., 2024; Turpin et al., 2023; Wen et al., 2025; Xiong et al., 2024). Any systematic score differences procedure should be developed and evaluated in separate samples. Comparisons with simple prediction baselines and trained human coders would also help establish the added value of LLM scoring relative to less complex approaches.

4.5 Limitations

Several limitations are warrant discussions. The sample included 43 parents, limiting precision and the ability to evaluate differences across demographic groups, child developmental stages, or family contexts. The large number of item predictions did not increase the number of independent participants. Floor and ceiling concentrations further limited the range of parenting behaviors represented. Prior research has documented limited evaluation of parenting measures across racial and ethnic groups and identified measurement nonequivalence in the MAPS (Rodriguez, Cadet, et al., 2023; Rodriguez, La Barrie, et al., 2023). These findings underscore the need to evaluate both the questionnaire comparison measure and model-inferred scores across populations. Replication should include larger and more heterogeneous samples, including interviews conducted in languages other than English. Overall convergence should be supplemented by direct examination of subgroup differences in error, systematic score differences, and construct convergence.

Both the interviews and questionnaires relied on the same parent as the source of information. Their convergence may partly reflect shared perceptions, disclosure patterns, or presentation of parenting across formats. A multitrait-multimethod perspective emphasizes the need to distinguish evidence about the intended constructs from effects associated with the assessment methods (Campbell & Fiske, 1959). Without observational assessments, other informants, or external criteria, these results cannot establish correspondence with independently assessed parenting behavior. Future studies should examine convergence with coparent and youth reports, observational measures, and relevant child and family outcomes. Repeated assessments could establish the temporal stability of inferred scores and their sensitivity to changes in parenting.

Preliminary comparisons supported selecting parent-only introductory segments for the assessment targets examined here. Replication in an independent sample would help establish how well this transcript-selection approach generalizes (Cawley & Talbot, 2010; Yarkoni & Westfall, 2017). Future studies can extend this work by testing stand-alone brief interview or dailu audio recordings. The current findings therefore do not demonstrate that shorter interviews and examining how interviewer speech contributes to scoring across assessment targets. Repeated scoring runs and alternative prompts, and independently evaluated transcript-selection procedures would further clarify the reproducibility of the approach.

5. Conclusion

LLM-inferred scores from parents' introductory interview narratives correlated with several self-reported parenting dimensions, with the most consistently Warmth and combined Positive Parenting. Performance varied across dimensions, and models tended to assign lower response categories than parents. These findings provide an initial basis for developing brief narrative approaches to parenting assessment. Testing how much spoken information is needed for different dimensions, and whether brief daily recordings reduce burden while capturing changes in parenting, offers a practical direction for further development.
