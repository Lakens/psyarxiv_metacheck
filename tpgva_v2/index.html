Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Marginal Significance works

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). Abstract

Purpose: Individual differences in literacy and numeracy -that is, how well one can read, write, and work with numbers -emerge early in childhood and are crucial indicators of later educational success. Twin and family studies revealed substantial heritability of literacy and numeracy, and genetic overlap between these domains. Yet, it is unclear whether measured genomic variation shows a similarly overlapping or domain-specific pattern, and whether such molecular signals also predict adults' educational attainment.

Method: In up to N=15,669 participants (52.7% female; Netherlands Twin Register), we predicted children's literacy, numeracy, and academic achievement, and adults' educational attainment from genetic predispositions. Children's learning outcomes were assessed via teacher and parent reports and a nationwide test (age M=12.25 years). Adults reported their highest educational qualification (age M= 42.47 years). We derived polygenic scores (PGSs) for years of education, dyslexia, and reading skills to predict these outcomes.

Results: All three PGSs significantly predicted educational outcomes, but the broad years-ofeducation PGS exceeded the domain-specific dyslexia and reading-skills PGSs. The years-ofeducation PGS explained up to 10.7% in children's academic achievement and 13.2% in adults' attainment, suggesting that broader, large-scale PGS of general education capture much of the genetic signal shared across learning domains.

Conclusion: Findings indicate both shared and specific genetic influences on learning. Broad, high-power PGSs maximise prediction, whereas literacy-focused PGSs highlight domainspecific signal. While current PGSs are not suitable for individual prediction, they are valuable for accounting for genetic propensities and improving causal inference in developmental and education research.

Literacy and numeracy -that is, children's capacity to read, write, and work with numbersare foundational for learning in school and beyond. Substantial individual differences are already evident before school entry; they manifest and increase over time, and forecast later language and mathematics achievement, as well as adult educational attainment and cognitive performance (G. J. Duncan et al., 2008; Ferrer et al., 2015; Kaufman et al., 2012; Liu et al., 2025; Ritchie & Bates, 2013). Twin studies repeatedly confirmed that learning ability clusters in families due to genetic as well as environmental factors, with literacy and numeracy in primary school typically showing heritability estimates ranging between 40 and 85% (de Zeeuw et al., 2015; Eifler et al., 2019; Grasby et al., 2016; Haughbrook et al., 2017; Kovas et al., 2007; Polderman et al., 2015; van Bergen et al., 2018). Recognizing genetic effects that shape variation in learning ability is crucial in developmental and education research, because neglecting genetics can otherwise lead to overestimating environmental influences and misdirecting intervention efforts. Genetic factors help explain why children differ in their learning, and accounting for them improves accurate estimation of environmental effects (Hart et al., 2021; Kendler & Baker, 2007; Pingault et al., 2022; Plomin & Bergeman, 1991; Wertz et al., 2019).

Learning skills are largely domain-general: the same children who excel (or struggle) in reading often show parallel patterns in mathematics (Cameron et al., 2019; Singer & Strasser, 2017). At the lower tail, this interdependence is reflected in the co-occurrence of learning difficulties, like dyslexia and dyscalculia (van Bergen et al., 2025). Population-based studies showed that children with mathematics difficulties have a two-to three-times increased risk of also having reading difficulties, while the overlap likely is even stronger in clinical samples (Joyner & Wagner, 2020; Landerl & Moll, 2010; Visser et al., 2020). Genetically sensitive studies showed that domain-general risk factors mainly reflect shared genetic influences that contribute to learning difficulties across domains (van Bergen et al., 2025; Willcutt et al., 2019). However, given that genetic correlations from twin studies between literacy and numeracy domains emerged as significantly less than 1, some degree of skill-specific genetic influence likely exists alongside the domain-general genetic signal (Grasby et al., 2016). In addition, genetic influences are also general in the sense of shaping learning across the normal range as well as learning difficulties, which is referred to as the "generalist genes" hypothesis for learning abilities and disabilities (Haworth et al., 2009; Kovas & Plomin, 2007; Plomin & Kovas, 2005).

Beyond twin and family designs, which estimate genetic influence at the group level, recent molecular genetic approaches offer tools to index genetic propensities directly from DNA differences at the level of individuals. A polygenic score (PGS) aggregates the small effects of many trait-associated genetic variants (single-nucleotide polymorphisms, SNPs) discovered in genome-wide association studies (GWAS), providing individual-level predictors that can be used in population cohorts (Lewis & Vassos, 2020; Pingault et al., 2022; Plomin & von Stumm, 2018). For educational attainment, contemporary PGSs account for about 12-16% of variance in adults' years of schooling across European-ancestry samples (Lee et al., 2018; Okbay et al., 2022; Wilding et al., 2024). Since an individual's genetic makeup is fixed from birth, the genetic variants and effect sizes identified in the adult GWAS on years of education can be applied to children to estimate their inherited propensity for the same trait.

First evidence suggests that the PGS for years of education predicts children's academic performance in terms of standardised test results and school grades from as early as 7 years, yet predictive power varies greatly across samples and developmental ages (=.16-.37) (Allegrini et al., 2019; Starr et al., 2025). Recently developed PGSs for dyslexia and reading skills offer the possibility of more domain-specific prediction, and can indicate the extent to which the genetic architecture of literacy and numeracy overlaps (Allegrini et al., 2019; Daucourt et al., 2020; Doust et al., 2022; Eising et al., 2022; van Bergen et al., 2025).

Yet, previous research is limited in three ways: It has focused mainly on adult samples, relied on earlier or narrower PGSs, and seldom compared domain-general with domainspecific predictions. First, most work has focused on educational attainment in adulthood, with fewer studies testing education-related PGS predictions of children's learning outcomes (Allegrini et al., 2019; Starr et al., 2025). Second, the GWASs for dyslexia and reading skills are comparably new and niche, meaning the value of their derived PGSs is mostly unknown (Doust et al., 2022; Eising et al., 2022). Yet, these PGSs are likely well suited for domainspecific predictions of children's learning and might elucidate to what extent the genetic aetiology of literacy and numeracy is distinct or domain-general, corresponding to prior phenotypic and twin study findings (Daucourt et al., 2020; van Bergen et al., 2025). Third, the predictive landscape is rapidly evolving as GWAS sample sizes increase and variant coverage improves. The most recent GWAS on years of education and the updated PGS (EA4) have yet to be fully leveraged in developmental cohorts, with most previous research relying on the older versions of the PGS for years of education -that is, those based on EA3 or EA4 excluding data from 23&me (Lee et al., 2018; Okbay et al., 2022).

In this study, we tested the predictive power of three learning-related PGSs -years of education (EA4), dyslexia, and reading skills -for the outcomes of literacy, numeracy, and a standardised academic test score at age 12 years, and adults' educational attainment, using data from the Netherlands Twin Register (NTR). The research questions were preregistered on the Open Science Framework (https://osf.io/8e3mr/overview?view_only=83f7a252a317482693240bc0ee9b15a4; blinded for peer review). First, we assessed how strongly learning-related PGSs predict educational outcomes in childhood compared to adulthood. Second, we tested whether cross-domain predictions reflect previously shown genetic overlap between children's literacy and numeracy (i.e., PGS for reading skills and dyslexia predicting numeracy skills). In additional non-preregistered analyses, we also benchmarked PGS predictions of children's learning against parental educational attainment, tested whether the three PGSs explain incremental variance when combined in a single model, and examined whether parental educational attainment and the PGSs each explain partially non-overlapping variance in children's learning outcomes. By addressing these questions, we aim to clarify how genetic factors shape differences in literacy, numeracy, and educational attainment, and to evaluate the utility of PGSs as tools in education research.

Methods

Sample

The sample was drawn from the Netherlands Twin Register (NTR), which has been enrolling about 52% of all Dutch twins since 1987 and regularly collects data from these twins as well as their parents and siblings. The recruitment process and sample are described in detail elsewhere (Ligthart et al., 2019). Participating families are slightly higher educated compared to the general Dutch population, however NTR twins do not differ significantly in their educational achievement from other children, including their non-twin siblings (van Bergen et al., 2018). Prior to analysis, we applied general data cleaning procedures and removed participants without data on at least one educational outcome or valid genotype data from the sample. Filtering was applied to only include participants of European ancestry as GWASs are largely based on European-ancestry samples, thus PGS predictions are substantially less accurate for participants of non-European ancestry (L. Duncan et al., 2019). Final sample sizes for the main analyses differed between the considered outcomes and consisted of N=2,140-15,669 participants (see Supplementary Table S1 for variable-specific information on missing data). Children were on average 12.25 (SD=0.43) years old when data on literacy, numeracy, and the standardised test score was collected. Adult participants were on average 42.47 (SD=10.64) years old when they reported educational attainment. 52.7% of participants were female.

Measures Polygenic scores (PGSs).

In NTR, genetic material was collected from twins and their families via saliva samples. Based on participants' extracted genetic data and summary statistics from the three GWAS for years of education, dyslexia, and reading skills, we derived polygenic scores using the SBayesRC approach (Zeng et al., 2018; Zheng et al., 2024). Detailed information on genotyping and the polygenic score calculation is included in the Supplementary Materials S2.

Years-of-education PGS.

Based on a GWAS of N=3,037,499 individuals (Okbay et al., 2022) coming mostly from the US and UK, 3,592 significant genetic variants (SNPs) were identified that relate to adults' educational attainment indexed by the number of years spent in fulltime education. The derived PGS explained 12-16% of variance in educational attainment in independent samples.

Dyslexia PGS.

The PGS for dyslexia was derived from a GWAS including N=51,800 US-American adult cases with a self-reported dyslexia diagnosis and N=1,087,070 controls (Doust et al., 2022). In the discovery sample, 42 significant SNPs were identified that relate to the dyslexia phenotype. The dyslexia PGS accounted for 3.6-5.6% of variance in reading traits when tested in an independent sample.

Reading skills PGS.

A GWAS on N=13,633-33,595 child and adult participants from different European countries, North America, and Australia, identified SNPs associated with five language-related phenotypes, including word reading, nonword reading, spelling, phoneme awareness, and nonword repetition (Eising et al., 2022). SNP heritability varied across phenotypes, accounting for 13-26% of variation (no predictiveness of the derived PGS in an independent sample was reported). For use in this study, we derived one comprehensive multi-trait PGS across all five phenotypes.

Educational outcome measures

Literacy.

For twin children and their siblings, literacy was assessed at age 12 years through parent and teacher reports on four items. Teachers indicated whether the child was not reading up to par (0=not true at all to3=very much true), and rated the child's reading and literacy performance (1=poor to5=very good). Parents reported the child's current school results in the literacy domain (same scale as for teachers). After z-transforming and reversing items where necessary, a mean score was generated. The mean score shows high reliability (α=.86) and is strongly correlated with standardised literacy test scores (r=.67; based on CITO results; see also Supplementary Table S2), which were available for a subsample of our study sample.

Numeracy.

Numeracy was assessed in twin children and siblings at age 12 via three items. Teachers rated whether the child performed poorly in arithmetic (0=not true at all to3=very much true). Both the teacher and parents reported on the child's current school performance in arithmetic (1=poor to5=very good). A mean score was generated after z-transforming and reversing items where necessary. The mean score shows high reliability (α=.87) and is strongly correlated with standardised numeracy test scores (r=.79; see also Supplementary Table S2).

Standardised academic test score. Scores on the Dutch nationwide standardised achievement test CITO (Centraal Instituut voor Toets Ontwikkeling, 2002; de Zeeuw et al., 2020) were used as an indicator of academic achievement. CITO is a high-stakes test administered at the end of Genetic signals shape educational outcomes September 2026 Starr et al. Teachers reported total scores ranging from 500 to 550, which were z-standardised to ensure comparability with other educational outcome measures.

Educational attainment.

All adult participants in the NTR (that is, adult twins, siblings, and parents) aged 25 years and above reported their highest level of education completed, which was aggregated into a 7-level measure of educational attainment (1=primary school to7=university degree).

Statistical analysis

The analysis plan was preregistered on the OSF (https://osf.io/8e3mr/overview?view_only=83f7a252a317482693240bc0ee9b15a4; blinded for peer review), with deviations being reported in Supplementary Materials S1. All analyses were conducted in R (version 4.5.1; R Core Team, 2025) and RStudio (version 2025.05.1; Posit team, 2025), using the gee package to fit Generalized Estimating Equation (GEE) models (Carey, 2024).

To examine the predictive strength of the three PGSs (Years of education, Dyslexia, and Reading skills) for educational outcomes, we used GEE models, which account for data non-independence due to the twin family structure of the sample. Missing data were handled using listwise omission, the standard approach in GEE analyses. In total, 18 GEE models were estimated, covering all combinations of the three PGSs and four educational outcomes (literacy, numeracy, standardised test score at age 12, and adult educational attainment), and additional analyses mentioned below.

Each model was fitted in two steps. First, an "empty" model included age, sex, genotyping platform, and the first 20 genomic principal components as covariates. Second, the respective PGS was added as a predictor. PGS effect sizes were indexed by standardised beta coefficients with robust standard errors, and incremental variance explained was quantified using pseudo-R². All significance tests were one-sided and Bonferroni-corrected for multiple testing (α = 0.05/18 = 0.003). To compare the relative predictive strength of the PGSs, we tested differences between standardised betas using bootstrapped standard errors.

In additional non-preregistered analyses, we predicted children's educational outcomes from parental educational attainment, a frequently-used and strong predictor of children's learning, which served as a benchmark for the PGS predictions. We also specified additional models that tested the incremental explained variance from the PGSs for dyslexia and reading skills, respectively, beyond the prediction effect of the years of education PGS, and the extent to which the PGSs and parental educational attainment contribute significantly to the overall explained variance in children's learning outcomes.

Domain-general and -specific genetic prediction of educational outcomes

We next compared the relative predictive strength of the three learning-related PGSs. Across all outcomes, the PGS for years of education was the most powerful predictor. For adult educational attainment, the years-of-education PGS was significantly more predictive than both the PGS for dyslexia (Z=27.55, p<.001), and the PGS for reading skills (Z=27.64, p<.001).

The same pattern was observed for children's standardised test score (Z=9.57 and Z=10.76 and, both p<.001) and numeracy (Z=5.75, p<.001; PGS for reading skills did not significantly predict numeracy in the first place). For literacy, the years-of-education PGS was more predictive than the reading skills PGS (Z=6.14, p<.001), but did not significantly outperform the PGS for dyslexia (Z=1.33, p=.183).

Comparisons among the PGS for dyslexia and reading skills showed that the PGS for dyslexia explained significantly more variance in literacy (Z=-3.82, p<.001), whereas their predictive strength did not differ for the standardised test score (Z=0.27, p=.785). For adult educational attainment, the PGS for reading skills was significantly more predictive (Z=4.22, p<.001), despite the overall modest predictive effects of both PGSs. When testing the PGSs for dyslexia and reading skills for domain specificity in children's literacy and numeracy, both PGSs emerged as significantly more predictive for literacy (dyslexia: Z=-4.77, p<.001; reading skills: Z=2.15, p=.032). While the difference in predicting literacy vs. numeracy was substantial for the dyslexia PGS, the difference was very small for the reading skills PGS, indicating weaker evidence for domain specificity; given its modest prediction of literacy, it is likely less meaningful in practice.

The exploratory, benchmarking analyses showed that PGS predictions of children's learning are comparable in effect size to predictions from parents' educational attainment, with explaining about 6% and 10% of variance in children's literacy and numeracy, and the standardised test score, respectively. In further exploratory models, we combined several PGSs and parental EA to test incremental prediction over and above the years-of-education PGS. Across outcomes, the dyslexia PGS was the more consistent incremental predictor, with added significant variance of up to ΔR²=3.2% for literacy and smaller increments for numeracy and the standardised tested score. The reading skills PGS added comparatively little, reaching significance only for the standardised test score (ΔR²=0.3%). In full models including all three PGSs and both parents' EA, the years-of-education and dyslexia PGSs contributed significantly across all three outcomes, as did parental EA. Overall, the results demonstrate that the PGS for years of education -based on a rather coarse phenotype measure -captures the largest proportion of variance across all educational outcomes, thus indexes genetic effects relevant to learning at a domain-general level. The PGSs for dyslexia and reading skills, however, show comparatively more domainspecific predictive patterns: Children's genetic propensity for dyslexia in particular predicts their differences in literacy skills well, whereas the PGS for reading skills does not significantly predict differences in numeracy skills. These effects in part remain significant over and above the prediction of the PGS for years of education and indicate that children's learning skills are shaped by both shared and unique genetic influences.

Discussion

We tested how well three learning-related polygenic scores (PGSs) -indexing genetic propensities for years of education, dyslexia, and reading skills -predict children's literacy, numeracy, and standardised academic test results at age 12 years, and adults' educational attainment. Our findings make three main contributions. First, all three PGSs robustly predict educational outcomes in children and adults, but differ markedly in predictive strength and specificity. Second, the results pattern aligns with the "generalist genes" hypothesis and confirms learning being shaped largely by domain-general genetic effects, yet also indicating domain-specific genetic signal. Third, when benchmarked against parents' educational attainment, PGSs demonstrate comparable predictive strength for children's learning. Below, we discuss these three main contributions and their implications. The dyslexia PGS might mainly reflect genetic effects on foundational decoding processes (e.g., word-level reading, phonological skills, processing speed), whereas the years-ofeducation PGS may index broader genetic signal on higher-order (non-)cognitive traits and socio-emotional skills that support learning more generally, as well as potentially capturing the family's socio-economic background (Abdellaoui et al., 2025). It is possible that the dyslexia PGS is particularly predictive of teacher-and parent-reported literacy because informants might be especially sensitive to dysfunctional or clinically relevant traits aligned with dyslexia. Whether objective, trait-specific measures (e.g., standardised tests of reading and spelling) would yield similar predictions remains open. Overall, findings are largely consistent with the "generalist genes" hypothesis, while also revealing domain-specific signals. In particular, we found the PGSs for dyslexia and reading skills predicting literacy more strongly than numeracy, which echoes twin-based evidence for shared and unique genetic influences (Daucourt et al., 2020; de Zeeuw et al., 2015; Grasby et al., 2016; Kovas et al., 2007; Plomin & Kovas, 2005; van Bergen et al., 2025).

Third, we showed that PGSs can predict children's learning to approximately the same extent as common phenotypic markers of family background, such as parents' educational attainment. In our benchmarking analyses, both the PGS for years of education and mothers' and fathers' educational attainment each explained about 6-10% of the variance in children's learning. Moreover, exploratory analyses combining PGSs and parental educational attainment within a single model showed that both remained significant predictors, indicating that they capture partly non-overlapping variance. The comparable, and partly unique, predictive strength indicates that PGSs offer the possibility to account for a substantial portion of genetic variance in children's learning, when based on large, sufficiently powerful GWAS.

Because genotype data are stable from birth, PGSs can support early prediction of educational potential and complement, rather than merely duplicate, family sociodemographic predictors.

Strengths and limitations

Strengths of this study included a large, population-based, genotyped sample, data from multiple informants and a nationwide standardised test of children's learning, and a state-ofthe-art modelling approach. However, some limitations should be noted. First, the sample predominantly comprises individuals of European ancestry, limiting generalisability given the reduced portability of PGS across ancestries (L. Duncan et al., 2019). Second, literacy and numeracy were assessed via teacher and parent reports, which may introduce rater-specific bias and additional noise relative to test-based measures, although such reports have been shown to be valid and reliable (Rimfeld et al., 2019; van Bergen et al., 2018). Third, our design was cross-sectional at age 12, which precludes examining developmental changes in genetic architecture (Ferrer et al., 2015). Lastly, we did not explicitly model direct and indirect genetic effects. Thus, the PGS predictions might partly capture environmentally driven effects due to heritable parental behaviour. Such indirect genetic effects have been demonstrated for educational phenotypes (Howe et al., 2022; Starr et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2021; Wertz et al., 2019), yet prior studies did not find evidence for indirect genetic effects on children's learning in the Dutch context specifically (de Zeeuw et al., 2020; Demange et al., 2022; Swagerman et al., 2017).

Implications and future research Our findings have several implications for developmental and education research. We showed that PGS predictions are largely in line with the "generalist genes" hypothesis, as reflected by the strong signal of the PGS for years of education across domains. Yet, indications of domainspecificity emerged as well, especially with the dyslexia PGS being significantly more predictive for literacy than numeracy. Using multiple PGSs may therefore help disentangle distinct genetic signals that would be obscured when relying on a single, broad PGS, particularly for literacy outcomes. Yet, our results underscore the clear trade-off between sample size and phenotype specificity: large GWASs with less precise phenotyping currently yield more predictive PGSs than smaller, phenotype-specific GWASs. Future efforts combining large samples with deep phenotyping will likely improve domain-specific prediction.

Despite explaining meaningful variance, PGS effects remain substantially smaller than heritability estimates from twin studies -a phenomenon referred to as "missing heritability" (Kim et al., 2017; Manolio et al., 2009; Wainschtein et al., 2025). For educational traits, twin studies estimate heritability of roughly 60%, whereas the PGS for years of education explains only 12-16% of the variance in attainment (Okbay et al., 2022; Polderman et al., 2015).

Consequently, while PGSs can help account for part of a trait's genetic liability and reduce genetic confounding of putatively environmental predictors, adjusting for current PGSs alone will under-correct for total genetic influences (Fisher, 2025; Pingault et al., 2022; Plomin & von Stumm, 2022). Applying complementary approaches, such as the Familial Control Method, using parental phenotypes as proxies for genetic and overall family-level confounding, may be particularly suitable for education-related traits with predominantly genetic intergenerational transmission (de Zeeuw et al., 2020; Grande et al., 2025; Hart et al., 2021; Kleppesto et al., 2024; Wolfram et al., 2024). to disentangle direct and indirect genetic effects, which have been shown for the PGS for years of education (Demange et al., 2022; Nivard et al., 2024; Starr et al., 2025; Wertz et al., 2019). Yet, the extent to which family environments mediate genetic influences captured by the dyslexia and reading-skills PGSs remains to be determined.

Conclusion

Our findings show that the polygenic score for years of education captures substantial genetic signal across learning domains and adult educational attainment, consistent with the "generalist genes" hypothesis and twin evidence. Comparisons with more phenotype-specific PGSs for reading skills and dyslexia highlight that large GWAS sample size currently outweighs close phenotype matching. These results might guide researchers' choice of PGSs as control for genetic confounding when studying environmental prediction of child development.

Nevertheless, control for genetic confounding using current PGSs remains incomplete (Hart et al., 2021). Although domain-specific genetic signals for learning are presently modest, increasing GWAS sample sizes for reading and dyslexia are expected to strengthen literacy prediction. Until then, combining well-powered PGSs with complementary designs, such as the Familial Control Method, remains a valuable strategy for studying environmental effects and causal inference in development.
