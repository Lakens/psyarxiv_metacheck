To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 21 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. Abstract

Affect labeling reduces emotional responses during and immediately after use, but evidence for lasting regulatory effects remains mixed. While there is no experiential evidence for lasting effects, psychophysiological studies do hint at a longer-lasting impact. However, neuroimaging research has mainly compared affect labeling with affect matching. Although both involve emotional processing, affect labeling additionally engages linguistic processing. Thus, it remains unclear whether observed neural differences reflect emotion-focused labeling or mere linguistic processing, and whether such effects endure over time. We recorded EEG to examine the effects of affect labeling on negative emotion across two re-exposure phases: immediate and after a short interval (15 minutes). Affect labeling (emotion-specific words) was compared with affect matching (emotion-focused perceptual processing) and feature labeling (linguistic processing without emotional content), allowing us to dissociate emotional and linguistic components. Event-related potentials and EEG oscillatory activity were analyzed in both phases, with pre-intervention neural responses included as baseline. Robust emotion-related neural activity was observed. Negative (vs. neutral) stimuli elicited increased late positive potential (LPP) amplitudes, enhanced fronto-central-parietal theta power, and greater righthemispheric alpha activity. However, LPP, theta-, and alpha-band power did not show effects of affect labeling compared to the control conditions. Thus, the present findings provide no evidence for a lasting labeling effect. Because we focused on re-exposure, we may have missed potential regulatory effects during the intervention itself. Future research should further identify factors that influence the effectiveness of labeling and clarify when this implicit emotion regulation pathway is engaged.

1. Introduction

Affect labeling is an implicit emotion regulation (ER) strategy in which individuals use words to describe their feelings, often without realizing they are regulating their emotions (for a review see Torre & Lieberman, 2018). In laboratory settings, participants are typically presented with affective stimuli alongside emotion words and asked to select the word that best matches their feelings in response to the stimuli (Levy-Gigi & Shamay-Tsoory, 2022; Yoshimura et al., 2023). Affect labeling can immediately reduce negative emotional responses, reflected in higher valence and lower arousal ratings (Constantinescu et al., 2017; Levy-Gigi & Shamay-Tsoory, 2022; Lieberman et al., 2011).

However, findings at the subjective level are not fully consistent, as others failed to observe such immediate reductions (Nook et al., 2021; Vlasenko et al., 2021).

As an active control condition, previous studies have often used affect matching, in which participants select an emotion-related image that corresponds to their emotional response (Tupak et al., 2014; Yoshimura & Morimoto, 2018). Although both tasks involve evaluating emotional states, affect labeling requires additional linguistic processing, whereas affect matching relies on perceptual emotional processing. Alternatively, some studies have used non-affective labeling conditions (Constantinou et al., 2014; Vlasenko et al., 2021), in which participants select words related to the stimulus content rather than its emotional meaning. Notably, Constantinou et al. (2014) found that non-affective labeling could also reduce negative emotion, similar to affect labeling, whereas Vlasenko et al. (2021) found effects only for non-affective labeling. These tasks involve similar linguistic processes, but differ in the focus of labeling: affect labeling targets emotional information, whereas non-affective labeling targets other stimulus-related features (referred to here as feature labeling). This raises the question of whether the regulatory effects arise from labeling and the associated linguistic processing in general, or whether processing emotional information is necessary for these effects to occur. Inconsistencies are also observed when examining lasting effects of affect labeling. Some self-report studies report immediate reductions that do not persist into short-interval re-exposure (after the labeling task, Liang & Lin, 2023), whereas others fail to find effects both immediately and at oneweek follow-up (Kircanski et al., 2012; Niles et al., 2015). In these studies, physiological findings appear somewhat more promising but are also inconsistent, particularly in the timing of the effects.

For example, Kircanski et al. (2012) and Niles et al. (2015) report reduced physiological responses after affect labeling only at long-interval re-exposure (e.g., one week later), but not during immediate or relatively short-interval phases (e.g., two to three days). In contrast, Liang & Li (2023) observed reduced neural responses during immediate and short-interval re-exposure, yet did not include a long-term follow-up. Thus, while there is no experiential evidence for lasting effects of labeling, physiological evidence is mixed.

According to Gross's process model, ER strategies involve altering the trajectory of emotional responses, resulting in changes in the quality, duration, or intensity thereof (Gross, 2015; Gyurak et al., 2011). Self-report studies show that affect labeling reduces negative emotional feelings similarly to reappraisal, with both strategies resulting in reduced negative affect compared to passive viewing (Lieberman et al., 2011). Neuroimaging research also suggests overlap in the mechanisms supporting affect labeling and reappraisal. Both engage prefrontal regulatory regions, involved in emotion regulation, and are associated with decreased amygdala activity, which is linked to emotion generation (Burklund et al., 2014; Yoshimura et al., 2023). Together, these findings suggest that affect labeling may produce neural patterns similar to reappraisal (Yoshimura et al., 2023).

While EEG research into reappraisal is extensive, studies on affect labeling remain limited.

Nevertheless, both strategies seem to show similar effects on the Late Positive Potential (LPP). The LPP is a well-established ERP marker of emotional arousal that begins around 400 ms after stimulus onset and peaks at central-parietal sites (Hajcak & Foti, 2020; MacNamara et al., 2022). Emotional stimuli typically elicit larger LPP amplitudes than neutral stimuli, reflecting emotional reactivity (Hajcak & Foti, 2020; MacNamara et al., 2022). This effect is most pronounced over central-parietal regions but also observable at frontal sites (Chen et al., 2025).

The amplitude of the LPP elicited by emotional stimuli can be modulated by ER strategies, resulting in reduced LPP amplitudes for regulation compared to non-regulation conditions (Hajcak & Foti, 2020; MacNamara et al., 2022). Numerous studies report a reduced LPP for negative stimuli during reappraisal compared to passive viewing at central-parietal sites (see for a review MacNamara et al., 2022). Such a decreased LPP was also observed during short-interval re-exposure (i.e., following the reappraisal task) to previously reappraised negative stimuli (Bautista et al., 2022). Additionally, in the context of reappraisal, an enhanced frontal regulation-LPP (FR-LPP, regulation vs. non-regulation) has been associated with the engagement of top-down control processes (Doornweerd, 2024; Moser et al., 2006).

EEG evidence for the impact of affect labeling on the LPP is limited and less consistent. A few studies observed a similar reduced LPP during affect labeling compared to non-labeling conditions, indicating modulation of neural responses while labeling was being performed (Sun et al., 2025; Yue et al., 2024). One study further found a reduced LPP during re-exposure to previously labeled negative stimuli compared to non-labeled stimuli (Liang & Lin, 2023). However, this regulation-related LPP effect depended on the timing and comparison condition: during the immediate re-exposure phase, previously labeled negative stimuli elicited smaller LPP amplitudes than previously matched stimuli, whereas during short-interval re-exposure, smaller LPP amplitudes were observed for previously labeled relative to passively viewed negative stimuli.

Beyond the LPP, theta-band oscillatory activity is also sensitive to emotional valence. Previous research has shown that theta power increases in response to negative compared to neutral stimuli, particularly over parietal-temporal-occipital regions during the first second after stimulus onset (Aftanas et al., 2001 (Aftanas et al., , 2004 (Aftanas et al., , 2005;; Sulpizio et al., 2021). Increased frontal theta power is also seen during reappraisal, both when participants increase their response to negative stimuli ('increase' versus 'maintain' conditions), as well as when comparing 'decrease' versus 'maintain' conditions (Ertl et al., 2013). However, our previous study found no evidence of differences in theta related to emotional processing or regulation in the context of reappraisal (Chen et al., 2025).

Evidence regarding theta activity in the context of affect labeling is even more limited. To our knowledge, only one study has reported decreased prefrontal theta-band activity during affect labeling (Sun et al., 2025, versus non-affect labeling conditions), and no study has examined whether such theta modulation persists during re-exposure.

Furthermore, frontal alpha asymmetry has also been studied in the context of reappraisal (Parvaz et al., 2012) as it is linked to inhibitory control (Güntekin & Başar, 2014; Schubring & Schupp, 2021) and approach-avoidance motivation (Sabu et al., 2022). Some authors hypothesized that ER involves inhibitory control to suppress the emotional response, which requires engagement of prefrontal regions, and found that it is indeed associated with decreased alpha power (Güntekin & Başar, 2014; Klimesch, 1999; Knyazev, 2007; Schubring & Schupp, 2021). Reappraisal of negative stimuli was associated with reduced alpha power over the left frontal region, indicating regulatory activity, but this was in the context of increased bilateral frontal alpha power for negative than neutral stimuli (Parvaz et al., 2012). Similarly, affect labeling has been associated with left-lateralized prefrontal engagement evidence by fMRI studies (Burklund et al., 2014; Yoshimura et al., 2023).

Summing up, evidence for the lasting effects of affect labeling at the experiential and neural levels is limited. In our previous study (Chen, Baas, et al, in preparation), no evidence of lasting effects at the subjective level was found across immediate, short-interval (~15 minutes), and long-interval (one week) re-exposure phases. One limitation of the analyses of the subjective ratings was the absence of an appropriate pre-intervention baseline rating. Although the null findings suggest that a lasting effect of affect labeling was not supported, subjective ratings may lack the sensitivity to detect subtle regulatory changes. Importantly, the neural activity collected during the immediate and shortinterval re-exposure phases was not yet examined. Our EEG data do include a baseline measure of neural responses to emotional stimuli prior to any intervention, allowing assessment of regulationrelated modulation relative to pre-intervention brain activity. Moreover, evidence regarding neural activity during re-exposure phases remains scarce. While the neural markers of emotion regulation (LPP, theta, alpha) are well established in reappraisal research, whether they can also reflect a lasting effect of affect labeling still has to be investigated.

Additionally, as mentioned above, different active control conditions have been implemented using either affect matching or feature labeling. Furthermore, prior studies have varied the types of words used for labeling, including emotion-specific words (Lieberman et al., 2011), emotion-category words (e.g., negative, neutral; Liang & Lin, 2023), content-related words (Tabibnia et al., 2008), and general categorical words (e.g., neutral, artificial; Tupak et al., 2014). Importantly, it remains unclear whether the neural markers of regulatory effects are driven by the labeling process itself, the emotional content of the words, or both. To address these issues, the present study included both affect matching and feature labeling as active control conditions.

In this study, we adopted the affect labeling paradigm, in which corresponding word or image pairs were presented across affect labeling and two control conditions. Specifically, affect labeling involved emotion-focused linguistic processing, in which participants selected emotion-specific words to describe their subjective feelings. Feature labeling involved linguistic processing without emotional content, requiring participants to select words related to image characteristics (e.g., "colorful", "zoomed-out") referring to visual features of the images. Affect matching involved emotion-focused processing without a linguistic component, requiring participants to select emotional images that matched their feelings. Together, these conditions allowed us to determine whether the downregulation of negative affect was uniquely driven by affect labeling.

At the neural level, we examined event-related potentials (LPP), and EEG oscillatory activity (theta and alpha power) during the immediate and short-interval re-exposure phases. Pre-intervention neural responses were included as baseline measure to control for individual differences in emotional reactivity. Although the LPP is typically maximal over centro-parietal electrodes, previous studies have shown that its scalp distribution can vary depending on processing stages and task demands. Early LPP activity over posterior regions has been associated with perceptual processing of emotional stimuli, whereas later activity in the frontal site may involve cognitive control (MacNamara et al., 2022; Moser et al., 2014). Moreover, our previous findings in emotion regulation research showed that later LPP modulation could extend to occipital regions, suggesting that emotion-related processing was sustained, driven by the regulation cue (Chen et al., 2025). Similarly, studies using theta power as an index of emotional processing have reported enhanced theta activity for negative relative to neutral stimuli over parietal-temporal-occipital regions (Aftanas et al., 2001 (Aftanas et al., , 2004 (Aftanas et al., , 2005)), whereas emotion regulation has been associated with increased frontal theta activity (Ertl et al., 2013). Thus, because this neural marker is known to vary across scalp regions and processing stages, we further examined whether the predicted effects of Picture Valence and Condition differed across electrode sites and re-exposure phases, allowing us to characterize the spatial and temporal dynamics of affect-labeling effects.

Given the largely overlapping neural mechanisms between affect labeling and reappraisal, and the established LPP, theta, and alpha markers of emotion processing and regulation in reappraisal research, we hypothesized to find a similar pattern of results for affect labeling. First, we expected successful emotion elicitation, reflected in more positive frontal-central-parietal LPP amplitudes, greater frontal-parietal-occipital theta power, and increased bilateral frontal alpha power for negative relative to neutral stimuli. Second, if affect labeling would produce lasting regulatory effects, we predicted a reduced central-parietal LPP and decreased parietal-occipital theta power, an increased regulation-related FR-LPP and frontal theta power, and reduced left-frontal alpha power during re-exposure to previously labeled negative stimuli, compared with both control conditions.

2. Materials and Methods

>Materials and Methods

2.1 Participants

Participants were recruited through advertisements posted on the university campus and local community. Eligible individuals were between 18 and 50 years old, native Dutch speakers, had normal or corrected-to-normal vision, and reported no history of affective disorders or use of psychotropic medication. Ethical approval was granted by the Faculty Ethics Review Board of Social Sciences at Utrecht University (FETC 24-0525). A total of 50 participants were recruited based on the previous study (Liang & Lin, 2023), provided informed consent, and received either course credit or monetary compensation (€32) for completing the test. The EEG experiment, including equipment preparation, took approximately 3 hours in total.

2.2. Materials

The stimulus set consisted of 240 images selected from five standardized databases: International Affective Picture System (IAPS; Lang et al., 2008), the Geneva Affective Picture Database (GAPED; Dan-Glauser & Scherer, 2011), the Nencki Affective Picture System (NAPS; Marchewka et al., 2014), the Open Affective Standardized Image Set (OASIS; Kurdi et al., 2017), and the EmoMadrid (Carretié et al., 2019). As only the IAPS and NAPS datasets use a 1-9 Likert scale, ratings from the GAPED, OASIS, and EmoMadrid datasets were transformed to this scale using established transformation methods (Felix, 2011; Tarka, 2015). Based on transformed norm values, the set included 120 negative images with a mean valence rating of 2.41 (SD= .74) and a mean arousal rating of 6.83 (SD =1.11); and 120 neutral images with a mean valence rating of 5.15 (SD= .34) and a mean arousal rating of 3.57 (SD =1.08). Images were randomly distributed across Picture Valence (negative, neutral) and Conditions (affect labeling, affect matching, feature labeling), resulting in 40 images per condition.

To create the selection options for the affect-labeling and feature-labeling conditions, word pairs were constructed (Supplementary Table 1; Dutch and English words). For the affect labeling condition, each pair contained two emotion-specific words representing distinct emotional states.

For negative stimuli, pairs included combinations such as "fearful" or "scared" with "sad" or "depressed." For neutral stimuli, pairs included "calm" or "peaceful" with "bored" or "indifferent."

For the feature labeling condition, word pairs referred to visual characteristics of the images, such as "colorful" versus "colorless" or "zoomed-out" versus "zoomed-in," and were presented irrespective of picture valence. In both labeling conditions, the two words were displayed below the target image in separate boxes, each superimposed on one of eight distinct noise backgrounds. These backgrounds were derived from the image pairs used in the affect-matching condition and were matched for size and contrast (see Supplementary Materials). In the affect-matching condition, eight images were selected to represent scenes corresponding to the same emotional categories as the word pairs used in the affect labeling. These images were combined into pairs that visually conveyed the specific emotions represented in the labeling conditions.

2.3 Procedure

The study consisted of two stages: a label-or-match intervention stage and an emotion rating stage.

Each stage included five blocks, with 16 trials per condition in each block, resulting in 48 trials per block. Of the 16 trials per condition, 8 involved negative images and 8 involved neutral images.

During the label-or-match intervention stage, each trial was randomly assigned to one of three conditions: affect labeling, affect matching, or feature labeling. Notably, the emotional images used in each condition were assigned independently and randomly to the blocks, and the same image was never repeated within a (re)exposure phase or across conditions.

In each trial, a target image was presented at the top of the screen with two response options displayed below. In the affect labeling condition, participants selected the emotion word that best described their feelings in response to the image. In the affect matching condition, participants selected the emotion-related image that best matched their feelings. In the feature labeling condition (e.g., referring to color or visual characteristics), participants selected the feature-related word that best described the image. Additionally, the target stimulus images were never used as matching images, and vice versa.

The emotion rating stage included three (re)exposure phases, all involving pictures previously seen during the intervention stage: 1) immediate exposure: approximately 1500 ms after initial presentation; 2) short-interval re-exposure, approximately 15 minutes later; 3) long-interval reexposure, one week later. The intervention stage and the emotion rating stage for the immediate and short-interval (re)exposure phases were conducted in the laboratory, with stimuli presented using Presentation software (Neurobehavioral Systems, Inc.). The emotion rating stage for the long-interval re-exposure phase was conducted online via the Gorilla platform. Because no EEG data was collected during long-interval re-exposure, the methods and results from this phase will not be further discussed here. For a more detailed analysis of subjective ratings including this phase, see Chen, Baas et al. (in preparation).

The task (Figure 1) comprised five intervention blocks and five corresponding short-interval reexposure blocks. Each intervention block consisted of the label-or-match task, with each trial immediately followed by an emotion rating (immediate exposure). The task began with the first two intervention blocks, followed by the first short-interval re-exposure block. The remaining three intervention blocks and four short-interval re-exposure blocks were then arranged in an alternating sequence. The first short-interval block included emotion ratings for images presented in the first intervention block, and so forth. The task concluded with the fifth short-interval re-exposure block. Short breaks were provided between blocks. After each block, participants rated their level of concentration on a scale from 0 to 100.

Before EEG recording, participants first completed a series of questionnaires: the trait anxiety subscale of the State-Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI Form Y-2; Spielberger, 1983; Dutch version: Van der Ploeg, 1985), the Five Facet Mindfulness Questionnaire (Baer et al., 2006; Dutch Version: Bohlmeijer et al., 2011), the Affect Labeling Questionnaire (Sahi et al., 2023).

This stage included six conditions, formed by crossing Picture Valence (negative, neutral) with Condition (affect labeling, feature labeling, affect matching), resulting in a total of 240 trials (40 per condition). Each trial of the intervention stage (Figure 2A) began with a fixation cross displayed for a randomly jittered duration between 1500 and 2000 ms. Next, a pair of emotion-specific words (affect labeling), feature-specific words (feature labeling), or a pair of emotional images (affect matching) appeared below the target picture, indicating the task type for that trial. Participants were instructed to select the option that best reflected their emotional response (affect labeling and affect matching) or the perceptual characteristics of the target image (feature labeling). Responses were made using the mouse, with the left and right buttons corresponding to the left and right options displayed below the target image.

After an additional 1000-ms delay, participants rated their emotional response to the image using arousal and valence scales (Figure 2B). 2.4. Electrophysiological recordings

The study was conducted in a sound-attenuated room where participants were seated in front of a 19-inch computer monitor at a distance of approximately 60 cm. During the phase of interest, each trial presented one image, which covered a visual angle of approximately 10.46° × 8.37°. EEG recordings were performed using the Biosemi ActiveTwo system (Biosemi, the Netherlands), with a 64-channel cap. Electrode positions adhered to the 10/20 system. Two external electrodes were placed above and below the left eye to measure vertical eye movements, and two electrodes were positioned on the left and right outer edges of the eyes to measure horizontal eye movements. Two electrodes on the right and left earlobes were used for offline re-referencing. Data were collected at a sampling rate of 2048 Hz. Electrode impedances were reduced as much as possible and were kept below 10 kΩ for the majority of channels.

2.5. EEG data pre-preprocessing

Offline preprocessing of the EEG data was performed using BrainVision Analyzer Software, Version 2.1 (Brain Products). The data were first down-sampled to 256 Hz, and visually inspected for bad channels, which were interpolated when necessary. Channels exhibiting abnormally large amplitude fluctuations or noise patterns inconsistent with neighboring electrodes were identified as bad.

Channel interpolation was required: two channels were interpolated in six participants (P1&P2; P1&PO3; F3&CP5; AF7&FT7; FT7&PO7; PO4&P9), and three channels in another participant (C5, POz, and PO2). The data were then band-pass filtered between 0.01 and 40 Hz, and re-referenced to the averaged earlobe electrodes. Continuous data were segmented into epochs corresponding to the three experimental phases, with time 0 defined as stimulus onset: 1) pre-intervention phase: epochs lasted 7000 ms (-1500 to 5000 ms), including 2000 ms of stimulus presentation, approximately 2000 ms for the response period, and a 1500 ms post-response period, 2) immediate exposure phase: epochs lasted 4000 ms (-1500 to 2500 ms) including a 1000 ms picture presentation, and 3) short-interval re-exposure phase: epochs lasted 5000 ms (-1500 to 3500 ms), with the emotional image presented for 2000 ms.

Epochs from each phase underwent the following preprocessing steps. A first round of automatic artifact rejection was conducted using criteria adapted from Bautista et al. (2022): epochs were marked as artifacts if (a) a voltage step exceeded 50 µV /ms (gradient criterion), (b) the signal amplitude exceeded ± 300 µV within a trial (amplitude criterion), and (c) voltage variation was lower than 0.5 µV within any 100 ms interval (low-activity criterion). In each case, a 200 ms interval before and after the detected artifact was additionally marked as bad. If fewer than 200 clean epochs remained out of the total 240 after this initial artifact rejection step, the amplitude difference threshold was increased to 350 µV, while the other criteria remained unchanged.

To reduce artifacts caused by eye blinks and saccades, especially given the complexity of the visual stimuli that induce eye movements for scanning, a combination of independent component analysis (ICA; Jung et al., 2000) and regression-based correction (Gratton et al.,1983) was applied. First, ICA was used to identify components associated with VEOG and HEOG. Components were automatically rejected based on the largest squared correlation with the respective EOG channel, using a 1% threshold of total required squared correlation, which resulted in one component being removed for the VEOG and one for the HEOG. In cases where a component showed strong associations with both VEOG and HEOG activity, the same component was removed. Subsequently, the Gratton and Coles eye movement correction procedure was used to remove any remaining eye movement artifacts. A second round of AR was then performed using the same criteria as the first, except with a stricter voltage difference threshold of 150 µV within a trial. This step was carried out semi-automatically, including visual inspection and, if necessary, manual adjustment of segments identified as artifacts.

All subsequent data processing was performed in MATLAB using the EEGLAB toolbox and customwritten scripts. For emotional reactivity and regulation ERPs, baseline correction was performed using a -200 to 0 ms window before picture onset. To analyze theta and alpha power, complex Morlet wavelet convolution was applied to extract the time-frequency dynamics, which included frequencies from 1 to 40 Hz in 40 logarithmically spaced steps, with a wavelet width increasing from 3 to 8 cycles over frequencies (Cohen, 2019; Van De Vijver & Cohen, 2019). Power per condition was subsequently converted to a decibel scale using a -500 to -200 ms pre-picture baseline (10log10[power/baseline]). Repeated-measures ANOVAs were used on both subjective ratings and EEG data. The within-subjects factors included Picture Valence (negative, neutral), Condition (affect labeling, feature labeling, affect matching), re-exposure Phase (immediate, short-interval). For EEG analyses (LPP, theta, alpha), EEG values from the passive viewing of target images prior to any intervention were included as an additional pre-intervention level in the Phase factor. The a priori defined time window (400-1000 ms) was examined. Electrode was included as an additional factor for the LPP and theta analyses, whereas Hemisphere was included for the alpha analysis (see Section 2.6.2). In sum, Phase and Electrode were included in the LPP and theta analyses, whereas Hemisphere was included in the alpha analysis, to examine whether the hypothesized effects of Picture Valence and Condition varied across re-exposure phases and spatial dimensions of neural processing. This approach was consistent with previous evidence that emotional processing versus regulation-related EEG markers exhibit distinct temporal and spatial patterns.

2.6 Data analysis

The Greenhouse-Geisser correction was used when Mauchly's test indicated a violation of the sphericity assumption. This GG epsilon (ε) was reported along with the original degrees of freedom.

Given our focus on emotional processing and regulation, only effects involving Picture Valence and/or Condition are reported in the main text. Planned contrasts were applied consistently across all measures to test whether affect labeling reduced negative emotional responses relative to feature labeling and affect matching across (re)exposure phases, and whether neural effects varied by electrode location. For EEG analyses, electrode-related interactions were further examined using planned linear and quadratic polynomial trend contrasts to characterize spatial patterns across electrodes while reducing the number of comparisons relative to electrode-by-electrode analyses ( 7electrodes, which would involve 21 pairwise comparisons). Additional follow-up analyses reported in the Supplementary Materials were corrected for multiple comparisons using Bonferroni correction based on the number of tested comparisons.

2.6.2 EEG data EEG data were recorded from 49 participants who completed the laboratory session (one participant withdrew due to fatigue). One dataset could not be used due to technical issues, and three additional datasets were excluded due to low signal quality and excessive noise: after two rounds of artifact rejection, fewer than 100 usable trials per phase remained on average. This resulted in 45 datasets that underwent the two rounds of artifact rejection and were included in the ANONA analyses.

After completing the two rounds of artifact rejection, the average number of trials per Condition and Picture Valence was calculated for each phase (see Supplementary Table 2). To maximize trial retention and participant inclusion in cross-phase comparisons, participants were excluded from further analyses if, in any phase, more than one condition had fewer than 20 remaining trials. This led to the exclusion of 6 participants, resulting in a final EEG sample of 39 participants (Mage= 21.49, SDage= 2.01, Mtrait-anxiety = 36.97, SDtrait-anxiety =7.61; 26 females), of whom 13 scored 40 or above on the trait anxiety scale. In all retained datasets, each condition included at least 16 trials. After excluding six participants with missing concentration data, the final sample of 38 participants reported a mean concentration level of 71.78 (SD = 11.89).

Seven midline electrodes (Fz, FCz, Cz, CPz, Pz, POz, Oz) were selected based on previous research showing that the LPP tends to peak over the central-parietal area (Hajcak & Foti, 2020; MacNamara et al., 2022), while also allowing examination of potential frontal modulation (Bernat et al., 2011; Doornweerd, 2024; Moser et al., 2014). Considering the duration of target image presentation across the three phases, the LPP was quantified in the 400-1000 ms time window. Accordingly, mean LPP amplitudes were analyzed within this time window at the selected midline electrodes. Theta power (4-8 Hz) was examined in the same time window and at the same midline electrodes.

3. Results

Before conducting the hypothesis-driven analyses of subjective ratings and EEG data, we performed a manipulation check of the label-or-match intervention stage by analyzing response times (RTs) across the three conditions.

RTs preprocessing was conducted using a fixed cutoff of 200-5000 ms to remove outliers. Responses faster than 200 ms were considered "fast guesses," whereas responses longer than 5000 ms were considered indicative of participants not performing the task as intended (Miller, 2023). We used generalized linear mixed models (GLMMs) to analyze RT, which relaxes the assumption that the dependent variable and residuals follow a normal (Gaussian) distribution. In the model, Picture Valence, Condition, and their interaction were included as fixed effects, while subject was included as a random effect. The reference levels were set as neutral for Picture Valence and affect labeling for Condition. The model assumed a Gamma distribution with an identity link function following the previous procedure (Chen et al., 2025).

The analysis revealed significant main effects of Picture Valence, χ²(1) = 6.224, p = .013, and Condition, χ²(2) = 88.554, p < .001. Importantly, no significant interaction was observed between Picture Valence and Condition, p = .202. Participants responded more quickly to negative pictures (estimated mean = 2084 ms, SE = 42.1) than to neutral pictures (estimated mean = 2135 ms, SE = 42.1), z = -2.495, p = .013, d = -.080. Regarding Condition, affect matching (estimated mean = 1981 ms, SE = 42.2) was associated with faster responses than both affect labeling (estimated mean = 2140 ms, SE = 43.7), z = -6.648, p < .001, d = -.340, and feature labeling (estimated mean = 2208 ms, SE = 44.0), z = -8.958, p < .001, d = -.500. Importantly, affect labeling also resulted in faster responses than feature labeling, z = -2.645, p = .025, d = -.140.

3.1 Subjective Ratings

The behavioral results of this experiment were previously reported in detail in Chen, Baas et al. (in preparation). Here, we only report the behavioral findings of the immediate and short-delay reexposure phases to provide context for the EEG analyses, which are the focus of the present paper.

Subjective ratings were analyzed for 47 participants (M = 21.45 years, SD = 2.24; trait anxiety: M = 37.09, SD = 7.77; 34 females), of whom 15 scored ≥ 40 on the trait anxiety scale.

Arousal ratings showed significant main effects of Picture Valence, F(1, 46) = 220.004, p < .001, η² p = .827, and Phase, F(1, 46) = 18.343, p < .001, η² p = .285. However, there was no main effect of Condition (p = .679), and there were no significant interactions (all p-values> .303). Valence ratings also showed significant main effects of Picture Valence, F(1, 46) = 348.154, p < .001, η² p = .883, and Phase, F(1, 46) = 6.204, p = .016, η² p = .119. Again, there was no main effect of Condition (p = .085), and there were no significant interactions (all p-values> .103). In sum, subjective ratings of arousal and valence showed that negative images were consistently rated as more arousing and more unpleasant than neutral images across phases, independent of Condition, confirming successful emotion elicitation. Additionally, arousal ratings decreased from the immediate exposure phase to the short-interval re-exposure phase, while valence ratings increased over the same interval.

Importantly, affect labeling did not reduce negative emotional experience relative to the control conditions at any re-exposure phase. Thus, no evidence for regulatory benefits of affect labeling was observed at the subjective level.

4. Discussion

Our current study focused on EEG measures to examine the effect of affect labeling at the neural level. Affect labeling involved emotion-focused linguistic processing and was compared with two control conditions: feature labeling, which involved linguistic processing without emotional content, and affect matching, which involved emotion-focused processing without a linguistic component.

This dual comparison allowed us to examine whether the down-regulation of negative affect was specific to emotion-focused labeling rather than to the labeling act itself. We hypothesized that affect labeling would produce regulatory effects at the neural level that would persist during both immediate (1500 ms) and short-interval re-exposure phases (~ 15 minutes).re-exposure.

At the experiential level, negative stimuli elicited robust emotional responses across both phases, reflected in higher arousal and unpleasantness than neutral stimuli, confirming successful emotion elicitation. However, subjective ratings showed no evidence that affect labeling resulted in regulatory effects during re-exposure: No differences were observed between affect labeling and the two control conditions.

At the neutral level, we examined event-related potentials (frontal-central-parietal LPP) and EEG oscillatory activity (theta and alpha power) were analyzed to index emotional activity and emotion regulation during the immediate and short-interval (re)exposure phases. Pre-intervention EEG values were included to control for individual differences in baseline responses to emotional stimuli.

Overall, the neural results indicate robust emotional activity across phases, but no evidence for regulation-specific modulation following affect labeling under the present experimental conditions.

Instead, neural responses varied as a function of phase and scalp distribution. In the following sections, we discuss the phase-specific patterns in more detail.Consistent with our hypothesis, emotional activity was reflected in more positive frontal-central-parietal LPP amplitudes for negative than neutral stimuli. This neural pattern aligns with the subjective ratings, suggesting a robust emotion effect (Hajcak & Foti, 2020; MacNamara et al., 2022). During the short-interval re-exposure phase, the spatial distribution of the frontal-central-parietal LPP difference (negative versus neutral) was nearly identical to that observed in the pre-intervention phase. In contrast, during immediate exposure following the intervention, such an LPP difference (negative versus neutral) was observed only at parietal sites with a smaller magnitude.

A similar emotion activity pattern was observed in the time-frequency domain. Specifically, negative relative to neutral stimuli elicited enhanced early frontal-central-parietal theta power during the preintervention phase. Such an enhanced theta power (negative versus neutral) reemerged during the short-interval re-exposure phase, except in the frontal regions. In contrast, this theta difference (negative vs. neutral) was not observed during the immediate exposure phase. As expected, negative stimuli elicited greater parietal theta power, indicating emotional activation (Aftanas et al., 2001 (Aftanas et al., , 2004 (Aftanas et al., , 2005;; Sulpizio et al., 2021). This theta effect is conspicuous because of its early latency, clearly preceding any valence effect on the LPP. This attests to the existence of more rapid route for the detection of emotional quality than that suggested by the LPP. Future analysis should reveal the precise time course: Where between 0 and 400 ms is the effect of emotional quality significant?

This theta effect is also puzzling, as it was not observed in Chen et al. (2025). One possible explanation is that the global context was different in that experiment, where the predictability of negative stimuli after a reappraisal instruction was 100%. This sheer predictability may have activated regulating processes before the actual negative stimulus had occurred, which in turn fully reduced the theta response to the negative quality of the stimulus.

Together, the emotional activity-related LPP and theta results showed a similar spatial and phasedependent pattern: strong emotional activation during pre-intervention, attenuation during immediate exposure, and reemergence during short-interval re-exposure. The central-parietal LPP and theta during pre-intervention may reflect emotional evaluation (Hajcak & Foti, 2020; Sulpizio et al., 2021), whereas their reappearance during short-interval re-exposure may index renewed evaluative processing (Sulpizio et al., 2021; Thiruchselvam et al., 2011). The reduced responses during immediate exposure likely reflect emotion habituation (Paul et al., 2016; Thiruchselvam et al., 2011). The same emotional stimuli had already been presented during the intervention phase for at least 2 seconds and were shown again after a brief interval (~1500 ms), leading to rapid stimulus repetition and reduced neural responsiveness. Such rapid repetition may reduce stimulus novelty, potentially attenuating frontal LPP and theta activity (Hajcak & Olvet, 2008; Yamasaki et al., 2002), while also increasing predictability, further leading to the attenuation of central-parietal theta power (Güntekin & Başar, 2014). The remaining parietal LPP activity during immediate exposure likely reflects sustained motivational relevance of emotional stimuli (Bradley et al., 2008; Hajcak & Nieuwenhuis, 2006).

The emotion-related frontal LPP finding was consistent with our previous studies (Chen et al., 2025), and enhanced frontal theta activity was also observed, which aligns with a previous study with an emotion focus (Ertl et al., 2013). As both effects were strongest during the pre-intervention period, they may reflect the motivational salience of emotional stimuli indexed by sustained attention (LPP, Hajcak et al., 2009; Hajcak & Olvet, 2008) and increased cognitive control recruitment (theta, Ertl et al., 2013).

Contrary to our expectation of a bilateral frontal alpha increase, negative stimuli elicited increased frontal alpha power exclusively in the right hemisphere. Previous research suggested that weaker, not stronger, right frontal alpha activity is associated with avoidance-related motivational tendencies (Sabu et al., 2022). While the finding of stronger right-hemisphere activation for negative stimuli needs to be replicated first, it could indicate that at some levels of processing negative stimuli actually activate approach tendencies, perhaps due to the context in which negative materials are continuously combined with information that was intended to aid regulation.

Following affect matching, both negative and neutral stimuli elicited a more negative frontal-central LPP (FCz) than the two labeling conditions, and this was observed only during the immediate exposure phase. Because this effect did not depend on valence, it cannot be interpreted as a regulation effect. Such immediate frontal ERP intervention differences may reflect continuity versus switching of processing modes across phases (Hsieh & Liu, 2005). Image-based processing was maintained across phases in the matching condition, whereas labeling required a shift from imagebased processing to word-based processing and back. From this perspective, the LPP differences may reflect attentional reorientation (Olofsson et al., 2008; Schupp et al., 2006; Yamasaki et al., 2002) rather than emotion regulation.

Alternatively, this effect might imply a basic difference between labeling and matching in cognitive processing during the intervention that transiently influenced neural responses during immediate exposure. Neuroimaging findings indicate that affect labeling and affect matching engage frontal regions differently during the intervention phase (Torre & Lieberman, 2018; Torrisi et al., 2013), and Liang and Lin (2023) reported that intervention-related ERP differences occurred during intervention.

Our manipulation check showed that participants responded faster in the affect matching condition than in both labeling conditions, which may have been caused by the additional linguistic processing demands in both labeling conditions. In contrast, Liang and Lin (2023) reported no differences in response times between affect labeling and affect matching. Although direct comparisons between studies should be interpreted cautiously, the longer response times observed in our labeling conditions suggest that the intervention stage involved greater processing differences between conditions based on whether labeling or matching was required than based on whether the intervention was aimed at emotional versus non-emotional processing. Furthermore, during the immediate re-exposure stage following the intervention, pictures that had previously been matched elicited lower LPP amplitudes than those from the two labeling conditions. Taken together, the processing differences during the initial intervention phase may have influenced neural activity upon re-exposure, and contribute to the unexpected significant difference between matching and the two labeling conditions during the immediate re-exposure stage.

Although intervention-related cognitive differences have been associated with regulatory effects in Liang and Lin's study, such effects were not observed in the present study. This discrepancy suggests that differences between conditions alone may not be sufficient to produce sustained regulatory effects; instead, other design factors, such as the timing of target stimulus presentation, may be critical. In Liang and Lin (2023), labels were presented simultaneously with the target image, whereas in our study, the target image was presented before the labels were added. This temporal difference determines whether affect labeling operates as an antecedent-focused strategy or as a responsefocused strategy (Gross, 2015; Gyurak et al., 2011; Levy-Gigi & Shamay-Tsoory, 2022). In line with the Dual Mechanisms of Control framework, antecedent-focused strategies resemble proactive control by engaging regulatory processes in advance, whereas response-focused strategies are more akin to reactive control, as regulation occurs after emotional activation has begun (Martins-Klein et al., 2020). Consistent with this distinction, differences in LPP modulation have been observed between proactive and reactive designs in the reappraisal literature, a point we also emphasized in our previous study of reappraisal (Chen et al., 2025; MacNamara et al., 2022). In contrast, EEG evidence in the affect labeling literature remains limited, and the distinction between proactive and reactive regulation is less clearly defined. Therefore, such a conclusion may be considered as a possible interpretation but cannot be confirmed yet.

Added to this, these assumption was based on comparing with the LPP we find and Liang and lin (2023) during the immediate phase, differences in scalp topography across studies may partly reflect methodological differences in EEG preprocessing. Although the preprocessing pipeline used in the present study was broadly comparable to that of Liang and Lin (2023), there were several differences, including the reference scheme, filtering parameters, and ocular artifact correction procedures. Such methodological differences, together with variations in stimulus characteristics and participant samples, may contribute to differences in the spatial distribution of scalp activity.

Therefore, caution is warranted when directly comparing scalp topographies across studies.

Another important difference may be the type of emotional stimuli. The images used in our study and in Liang and Lin's study were highly comparable in normative valence ratings, whereas the normative arousal ratings of our selected images were somewhat higher. Liang and Lin observed an immediate labeling effect on valence but not on arousal. We did not find a labeling effect in either valence or arousal. The current study has two key limitations. First, neural activity during the intervention phase was not examined. Neural activity during this phase likely reflects overlapping processes, including emotional evaluation, decision-making, motor preparation, and cognitive control, making it difficult to disentangle regulation-related activity from these other task-related components. Although the immediate frontal intervention-related ERP difference may be an effect of cognitive differences during the intervention, this interpretation should be made with caution.

Importantly, we focused on regulatory effects during re-exposure, and neural activity was therefore examined only during these phases. Future studies could explore how neural activity during the intervention relates to the subsequent re-exposure effect.

Second, the intervention used a two-alternative forced-choice task, which may have limited participants' ability to fully express their emotional experiences. In contrast, some studies reporting lasting effects allowed self-generated emotion descriptions. For example, individuals with spider fear verbally described their feelings while viewing a real spider (Kircanski et al., 2012), and Twitter users who post phrases such as "I feel…" actively formulate their emotions (Fan et al., 2018). Future studies could use self-generated labels to better capture individual emotional experiences.

In summary, in our study, emotional responses at the neural level were robust across phases.

Intervention-related ERP differences were only observed during the immediate exposure phase and did not depend on valence. Therefore, the observed null effects were consistent and reliable, suggesting that affect labeling did not produce detectable regulatory effects within the specific design and task demands examined here. Importantly, our study implemented two active control conditions, affect matching and feature labeling. This dual-control design allowed us to more closely match attention, motor, and task demands than previous studies that relied on passive viewing as a control condition (Kircanski et al., 2012; Lieberman et al., 2011; Niles et al., 2015). Accordingly, the absence of a labeling effect in the immediate phase cannot easily be attributed to mismatched task demands.

Nevertheless, these null findings should be interpreted cautiously. Our manipulation check indicated differences in processing demands across conditions during the intervention stage, with faster responses in the affect matching condition than in the two labeling conditions. Although the reexposure phase was designed to minimize the impact of these differences, their influence on subsequent processing cannot be ruled out. Thus, the absence of re-exposure effects may reflect characteristics of the present experimental design rather than the absence of affect labeling-related regulatory mechanisms.

Although our findings differ from many previous studies reporting regulatory benefits of affect labeling, our results specifically concern its sustained effects during re-exposure. This therefore does not contradict the immediate regulatory effects during labeling itself. Yet, our findings suggest caution is required when applying affect labeling as a standalone therapeutic method. Some studies have reported benefits of affect labeling when combined with other approaches such as exposure therapy (Kircanski et al., 2012; Niles et al., 2016) or cognitive reappraisal (Yoshimura et al., 2023), while others have reported null effects (Nook et al., 2021), including ours. Future research should directly compare conditions in which affect labeling is effective with those in which it is not. Such work may help identify the factors that influence its effectiveness and clarify the conditions under which this implicit emotion regulation pathway is activated.
