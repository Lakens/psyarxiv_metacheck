Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). Abstract

Social Anxiety Disorder (SAD) presents a significant barrier to social and work functioning, and while exposure therapy has been a cornerstone of treatment, its effectiveness varies across individuals. This paper discusses how 3,4-Methylenedioxymethamphetamine (MDMA) may be used to enhance the effectiveness of an exposure-based intervention in SAD treatment. MDMA, known for its capacity to enhance feelings of social safeness, may enhance exposure therapy through a variety of mechanisms such as reducing safety behaviors, increasing contact with cues for core SAD fears, increasing expectancy violations, and increasing willingness. We explore the neurobiological and psychological underpinnings relevant to this augmentation strategy, particularly MDMA's modulation of serotonin and dopamine pathways and its tendency to elicit self-transcendent positive emotions such as love, compassion, and gratitude. We also discuss recent findings suggesting that MDMA may open a window of neuroplasticity after administration during which effective exposure practice may have larger effects. To provide an illustration of these concepts, we describe how these ideas are being used to guide in vivo and imaginal exposure interventions as part of our clinical trial of MDMA-AT for social anxiety disorder. Ethical considerations, potential risks, and a framework for future empirical studies to test this theory are outlined. This approach could potentially offer a more profound and enduring therapeutic impact for those struggling with SAD, suggesting a promising area for further research.

MDMA AS A MEANS OF AUGMENTING EXPOSURE THERAPY

Social Anxiety Disorder (SAD) represents a significant mental health challenge, marked by an overwhelming fear of social interactions and scrutiny by others. This condition affects a considerable portion of the global population, with estimates suggesting a prevalence of around 4% (WHO World Mental Health Survey Collaborators et al., 2017). The impact of SAD extends beyond immediate social discomfort, often leading to reduced economic opportunities (Patel et al., 2002) and heightened vulnerability to other mental health issues, including depression (Adams et al., 2016) and problematic alcohol use (Morris et al., 2005). Current therapeutic approaches, particularly Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) with its emphasis on exposure techniques, have shown efficacy in treating SAD (Mayo-Wilson et al., 2014). However, there is still room for improvement as evidenced by a substantial number of patients who either discontinue therapy prematurely or fail to achieve full remission, especially among those with more severe symptoms (Eskildsen et al., 2010; Hans & Hiller, 2013; Springer et al., 2018).

In response to these challenges, researchers are exploring novel therapeutic avenues for SAD treatment. One such approach gaining attention is the use of MDMA (3,4methylenedioxymethamphetamine) as an adjunct to psychotherapy. MDMA's unique pharmacological profile, which includes modulation of social behavior-related neurotransmitters and hormones, presents intriguing possibilities for addressing the core difficulties associated with SAD, such as social anhedonia, increased social threat perception, and decreased positive affect and affective empathy (Luoma & Lear, 2021; Lyubomirsky, 2022). Preliminary research into MDMA-Assisted Therapy (MDMA-AT) for reducing social anxiety in autistic adults yielded encouraging results (Danforth et al., 2018). Building on these initial findings, ongoing clinical trials are further investigating the potential of MDMA-AT in treating SAD (Lear et al., 2023). Exposure therapy involves the repeated presentation of a stimulus that has been previously associated with other negative stimuli and now elicits avoidance and escape behaviors. In the case of SAD, the core fear is that certain "flawed" aspects of the person's self they believe to be socially undesirable will lead to negative evaluation, rejection, and ostracism if they are revealed (Elliott & Shahar, 2019; Moscovitch, 2009; Moscovitch & Huppert, 2024).

Such fears have presumably developed because of repeated experiences with negative evaluation, rejection, neglect, and ostracism in which the person learns these aspects of self are not valued by others and internalize this belief (Norton & Abbott, 2017). As a result, the person learns to fear and avoid revealing these vulnerable aspects of self (e.g., aspects of character, physical appearance, signs of anxiety) through overt situational avoidance and more subtly through safety behaviors. For effective exposure in the context of SAD, the stimulus that needs to be targeted during exposure is the authentic revealing of aspects of self that are experienced as flawed, risking negative social outcomes. This often involves revealing thoughts, feelings, memories, and urges that are ordinarily concealed behind safety behaviors or avoided altogether through situational avoidance. In other words, a person with SAD behaves in an inauthentic manner in relevant situations -they are showing one thing to the world but experiencing something else inside. Another way to say this is that one core feature of effective exposure therapy for SAD is helping the person be authentic about aspects of self where they are often not authentic.

In order for learning to occur during exposure, it is not sufficient to simply present the feared stimulus repeatedly. That is, effective exposure is not simply about helping the person MDMA AS A MEANS OF AUGMENTING EXPOSURE THERAPY with SAD be authentic in an indiscriminate or haphazard manner. Instead, according to several popular models of exposure, such as inhibitory learning theory (Sewart & Craske, 2020) and memory reconsolidation models (Smith et al., 2017), an expectancy violation must also occur for learning to take place. An expectancy violation occurs when there is a mismatch between what a person expects to occur and what actually occurs in a given context. Indicators of safeness in the context of a fear cue are thought to result in powerful expectancy violations in the case of fear learning. In theory, the more salient and impactful these indicators of safeness are, the more learning will occur during exposure, resulting in more positive outcomes.

Returning to SAD, when the expected outcome of negative evaluation, rejection, and ostracism is perceived to have occurred, this tends to be accompanied by feelings of shame, humiliation, or embarrassment. Thus, the occurrence of these emotions is a good marker of the expectancy having been met. In contrast, if a person perceived themselves as having not been negatively evaluated or rejected, there might be a relative lack of embarrassed or ashamed emotions. Low or absent embarrassment or shame might result in a relatively weak expectancy violation as they may not be particularly salient. In contrast, if events result in strong positive emotions, such as safeness, warmth, relaxation, love, compassion, or reciprocal feelings of fondness, these might lead to a stronger expectancy violation. These might occur from interpersonal consequences that are akin to the opposite of what is feared, such as feeling valued, prized, accepted, or even loved. A central focus of this paper is that MDMA may be useful in fostering more effective exposure largely because it can foster this second, more salient and powerful type of expectancy violation.

Reductions in Avoidance-Related Dropout

A first way that MDMA may enhance outcomes from exposure therapy is that it may reduce avoidance-related dropout, allowing a higher proportion of clients to benefit. Research shows that around 15% of clients drop out from CBT for SAD, which includes a significant exposure therapy component (Hans & Hiller, 2013), and others are hesitant to engage in the therapy in the first place because of avoidance. In experimental contexts, MDMA appears to reduce negative responses to social stimuli including simulated rejection in the Cyberball task (Frye et al., 2014) and on neurophysiological measures (Bershad et al., 2016). Clinicians have noted that MDMA reduced rejection sensitivity, increases disclosure in therapy, and enables people to receive both praise and criticism with more acceptance (Passie, 2018). Similarly, MDMA enhances emotional responses to positive social stimuli (Hysek et al., 2014; Jungaberle et al., 2018), which may increase the reinforcing qualities of exposure situations. This may reduce dropout through two avenues. First, during the preparation phase before the person has ever done an exposure, the therapist can discuss how MDMA is likely to make social interactions temporarily easier during exposure practices, thereby making exposure appear more palatable and preventing dropout. Second, attenuated responses to negative social stimuli and increased positive responding to social stimuli may make exposure less unpleasant and more rewarding, thereby increasing engagement in exposure tasks and sustaining motivation to keep going. Thus, if MDMA as an adjunct to exposure can reduce dropout and increase acceptability of treatment, more people may be able to benefit from exposure.

Neurobiological Changes

Neurobiological changes due to MDMA may also increase the potential for new learning during exposure, possibly through altered action of the medial pre-frontal cortex (Carhart-Harris et al., 2014) or changes in Brain Derived Neurotropic Factor that may enhance memory reconsolidation of fear memories, as has been observed in rodents (Hake et al., 2019; Young et al., 2015). In addition, studies on mice suggest that MDMA's prosocial effects may extend beyond acute dosing, potentially enhancing the impact of exposure after the MDMA sessions themselves. A series of experiments (Nardou et al., 2019 (Nardou et al., , 2023) ) showed that MDMA increased sensitivity to social reinforcement among mice who received MDMA when in the presence of another mouse and that this effect lasted at least 2 weeks after administration. This suggests that MDMA may alter the balance of social reward versus social threat-related responding for some unknown period of time after the acute effects of the drug have worn off. Consequently, MDMA-AT clients may find themselves more motivated to connect with others and experience greater rates of social reward for a period after administration. This could have important implications for clients struggling with SAD, as social anhedonia is a well-documented feature of the disorder (Amir et al., 2012; Richey et al., 2019). A lasting period of heightened social reward sensitivity following MDMA sessions could create a temporarily altered psychological context where clients with SAD are more sensitive to, and perceptive of, socially reinforcing or positive information. By making social interactions more rewarding and less punishing than the client predicted, there could be increased opportunities for the client to experience expectancy violations during exposures conducted in non-drug integration sessions and between-session MDMA AS A MEANS OF AUGMENTING EXPOSURE THERAPY exposure homeworks. With repeated expectancy violations during this window of enhanced social reward sensitivity, the client may be more likely to experience increased motivation to engage in future social interactions, resulting in more durable shifts in social motivation and cognitions.

Enhancing Contact with Fear Stimuli

MDMA may also enhance exposure by increasing the likelihood, duration, and range of activation of feared stimuli central to the maintenance of SAD. Clinical models of SAD conceptualize the core feared stimulus in SAD as the revealing of (actual or potential) unacceptable flaws or shameful aspects of the self to others, which will result in the feared outcome: rejection associated with loss of social status or connection (Elliott & Shahar, 2019; Moscovitch, 2009; Moscovitch & Huppert, 2024). The content of each person's perceived deficits, and the contexts in which they are elicited, is unique and informed by their learning history interacting with family, peer, and sociocultural norms and expectations (Hope et al., 2019; Wong & Rapee, 2016). Thus, effective exposure to the feared stimulus in the context of SAD will vary from person-to-person, but what is central is that the person a) sees themselves as having revealed the self-attribute they perceive to be flawed and b) experiences an outcome different than they expected. One reason why exposure fails in SAD treatment is that people engage in exposure tasks, such as going to a party or giving a speech, while simultaneously engaging in safety behaviors that conceal the relevant self-attribute, e.g., by scripting what they will say to conceal perceived social incompetence. Enhancing Expectancy Violations

Reductions in fear responding are strengthened when exposures are designed to maximally contrast the person's experiences with their expectancies about the frequency or intensity of their feared outcome in the presence of the feared stimulus (Craske et al., 2014).

MDMA may enhance expectancy violations during in vivo exposure by increasing the chance that when the person authentically reveals shameful aspects of themselves they normally conceal, they will experience acceptance and compassion instead anticipated rejection or judgment. MDMA may accomplish this via several pathways. First, MDMA appears to enhance the emotional bond of the therapeutic relationship (Luoma et al., 2021) via inducing a state where people feel exceptionally safe and less sensitive to rejection. It's common for MDMA-AT clients to report feeling deeply calm and safe with their therapists, which may create a stark contrast to the fear and anxiety typically experienced in social situations by those with SAD.

Additionally, MDMA promotes friendly, playful, and loving feelings towards others, including therapists (Bedi et al., 2010) and may promote prosocial behavior (Kamilar-Britt & Bedi, 2015).

Increased warmth and positive expression from the client will likely elicit reciprocal affiliative responses from the therapist, creating a positive feedback loop of warmth and acceptance (Markey et al., 2003; Sadler et al., 2009). The heightened emotional empathy induced by MDMA (Bedi et al., 2009; Hysek et al., 2014; Jungaberle et al., 2018; Kuypers et al., 2017; Wardle & De Wit, 2014; Wardle et al., 2014) should also make the client more attuned to and affected by the therapist's expressions of compassion and acceptance. This intensified experience of a warm, accepting relationship stands in sharp contrast to the client's typical fearful expectations in social situations, potentially creating a more powerful expectancy violation when relevant material about the self is revealed. MDMA may also enhance expectancy mismatch by producing strong positive emotions, particularly self-transcendent emotions such as compassion, awe, love, and gratitude, and appreciation (Passie, 2018; Studerus et al., 2010). Self-transcendent emotions are key in the formation of social bonds and are associated with an increased sense of social connectedness (Shiota et al., 2007) and prosocial behavior (Goetz et al., 2010; Piff et al., 2015). The experience of these emotions stands in contrast to the shame, fear, and isolation typically associated with social situations for those with SAD. When clients experience these profound positive emotions in the context of revealing vulnerable aspects of themselves, it creates a powerful mismatch between their fearful expectations and their actual experience, potentially leading to more effective memory reconsolidation and learning.

MDMA AS A MEANS OF AUGMENTING EXPOSURE THERAPY

This paper reviewed several pathways through which MDMA might potentiate exposurebased interventions in the context of SAD (see Figure 1). In particular, we outlined how MDMA might reduce avoidance-related dropout, induce neurobiological changes that could enhance the effects of exposure, increase contact with SAD-relevant fear cues, and enhance expectancy mismatch. These hypotheses are yet to be tested, but preliminary data is being collected in our clinical trial on the treatment of SAD with MDMA-AT that we hope will begin to examine the various variables hypothesized in this paper (Lear et al., 2023).

In understanding how MDMA may be used to augment exposure, it's important to note that traditional CBT-based exposure protocols may need to be tailored to make use of MDMA's potential prosocial effects. For example, in order to take advantage of the possible heightened response to social reinforcement, exposure-based homework assignments in our study are framed as social activation and focus more on increasing the person's chance of experiencing social reinforcement in previously avoided situations, and less on targeting high fear situations from an exposure hierarchy. In addition, instead of traditional imaginal exposure exercises, we developed a task called the Empathic Reimagining Task (Luoma et al., 2024) that is informed by the principles of imagery rescripting and explicitly focuses on bringing self-transcendent emotions such as compassion into the imaginal exposure context. This is to take advantage of temporarily heightened experience of self-transcendent emotions associated with MDMA and utilize it directly in exposure exercises. We also conducted exposure exercises during MDMA sessions after at least 3 hours had elapsed in order to give the person time to experience states of empathy, safeness, and self-transcendence that could potentiate subsequent exposure. prerequisites for powerful exposure. Furthermore, therapists were guided to use self-disclosure and (if consented to) supportive touch to increase felt safeness and enhance expectancy violations following MDMA AS A MEANS OF AUGMENTING EXPOSURE THERAPY contact with both spontaneously arising and structured contact with fear cues. In the presence of fear cues, the instruction to offer social support cues was aimed to facilitate participants' mindful and willing engagement with fear cues as suggested by recent extensions of Inhibitory Learning Theory (Hornstein et al., 2022). In addition, MDMA can also increase forgetting (Pantoni et al., 2022), so it may also be important to identify ways to enhance remembering from MDMA sessions such as by explicitly practicing exposures initially begun in MDMA sessions after session or including practice such as written exposure (Sloan et al., 2023) or journaling to enhance remembering. For more detail on how we tailored exposure interventions to the MDMA context, we have published a case report from our clinical trial (Luoma et al., in press).

When considering adding MDMA to exposure therapy protocols it is also important to keep in mind treatment efficiency, safety considerations, and durability of effects. Participants in our clinical trial received approximately 30 hours of therapy, which is more than a typical course of treatment of CBT for SAD. In addition, the use of MDMA includes additional medical risks that require additional screening for safety purposes, such as for cardiovascular or other health issues. Additionally, data on durability of effects is essential and therefore outcome studies should include long-term follow-ups. It's also important to note that assessing the efficacy of MDMA-AT may need more complicated trial designs due to difficulty blinding participants to subjective effects of MDMA (Wen et al., 2024). Additionally, future studies should examine ways to reduce the cost of MDMA-AT such as through less treatment contact or the use of adjunctive digital tools. In sum, more data is needed to consider whether MDMA-AT for SAD results in increased efficacy that would justify increased cost, medical risk, and other adverse effects; otherwise MDMA-AT might be best considered as a secondary line of treatment in the case of non-response to CBT.

Additional research on MDMA-AT for SAD is needed to not only better understand efficacy, but to better understand underlying processes of change involved in this novel form of treatment. For example, data suggesting a window of increased sensitivity to social reinforcement in rodents (Nardou et al., 2019 (Nardou et al., , 2023) ) post MDMA-administration should lead to similar studies in humans to see if MDMA alters social motivation in humans, and if so, for how long. In our clinical trial, we have included measures of a number self-report measures intended to assess change in these processes (Lear et al., 2023). For example, we measure changes in social motivation via session-by-session assessments of social pleasure and also via a daily survey assessments of social approach motivation in the week before and after each MDMA session. We also included measures of safety behaviors in the daily diary and acceptance of shame and embarrassment at pre-, post-, and follow-up assessments. Having a greater understanding of how MDMA-AT might be effective in treating SAD can potentially increase efficiency by highlighting key treatment targets. Besides the proposed pathways for therapeutic change purported in this paper, there may be other mechanisms that are worthy of consideration and research.

We want to emphasize that it is not just the MDMA experience alone that is therapeutic, but it is what therapists do before, during, and after MDMA experiences that have the greatest impact on therapeutic outcomes. This paper represents an effort to integrate evidence-based theory and practice related to SAD with the therapeutic potential of MDMA. Beyond SAD specifically, this integration offers a blueprint for how psychedelic-assisted therapies might enhance established therapeutic modalities for treatment-resistant conditions more broadly. The paradigm we propose-using MDMA to create powerful expectancy violations, enhance neuroplasticity, reduce avoidance, and deepen therapeutic engagement-has the potential to MDMA AS A MEANS OF AUGMENTING EXPOSURE THERAPY reshape how exposure therapy is conducted with not just SAD, but with other fear and shamerelated conditions that have historically shown limited response to conventional treatments. As research in this field progresses, MDMA-AT and similar approaches could help catalyze a significant evolution in mental healthcare, offering new pathways to recovery and enhanced well-being for those who have found little relief through existing options. By continuing to rigorously investigate these approaches, we may discover transformative treatment models that bridge psychopharmacology and psychotherapy in ways that address not just symptoms, but the underlying psychological, emotional, and neurobiological patterns that maintain psychological suffering.
