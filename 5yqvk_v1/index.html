Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Marginal Significance works

The marginal module searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The list of terms is a subset of those listed in a blog post by Matthew Hankins. The module returns all sentences that match terms describing ‘marginally significant’ results.

Some of the terms identified might not be problematic in some contexts, and there are ways to describe ‘marginal significance’ that are not detected by the module.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. Some differences come from the ground truth: it lacks p-values with a correction name (“p Bonf = .061”), and counts partial eta squared written with its subscript after it (“η 2 p = .05”), “p = .05”, and some table notes and figure legends (“† p < .10, *p < .05”), which this module leaves out, as non-significant p-values. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. Abstract

The importance of cognitive variability in development has been discussed for over a century, but empirical work has lagged behind. However, recent technological and methodological advances have led to a substantial increase in studies focused on cognitive variability in developmental populations. Here we review recent empirical work on cognitive variability during development, examining the cognitive and neural mechanisms underlying individual differences and developmental changes in cognitive variability. We synthesize empirical evidence to show that cognitive variability: (1) decreases with expertise and age during development; (2) carries unique information about cognition above and beyond mean performance; (3) is observed across a wide range of cognitive domains, with more domain-specific sources of variation than those underlying mean performance; (4) is a developmentally relevant correlate of neurodevelopmental disorders; (5) can be either adaptive or maladaptive depending on the context; and (6) can be modified through behavioral and pharmacological interventions.

We discuss variations in methods and tasks to quantify cognitive variability, and provide a hands-on tutorial for applied researchers to estimate different measures of (cognitive) variability for trial level data to guide current and future work on cognitive variability.

Introduction

A child's cognitive performance waxes and wanes across seconds, hours, and days, performing the same task faster or slower, performing better one moment, worse another. Such cognitive variability, or cognitive fluctuation, reflects momentary, reversible, withinperson inconsistencies in cognitive performance across various tasks and timescales (MacDonald et al., 2006; Nesselroade, 1991; Siegler, 2007). Nesselroade (1991) made this distinction explicit using a carpet metaphor, to conceptualize the distinction between the 'warp' of slower developmental growth, and the 'woof' of short-term fluctuations around that change trajectory. Rather than focusing only the mean, or sum score, across items or subtests, cognitive variability instead focuses on the degree of dispersion around a personspecific mean. This property of cognitive performance is, at least in principle, independent of the mean -two children may have the same average performance but greatly different cognitive variabilities, or very similar degrees of variability yet very different overall mean performance (Figure 1). The relative poverty of empirical work on cognitive variability may be in part due to cognitive ability being perceived as the example of a trait, with relatively stable lifespan rank-ordering (Deary, 2014), remarkably predictive of a range of outcomes (Gottfredson & Deary, 2004), likely contributing to the implicit interpretation that cognitive abilities differ between, but not within individuals to be dealt with by, for instance, averaging. However, early scholars highlighted the importance of variability (Dodge, 1924; Fiske & Rice, 1955; Head, 1920; Hull, 1943; Jensen, 1982; Nesselroade, 1991; Siegler, 1989), offering a range of theoretical explanations for cognitive variability. Other explanations have included 'oscillating factors' that steer efficiency and accuracy in cognitive performance (Dodge, 1924), to 'behavioral oscillations' (i.e., variability, inconsistency, and unpredictability) being 'chief molar distinctions between organisms and inorganic machines' (Hull, 1943, p. 304). Demonstrating early clinical relevance, Head (1920) described how greater differences in performance across time are characteristic of brain lesion patients.

Current perspectives on cognitive variability

Until recently, empirical work on cognitive variability has been limited due to complex logistics to facilitate data collection at scale, and a dearth of appropriate quantitative techniques to quantify variability. Leveraging tools such as tablets, recent studies have demonstrated that developmental studies of cognitive variability are feasible (Bignardi et al., 2021; Coolen et al., 2024; Judd et al., 2024), and capture fundamental features of development (Lövdén et al., 2020; Schurr et al., 2024; Siegler, 1994; Waschke et al., 2021), predicting outcomes of interest above and beyond mean performance (Bielak et al., 2010; Ghisletta et al., 2013; Schmiedek et al., 2007). Focusing on development, lower variability predicted better mathematics performance in kindergarten and first grade beyond accuracy (Peters et al., 2026) and more mathematics improvement from age 6 to age 9 (Judd et al., 2021; β = -0.30).

Cognitive variability is of considerable importance across the lifespan, but especially so in early development, a period of rapid cognitive changes (Galeano Weber et al., 2018; Siegler, 1994 Siegler, , 2007)), thus providing a unique window to understand the processes that affect, and are affected by, variability. Moreover, unlike greater cognitive variability in later life -which is generally associated with, for instance, more rapid cognitive decline -cognitive variability may in fact be adaptive, for instance, (second) language acquisition (Lowie & Verspoor, 2019).

Below, we review a body of recent empirical work to understand how cognitive variability develops, examining cognitive variability across multiple cognitive domains, including attention, processing speed, flexibility, working memory, mathematics, and executive functioning, primarily focusing on studies reporting empirical findings in individuals from birth to 24 years (the end of adolescence).

Longitudinal evidence: within-person change in cognitive variability

In the domain of inhibitory control, Zhang and Wang (2024) followed 169 primary school children aged 7-10 (M = 7.15, SD = 0.33) for 3 measurement occasions across three years of testing. Using a Go/No-Go task to measure attentional control, they found that response-time variability (iSD of correct-GO trials), decreased at each successive timepoint within the same child ( η² = 0.19), significantly so between waves 1 and 2 and 2 and 3. Boen et al. (2021) studied individuals from late childhood to young adulthood (age 8.5-26.7, mean follow-up interval = 4.4 years) using a cross-sectional (N = 166) and longitudinal design (N = 121). Examining developmental changes in the Attention Network Test (ANT), which captures distinct aspects of attention, namely orienting, alerting and executive control, response-time variability was estimated through linear mixed effects modeling, finding distinct developmental patterns. Variability in orienting performance increased with age, but variability in alerting performance decreased with age and executive-control variability showed no age-related changes. These results suggest that the age-effect of cognitive variability, at least within the attentional domain, depends on the attentional mechanisms being studied. Thomson et al. (2020) estimated longitudinal trajectories of response-time variability using GAMMs in 120 individuals (9-14) with ADHD and 123 neurotypical controls.

Across three timepoints, sustained attention was measured through the Sustained Attention to Response Task (SART) and variability was quantified by the iSD and ex-Gaussian modeling.

They observed greater variability in the ADHD group than neurotypical controls, and reliable decreases in variability within both groups. In Montez et al. (2017) 126 individuals aged 8-33 were repeatedly measured over a 10-year period on a memory-guided saccade task during fMRI. Behavioral variability was quantified through the SD of response times and saccadic error. Response-time variability decreased sharply until age 15 with a flatter decrease thereafter, stabilizing around age 30. Interestingly, trajectories of brain-state variability covaried with trajectories of response-time variability (r = -0.48), suggesting that developmental reduction in variability may be explained, at least in part, by a concurrent increase in stability of brain activity. Suades-González et al. (2017) studied 39 schools with 2835 children aged 7-11 spanning three grades: Grade 2: (N = 1064, M = 7.7, SD = 0.33), Grade 3: N = 1015, M = 8.7, SD = 0.37), Grade 4: (N = 756, M = 9.7, SD = 0.36). Children performed the ANT with variability being quantified at the trial and task-block level.

Response-time variability decreased with age, and children with (teacher-scored) ADHD had higher variability across task-blocks compared to controls. Inattentiveness, but not hyperactivity, was not associated with an age-related decrease suggesting that the speed of variability changing is tied to ADHD symptomatology.

In summary, and largely in line with cross-sectional findings, most longitudinal evidence suggests a decrease in cognitive variability during development, but the strength and direction of these changes are domain specific, and partially contingent on the presence and severity of neurodevelopmental symptomatology (most notably, inattentiveness).

However, more longitudinal research is needed to determine when variability declines, for which cognitive domains, and whether such changes are best characterized as general developmental reductions or as more differentiated, domain-specific trajectories.

Two distinct drivers of cognitive variability: learning and development

Although longitudinal designs are crucial to better understand developmental change, a key challenge in repeated measurements of any skill is that multiple developmental processes are unfolding in parallel, most importantly (short term) learning and (longer term) development.

Variability as a consequence of learning

Within the slower process of development, there are bursts of learning happening at different levels of temporal resolution. This is a challenge, as learning (for instance, during a novel task) can cause substantial decreases in variability that we can see over seconds or minutes, as well as slightly slower performance gains across sessions (Campbell et al., 2025; Cochrane et al., 2023; Lövdén et al., 2020). On the other hand, development has a slower effect on variability that becomes visible over the course of months or years (Williams et al., 2005).

As individuals gain expertise and acquire skills through repeated task experience, cognitive variability may decrease as behavior shifts from exploring different strategies to exploiting (perfecting) those that have proved effective (Lövdén et al., 2020; Sternad, 2018), which can be explained by the Exploration-Selection-Refinement model (Lindenberger & Lövdén, 2019). Early in learning, individuals explore different strategies, which can contribute to greater variability in performance (Siegler, 2007). With further practice, strategy selection and execution become more refined, potentially leading to improved and less variable performance (Lövdén et al., 2020).

A possible neurochemical basis specific to these learning-related changes is described by Shine (2019), describing how both noradrenergic and cholinergic systems modulate variation in neural activity and the selection of activity patterns during learning.

These mechanisms support the initial exploration of potential solutions and, with continued learning, the stabilization of effective patterns and faster and less variable behavior.

However, variability may increase again when environmental uncertainty rises or previously effective strategies no longer suffice, making renewed exploration potentially adaptive. We emphasize the relative dearth of empirical and theoretical work on this topic, likely leaving other neurobiological pathways underexplored.

Variability as a consequence of development

An interesting framework to think about developmental changes in cognitive variability is the dynamical systems perspective, where variability is both a trait-like property of a system (or individual) but also a state-like property of a system destabilising as it approaches -or transitions into -a new stable state (Siegler, 1994 (Siegler, , 1995;; Verspoor & Van Dijk, 2012). Whereas Lövden et al. (2020) focused on within-stage learning, the dynamical systems perspective adds between-stage learning as an additional component crucial in variability. Concretely, right before children reach a new level in their learning they exhibit a surge of variable performance before settling into a new level of proficiency. In the cognitive domain, this has been exhibited in studies of language acquisition (Lowie & Verspoor, 2019) and strategy shifting (see below).

Summarizing, these findings suggest that changes in cognitive variability due to short-term learning and long-term developmental changes align in their general direction (decreasing from early childhood to late adolescence) but do so on two different timescales.

As children age, certain skills become automated, strategies become more refined through processes like memorization and automatization, and the need for environmental sampling to reduce uncertainty. Strategy use and developmental changes in cognitive variability

Thus far, we have focused on childhood and adolescence, as this is a key developmental period characterized by relatively rapid changes, and a period where cognitive skills can be relatively well measured. However, focusing even earlier in development may provide additional insight into the childhood developmental changes. In infancy, it is notoriously difficult to construct cognitive tasks for babies, so empirical work is dominated by observing behaviour thought to be precursors of cognitive processes. Moreover, infancy is a period where almost every skill is (being) newly acquired, providing an especially rich period to study how infants adapt to unfamiliar challenges (e.g. descending a ramp, Libertus & Hauf, 2017), often through strategy exploration. Across repeated attempts at a task, children may explore multiple strategies rather than consistently applying a single approach (Siegler, 1994; van Geert & van Dijk, 2002), and demonstrate differences in how consistently a selected strategy is executed. Adolph (1997) examined infants from their first week of crawling until several weeks after they began walking, studying longitudinal changes in infants' responses to slopes. Over weeks of crawling, infants' judgments became more accurate and their exploration became more efficient, alongside differentiation and selection of locomotor responses. Importantly, expertise gained through crawling did not transfer automatically to walking. In other words, infants had to relearn how to descend slopes from an upright position.

Moving from motor behaviour to more clear cognitive precursors, Chen and Siegler (2000) studied 86 infants aged 18-35 months, who were asked to select the correct tool to be able to grab their preferred toy. Strategies varied widely, including reaching with their hands, requesting help from an adult, or staring and not taking any action. Results indicated that trial-to-trial strategy use varied extensively, but as the experiment progressed, infants became more efficient in selecting more optimal strategies. Variability in approaches to problem solving also extends beyond infancy and toddlerhood. Four-year-olds show intraindividual variability when applying analogical solutions to problems (Tunteler & Resing, 2002), and four-and five-year-olds also use multiple strategies when solving arithmetic problems (Siegler & Jenkins, 1989). In older children, Alibali (1999) examined 143 children aged 8-11 years (M = 9.7) solving mathematical equivalence problems. Six incorrect and four correct strategies were inferred from children's speech and gestures, illustrating the importance of going beyond just the number of strategies: a repertoire of strategies may include both successful and unsuccessful approaches.

The distinction between strategy diversity and switching is particularly clear in Coyle and Bjorklund's (1997) study of second-, third-, and fourth-grade children completing sortrecall trials. Two key variables were related to recall performance: children could exhibit one to four strategies per trial (within-trial switching) and the degree of change in the combination of strategies across trials (between-trial). The use of multiple strategies within a trial increased with grade and was associated with higher recall. Children in all grades showed substantial strategy changes across trials, but greater stability in strategy combinations was associated with better performance, whereas instability in strategy combinations did not. In a developmental comparison, Schulz et al. (2019) used a spatial reward-search task with 55 children aged 7-8 years, 55 children aged 9-11 years, and 50 adults aged 19-55 years, finding that children explored more extensively than adults but obtained lower rewards. Computational modelling indicated that children generalized less broadly and relied more strongly on directed exploration, with no reliable age differences in random sampling. These results suggest that children's variable choices can partly reflect information-seeking rather than simply undirected responding, and that these processes respond differently to changes in uncertainty (Lee et al., 2023).

Together, these studies show that children can initially display diverse repertoires, but often become more selective and consistent in how they use them over developmental time and/or learning. This pattern also demonstrates why strategy variability is neither universally beneficial or detrimental. The overlapping waves model provides an overarching framework to better understand developmental changes in strategy exploration and implementation.

Overlapping waves model Siegler (1996) proposed the 'overlapping waves model' (Figure 3) to explain the sometimes conflicting patterns of strategy variability in development. This model describes developmental changes in terms of the coexistence of multiple strategies, shifts in their relative frequency of use, and the discovery of new strategies. If these strategies differ in their response-time and accuracy profiles, increasingly consistent selection of effective strategies could contribute to declining variability. Conversely, the introduction and exploration of new strategies could temporarily increase variability. The relationship between strategy exploration and variability in, say, response times follows naturally: Assuming each individual strategy will be characterized by a slightly different response profile in both accuracy and response time (e.g. slow deliberation, guessing), then younger children, who may deploy more, and more diverse, strategies are also likely to be more variable in their performance. One example of such a developmental pattern is work focused on the balance scale task, where children must assess whether a balance with two weights placed left and right in a balance arm will fall left, right, or stay in balance. Jansen and van der Maas (2002) demonstrated in 805 individuals, aged 5-19, that children had a distinct progression of rules and heuristics. Jansen and van der Maas indeed observed distinct classes of strategies, applied to varying degrees by different children. In line with the overlapping waves model, the authors observed a clear age-linked progression in terms of the complexity of the rules deployed, even with some paradoxical patterns: sometimes more advanced rules would lead to lower accuracy on a given set of items, as sometimes a simpler strategy may yield the correct response for the wrong reasons. Crucially, in line with the overlapping waves hypothesis, these strategies were applied inconsistently even within the same child. The overlapping waves model proposes that children have multiple strategies available at any given point in development, depending in part on the cognitive demands of each strategy, and may select different strategies across successive attempts at the same problem. Similar findings were observed by Coyle and Bjorklund (1996). When children are given identical problems in a short time, children tend to deploy distinct strategies for each duplicate version of the same problem. According to the overlapping waves model, this can be explained by the idea that children understand a diverse set of strategies and are able to deploy these at any time. In summary, strategy selection is probabilistic rather than deterministic, allowing several strategies to coexist within a child's repertoire while their relative frequencies of use change with learning and development, increasing in efficiency over (developmental) time. If strategy use and deployment is stochastic even within identical items given repeatedly, we would expect to find task-or domain-specific patterns of variability. Judd et al. (2024) examined whether individual differences in cognitive variability were related across a battery of 11 cognitive tasks targeting reasoning, mathematics and working memory. They found in 2608 children, aged 6-8, that individual differences in trial-to-trial response-time variability could be reliably estimated, but were only modestly correlated across tasks (r = .19), much more weakly than average performance (r = .57), further suggesting domain specificity in variability. Finding specificity not just in cognitive domain but in temporal resolution, Galeano-Keiner et al. (2022) found that cognitive variability at one temporal resolution (item-to-item) was only weakly correlated with variability at other temporal resolutions (e.g. day-to-day).

Variability and neurodevelopmental disorders

Above, we have shown that cognitive variability is a ubiquitous feature in typical development. However, it is also likely that cognitive variability is a particularly sensitive measure to capture atypical development such as neurodevelopmental conditions. For instance, fluctuations in processing speed are considered one of the most robust cognitive correlates of ADHD (Hauser et al., 2016; Karalunas et al., 2014; Kofler et al., 2013). This pattern, of elevated variability in children with ADHD symptomatology, has been observed in a wide range of outcomes. Aristodemou et al. (2024) Kiddie Continuous Performance Test (K-CPT), a visual task assessing attention, they found that children at risk for ADHD showed greater response-time variability than typically developing controls. The same pattern, of children with an ADHD diagnosis having greater response-time variability than controls, was found in many other studies (Ali et al., 2026; Friedman et al., 2022; Gilbert et al., 2019; Mühlberger et al., 2020; Saito et al., 2019; Salum et al., 2019; Weigard et al., 2019). This association also extends to differences in symptom severity, where higher levels of ADHD symptoms are associated with greater response-time variability (Ali et al., 2019 (Ali et al., , 2026;; Aristodemou et al., 2024; Machida et al., 2019; Moses et al., 2022; Wiker et al., 2023). Given the prevalence of comorbidity of mental health and neurodevelopmental symptomatology, one key question is whether elevated response-time variability reflects ADHD-related symptoms specifically, or more general mental health or neurodevelopmental symptomatology. For instance, Kofler et al. (2013) propose responsetime variability as a broader transdiagnostic marker of clinical severity. However, in contrast, studies examining symptom dimensions within diagnostic categories suggest more specific associations with ADHD symptom severity (Aristodemou et al., 2024; Salum et al., 2019; Wiker et al., 2023). For instance, a review of studies on Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD) found that elevated response-time variability was associated with diagnostic status only among individuals with co-occurring ADHD symptoms (Karalunas et al., 2014). This suggests that ADHD symptoms may help explain elevated response-time variability observed in ASD.

Even within the domain of ADHD, response-time variability seems to be specific to inattentive, but not hyperactive symptoms: Three independent studies observed this (inattentive, not hyperactive) symptom specificity of response-time variability in large samples using diverse methods (Aristodemou et al., 2024; Salum et al., 2019; Wiker et al., 2023). In contrast, Ali et al. (2026) found an association with both ADHD domains, but comorbidity of the two dimensions may explain this observation. Thus the current empirical landscape largely supports the view that cognitive variability in response-time tasks are a specific correlate of ADHD-related symptoms, specifically of inattention severity, and that inattentive lapses may represent a key mechanism of cognitive variability.

Momentary lapses in attention may disrupt otherwise consistent task performance, producing intermittent slow responses that increase overall response-time variability.

Computational modelling work shows that ADHD children's variability in a stop-signal task can be attributed to more frequent attentional lapses during the stop signal responding process (Weigard et al., 2019). This explanation is in line with a more recent study that showed that, among a battery of choice reaction time tasks, those tasks that specifically tax attentiveness, the increase in response-time variability was greatest for children with greater inattentive symptomatology children (Aristodemou et al., 2024). A plausible route to explain this pattern was offered by Unsworth and Robison (2016). Although focusing on an adult, rather than childhood sample, their use of behavioural proxies offers insight into distinct modes of inattention, which can be captured by ex-Gaussian models. Adults with greater response-time variability showed a bigger increase in response-time variability as a function of time spent on-task (Unsworth & Robison, 2016). Interestingly, two behavioural proxies of attentional lapses (thought probes and pupil dilation) were correlated with this change in response-time variability, and baseline arousal levels indicated that at least two modes of inattentiveness: (1) distractibility and (2) mind-wandering or internal inattention can drive such fluctuations. By extending these findings to children with inattention symptoms, we would predict that the association between response-time variability and ADHD, in simple response-time tasks, is at least in part driven by an internally focused mode of inattention. This is because theoretical work predicts that inattentiveness in the absence of hyperactivity is particularly related to low baseline arousal levels, which in turn drive an internally focused mode of inattention and, in turn, higher response-time variability (Aston-Jones & Cohen, 2005a).

At the neural level, recent work has linked these behavioral fluctuations to largescale brain network organization. Cai et al. (2021) studied 52 children aged 9-12 (ADHD group: N = 29, M = 10.7, SD = 1.2; Typical controls: N = 23, M = 10.5, SD = 1.2) on a choiceresponse task during fMRI. Combining computational modelling with neuroimaging they show that inattentiveness is associated with weaker functional network connectivity and greater response-time variability. This aligns with Wiker et al. (2023) who showed that children with greater severity in a data-driven inattention factor also showed poorer whitematter microstructure, a structural correlate of functional connectivity, and higher responsetime variability.

Taken together, these findings suggest that arousal processes and large-scale network connectivity-at both functional and structural levels-are closely linked to attentional lapses that manifest as elevated response-time variability. However, there are likely also settings where (greater) variability may be beneficial, rather than a sign of underlying challenges.

Variability can be adaptive

So far, we have focused largely on (relatively) simple cognitive tasks and associated response-time variability, often demanding simple strategies and the suppression of unrelated signals (Lavie et al., 2004; Nobre & Kastner, 2014). A neurochemical explanation of this pattern is that children with high tonic levels of noradrenaline may display greater variability to the detriment of their performance (Aston-Jones & Cohen, 2005a). This process reflects the classic Yerkes-Dodson curve: An inverted-U shape capturing the association between arousal and performance. Generally, task performance is optimal at intermediate levels of arousal and worse at the outer ranges of the spectrum, either due to inattentiveness or distractibility (Aston-Jones & Cohen, 2005b). The same children who may show worse and more variable performance on simple tasks, may instead thrive in a foraging environment. Pfeffer et al. (2021) showed that in a value-based choice task manipulating catecholamine levels through the administration of atomoxetine, a noradrenaline reuptake inhibitor, led to increased decision noise. They explain that this might lead to worse performance in predictable environments, but adaptive in unpredictable environments which stimulate exploration. Similarly, in the domain of language, Lowie and Verspoor (2019) show that higher variability was related to higher learning rates (r = .53), whereas Huang et al. (2021) demonstrate that higher variability in writing scores was associated with higher learning rates. These findings highlight an adaptive aspect of cognitive variability often overlooked and worthy of further investigation.

Modifying cognitive variability through behavioral and pharmacological interventions

Above, we review an increasing body of mostly observational empirical work, demonstrating developmental patterns in cognitive variability across tasks and populations, and observational associations with outcomes of interest. However, this leaves a key question unanswered: Can (experimental) manipulations meaningfully affect their variability (up and down)? If so, does manipulating variability have downstream consequences on phenotypes as we might expect based on purely observational studies? Behavioural interventions provide evidence that response-time variability can change over short timescales. Garrett et al. (2012) gave performance feedback in a choice response-time task in younger adults aged 18-34 years and older adults aged 60-82 years. Feedback did not significantly reduce variability in younger adults, but reductions in cognitive variaiblity emerged within minutes among older adults, particularly those with higher educational attainment. These findings demonstrate that response-time variability is modifiable, but that the degree of modifiability may depend on participants' initial performance. Crucially, improved performance does not need to always accompany a reduction in variability. Pharmacological studies provide a complementary approach to examining the malleability of cognitive variability and the contribution of neuromodulatory mechanisms.

Theoretical accounts implicate catecholaminergic modulation in the regulation of attention and cognitive performance, providing a rationale for examining how drugs acting on these systems affect variability (Aston-Jones & Cohen, 2005b; Hauser et al., 2016). ADHD research offers a substantial body of relevant evidence. A range of studies and meta-analyses have demonstrated that methylphenidate treatment is associated with reductions in responsetime variability in children and adults with ADHD (Coghill et al., 2014; Fredriksen et al., 2021; Johnson et al., 2008; Kim et al., 2013; Kofler et al., 2013). This supports the malleability of variability, although it does not uniquely identify the attentional or neurobiological processes responsible. Consistent with this evidence, Mühlberger et al. (2020) compared performance on a continuous performance task in a virtual classroom among 94 children with ADHD, of whom 26 received methylphenidate and 68 were unmedicated, and 34 neurotypical controls. Response-time variability was higher in the unmedicated ADHD group than in either the medicated ADHD group or the control group. Although this study was observational instead of experimental, we cannot ascertain whether medication caused a restoration variability to an equivalent level to controls.

Together, these findings indicate that cognitive variability is, in principle, through behavioural and pharmacological means. Future studies should combine controlled interventions with repeated assessments and computational modelling, complemented by non-invasive brain stimulation, to distinguish changes in attention, strategy use, and decision processes, and to test whether changes in variability are (part of) the underlying mechanism to improve long term outcomes.

Moving forward

Methodological & Technological advancements

Our review demonstrates that reliable assessment of cognitive variability through the use of ecological momentary assessment methods is feasible, both in children and older (clinical) populations (Bignardi et al., 2021; Welhaf et al., 2024; Zhao et al., 2026). (Individual differences in) cognitive fluctuations are found in virtually every cognitive domain studied to date. Table 1 provides a comprehensive overview of empirical studies to date, their populations, key findings, and cognitive task or domain studied. Although many cognitive domains are, in principle, suited, the precise design and measurement frequency should be aligned with the substantive question of interest. If researchers hypothesize that sleep quality plays a role in day-to-day difference in mean performance, then measuring once a day for a substantial number of days will provide more insight than frequent within-day measurements or large number of trials. In contrast, if cognitive differences are thought to vary as a function of domains that fluctuate more frequently (e.g. within-day mood changes), it is sensible to have multiple measurements within a day.

Open questions

Above we review an increasing and substantial body of findings concerning developmental differences and changes in cognitive variability. However, major open questions remain. One central question is developmental: To what degree is high, or low, variability (and what kind) associated with poorer (or better) cognitive and neurodevelopmental outcomes across the lifespan? Relatedly, how well do measures of cognitive variability perform as potential tools to signal early, atypically developing profiles such as inattentive challenges? Such observational studies and their findings may immediately imply valuable potential avenues for intervention studies: Can early interventions focused in cognitive variability induce improvements in cognitive performance and development in the medium or long term, and if so, at what age would such interventions be most beneficial?

Conclusion

Here, we review a rapidly growing body of literature on cognitive variability with a specific focus on development. Cognitive variability is a ubiquitous phenotypic feature of early development with pronounced individual differences across cognitive domains. On two distinct timescales, cognitive variability decreases with age and expertise, correlates with various outcomes, ranging from 'near' (greater variability is associated with poorer task performance) to 'far' (risk for neurodevelopmental disorders and atypical brain function and structure) and carries unique information about cognition beyond mean performance, despite substantial covariation. Crucially, we highlight that cognitive variability can both be adaptive and maladaptive depending on the context and can be manipulated through behavioural and pharmacological interventions. The precise neurobiological mechanisms, functional and structural, underlying cognitive variability remain topics for ongoing and future studies. Variability has been extensively studied within the domains of inhibition, processing speed, and working memory, but less frequently in for instance social cognition or metacognition at both cross-sectional and longitudinal levels. Together, our summary demonstrates the considerable future potential of cognitive variability as a lens through which to understand the many concurrent processes and tradeoffs in cognitive development.

Cañigueral et al. (2023a) randomly allocated 208 children aged 6-13 years to eight weeks of training targeting either inhibitory control ("Stopping") or response speed ("Going"), with "Going" serving as an active comparison. The intervention focused on performance (not variability) which was assessed using a stop-signal task before and after training. Training in "Stopping" increased variability in inhibitory control, alongside improved inhibitory performance, whereas "Going" reduced variability in response speed alongside faster responding. The authors interpreted these contrasting patterns as reflecting the different demands of the two processes: flexibility in "Stopping" and consistency in "Going". Evidence also suggests that post-training variability may be informative about longer-term outcomes.Cubillo et al. (2023)examined children following a five-week adaptive working-memory training program and found lower response-time variability in the training group than in the comparison group. Post-training cognitive variability was a better predictor than task accuracy of academic skills and well-being assessed 6-12 months later. These findings suggest that variability may capture aspects of training-related change that are not adequately represented by accuracy alone. Nevertheless, whether reducing cognitive variability is the underlying cause of improvements following training remains a largely open question.
