P values are never exactly zero. A p value of .000 is a rounding artifact — the actual value is simply smaller than the reported precision. Very small p values should be reported as p < .001 rather than p = .000. A p = 0 can also be a model parameter, such as an ARIMA order; check that it is a p value.

To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 5 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). Abstract

Teacher help is crucial for learning and boosts children's development. Receiving help may also have unintended negative consequences as it may signal that recipients are not competent enough to solve tasks independently. Teachers often provide more help to children from lower-socioeconomic status (SES) backgrounds, and lab-based studies show that when help is distributed unequally it can reinforce negative ability stereotypes. In this preregistered study, we examined whether children (7-13 years) perceive differences in who receives help in their own classrooms and whether perceived SESbased differences in help lead to stronger SES stereotypes over time. At three time points across one school year Dutch children (N= 565, 65 classrooms, 49.5% boys, 49.5% girls, 1.1% other/prefer not to say; 65.2% native Dutch; mixed SES backgrounds) identified which classmates most often received help from their teacher and how much they endorsed ability stereotypes about SES groups. Across all time points, children perceived that teachers helped lower-SES classmates more than higher-SES classmates. This pattern emerged for two types of help, and was more pronounced among children from lower-SES backgrounds and older children. Moreover, perceived SES-based differences in teacher help at the start of the school year predicted stronger SES-based ability stereotypes halfway through the year, although this association was limited to one type of help. These findings suggest that everyday classroom practices intended to support children who need additional help may inadvertently shape which children and societal groups are seen as less capable. This has important implications for how SES-based stereotypes take root.

Sometimes teachers also tell children exactly what to do, because they may think that children who experience difficulty benefit from more directive support (cf. Hornstra et al., 2015). To determine who needs help and how much help, teachers rely on their impression of whether children struggle or have struggled in the past (Timmermans et al., 2016; Van de Pol et al., 2010).

Perceptions of need and ability, however, are not always objective. One factor that influences whether people are perceived as needing support is their SES background (Bauer et al., 2025).

Specifically, a common and widely held stereotype about individuals from low-SES backgrounds is that they are less competent (Durante et al., 2017). Several studies show that teachers think that children from low-SES backgrounds have lower ability and improvement is less likely compared to their higher SES classmates (Denessen et al., 2022; Geven et al., 2018; Pit-ten Cate & Glock, 2019; Wang et al., 2018; Cuddy et al., 2007; Turetsky et al., 2021). One experimental study showed, for example, that teachers think children in fifth-grade with low-SES (vs. high-SES) backgrounds are less likely to complete a Bachelor's degree (Geven et al., 2021). Another study showed that teachers in different countries thought it was hard for children with lower educated parents to succeed at school (Sabarwal et al., 2022).

These stereotypes can lead teachers to infer that children with low-SES backgrounds need more help. Indeed, research suggest that teachers provide more explanations to children from lower-SES backgrounds and more often tell them exactly what to do (Atlay et al., 2019; Bloem et al., 2024; Hornstra et al., 2015; Turetsky et al., 2021). Teachers do this with the best intentions and it can promote learning (Jager et al., 2022). Yet, studies suggest that differences in help can also backfire:

Receiving more help leads to negative self-views and reduces children's motivation (e.g., Bonawitz et al., 2011; Leonard et al., 2021; Shell & Eisenberg., 1996; Sierksma & Brummelman, 2025; Reeve, 2009). Receiving help also has implications for how recipients are seen by others.

What Children Infer From Observing Differences in Help

Children make different inferences when they observe helping behavior. Often they think helpers are nice and kind (Gill et al., 2023; Sierksma et al., 2022). Yet, recipients of help are viewed less positively. U.S. children as young as four years think that groups who receive help are less smart than groups that do not (Sierksma & Shutts, 2020). Dutch 7-to-9-year-old children assume that children who receive more directive help are less smart and learn less (Sierksma, 2023). And when U.S. children (5-12 years) observe that a child receives help from a teacher when solving math problems (e.g., "Don't forget to carry your tens"), they think this child is less smart than those that did not receive help (Graham & Barker, 1990). Receiving help thus signals incompetence to children, which means that if help is distributed along group lines it could perpetuate ability stereotypes.

It is clear that children endorse negative ability stereotypes about people from lower-SES backgrounds. Across childhood (i.e., 4-14 years), children rate rich people as smarter than poor people (Mistry et al., 2015; Roussos & Dunham, 2016; Sigelman, 2012), and wealth cues (i.e., a branded vs. generic shirt) lead 4-to-5-year-old U.S. children to think people are smarter (Shutts et al., 2016; Woods et al., 2005). It is less clear how children, at such young ages, develop these stereotypes. The social environment may play an important role (Bandura, 1977; Bigler & Liben, 2007; Heck et al., 2022; Over & McCall, 2018). Here we propose that observing differences in teacher help could foster the development of SES-based stereotypes (Sierksma & Shutts, 2025). That is, when teachers help children with lower-SES backgrounds more to support their development, and children pick up on this differentiation, it might lead them to think that peers from lower-SES backgrounds are less smart. Do children notice that their teacher helps their peers differently? Emerging research suggests that they do. In a study, Borst et al. (under review), asked Dutch children (9-12 years) to report how their teacher interacted with classmates and teachers reported how they interacted with their students.

Results showed that the types of help children thought peers got was in line with the type of help that teachers said they provided. The current study builds on this work and assesses whether children also think their teacher helps classmates from low-SES backgrounds more. If they do, it might help explain how SES-based stereotypes develop.

Children's Own SES Background and Age

Whether children notice and are impacted by differences in their teacher's help might depend on their own SES background. We considered three possibilities. First, children from lower-SES backgrounds may receive more help themselves (Bloem et al., 2024; Hornstra et al., 2015), and therefore more readily notice when teachers differentiate in their helping behavior. Second, children from higher-SES backgrounds may be more sensitive to SES-based differences in teacher help, as it allows them to maintain a positive view of their group (Burkholder et al., 2020; Jost & Banaji, 1994; Rizzo & Killen, 2020; Tajfel & Turner, 1979). A last possibility is that children's own SES does not influence how they view teacher help. During early and middle childhood, children's perception of their own SES is often inaccurate (i.e., more positive or 'in the middle'; Ackerman & Elenbaas, 2023; Kostet et al., 2022; Mistry et al., 2015) and several studies show that children's SES background is not associated with their endorsement of SES-based stereotypes (Ackerman & Elenbaas, 2023; Désert et al., 2009). We explore these possibilities.

We also explore the role of children's age. It could be that older children are more aware and knowledgeable about social groups and inequality and thus notice SES-based differences in help more than young children (e.g., Aboud, 2008; Bigler & Liben, 2007). This might mean that they report observing differences in help more often. But it could also be that younger children are more susceptible to differences in help. Young children's group-based biases tend to be more malleable than those of older children (Bigler & Liben, 2007). They might therefore pay more attention to their teachers' behavior to learn about societal groups. As a consequence, they may report greater differences in help.

The Present Study

With the current study we aim to understand the potential unintended consequences of teacher help. Do children perceive SES-based differences in teachers' help in their own classroom and does that foster the development of SES-based ability stereotypes? We preregistered the following hypotheses: 1) children think that their teacher provides more help to classmates from a lowercompared to higher-SES background, and 2) children's perceived SES-based differences in teacher help lead them to develop stronger SES-based ability stereotypes (i.e., seeing children with poor and low-educated parents as less smart compared to children with rich and high-educated parents). To evaluate the causal impact of perceived SES-based differences in teacher help on the endorsement of stereotypes we used longitudinal cross-lagged panel models across three time points in one school year.

SES is a broad construct and often includes indicators of wealth, educational level, and occupational status (Duncan & Magnuson, 2003). Researchers sometimes treat these indicators as interchangeable or combine them into one variable (see Antonoplis, 2023). But research also shows specificity in how these indicators influence children's life (e.g., Bukodi & Goldthorpe, 2013) and the stereotypes that they endorse (Boer et al., 2025). We therefore analyze the impact of SES-based differences in teacher help separately for stereotypes about groups that differs in wealth and educational level.

We included two forms of help in our study: Providing more explanations and telling children exactly what to do. Both types of help are common in the classroom (Howard et al., 2025) and more often provided to lower-SES children (Bloem et al., 2024; Hornstra et al., 2015; Howard et al., 2025) or those perceived to be incompetent (Nadler & Chernyak-Hai, 2015; Sierksma, 2023). We analyze each type of help separately but did not expect differences.

We also explored the role of achievement in children's perceived differences in help. Unequal opportunities persist in education, and children with lower-SES backgrounds often have lower achievement at school (Liu et al., 2022; see also Brummelman & Sedikides, 2023). This raises the question of whether children's perception of differences in teacher help reflect SES, or is also based on achievement differences between classmates. Examining achievement therefore allowed us to disentangle the complex interplay between these two factors and better understand what drives children's perceptions of differences in their teachers' help.

Method Participants

We used data from a longitudinal project with three time points collected in the Netherlands during one school year (2023-2024) in 68 classrooms (three classrooms were excluded: Two because the teacher changed during the school year and one because the teacher provided no SES data for any child). A total of 1186 7-to-13-year-old children participated (M = 10.16, SD = 1.00, 47.8% girls, 51.1% boys, 1.0% other/prefer not to say; for a description of the project, see https://osf.io/pt2g8/overview?view_only=4937606c1c4b437d936ac3521a40ad0a). To reduce response fatigue, we randomly assigned children at each time point to answer a subset of questions about receiving help. For the current study, we only included children who filled out both help questions for at least one time point (n = 734).

We also excluded children when they did not nominate any peers when asked who their teacher helped (n = 169). The absence of peer nominations may reflect nonresponse or that children thought none of their classmates received more help. We compared children who were included and excluded. Both groups were similar in SES background and reported similar levels of SES-based stereotypes at T1, but SES-based stereotypes differed slightly at T2 and T3: Children that were excluded due to nonresponse had somewhat lower SES-based stereotypes compared to children that did answer the questions during at least one time point (see Supplementary Materials I for details).

Our final sample included 565 children of 65 classes (Mage = 10.09, SDage = 1.01, 49.5% boys, 49.5% girls and 1.1% other/prefer not to say). Of these children, 49.9% had at least one parent who obtained an (applied) university Bachelor or Master degree, 33.7% had parents who obtained primary, secondary, or vocational education, and for 16.5% information on parental education was missing (i.e., Dutch population average for a Bachelor's/Master's degree is 36.4%; Statistics Netherlands, 2024). 65.2% of the children reported they had a native Dutch background (i.e., they and their parents were born in the Netherlands). Children with a migration background mainly originated from Turkey, Morocco, Syria, and Poland.

Procedure

To recruit classes, 1700 public primary school principals from different regions throughout the Netherlands were invited to participate. On average, 71.84% of the children in a classroom had parental consent to participate (range: 36.36% -100%). Researchers visited the schools three times during one school year (fall, winter, spring). The school year had started at least four weeks before the first school visit. The time between T1 and T2 was approximately 15 weeks, and 11 weeks between T2 and T3.

During the school visits children filled out questionnaires on tablets. They first reported their background characteristics, followed by questions about their perceptions of their teachers' helping behavior, their attitudes toward children from different SES, and some additional questions as part of the larger project -e.g., about their motivation, engagement, and friendships (see files at https://osf.io/pt2g8/overview?view_only=4937606c1c4b437d936ac3521a40ad0a). The questionnaires took approximately 30-45 minutes each time. Teachers and classrooms received a voucher for their participation.

Measures

Children's Socioeconomic Status

As our research question concerns differences in teacher's helping based on SES, we used teacher ratings of their children' SES in our analyses. Teachers were told about different factors that typically affect children's socioeconomic background (i.e., parents educational level, occupation and wealth, museum visits; Currie et al., 1997) and then provided SES for each child by moving a slider that ranged from 0 'lowest SES' to 10 'highest SES' (M = 7.11, SD = 1.89). We also retrieved parent reports on their educational attainment, but these could not be used due to substantial amount of missing data. Teacher SES ratings correlated significantly with parental reported educational level (r = .54, p < .001, n = 465).

Perceived SES-Based Differences in Teacher Help

Children's perceptions of their teachers' helping behavior were measured by asking: "Which classmates most often receive explanations from your teacher?" (i.e., explanations item), and "Which classmates does your teacher often have to tell exactly what to do?" (i.e., telling what to do item).

Children could nominate a maximum of 10 of their participating classmates (there was no minimum; Cillessen & Marks, 2017). Items were selected from a 7-item survey aimed to capture perceptions of teacher behaviors, adapted from the student and teacher version of the Need Supportive Need Thwarting Teaching Questionnaire (Bloem et al., 2026).

Following sociometric research on friendship and homophily (see e.g., Currarini et al., 2010) we computed a new variable, for each time point, to capture whether children perceived SES-based differences in whom their teacher helped. First, we used the teacher ratings to determine the SES of each child in the data. Second, for each help question we calculated the average SES of all peers that were nominated by a child. Third, for each classroom, we calculated the average SES of all children in that classroom and subtracted the SES of the participant themselves. Finally, to control for the average SES in classrooms, we subtracted the classroom-level average from the child-level average (of all nominated peers by that child) 1 . For example, if three peers are selected with a SES of 3, 6, and 4 (step 1), this results in an average SES-score of 4.33 (step 2), if the classroom average is 6.82 (step 3), this results in a negative score (4.33-6.83 = -2.50; step 4) and indicates that children thought lower SES peers received more help from the teacher than expected by chance. Thus, negative values indicate that children more often selected peers with a lower SES (given the class-level average). Positive values indicate that children more often selected peers with a higher SES than expected by chance.

SES-Based Ability Stereotypes

To measure SES-based ability stereotypes (Pfeifer et al., 2007; Rutland et al., 2007) we told children that sometimes people differ in wealth or educational level and asked them how much they agreed with the following statements: "I think most [poor vs. rich] children are smart" and "I think most children with parents who [did not study for a long time at all/studied for a long time] are smart."

Children used slider scales to indicate agreement (ranging from 0, completely disagree, to 10 completely agree). As preregistered, we created a bias score for ability stereotypes by subtracting ratings for low-SES targets from ratings of high-SES targets. A positive bias score reflects that children agreed more with the statements when targets were high (vs. low) in SES reflecting a negative stereotype about lower-SES groups. Note that we also explored children's endorsement of warmth stereotypes (see preregistration), and report these analyses in the Supplementary Materials IV.

Perceived Achievement-Based Differences in Teacher Help

To examine the role of achievement in observations of teacher help, we computed a new variable. Children's achievement in mathematics and reading was obtained via standardized test scores of the previous school year (June 2022). Schools in the Netherlands choose how to monitor children's achievement from a restricted set of valid and reliable tests (Rijksoverheid, n.d.). We standardized test scores within different tests and within grades (for the full procedure see Bloem et al., 2026) and combined children's test scores in mathematics and reading. We then computed a 'perceived achievement-based differences in teacher help' variable using the same procedure as for perceived SES-based differences in teacher help. Similar to the SES-based differences in teacher help, we controlled for the classroom-average achievement of all peers.

Analyses

Missing data on the perceived SES-based differences in teacher help ranged from 49.2% to 54% per time point, which was mostly due to missingness by design (i.e., some children did not receive the questions to reduce response fatigue). Missingness on the stereotype variables was relatively low (7.1%-8.1%). Missing data were handled using Full Information Maximum Likelihood (FIML). To examine whether children thought that lower-SES peers received more help from their teacher (Hypothesis 1), one-sample t-tests were conducted in RStudio (R Core Team, 2026).

To examine whether children's perceived SES-based differences in teacher help were related to their endorsement of SES-based ability stereotypes (Hypothesis 2) we preregistered using two Random-Intercept Cross-Lagged Panel Models (Hamaker et al., 2015) to disentangle within-and between-person associations. But these models did not converge. Also preregistered, we therefore used traditional Cross-Lagged Panel Models, which estimate autoregressive associations, cross-lagged associations, and within-time point correlations at the aggregate level. Autoregressive paths capture the stability of constructs over time and control for prior levels of the same variable. Cross-lagged paths test whether one construct predicts changes in another construct over time and were therefore central to our hypotheses that perceived SES-based differences in teacher help predict endorsement of SES-based stereotypes. Moreover, with cross-lagged paths we also tested reverse causality (i.e., whether SES-based stereotypes predict perceived SES-based differences in teacher help). Finally, within-time point correlations account for associations between the constructs measured at the same time point. In line with the preregistration, two separate models were estimated: Model 1 included the ability stereotypes about groups that differ in wealth and Model 2 included ability stereotypes about groups that differ in educational level. To account for the nested structure of the data (children within classrooms), we used TYPE = COMPLEX in Mplus. Standardized regression coefficients (β) are reported to interpret the effect size estimates. Several exploratory analyses were conducted. To explore the role of children's SES and age, we estimated the same models we used to test Hypothesis 2 and included SES and age as covariates.

Finally, to explore the role of children's achievement, we again estimated the same models we used to test Hypothesis 2, and included the perceived achievement-based (instead of SES-based) differences in teacher help.

Second, when children thought lower-SES peers were told more often exactly what to do by their teacher at T2, they thought that lower-SES peers received more explanations from their teacher at T3 (p = .031). Ability Stereotypes About Groups That Differ in Educational Level

In Model 2 we examined education-based stereotypes (see Table 2). Significant and relatively strong autoregressive effects again suggest substantial temporal stability. Cross-lagged effects for more explanations (ps < .001 and .021) and telling exactly what to do (ps < .001) by the teacher were the same as for Model 1. Two additional cross-lagged effects emerged. First, in line with our hypothesis, when children perceived stronger SES-based differences in teachers' explanations at T1 (i.e., a larger negative value), they were more likely to endorse positive ability stereotypes about peers with higher-educated parents at T2 (p = .002). Second, when children showed stronger endorsement of these ability stereotypes at T2, they reported weaker SES-based differences in teachers' explanations at T3 (p = .016). Within-time correlations between the two help items were positive and significant at T1 and T2. There was one additional significant within-time correlation at T2: SES-based differences in teachers' explanations was positively correlated with endorsement of ability stereotypes about groups that differed in educational level.

Exploratory Analyses

Children's SES and Age

We preregistered to test whether SES and age moderate the associations between perceived SES-based differences in teacher help and the endorsement of SES-based stereotypes, but this was not possible because we had to do Cross-Lagged Panel Models instead of Random-Intercept Cross-Lagged Panel Models (Hamaker et al., 2015). This means we did not have sufficient power to estimate interactions. Instead, we included children's SES and age as time-invariant covariates to the Cross-Lagged Panel Models for ability stereotypes about groups that differ in wealth and educational level.

Results showed that all significant cross-lagged findings remained the same and were thus not influenced by children's SES and age. See Supplementary Materials VII for details.

Perceived Achievement-Based Differences in Teacher Help

Children's achievement and SES correlated positively (r = .52, p <.001). We included children's perceived achievement-based differences in teacher help in our models. Children's perceived SES-based differences and perceived achievement-based differences in teacher help were highly correlated (r = .41-.56, ps < .001). We predicted these achievement-based differences in teacher help on children's endorsement of ability stereotypes, from T1 to T2 as well as from T2 to T3 (i.e., cross-lagged paths). The association we initially found between perceived SES-based differences in teachers' explanations and their endorsement of education-based stereotypes was no longer significant when we included achievement-based differences in teacher help in the model (see Supplementary Materials VIII for all details). This suggests that indeed the interplay between achievement and SES may drive stereotype endorsement.

Discussion

Receiving help from teachers is important to support children's learning. But help may have unintended consequences when it is distributed unequally and conveys a message that some children are less smart. Prior studies indicate that teachers sometimes help children with lower-versus higher-SES backgrounds more (Atlay et al., 2019; Bloem et al., 2024; Hornstra et al., 2015; Turetsky et al., 2021). Our results show that children see that too. That is, children as young as seven years reported that their teacher provided more help to lower-SES classmates than to higher-SES classmates. Our data also suggest that when children observe differences in how help is distributed in their classroom, this can lead to stronger endorsement of SES-based ability stereotypes. Everyday classroom practices intended to support children who need additional help may thus inadvertently shape which children and societal groups are seen as less capable. This has important implications for how SES-based stereotypes take root.

Perceived SES-Based differences in Teacher Help as Building Blocks of Ability Stereotypes

Survey research shows that teachers tend to help children with a lower-SES background more than their classmates with a higher SES (e.g., Bloem et al., 2024). They might do so because lower SES is often associated with lower achievement (Liu et al., 2022) and stereotypes lead them to infer that low-SES children are less competent and more in need of help (Bauer et al., 2021; Durante et al., 2017). Children with a lower SES also report themselves that others often assume they need more help and see them as less competent (see Brummelman & Sedikides, 2023). Experimental lab-based research shows the implications of perceived differences in help: Children think that those that receive more help are less smart (e.g., Brey & Shutts, 2018; Graham & Barker, 1990; Sierksma, 2023). And when adults help children from one group more than another group, children assume that new members of those groups, whom they never saw receiving help, are also less smart (Sierksma & Shutts, 2020). This is exemplary for how group-based stereotypes function in the real world: People generalize their biases about groups to individual group members (Nelson, 2025). Our findings lend support for the notion that these mechanisms not only can be made to occur in the lab (Brauer & Kennedy, 2023), but also exist in real-life classrooms.

Children in our study reported that their teacher provides more help to peers with lower-SES than peers with higher-SES backgrounds. The finding was robust: Children reported SES-based differences in teacher help for two types of helping behavior (i.e., providing more explanations and telling children exactly what to do) and it emerged throughout the school year at all three time points.

Children thus observe in their own classroom that help covaries with SES background. Our longitudinal findings show that children who perceive that their teacher explains materials more often to lower-versus higher-SES classmates early in the year, endorse stronger ability stereotypes about children with lower-educated parents in the middle of the school year. This is in line with our theoretical framework and suggests that perceived differences in teacher help functions as a building block in the development of ability stereotypes (Sierksma & Shutts, 2025).

Our findings also lend support for the notion that observations of differences in help may Observations of SES-based help influenced children's ability stereotypes about peers with lower-educated parents, but not their stereotypes about peers from less wealthy families. This pattern suggests that helping behaviors may be uniquely predictive for stereotypes related to societal groups that are lower educated, and do not generalize to less wealthy individuals or to SES-related stereotypes broadly. It points to the possibility that for different indicators of SES, stereotypes may develop via distinct routes. Other findings fit this view: Children endorse different stereotypes about lower educated and less wealthy groups (Boer et al., 2025; King et al., 2025), and in our data negative ability stereotypes were endorsed more strongly for education than for wealth. These findings have important implications for our understanding of the development of stereotypes: Rather than assuming that a single, general mechanism shapes the development of stereotypes, research may need to account for input that is specific to the particular social dimension or stereotype content in question. It also highlights that researchers should be cautious about collapsing different SES indicators, such as wealth, education, and occupation, into a single dimension, as children appear to treat these properties differently (Antonoplis, 2023; Diemer et al., 2013).

Boundaries and Moderators

Our study also provides insight into important boundary conditions and moderating variables.

First, older children and those belonging themselves to lower-SES backgrounds were more likely to report that their teachers helped lower-SES peers more. Why might this be? Research suggests that children from lower-SES backgrounds are more often treated paternalistically by others (i.e., receiving more inflated praise, unsolicited help, pity; Brummelman et al., 2026). They may therefore have internalized the negative psychological consequences that differences in help can carry more than their higher-SES counterparts. This could mean they also notice differences in help more when it happens to others. Further, older children are more aware and knowledgeable about social groups and inequality (e.g., Bigler & Liben, 2007), and therefore likely pick up on their teachers' differences in help more easily than younger children. As our data did not allow us to fully test the impact of age and SES on stereotype formation, future work is needed to better understand how these moderators shape how differences in help impact the development of stereotypes.

Second, our findings show that when children report which classmates receive help, these classmates differ in SES background but also in their achievement. That is, our data showed that perceived SES-based differences in help correlated with perceived achievement-based differences in help, and including perceived achievement-based differences reduced the longitudinal impact on the development of SES stereotypes. This is in line with a large literature documenting achievement gaps based on SES (Boman, 2023; OECD, 2023). Neverthelss, implications for the unintended consequences of teacher help remain the same: When children observe that help covaries with other dimensions, such as SES, it can signal to them who is and who is not capable and these impressions, over time, can translate into SES-based ability stereotypes. When teacher's help thus covaries with SES background, even when it is to tailor support based on children's prior achievement, it can communicate or reinforce the idea that children with a lower-SES background are less academically capable.

Third, our findings highlight that it is important to consider the role of time in the formation of stereotypes. Stereotypes develop through environmental input that is consistent and repeated over time, rather than through isolated instances (Bigler & Liben, 2007). Developmental theories of stereotype formation, however, seldom specify the timescale over which such environmental input is expected to translate into changes in children's beliefs (see Hamaker, 2025). If input is consistent, do children acquire stereotypes rapidly or over more prolonged periods? Does it take months, a year or several years? Our results suggests that perceived differences in help from teachers impacts ability stereotypes only early in the school year. But our data only covered three time points across roughly 26 weeks, so they offer just a few snapshots of a highly dynamic classroom environment. To truly capture developmental change and how stereotype formation unfolds, we need intensive longitudinal designs with repeated measures across shorter time intervals (e.g., daily-diary or experience-sampling approaches, see e.g., Kupers et al., 2023). But we also need theoretical work that formulates testable predictions about time and change in the development of stereotype learning and endorsement.

Strengths and Limitations

Our study has several strengths, including its longitudinal design, broad age range, diverse sample, and high ecological validity. There are also some limitations worth noting. First, teacher help can take various forms and our study focused on two types of help that teachers frequently provide to lower SES pupils (Howard et al., 2025; Turetsky et al., 2021). Children may also observe other, more empowering forms of teacher help which might be provided more to children with higher-SES backgrounds (Nadler & Chernyak-Hai, 2015; Sierksma, 2023). Future studies should broaden and compare the impact of these different types of help on the formation of stereotypes.

Second, some children did not answer the peer nomination questions and were thus not included in our study. To better understand why they did not, we recommend that future studies include response formats that allow children to explicitly indicate that they perceive no differences in teacher help or why they do not want to or can fill out the question. Third, we used teacher estimates of children's SES. Teacher estimates correlated with parent-reported educational attainment but were not in perfect agreement. Future studies should use parent reports, which would also allow analyzing how different SES indicators (wealth, education, occupation) shape children's views of help and their endorsement of SES-based stereotypes.

Practical Implications and Conclusions

Teachers face a double-edged sword. They should provide each pupil with the support that they need, and help that is tailored to children's prior understanding is particularly meaningful for those from low SES backgrounds (Bach et al., 2025; Dietrichson et al., 2017). At the same time, when they distribute help unequally it could have adverse consequences and perpetuate inequalities in their classroom. Solving this complex challenge requires changing how lower-SES groups are perceived as well as how children think about receiving help.

Lower-SES groups are often portrayed as more vulnerable and in need of help (Bauer et al., 2025; Bauer & Walton, 2024; Silverman et al., 2023; Szekeres et al., 2026). Transforming representations about people who experience disadvantage, from deficit to strength-based frames, could reduce paternalistic tendencies in teachers and, by extension, reduce inequality (Reeves et al., 2022). Another way to reduce unintended consequences of help could be to make the distribution of help less visible in classrooms. Teachers could, for example, use learning apps that make differentiation in learning material less visible (Dumont & Ready, 2023). It is also important to reduce children's tendency to associate help with incompetence. Promoting a classroom climate in which mistakes and being wrong are treated as part of the learning process benefits children's motivation and learning (e.g., McMillan & Moore, 2020; Steuer & Dresel, 2015), and may also mitigate negative perceptions of peers who receive more help. Teachers could, for example, foster a classroom climate in which intellectual humility -i.e., the willingness to acknowledge that one's knowledge is limited and that others' expertise is valuable (Porter et al., 2022 (Porter et al., , 2026) -is salient. Research shows that intellectual humility promotes receptivity to feedback and a greater willingness to seek help in adults (Krumrei-Mancuso et al., 2020; Porter et al., 2022 Porter et al., , 2026;; Wong & Wong, 2021) and could thus shift children's negative views of help to positive signals of motivation or curiosity. Future research should seek to better understand the role of these environmental factors in shaping the development of stereotypes. Using real-classroom data, we showed that children observe that their teacher helps low-SES classmates more than high-SES classmates which may serve as a building block for developing ability stereotypes. It is important that teachers recognize these unintended consequences of help, and research should identify what produces the effects and how to mitigate them. Such insight can inform help that benefits all children and promotes healthy intergroup relations early in life.

We also conducted the main analyses with SES-based warmth stereotypes (instead of ability stereotypes). Here, we describe its methods and results.

Warmth Stereotypes

To measure SES-based warmth stereotypes, we asked children how much they agreed with the following statements: "I think most [poor vs. rich] children are nice" and "I think most children with parents who [did not study for a long time at all/studied for a long time] are nice." Children used slider scales to indicate agreement (ranging from 0, completely disagree, to 10 completely agree). As preregistered, we created a bias score for warmth stereotypes by subtracting ratings for low-SES targets from ratings of high-SES targets. A positive bias score reflects that children agreed more with the statements when targets were high (vs. low) in SES.

Supplementary Materials VII Exploratory Analyses With SES and Age Added as Time-Invariant Covariates

As we could not use Random-Intercept Cross-Lagged Panel Models, we had to deviate from the preregistration for our exploratory analyses. In Cross-Lagged Panel Models, moderators can only be added as cross-lagged effects. But this was not possible due to power issues, partly because moderating effects cannot account for missingness when Full Information Maximum Likelihood is used. Hence, we added children' SES and age as time-invariant covariates to the Cross-Lagged Panel Models, to see whether they are related to our variables of interest at all time-points.

When we added SES and age to the models with stereotypes about groups that differ in wealth (model 1) and educational level (model 2; see Table 8), our significant cross-lagged findings remained the same. One additional association became significant: When children perceived larger SES-based differences in teachers' telling what to do at T1 (i.e., a larger negative value), they showed a weaker endorsement of ability stereotypes about groups that differed in educational level at T2 (i.e., positive association). Further, children's SES and age were to some extent associated with their observed SES-based difference in teacher help and endorsement of stereotypes -although only at baseline (T1) and not later in the school year. That is, children with a higher SES perceived smaller SES-based differences in teachers' explanations and telling what to do at T1 than children with a lower SES. Children with a higher SES also showed a weaker endorsement of ability stereotypes about groups that differed in educational level at T1. Further, older children showed a weaker endorsement of ability stereotypes about groups that differed in educational level at T1.

Children who perceived that their teacher gave more explanations to lower-SES peers early in the school year reported stronger SES-based differences in telling what to do halfway through the school year. In turn, perceived SES-based differences in telling exactly what to do in the middle of the year, predicted stronger perceived SES-based differences in explanations by the end of the year. Observations of unequal help may thus become self-reinforcing and, over time, generalize to societal groups and their group members. Importantly, our results do not lend support for the idea that it works the other way around: Endorsement of stereotypes never caused children to report stronger SES-based differences in teacher help throughout the school year. If anything, the opposite seemed to occur (i.e., stronger SES stereotypes led to less perceived SES-based differences in providing explanations).
