Power Analysis Check

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Full text

Abstract

Objective: By what mechanisms does parental monitoring affect youth adjustment? This paper aimed to review every published study that has tested a statistical mediator of monitoring→adjustment.

Method: We searched for studies that (a) enrolled elementary-, middle-, and/or high-school aged youth, (b) measured parental monitoring as parent action (vs., e.g., knowledge), and (c) tested a statistical mediation model linking monitoring to any form of youth adjustment.

Results: 39 papers have tested a mediator of the link from monitoring → adjustment. Most papers used cross-sectional data only (26 of 39) on typically developing samples (33 of 39). All papers were non-experimental. Only 1 of 39 papers made a substantial effort to rule out potential confounding -most adjusted for just a few measured covariates. Across the 39 papers, 79 out of 173 tests of mediation were statistically significant (46%). Of the significant tests, 59 indicated beneficial effects of monitoring, and 20 indicated detrimental effects of monitoring. There was at least one statistically significant test for 42 distinct pairs of mediator and outcome, but only 6 mediational chains had been replicated in more than one paper. Effect sizes for single mediators were typically small (median standardized ab = -0.02) and suggested multiple mediators would be needed to explain the total effect (median percent mediated = 24%). Of relevance to Stattin and Kerr's (2000) classic argument, we found only 1 paper testing whether youth disclosure mediates the link between monitoring and adjustment.

Conclusion: There are now many published tests of the mediators linking parental monitoring to youth adjustment. However, few have been replicated, and fewer still have been rigorous. As a result, the extant evidence can be reconciled with many (if not all) major theories about how parental monitoring works.

In their classic papers (Stattin & Kerr, 2000; Kerr & Stattin, 2000), Stattin and Kerr conceptualize youth disclosure as a competitor to parental monitoring -another independent variable that competes to explain adjustment (Figure 1, Model A). But youth disclosure could also be a mediator for parental monitoring -the parent asks for information (X), then the teen discloses information (M), and in turn we see improved adjustment (Y) (Figure 1, Model B). This is a perfectly natural mediation chain to hypothesize.

Unfortunately, the competitor vs. mediator possibilities cannot be empirically distinguished when monitoring and disclosure are measured at the same timepoint, as they were in Stattin and Kerr's landmark papers. The competitor and mediator models will give identical model fit, standardized coefficients, and p-values (MacKinnon et al., 2000). Thus, an alternative interpretation of Stattin and Kerr's classic finding is not that disclosure is a competitor or replacement for monitoring, as they argue, but simply a mediating mechanism by which monitoring affects adjustment. Stattin and Kerr (2000a, 2000b) did not acknowledge this possibility; in a later paper, Kerr, Stattin, and Burk (2010) discussed it but did not test it directly. 2 Pending tests of mediation in longitudinal data, what to make of Stattin and Kerr's classic finding remains unclear.

This paper aimed to review every published study that has tested a statistical mediator of the link between monitoring and adjustment -including those that have tested youth disclosure in particular. We aimed to synthesize and evaluate the evidence for each putative mediator, both in isolation and in comparison. The goal was to inform core questions first raised by Stattin and Kerr (2000) -whether and how monitoring works, and the role of disclosure within that picture.

We define "parental monitoring" as the set of behaviors parents perform with the goal of gaining information about their teenager's activities and life (Pelham et al., 2024). Thus, this review focuses exclusively on monitoring as parent action -a superset of the constructs Stattin shook up the field with a dramatic empirical claim.

Introduction

First, they showed that the burgeoning literature ostensibly linking "parental monitoring" to youth adjustment had in fact been linking parental knowledge to youth adjustment. The items on scales labeled "parental monitoring" mostly asked about what parents knew, not what parents did. Second, Stattin and Kerr fixed the measurement issue by creating new scales to carefully measure parental monitoring as a parent behavior. After fixing the measurement issue, they found that the cross-sectional association between parental monitoring and youth adjustment (specifically, normbreaking) went from (a) positive and statistically significant to (b) near zero and statistically nonsignificant when one adjusted for concomitant youth disclosure. This finding sparked a major shift in thinking about parental monitoring and has guided the past 25 years of research on an important topic.

Stattin and Kerr started a conversation about whether and how parental monitoring actually works. Yet it is difficult to test whether something works until you specify the mechanisms by which that something should produce its effects. The mechanistic theory guides what should be measured, when, and tells you what counts as evidence for or against efficacy.

Consider the analogy of testing a new medication for depression. Does it work via active concentration in the bloodstream? If so, a good way to test efficacy would be to administer the medication once in the lab and then measure momentary depressive symptoms after administration. But a bad way to test efficacy would be to prescribe the pill to be taken at home every day for 15 days, then measure depression in a lab visit 6 months later -you'd find no differences, because the medication would no longer be in the bloodstream. Now suppose the medication works via long-term accumulation in fat cells and becomes effective only once a certain concentration is reached. If so, the lab administration study would (incorrectly) yield null results and the at-home follow-up study would (correctly) yield positive results. So, when it comes to testing whether parental monitoring works, what are the hypothesized mechanisms? Unfortunately, the field has not reached a consensus (Pelham et al., 2025). Most articles linking monitoring to youth adjustment do not articulate any mechanistic theory, perhaps because the link seems self-explanatory, and those articles that do articulate a mechanism often propose conflicting ones. For example, some authors emphasize disciplinary practices (e.g., Hayes et al., 2003), others peer affiliation (e.g., Patterson & Dishion, 1985), others the internalization of parent values (e.g., Omer et al., 2016), and others still the development of self-control (e.g., Unnever & Cornell, 2003). When theories disagree to this extent, a logical next step is to evaluate them with empirical evidence -to collect and compare studies that have tested mediating mechanisms.

Disclosure as competitor vs. disclosure as mediator

Interestingly, Stattin and Kerr did not propose a mechanism that links parental monitoring to teen adjustment. 1 Rather they proposed there was no effect to explain, because their regression analysis showed that youth disclosure, not monitoring, is what causes differences in youth adjustment (or parental knowledge). One problem with that reasoning is the one described above: until you've articulated how something is supposed to work, it is difficult to test whether it works. But there is an even deeper methodological problem, demonstrated in Figure 1. and Kerr labeled "solicitation" and "control" in their landmark papers. We define "parental knowledge" as information the parent has about the teen's activities and life. We define "youth disclosure" as the youth intentionally communicating true information about their activities or life to their parent.

We stick to these definitions throughout this article. Stattin and Kerr (2000) and Stattin et al. (2010) documented that in many articles on the topic of parental monitoring, the labels given to the measures do not match the item content -scales measuring knowledge, a mix of knowledge and monitoring, or even a mix of knowledge, monitoring, and disclosure, all labeled "parental monitoring." For this reason, we always checked the item content of the "monitoring" measure in the studies we wished to include -going by what was actually measured, rather than the labels the authors gave the measure.

Search strategy

We searched for papers examining mediators of the association between parental monitoring and youth adjustment. We searched PubMed, Google Scholar, ProQuest, and the University of California, San Diego library databases. The first and third authors performed the searches between May 2025 and April 2026. We searched for every pairwise combination of the following search terms: ("parental monitoring" OR "parental solicitation" OR "parental control" OR "parental behavioral control" OR "parental supervision") + ("mediator" OR "mechanism" OR "mediation" OR "path analysis" OR "structural equation model" OR "mediating mechanism"). We also reviewed the reference lists in review papers on the topic of monitoring (Dishion & McMahon, 1998; Crouter et al., 2002; Stattin et al., 2010; Racz & McMahon, 2011; Anderson & Branstetter, 2012; Tilton-Weaver & Marshall, 2024; Pelham et al., 2024; Pelham et al., 2025; Aks et al., 2025) and of every article that we determined met the inclusion/exclusion criteria. Finally, we emailed the corresponding author of every included paper and several known experts in the field of parental monitoring to inquire whether they knew of any relevant articles we had missed.

Inclusion / exclusion criteria

Papers were considered for inclusion if they met four criteria: (a) reported on parental monitoring in a sample of elementary-, middle-, and/or high-school aged youth, (b) measured parental monitoring per the definition above, (c) measured any form of youth adjustment (e.g., behavioral, psychiatric, academic), and (d) statistically tested whether the association between monitoring and adjustment was mediated by a third variable.

Many papers measured parental monitoring using a rating scale that included some items that met our definition and some items that did not (e.g., Jin et al., 2022). In such cases, we included the study as long as ≥70% of the items on the measure met our definition of parental monitoring. If there was doubt about whether the measure qualified, reviewers searched for the exact item content and contacted the author for the complete list of items if necessary.

We included papers published in any year, whether or not they had been peer-reviewed. Every paper identified for inclusion was checked by a second reviewer to confirm eligibility. Disagreements were discussed among both reviewers and the senior author to reach a decision.

Data extraction

The first and third authors completed the data extraction. After one of these authors had extracted the data, the other author checked each value. Any discrepancies were resolved via discussion with the senior author.

For each paper, we extracted the following information: (a) the number of participants; (b) the sociodemographic composition; (c) the study design, (d) the temporal order in which X, M, and Y were measured; and (e) the specific measures used for X, M, and Y.

We categorized monitoring measures (X) using a typology we outlined in a prior paper (Pelham et al., 2024). Type 1 monitoring refers to asking a person for information (often called "solicitation"). Type 2 monitoring refers to setting or enforcing rules to gather information (often called "control"). Type 3 monitoring refers to using objective records to gather information (e.g., checking the teen's grades in an online portal). Type 4 monitoring refers to observing the youth to gather information (e.g., watching what the teen is doing while friends are over). For each paper, we examined the monitoring scale's item content and determined which type(s) of monitoring were being measured.

For each test of mediation, we extracted the following information: (a) the a path, (b) the b path, (c) the c' path, and (d) the covariates included in each regression. For each path, we extracted the coefficient, standard error, p-value, and 95% confidence interval. We also extracted the estimates of the standardized indirect effect (ab), the percent mediated (ab / c), the total effect (c), and the zero-order correlation between monitoring and youth adjustment (r xy ). If these values were not reported, we calculated them ourselves where possible (see supplement for details).

Data synthesis and evaluation

The scope and form of the located evidence did not permit meta-analysis. Instead, we qualitatively evaluated patterns of effect size and statistical significance, both as a whole and by characteristics of the sample/design. We also evaluated methodological features (e.g., number and choice of covariates) that might affect the rigor of the underlying test. Tests of sequential or moderated mediation were tabulated separately and are reported after the main results, in their own sections. Some youth adjustment outcomes were things one would want to prevent (e.g., delinquency) and others were things one would want to encourage (e.g., grade point average). As a result, the sign of the monitoring ↔ outcome association must be interpreted with care. To simplify presentation, we rescaled every effect size before tabulation so that a negative value indicates a beneficial effect of parental monitoring (e.g., greater monitoring → lower delinquency) and a positive value indicates a detrimental effect of parental monitoring (e.g., greater monitoring → greater delinquency).

Results

Search results

Our initial searches yielded 164 papers that might meet inclusion/exclusion criteria. Further review excluded all but 39 of these papers. 67 papers were excluded because, while the paper claimed to measure parental monitoring, the measure did not match the definition of parental monitoring given above (e.g., it actually measured parental knowledge, as in Patterson & Dishion, 1985). 22 papers were excluded because the measure of monitoring was too contaminated with items assessing other constructs (e.g., Ellis et al., 2007). Another 11 papers were excluded because the article's description of the monitoring measure was insufficient to evaluate the item content, and the authors did not respond to email queries trying to confirm what the items were. 13 papers were excluded because parental monitoring was a mediator or outcome, not the X variable (e.g., Almas et al., 2011). 9 papers were excluded because they did not report a mediation analysis (e.g., Padilla-Walker et al., 2011). 2 papers were excluded because the sampled youth were outside of our age range (e.g., Liang et al., 2026). 1 paper was excluded because the outcome variable was not a form of youth adjustment (Selcuk et al., 2026).

Thoroughness of reporting tests of mediation.

Most articles reported the mediation analysis only partially, which should be kept in mind when interpreting the findings below. In only 15 of the 173 tests of mediation (9%) did the authors report the coefficient, the standard error, and the statistical significance of the p-value for all three of a, b, and c' (or equivalently, the coefficient and the 95% confidence interval).

Design and handling of covariates. None of the 39 papers were experimental. 1 paper separated the between-and within-person associations in a multilevel model to account for time-invariant characteristics (Janssen et al., 2016). The remaining 38 papers used regression adjustment for measured covariates. The median number of included covariates on a given test of mediation was 4 (range = 0-9). The most common covariates included sex or gender (57 tests), youth disclosure (52 tests), parent psychological control (44 tests), youth age (41 tests), parental behavioral control (39 tests), parental knowledge (35 tests), socioeconomic status (21 tests), parental autonomy support (19 tests), parental solicitation (19 tests), and parental warmth (18 tests).

Findings

Overall findings. Across the 39 included articles, there were 173 unique tests of mediation. Table 2 summarizes the pattern of results as a function of several characteristics (e.g., by mediator; by outcome; by temporal ordering of X, M, and Y). Overall, 79 of the 173 tested indirect effects (46%) were statistically significant at p < .05. Of the 79 significant tests, 59 indicated a beneficial effect of monitoring and 20 indicated a detrimental effect of monitoring.

There were 71 unique pairings of a mediator and outcome, 42 of which had at least one statistically significant result. Only 6 significant mediational chains were replicated in more than one paper: parental monitoring → parental knowledge → substance use (3 papers), parental monitoring → parental knowledge → antisocial behavior (3 papers), parental monitoring → peer delinquency → antisocial behavior (3 papers), parental monitoring → autonomous motivation → creative thinking (3 papers), parental monitoring → attitudes toward risky sexual behavior → sexual risk behaviors (2 papers), and parental monitoring → invasive parenting → youth disclosure (2 papers). For the first five replicated chains, the monitoring effect was beneficial in every test. For the last replicated chain (parental monitoring → invasive parenting → youth disclosure), the monitoring effect was detrimental in both papers (Figure 2). 95 out of 173 paths from parental monitoring to the mediator (a path) were statistically significant at p<.05 (55%) and 119 out of 173 paths from the mediator to the outcome (b path) were significant (69%).

We could find or calculate the standardized mediated effect (ab) for 84 of the 173 tests. ab ranged from -0.15 to +0.12 with a median value of -0.02 (IQR = [-0.05, +0.01]). We could find or calculate the percent of the total effect that was mediated for 42 of the 173 tests. The percent mediated ranged from 0.5% to 86%, with a median value of 24% (IQR = [10, 40]).

Findings by mediator.

Mediation was tested for 37 unique mediators, which we grouped into 9 categories (Table 2). The most common categories of mediators were measures of youth personality / cognitions (39 tests), youth communication with parent (38 tests), and parent-youth relationship quality (28 tests). The pattern of results differed across categories of mediators (Table 2). Among categories with at least 20 tests, the rate of statistical significance ranged from as low as 32% (parent-youth relationship quality) to as high as 65% (youth personality/cognitions). Both parental knowledge and youth personality / cognitions had ≥10 significant tests, almost all of which indicated beneficial effects (≥96%). On the other hand, youth communication with parent had 14 significant tests, but only 57% were beneficial effects (the other 43% were detrimental), and parent-youth relationship quality had 9 significant tests, but only 22% were beneficial (the other 78% were detrimental). The standardized median effect size was considerably larger for parental knowledge (β= -0.04) and youth personality / cognitions (β= -0.04) than for youth communication with parent (β= -0.01) or parent-youth relationship quality (β= +0.01).

Findings by outcome.

Mediation was tested for 25 unique outcomes, which we grouped into 15 categories (Table 2). The most common categories of outcomes were substance use (58 tests), delinquency or antisocial behavior (34 tests), and youth personality / cognitions (18 tests). The results appeared to differ across categories of outcomes, with the rate of statistical significance ranging from only 26% of tests for substance use up to 75% of tests on youth aggression/bullying (Table 2). Nevertheless, many outcomes were tested in so few papers that the results could not be confidently compared to one another. The only outcomes without at least one statistically significant beneficial effect were (a) internalizing behavior (1 of 6 tests significant, indicating a detrimental effect) and (b) parent-youth communication (5 of 8 tests significant, all indicating detrimental effects).

Findings by type of parental monitoring.

Mediation was tested for the following categories of measures of monitoring: Type 1 only (46 tests), Type 2 only (72 tests), Type 3 only (2 tests), Type 4 only (6 tests), Types 1 & 2 (43 tests), and something else (4 tests). The pattern of results differed across the type of monitoring (Table 2). Measures that combined Type 1 and Type 2 monitoring were more likely to be statistically significant (63% significant) than measures of only Type 1 (35%) or only Type 2 (38%). Comparison to Types 3 & 4 monitoring was not possible given the small number of tests in each of those categories.

Findings by type of sample.

70 out of 145 tests of mediation performed in typically-developing youth samples were statistically significant (48%). Of these 70 tests, the indirect effect was beneficial for 55 tests and detrimental for 15 tests. 7 out of 23 tests performed in at-risk youth was statistically significant (30%). The indirect effect was beneficial for 2 tests and detrimental for 5 tests. Only 1 out of 4 tests completed on a special education sample were statistically significant -this test was beneficial. Only 1 test of mediation was completed on a sample of youth with a physical health condition (diabetes) -this test was statistically significant and the indirect effect was beneficial.

Findings by youth age. Samples included youth in late childhood (ages 8-10) (55 tests), early adolescence (ages 11-13) (127 tests), middle adolescence (ages 14-16) (144 tests), and late adolescence (ages 17-18) (98 tests). Most papers sampled across multiple age groups, so comparing results based on age was not possible.

Findings in cross-sectional vs. longitudinal studies. 59 out of 119 tests of mediation performed in cross-sectional data yielded statistically significant results (50%). Of the 59 statistically significant tests in cross-sectional data, the indirect effect of monitoring was beneficial in 43 tests and detrimental in 16 tests (73% beneficial). 20 out of 54 tests of mediation performed in longitudinal data were statistically significant (37%). Of the 20 statistically significant tests in longitudinal data, the indirect effect was beneficial in 16 tests and detrimental in 4 tests (80% beneficial).

Direct effects (c').

The direct effect of monitoring → adjustment was statistically significant (p<.05) in 43 of the 173 tests (25%). The direct effect was beneficial in 30 significant tests and detrimental in 13 significant tests.

The standardized direct effect was reported for 62 of the 173 tests (36%). The standardized direct effect ranged from -0.65 to 0.20, with a median value of 0.07 (mean = 0.02).

Total effects (c).

The total effect of monitoring → adjustment was reported for only 24 out of 173 tests. Of the 18 total effects that reported statistical significance, 14 were significant (78%). 12 significant tests were beneficial and 2 significant tests were detrimental.

The standardized total effect was reported for only 9 of the 173 tests (5%). The standardized total effect ranged from -0.18 to 0.09, with a median of 0.004 (mean = -0.04).

As a proxy for the total effect, we found a zero-order correlation between monitoring and adjustment for 80 of 173 tests. 56 of 80 zero-order correlations were statistically significant (70%). Of the significant correlations, 52 were beneficial and 4 were detrimental. The median zero-order correlation between monitoring and adjustment was -0.13 (range = [-0.33, 0.18]).

Tests of sequential mediation. 6 of 39 articles tested sequential mediation (Fletcher et al., 2004; Kiesner et al., 2010; Yang et al., 2013; Liu et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2024; Saputra et al., 2025). Every sequential chain included 2 mediators. 8 out of 17 tests of sequential mediation were statistically significant (47%), and the sequential indirect effect was beneficial in all 8 significant tests. There was no obvious pattern distinguishing the papers that decided to test sequential mediation vs. test single mediation.

Tests of moderated mediation. 6 of the 39 articles tested moderated mediation. The most commonly tested moderator was adolescent gender, tested in 4 articles. Neither Zhao et al. (2020) nor Fung and Liu (2026) found significant moderation by gender when testing the indirect effect of monitoring on delinquency and aggression. Hawk (2017) found significant moderation of b paths by gender: the association between privacy invasion (M) and youth secrecy (Y) was stronger in girls. Kapetanovic et al. (2020)also found significant moderation of two b paths by gender: the association between parental knowledge (M) and youth substance use (Y) was stronger in girls, but the association between the feeling of being overly controlled (M) and bullying perpetration (Y) was weaker in girls.

The remaining 2 articles each tested a different moderator. Wang et al. (2019) found the indirect effect of monitoring (X) on drinking/smoking via refusal self-efficacy (M) was stronger in teens higher in sensation seeking (p<.05). Fu et al. (2020) found the indirect effect of monitoring (X) on problematic smartphone use (Y) via smartphone use to cope with distress (M) was more detrimental among teens with higher shyness (p<.05). Stattin and Kerr (2000), this paper is the first review of the empirical evidence on mediators of the link between parental monitoring and youth adjustment. We found 39 such studies with a combined N of 48,709. Despite a large number of tests of mediation (k = 173), the studies had serious methodological limitations and the resulting body of evidence remains equivocal. Ultimately, the pattern of findings shown in Table 2 can be reconciled with many (if not all) major theories about how parental monitoring works.

Discussion

Discussion years after

The existing evidence is not comprehensive

The most striking finding of this review is that the field does not have rigorous, replicated evidence for any particular mediating mechanism and target outcome. There are over 1,640 articles in PubMed that mention parental monitoring in the title or abstract. 3 Yet we found only 39 articles that have tested a mediator of the effect of parental monitoring on youth adjustment, 2 of which were unpublished theses. Only 9 articles have tested mediation in a longitudinal sample and 0 articles have tested mediation in a clinical sample. Although 71 different combinations of mediator and outcome have been tested, only 6 have been replicated in more than one paper. Figure 2 tentatively summarizes the state of the evidence, depicting every mediational chain that has been statistically significant in at least 2 papers.

Parental knowledge was the most commonly tested mediator, comprising 23 of the 173 tests (coming from 6 papers). Yet even for parental knowledge, the evidence was equivocal. Only 10 of the 23 tests were statistically significant; only 2 of the 23 ensured X < M < Y, both coming from the same paper, and both adjusting for just 4 measured covariates.

Some widely assumed channels have never been tested. For example, no study has tested whether parental monitoring increases the probability of being punished for a given rule-breaking behavior and in turn decreases the rate of that behavior (operant conditioning).

Other widely assumed channels have been tested repeatedly, but with results that do not offer clear support for them. For example, there were 10 tests of peer affiliation as the mediator: 5 tests that were not statistically significant, 3 tests showing significant beneficial effects via increased prosocial peers, and 2 tests showing a significant detrimental effect via increased deviant peers. This package of results is hardly convincing evidence for peer affiliation as a mediator.

The bulk of the evidence pertained to mediators of externalizing outcomes such as delinquency, substance use, and aggression (~70% of all tests). There were very few tests of mediation with outcomes like internalizing problems, academic functioning, victimization, or sleep. The mediating mechanisms might be quite different when the target outcome is not a rule-breaking behavior that parents prohibit, but the literature to date cannot probe this possibility.

The existing evidence is not rigorous

The evidence we did find suffered from two major limitations on rigor. The first is the lack of clear temporal precedence. 69% of tests were in cross-sectional data, and only 5 of 39 papers (13%) used three waves of data to ensure X preceded M and M preceded Y. Tests of mediation in cross-sectional data can be highly misleading (Maxwell & Cole, 2007) and cannot rule out reverse causality -that Y or M causes X, or M causes X. Reverse causality is quite plausible in the context of the variables of interest here -monitoring, adjustment, and the putative mediating mechanisms (e.g., peers, parent-youth relationships) -making the heavy reliance on cross-sectional associations more concerning.

The second major limitation on rigor is that all 39 studies were non-experimental (i.e., uncontrolled). Non-experimental tests of mediation assume there is no unmeasured confounding of three different associations: X→M, M→Y, and X→Y (Valente et al., 2017). That is not a plausible assumption in the case of monitoring; prior work has confirmed families with different levels of monitoring also differ on dozens of characteristics that could explain differences in mediators or outcomes (Davis et al., 2024). The 39 included studies tended to control for just a few covariates -most commonly sociodemographic characteristics, rather than carefully selected and justified potential confounders. Thus, in every instance where a mediated effect was statistically significant, confounding by omitted variables remains a plausible alternative explanation for the observed associations. Indeed, the only paper that did make a substantial effort to address confounding yielded a concerning pattern of results. Janssen et al. (2016) used a fixed-effects strategy in two timepoints of data to account for time-invariant confounders when relating monitoring to delinquency. The within-person estimates of the mediated effect were 76-100% smaller than the between-person estimates of the mediated effect, and half as many were statistically significant. This pattern suggests there may be a large amount of time-invariant confounding, which may have distorted estimates in the other papers included in this review.

The existing evidence suggests effect sizes are small and multiple mediators operate in concert

Given the aforementioned methodological issues, all effect sizes should be taken with a major grain of salt. That being said, standardized indirect effect sizes were typically small, with a median value of -0.017. In other words, the median estimate was that a 1 SD change in monitoring would be expected to produce a 0.017 SD improvement in youth adjustment via the mediator. While there are no widely accepted benchmarks for gauging the size of the standardized indirect effect, -0.017 is smaller than those labeled "small" in prior simulation studies (e.g., Fritz & MacKinnon, 2007).

The median estimate of percent mediated by the mediator was 24%. One crude way to contextualize this effect is to notice that at 24% mediated per mediator, at least 4 completely non-overlapping mediating mechanisms would be needed to explain the full association between parental monitoring and youth adjustment. However, estimates of percent mediated are notoriously unstable and could be calculated only for a minority of tests.

No tests of monitoring → disclosure → adjustment in longitudinal data Stattin and Kerr (2000) conceptualized youth disclosure as a competitor to parental monitoring, not a mediator. But they did not have data that could empirically distinguish these possibilities. It turns out that neither does the published literature: we found only one study that has tested whether disclosure mediates the association between monitoring and adjustment (Fung & Liu, 2026), and like Stattin and Kerr (2000), the authors used cross-sectional data.

Thus, the competitor and mediator models (Figure 1) today remain equally valid interpretations of Stattin and Kerr's classic findings. This is concerning because most scientists working on the topic assume the competitor model is correct, and it guides their design of studies and interpretation of data. The mediator model has radically different implications not just for what Stattin and Kerr's (2000) findings actually mean, but also for how future studies should be conducted.

Our own view is that disclosure is almost certainly both a competitor and mediator of parental monitoring. This view would argue we need more careful measurement and methodology, and that the goal should be figuring out when and to what extent disclosure plays each role. Such work would help not only figure out the mechanistic role of disclosure in monitoring effects, but the relative and interactive importance of monitoring and disclosure in promoting healthy adjustment.

No tests across most timescales

The 13 papers that tested mediation in longitudinal data used waves spaced 6 to 60 months apart (most commonly, 12 months apart). The absence of tests across other timescales is thus an important weakness of the evidence (Pelham et al., 2023). No study tested mediation in monthly or weekly data, to look at mediators of shorter-term effects. No study tested mediation in daily diary or ecological momentary assessment data to examine acute effects. Only 1of the 39 studies included any justification for their choice of timescale (LaFreniere et al., 2022), suggesting a lack of attention to the issue rather than a careful theoretical choice.

Few tests of moderators

Nearly all tests of mediation were of the average effect of monitoring across an entire sample. We found only 6 papers that had tested moderated mediation -mostly by teen genderand no moderated mediation finding that has been replicated in multiple samples. The near-exclusive focus on average mediated effects is concerning because there is good reason to believe that the mechanisms by which monitoring works could differ across families and contexts (Laird et al., 2018; Pelham et al., 2025). For example, perhaps relationship quality is an important mechanism for girls but not boys, or peer affiliation is an important mechanism for older youth but not younger youth. Such possibilities remain almost entirely uninvestigated.

Many tests in which the "mediator" is actually a measure of youth adjustment 15% of the tests (26 of 173) tested a "mediator" that was itself a measure of youth adjustment. For example, Kiesner et al. (2010) tested whether using substances with friends mediated the association between parental monitoring and individual substance use. Testing adjustment as a mediator of the effect of monitoring → adjustment has limited mechanistic value because it simply relocates the explanatory question: if monitoring affects adjustment through another form of adjustment, the mechanism linking monitoring to that intermediate form of adjustment remains unexplained. Tests of adjustment as a mediator also had one of the highest rates of statistical significance (12 of 26 tests) among the categories of mediators -probably reflecting b paths that reflect construct continuity rather than causation per se.

Differences by type of parental monitoring?

The evidence did not support any firm conclusions about how mediators differ across types of parental monitoring behaviors. Effect sizes were similar in studies that measured asking a person for information (Type 1 monitoring, or "solicitation") versus setting or enforcing rules to gather information (Type 2 monitoring, or "control"). Just 1 paper each had tested mediation of Type 3 or Type 4 monitoring. Studies using measures that combined Types 1 and 2 monitoring yielded somewhat stronger evidence of mediation, but the choice of monitoring measure was confounded with particular outcomes and samples, rendering the difference hard to interpret.

Persistent problem of mislabeled measures Stattin and Kerr (2000) first brought attention to the issue of mislabeled measures in the study of parental monitoring. They clearly succeeded in raising awareness: before 2000, we could find only 1 article meeting inclusion criteria that had truly measured parental monitoring, compared to 38 articles published after 2000. At the same time, our findings suggest the mislabeling problem has persisted. We excluded 67 articles on the basis of a mislabeled measure of monitoring; we found many articles with "contaminated" measure of parental monitoring (e.g., 1-2 items measuring knowledge on an 8-item scale); and, since most articles do not report the full list of items on each scale, it often required contacting authors to even determine if a given paper had really measured parental monitoring. Thus, the measurement aspect of Stattin and Kerr's critique remains incisive and relevant in 2026.

Is there any effect of monitoring → adjustment to mediate? Stattin and Kerr (2000) were also first to question whether there is any effect of monitoring on adjustment to mediate. The results of our review suggest there is at least an association to mediate: the median zero-order correlation between monitoring and adjustment was r = -0.13, the majority of correlations were significant (70%), and nearly all significant correlations indicated beneficial effects. However, the same issues that reduce the rigor of the mediational analyses (e.g., exclusive use of non-experimental data) also hamper interpretation of these zero-order correlations. Thus, the size of the total effect of monitoring is another aspect of Stattin and Kerr's critique that remains more of an open question today than one would hope.

Recommendations for future work

Today, the field lacks the breadth and rigor of tests that would be needed to evaluate the mechanisms by which parental monitoring affects youth adjustment. 25 years after Stattin and Kerr first raised questions about whether and how monitoring works, we are still not in a good position to answer those questions. We offer the following recommendations for future research in this area.

Be careful with definitions and labels.

When planning a study, make sure your "monitoring" measure really measures monitoring. When performing secondary data analysis with a suboptimal measure, be transparent about the measure's item content and qualify your interpretation of results as needed. Given the field's fraught history, always consider listing the items on the monitoring measure verbatim in an appendix.

Focus on testing mechanistic mediators.

The goal is to figure out how monitoring affects adjustment -if the putative mediator is itself a form of adjustment, we've dodged the question rather than answered it.

Take confounding more seriously. In non-experimental designs, consider measuring and adjusting for earlier measurements of X, M, and Y (Cole & Maxwell, 2003) as well as other potential confounders (Davis et al., 2024). Collecting ≥3 waves of data to establish temporal precedence would help, as would performing sensitivity analyses for unmeasured confounding (Liu et al., 2013). Consider also experimental designs in which parental monitoring and/or the putative mediator are manipulated - Valente et al. (2017) and Bullock et al. (2010) describe some options.

Recognize that mechanisms may differ across monitoring behaviors, outcomes, timescales, families, or contexts. Avoid one-size-fits-all thinking and prematurely generalizing early findings. As more tests accumulate, finer testing for differences will become possible and may reveal the mechanisms by which monitoring affects adjustment vary along several characteristics.

Report the test(s) of mediation thoroughly.

Only 10% of papers reported the coefficient and standard error for every estimate of a, b, and c' -this proved a big obstacle to meta-analysis. Standardized effect sizes were reported in only 26% of papers. As the field performs more tests, authors could follow a mediation reporting checklist (e.g., Lee et al., 2021) to ensure the accumulated evidence will be amenable to meta-analytic synthesis.

Limitations

We have already discussed several important limitations of the underlying literature (e.g., cross-sectional samples, lack of attention to confounding) and how they seriously qualify the interpretation of results. We close with two important limitations of our review. The first is that we only reviewed studies that tested a statistical mediation model. Thus, we may be missing mechanistic evidence coming from other designs (e.g., experiments, qualitative interviews) or from studies that estimated just a, or just b and c'.

The second limitation is that we focused narrowly on mediators of a single, unidirectional path: that from parental monitoring to youth adjustment. The broader parent-youth monitoring process involves many other links of interest (Pelham et al., 2024) and this study could not evaluate the extent to which the mechanistic evidence for monitoring→adjustment is stronger or weaker than for the links between other constructs. A major goal for the next 25 years after Stattin and Kerr will be to gain a deeper mechanistic understanding of how the various pieces of the information management puzzle fit together.
