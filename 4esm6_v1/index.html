Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 3 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 128 papers with 246 instances of power statements, 203 were correctly detected (true positives), 22 were missed (false negatives) and 21 were incorrectly detected (false positives). Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

In five experiments, we investigated how people's metacognitive assessments influence their decisions about whether to rely on their own answers to questions or to obtain the answers from an external source (i.e., to offload). Participants provided answers to general information questions, rated their confidence, and then decided whether to stick with their answer or be given the correct answer by the computer. To examine how metacognitive accuracy impacts offloading, experiment 2-5 included condition which produced good resolution (using easy and difficult questions), and condition that produced poor resolution (using questions people typically think they know the answers to, but don't). The value of offloading was also varied across experiments via the assignment of differing numbers of "points" for sticking or offloading. In all experiments, participants were told to maximize the number of points earned, allowing us to investigate whether people optimize offloading decisions. Across all five experiments, participants' confidence in their answers predicted whether they would offload or not: when confidence was high, they did not offload; when it was low, they offloaded. They did this even when their metacognition was faulty. Further, participants were relatively insensitive to the value of the options, even in a condition in which the rational choice to maximize points was to offload everything and ignore metacognition entirely. These findings indicate that, even when metacognitive evaluations are either wrong or irrelevant (rather than being correct and germane), people's offloading choices are strongly influenced by their metacognition.

Introduction

Cognitive offloading is the process of delegating to outside resources, tools, or people, assistance in accomplishing a cognitive task. This article investigates the role of metacognition in people's decisions to offload. Offloading in some situations can be beneficial. People may sometimes use it to overcome their own shortcomings and still achieve a valuable outcome. Its use is sometimes assumed to imply that people have an intention to either reduce their own mental load and save mental energy for more important tasks (Grinschgl & Neubauer, 2022; Risko & Gilbert, 2016), while still achieving the desired goal. But, of course, offloading may not always be beneficial. Indeed, it may sometimes be counterproductive, and undermine human development, cognitive capabilities and wellbeing (Gerlich, 2025; Grinschgl, Papenmeier, & Meyerhoff, 2021; Henkel, 2014; Kelly & Risko, 2019; Marsh & Rajaram, 2019; Sparrow, Lui, & Wegner, 2011). This concern is particularly salient now that answers to questions small and large are readily, easily, and endlessly available through AI (Bastani et al, 2025; Rismanchian et al, 2026; Yu et al., 2026). To have the knowledge needed to promote the proactive, beneficial use of cognitive offloading, an understanding of the factors that affect whether and when people choose to offload to an external source or instead rely on their own knowledge is necessary.

Much prior work has examined prospective memory offloading, in which a future cognitive demand is outsourced. For example, people might set an external reminder to wish a friend happy birthday, or make a grocery list rather than trying to remember what they need to get once they are in the store. In these contexts, offloading may be beneficial and allow people to externalize future, potentially faulty, cognitive processes that would otherwise have to be performed internally, and thus it may increase the chance of a favorable outcome (Berry, Allen, Mon-Williams, & Waterman, 2019; Burnett & Richmond, 2026; Gilbert, 2024; Hertzog & Dunlosky, 2011; Martin et al, 2022; Shetye, 2024; Storm & Stone, 2015). But offloading need not always be prospective, and it does not require that no internal processing has occurred already: a decision to offload can occur even after a person has tried to do the task themselves. In the TV trivia show 'Who Wants to be a Millionaire?', for example, contestants are tasked with coming up with the correct answer to difficult questions. They often try hard to generate an answer on their own, but they may vary in their uncertainty about the result. To achieve their goal, they may decide to stick with their own answer, or they may use one of their 'lifelines,' thereby offloading--asking an external source for the answer.

People's knowledge about their own knowledge --their metacognitive evaluations on an item-by-item level--is likely to be key for understanding what they decide to do. To offload effectively, we propose that people, in most cases, need to be able to accurately identify when their own knowledge (and capabilities, in the prospective case) is sufficient to answer correctly (and see, Boldt & Gilbert, 2019; Gilbert, Bird, Carpenter, Fleming, Sachdeva, & Tsai, 2020; Grinschgl, Meyerhoff, Schwan, & Papenmeier,2021; Hu, Luo, & Fleming, 2019; Ngia & Gilbert, 2026). If they are highly confident that they have sufficient knowledge, they should anticipate no need to offload; if they are not as confident, the felt need should increase. The accuracy of those metacognitive assessments should, of course, alter how effective such as strategy is.

But effective offloading may depend not only on the goodness of metacognition, but also on the costs and benefits of taking the cognitive task upon oneself as compared to taking on external assistance. If there are a limited number of offloading opportunities, or if opportunities are more rather than less costly, offloading decisions should be reserved for when they are most needed. If, on the other hand, there is no cost to offloading or when offloading is more valuable than sticking with doing a task oneself, offloading may be a rational choice, regardless of how confident the person is in their own knowledge and abilities.

Across five experiments, we explored the relation between metacognition and offloading by asking participants to answer a series of general information questions. Participants gave their answers and their confidence judgments about whether they believed their answers to be correct, and then had to decide whether they wanted to stick with their answers or receive the correct answer from the computer (offload). Participants were given different number of points, across experiments, for choosing to stick or to offload. We then provided correct feedback and retested participants on all questions.

Across the five experiments, we used general information questions that showed a wide range of metacognitive judgments to examine the hypothesis that people's offloading decisions closely follow their individual item-specific metacognitive ratings in a systematic manner. The first experiment investigated the simple relation between metacognition and offloading, at the item-by-item level, and did not vary the materials to manipulate people's metacognitive accuracy.

The question was straightforward: Do people choose to substitute the computer's answer for their own answers to general knowledge questions that they think they might not know the answers to, and stick with their own answers when they believe that they know?

In Experiment 2 and the subsequent experiments, metacognitive accuracy was manipulated. In these four experiments, we used two types of questions. In the 'good metacognition' condition, the materials afforded excellent metacognition. Questions were either very easy or very difficult, and people's metacognitive judgments were objectively close to perfect: they knew which answers they knew and which they did not know. In the 'poor metacognition' condition, the materials used resulted in inaccurate judgments: People often thought they knew the answers when, in reality, they did not. While we still expected that their individual offloading decisions would follow their metacognitive judgments, we also expected that in the condition in which those judgments were highly accurate, their offloading would be close to 'optimal', that is, participants would make decisions about whether or not to offload that would come close to maximizing attainable points (see Murphy & Metcalfe, 2025). In contrast, in the condition in which those judgments were inaccurate, we predicted that their offloading optimality would suffer.

In Experiments 1 and 2, participants received 10 points for sticking if they were correct, and 0 points if they were wrong. In both experiments, they received 5 points if they chose to offload, regardless of whether the answer they had given was right or wrong. In Experiment 3 and 4, we increased and decreased value of the points earned for offloading or sticking to explore whether the cost of offloading influenced how people used their metacognitive judgments. In other words, did they adjust their metacognitive criterion for offloading as a function of how many points they would get for offloading? And did alterations in the magnitude of point values influence offloading optimality? In both experiments, they received 10 points if they stuck with their answer and were correct. Experiment 3 participants received 8 points for offloading, whereas in Experiment 4 they received only 2 points for offloading. Finally, in the fifth experiment, the assignment of offloading points was taken to a logical extreme: this time, if they offloaded, they got 10 points. Thus, rationally, to maximize points, participants should have disregarded their metacognition entirely: even if they were 99.99999% sure they were right, they should always chose the sure payout of 10 points, just in case they were wrong. Experiment 1

In Experiment 1, we sought to examine the relation between metacognitive judgments and offloading decisions in a straightforward manner. Participants were asked to give their answers to a series of general information questions, to give their confidence judgments about whether they believed their answers to be correct, and then to decide whether they wanted to stick with their answers or to offload to the computer. If they stuck they would get 10 points if correct, but zero points if wrong. If they offloaded, they would get 5 points for that question whether they were right or wrong. We hypothesized that when people were above some confidence criterion related to the relative payoff for sticking and offloading, they would stick with their answer, whereas when they were below that criterion, they would offload. We expected that the particular payoff structure in this first experiment would probably result in people choosing to stick with their answer if their confidence was above 50%, and offload if their confidence was below 50%.

Method

Participants. Forty participants were recruited via Prolific.co. Participants in all experiments were required to be located in the US, be native English speakers, and be using a desktop computer. Participants in all experiments gave consent before participating and were paid at a rate of $10/hour. All experiments took approximately 20 minutes to complete, and no bonus payments were given for performance. The procedures were approved by the Columbia University Institutional Review Board protocol ACYY0064. In Experiment 1 we recruited 40 participants. Four participants timed out before finishing the study, leaving a total of 36 adults (23 female, 13 male), ranging in age from 18 to 30 years (x̄ = 24.8, SD = 3.4) with x̄ = 14.8 (SD = 2.5) years of education in the current analysis. Participants identified as White (72.2%), Asian (16.7%), or Black or African American (11.1%), and 16.7% identified as Hispanic or Latino.

Design and Procedure.

The Gorilla Experiment Builder (www.gorilla.sc) was used in this and all subsequent experiments to create and host the experiment. Participants in this experiment were presented with 50 general information questions, each of which had a unique correct answer that was a noun, one at a time in a random order. Participants had up to 15 seconds to answer each question. They then provided a confidence rating indicating their feeling that their answer was correct on a scale from 0 ("sure wrong") to 100 ("sure right"). Finally, they choose whether they wanted to stick with their own answer or offload to the computer for the answer. They were told, before the task began, both about the point structure, and that their job was to maximize the number of points they obtained. After giving their confidence ratings and offloading decisions to all questions, they were shown the correct answers to all questions in a random order for 3 seconds each, and then they were given a final recall test in which questions were presented in a random order and participants had up to 15 seconds to respond to each.

Materials.

The questions derived from Funnell, Metcalfe, and Tsapkini (1996), and all answers to these questions were uniquely specified nouns. For instance, two questions were: "The largest bird on earth that cannot fly is the _______." and "The type of snake that Asia snake charmers' charm is the ____________."

We used this set of questions because it has several properties that make it well-suited to studying metacognition and offloading. First, the control participants in the Funnell et al. experiment got about half (i.e., 43%) correct. Thus, the set is neither so easy that everyone already knows the answers (which would leave no room for offloading to matter) nor so hard that no one had any relevant knowledge to draw on (which would leave no room for metacognitive judgments to be informative). Second, the distributions of metacognitive judgments on this set were fairly evenly distributed across the range of ratings (see Figure 3 in Funnell et al., 1996), indicating that people genuinely use the whole range of confidence when judging these items. Finally, the gamma correlation between their metacognitive judgments and the correctness of their immediate performance was near .4 -positive, but neither at ceiling nor floor. This baseline level thus leaves room to detect both increases and decreases in metacognitive sensitivity as a function of our experimental manipulations. The materials are provided in Appendix A.

MOOT scores.

To assess the optimality of offloading, it is insufficient to simply count the number of points attained, since the objective correctness of responding impacts this score: participants with more correct responses have more opportunities to do well and have higher scores than do those with fewer correct responses, since those correct scores are necessary for obtaining 10s--the value given to correct-stick responses. Experiment 2

In the second experiment, we investigated the consequences of good and poor metacognition on offloading behavior, which we manipulated using two sets of intermixed materials. The first set included both Easy and Difficult questions. Data from past research using these questions showed that people have very good metacognitive resolution for these questions.

An example of an easy question is: What do you call a female deer? Nearly everyone gets the correct answer, and nearly everyone is highly confident that they got it right. An example of a difficult question is: What is the last name of the current ruling dynasty of Monaco? Most people get this wrong --usually by omission--but they also know that they are wrong. Thus, metacognitive resolution on the set of Easy/Difficult items is high. The other set of questions included items which frequently evoke an illusion of knowing. The questions in this set are items on which people often think they know the answer --and express high confidence in their answers--but don't. We call this set of items the "ThoughtYouKnew" condition.

The kind of materials in the ThoughtYouKnew condition are of considerable interest and have been studied by many theorists. An example is "What country was the new Volkswagen Beetle produced in?" Most people get this wrong, but yet think, often with high confidence, that they know the answer.

There is an answer comes to mind easily and fluently but yet is wrong.

These types of questions are frequently associated with high intersubject consensuality on a particular incorrect answer. North American participants often are highly confident that the new Volkswagen Beetle was produced in Germany, that the capital of Australia is Sydney, that the longest river in the United States is the Mississippi, that the eastern-most state is Maine, and that bats navigate over long distances by echolocation. Similar materials, on which people are consensually wrong, have been extensively studied by Koriat. Although he sometimes used line drawings and figures with these characteristics (Koriat, 2011), we here used general information questions. The otherwise paradoxical results his group has reported with such materials (Koriat, 2017; Koriat & Adiv, 2016) --that increased confidence about being right is related to a higher probability of being wrong--constitute strong evidence for his self-consistency theory of the inferential basis of metacognition (Koriat, 2008 (Koriat, , 2011)). Brewer and Sampaio (2012, and see Sampaio & Brewer, 2009) have also conducted important research focusing on such items, which they call 'deceptive' items. We here, chose to refer to these items as ThoughtYouKnew, to avoid any insinuation that these items contain any nefarious intent to make people believe something that is false (which would likewise be the case if we referred to them as 'Misconceptions'). Instead, we call them ThoughtYouKnew, simply because people tend, fairly consistently, to get the answers wrong while thinking that they are right, which is, by definition, poor metacognition--they don't know that they don't know.

The fact that these tend to be items on which people frequently make high confidence errors is also of interest because (a) people are especially curious about such items (Metcalfe, Vuorre, Towner, & Eich, 2023) (b) people often agree in their spurious judgments that the wrong answers are correct, that is, there is consensus concerning these items (Brewer & Sampaio, 2012) (c) they strongly contribute to pervasive and deleterious overconfidence effects, (d) once they are told the correct answer, both adults and children often say that they knew it all along (Finn & Metcalfe, 2007 , 2008 , Metcalfe & Finn, 2011), (e) they often have considerable amount of information surrounding such items; that is such items come from dense semantic landscapes (Sitzman, Rhodes, Tauber, & Liceralde, 2015; Eich et al, 2026), and (f) rather than doubling down on the wrong answer, once people are provided with the correct answer, they have a tendency to hypercorrect these items, that is to remember the correct answer when it is provided (Butterfield & Metcalfe, 2006, though note there is some regression toward the original, erroneous response over time, Butler, Fazio, & Marsh, 2011).

Despite their interest, such high confidence errors (or items that people 'thought they knew' but didn't) tend to be relatively rare.

Accordingly, we built a larger-than-usual pool of such items for use here (see Appendix A).

To investigate the relation between metacognition and offloading, we here contrasted people's offloading behavior on the set of items on which their metacognition was good (the Easy/Difficult condition) with their offloading behavior on the set on which their metacognition was poor (the ThoughtYouKnew condition). Our hypothesis was straightforward: When the influence of other factors--factors that we acknowledge could often play an important role in real-world offloading such as feelings of curiosity or boredom, stress, severe time constraints, and so on--was minimized, offloading should be more optimal when metacognition was good than poor. We tried to eliminate other factors by using a game framing, as in Experiment 1, where participants tried to get as many points as possible by either sticking with their own answer or offloading to the computer to get the answer right and attain points. And we used the same point allocation as in Experiment 1.

We expected, then, that people would produce higher gamma scores indicating better metacognitive resolution in the Easy/Difficult condition than in the ThoughtYouKnew condition.

If offloading was related to metacognition, we hypothesized that offloading optimality would also be higher in the Easy/Difficult condition than in the ThoughtYouKnew condition.

Discussion

The manipulation of metacognitive accuracy produced the expected differences in both metacognitive accuracy and offloading optimality. When metacognition was excellent, as was the case in the Easy/Difficult condition, people offloaded close to optimally; when it was mediocre, as was the case in the ThoughtYouKnew condition, the optimality of their offloading suffered. This relation provides clear evidence for the strong connection between metacognition and offloading.

Experiments 3 and 4

If offloading is titrated to the payoff structure of the situation, it should be based not only on people's metacognition, but also on the relative point value assigned to sticking as compared to offloading. In the first two experiments, the confidence scale was divided at the midpoint, and sticking was worth 10 points if correct and 0 points if wrong, whereas offloading was worth 5 points regardless. In both experiments, the inflection point was also at the midpoint. That is, because the points awarded for offloading were set at exactly half the points awarded for a correct stick response, the confidence level at which the expected value of sticking equals the guaranteed value of offloading is 50% -the midpoint of the confidence scale. Thus, a participant should stick whenever their confidence exceeds 50% (since expected value of sticking then exceeds the guaranteed offload payoff) and offload whenever it falls below 50%.

But, if the values changed, so too should the inflection point. If offloaded items were worth almost as much as items on which people gave the answers themselves, they might be inclined to offload more. Conversely, if offloaded items were worth very little, they might be inclined to offload only infrequently.

There is some experimental support for the hypothesis that people's offloading behavior is sensitive to the value of the offloaded items. For instance, Murphy (2023) created a prospective memory situation in which items could be offloaded to an external memory store rather than remembered at a later time. When he indicated to participants that the external store might be unreliable or limited, people offloaded to it less frequently than they did when they were told it was reliable and unlimited. It was as if they had devalued the offloading option, and altered their behavior accordingly. Similarly, when Murphy and Metcalfe (2025), again in a prospective memory paradigm, varied the point values given for later remembering items oneself as compared to offloading them to be remembered by the computer, people's choices to offload were affected. When points assigned for offloaded items were high, people offloaded more than when the points given for offloaded items were low. This prospective memory task was different from the present task, since the metacognitive judgments as well as the goal of the task, were directed at the probability of future (not present) correct responding. Additionally, people may have taken into account the effort that might be needed (and perhaps how valuable that cognitive effort itself was in relation to the point payoff) to make that later correct responding possible.

Nevertheless, these studies suggested that people were likely to be sensitive to the value of the offloaded items, and might alter their metacognitive threshold for offloading depending upon that value.

To investigate the hypothesis that, in this paradigm, too, people would be sensitive to the value of the offloaded items, Experiments 3 and 4 investigated the effect of changing those values in opposite ways.

Experiment 3 made offloading more valuable (worth 8 points), while Experiment 4 made it less valuable (worth 2 points) than the midpoint value (5 points) investigated in Experiments 1 and 2. If people were sensitive to the value of sticking versus offloading, then this change in point value assigned to 'offloaded' as compared to 'stick' items should produce a shift in the inflection point of the metacognitive function relating confidence to offloading. They should offload at a lower confidence level when offloaded items were worth more and offload less --at a higher confidence threshold--when they were worth less.

The third experiment, then, was like the second, except that the point values in the third experiment gave a much greater weight to offloading, whilst still prioritizing sticking if one's certainty that one was right was very high. In this experiment, instead of allocating 5 points for offloading, 8 points were allocated. We expected that people would offload more in this experiment than in the previous one, and that the confidence inflection point at which they chose to offload would be lower.

Discussion of Experiments 3 and 4

In both experiment 3 and 4, just as in Experiment 2, people were highly sensitive to their metacognitions. They apparently used their confidence ratings to determine whether or not to offload. Furthermore, when their metacognition was highly accurate, the optimality of their offloading decisions was high whereas when their metacognitive accuracy was low--in the ThoughtYouKnew condition--their offloading optimality was low. It can be concluded from these findings that people were using their metacognition as the basis for their offloading decisions.

But, surprisingly, they appeared not to take the value of the offloaded items into account.

As is shown in Figure 5, there was no shift in the offloading threshold--the inflection point in people's metacognitive that marked the point at which they shifted from sticking to offloading-between any of the three experiments. They shifted at around a confidence rating of .5 whether offloading was valued at 2 points per offloaded item, 5 points per offloaded item, or 8 points per offloaded item. This failure to respond to the differential value of the offloaded items was a surprise to us. Accordingly, we conducted some additional analyses to investigate why this might have happened.

Why did people fail to respond to changes in the value of the offloaded items?

One possibility for why people may not have altered the metacognitive threshold for offloading in these three experiments, is that the metacognitive judgments themselves may have been extremely bimodal. People may not have based their judgments on intermediate analogue values of their confidence that took nuances about their degree of confidence that they were right into account, if those intermediate values were unavailable. Perhaps they had only made a crude assessment of whether they thought they were right or not. Such a binary assessment is still metacognition, but it would not reflect the nuances of a graded judgment that could support a change in the inflection point along a confidence continuum. It was possible, for example, that people only used two points on our continuous confidence measure--100% sure they were right and 0% sure they were wrong. If the confidence data were extremely bimodal, people might have changed their offload/stick criterion based on value, but it would not have mattered--we would not be able to discern it in the results.

To illustrate with an example, suppose all of people's responses were either those of which the were very sure of and gave a high confidence rating to of, let's say, over 80% or they were responses that they knew were wrong, to which they gave a confidence rating of less than 20%. For sake of argument, suppose that half of their responses were .8 or above and the other half were 20% and below, and there were no rating between 20% and 80% confidence. And, suppose they used a general strategy of sticking with high confidence items, and offloading low confidence items (as we know from the data they did, see Figure 1). In Experiment 2 we would expect them to set the offloading criterion at 50% confidence, in Experiment 3 at 20% confidence and in Experiment 4 at 80% confidence. Would the expected changes in the criterion make any difference in the observable data? The answer is no. In all three experiments, even with those very different offloading criteria, they would end up offloading all of the low confidence items and sticking with all of the high confidence items (and it would look like the inflection point was 50% in all three cases even though people might have actually changed their criterion according to the difference in value in the three experiments). There would be no difference in the observable criterion since there would be no items which had confidence ratings between 20% and 80% which would allow us to differentiate between the three different hypothetical criterion settings.

We had designed the 'good metacognition' (Easy/Difficult) condition to allow people to know when they were right and when they were wrong: they should have shown very high confidence on the easy items and very low confidence on the difficult items (as, indeed, they did as shown in Figure 2). And there should have been few items of intermediate confidence. By the nature of the materials in the design for the Easy/Difficult conditions, then, we would expect such bi-model confidence distributions that would fail to afford observation of a criterion shift.

The faulty metacognition condition was different. While acknowledging that it was possible, in principle, that extreme bimodality could have occurred in the ThoughtYouKnew condition, it was nevertheless the case that we had endeavored to include items that would elicit intermediate confidence judgments. We had tried to include items that were high confidence errors usually or that were so-called 'deceptive' items. We expected that people would probably have partial information in that condition (e.g., they would name a Canadian city as the capital of Canada, not an American city), and that such partial information would lend itself to more intermediate confidence ratings. If so, then, although the Easy/Difficult condition perhaps could not, the ThoughtYouKnew condition could, potentially at least, allow observation of a metacognitively based criterion shift in offloading attributable to the different value of the offloaded items in Experiment 2, 3, and 4.

The fifth experiment looked at whether there was any value manipulation that would induce participants to take value into account. This final experiment took the value manipulation to its logical extreme--an extreme at which participants could and should have optimized their scores by simply ignoring their metacognition entirely and offloading everything. General Discussion

This set of experiments establishes that people's metacognition about whether they think they know or do not know the answers is a crucial factor in determining their offloading behavior. If they think they know the answer to the question, they stick with their own answer. If they think that they don't know the answer, they offload. This item-level effect was replicated across all five experiments.

But, even though people stick with their answer when they are confident that they know and offload when they judge that they don't know, those metacognitive judgments are not, themselves, always accurate. Experiments 2 through 5 investigated offloading optimality under conditions associated with good versus poor metacognitive resolution. These experiments included a set of items on which people had good metacognitive resolution--they knew what they knew and didn't know--and a set on which they had poor metacognitive resolution--they thought they knew many of the answers that, objectively, they did not know. In these experiments, when people's metacognitive resolution was excellent, their offloading was close to optimal. When their metacognitive resolution was poor, on the other hand, their offloading suffered. Here, optimality refers specifically and only to stated payoff optimality, that is, whether decisions about offloading maximized the attainable payoff within the task. Its use, here, does not imply anything about the effects of offloading on people's longer-term cognitive abilities, their learning, subsequent independent performance, or enhanced human welfare.

Experiments 2, 3, and 4 also investigated whether people were sensitive to the value of the offloaded items. Past research on value-directed remembering and cognitive offloading has demonstrated that individuals are often sensitive to payoff structures and can adjust their reliance on external memory stores to maximize objective rewards (e.g., Knowlton & Castel, 2022; Murphy, 2023; Murphy & Castel, 2023; Park, Kelly, Hargis, & Risko, 2022). For instance, as noted earlier, when told that the device onto which one is offloading may be limited, individuals selectively offload, taking that unreliability into account (Murphy, 2023). In prospective memory situations, they are also, often, sensitive to the value of items they offload (Murphy & Metcalfe, 2025). The present results, though, showed little sensitivity to the value assigned to offloaded items. We had hypothesized that if we made offloaded items more valuable, people would have been more inclined to offload them and maximize their score. Conversely, if offloaded items were worth less, people should have shifted their criterion to be willing to stick with items about which they had less confidence. Experiments 3, and 4, however, failed to show this pattern of a shift in the confidence criterion for offloading as a function of the assigned value of the offloaded items. One possibility for why there was no observable change in offloading criterion was that the metacognition itself might have been strongly bimodal. If there were no intermediate assessments of partial knowing available to allow titration of the exact intermediate degree of confidence, people might be unable to be responsive to changes in value. This was the case in the Easy/difficult condition in Experiments 2-4, but it was not the case in the ThoughtYouKnew condition. However, both conditions equally failed to show the expected change in inflection point with changes in value that would have indicted that subtle metacognitively-dependent choice criterion changes that reflected the changes in values.

It is possible that in the condition in which people made a large number of intermediate confidence judgments (that potentially could have shown an effect of shifts in the value of offloading) that people used only a crude summary judgment of know/don't know in order to make their offloading decisions. The failure to show a sensitivity to the value manipulation might have occurred at the decision level not the metacognitive level. The lack of betweencondition differences in the inflection points, in combination with a notable difference in the amount of information at intermediate levels of confidence in the two conditions, suggests that although people had intermediate metacognitive information available that could have been used in response to changes in value, selectively, in the ThoughtYouKnew condition, they did not use that intermediate information in their offloading decisions.

The metacognition was subtle but the decisions were crude.

Importantly, though, in Experiment 5, when we increased the value of offloading items to an extreme extent, we were able to induce a shift in the offloading criterion. That criterion went from approximately 50% confidence in Experiments 2-4 to 70% in Experiment 5.

Interestingly, though, even that shift was not enough to be considered fully rational. In order to optimize reward, people, in Experiment 5, should have completely disregarded their metacognitive judgements, and offloaded 100% of the time. Since people were sure of getting 10 points if they offloaded, and yet there was uncertainty about getting 10 points if they stuck with any of their own answers, people should have always taken the sure path. But only 4 participants did this. The remainder of the participants continued, irrationally, to use a metacognitive strategy that was almost indistinguishable from the one that was used in the four other experiments in this series. They still stuck with their answers when they thought they knew and offloaded when they thought they did not know. We speculate that, although this could certainly be viewed as irrational behavior, a less disparaging possibility is that sticking with one's own answers when one thinks one is correct (and offloading only selectively) has some intrinsic value in the real world even in the face of potentially decreased rewards in the context of our experiment. Perhaps this strategy enhances learning, or perhaps it taps a deeply adaptive need for agency. It may be highly beneficial in other contexts, where there is nearly always a tradeoff and one rarely gets things for free. Further research is clearly needed to investigate why and when people engage in what seems, in this case, to objectively be a dysfunctional use of metacognition.

In conclusion, these results indicate that people do base their offloading decisions on their metacognition, and do so in a simple way: When they think they are right, they stick; when they think they are wrong, they offload. When these metacognitive judgments are accurate, this strategy can lead to results that are close to optimal. When the metacognitive judgments are wrong, however, as they frequently were in the ThoughtYouKnew condition of these experiments, this reliance on their metacognition results in suboptimal offloading. Furthermore, the data presented here suggest--though more follow-up research is needed--that the strategy of depending upon a simple binary metacognitive signal may produce an insensitivity to the value of the items. People used this same strategy, even when, as in Experiment 5, it was counterproductive to use a metacognitive strategy at all--they should have just offloaded everything to obtain maximum rewards. But even when using this same metacognitive strategy was disadvantageous, people, nevertheless, persisted in it use.
