What we found

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Full text

Abstract

Recent studies suggest that older adults maintain high levels of state curiosity when presented with epistemic materials, despite age-related declines in personality traits related to curiosity. We hypothesized that this age-related preservation in state curiosity is explained by older adults' rich prior knowledge, given that knowledge acts as a key driver of state curiosity across the adult lifespan. To test this idea, we conducted two experiments that examined older and younger adults' curiosity to learn the answers to questions in low-and high-knowledge domains. Experiment 1 (N = 216) used learning materials characterized by large population-level differences in prior knowledge, but limited variation across age groups. The results showed that prior knowledge substantially affects state curiosity, irrespective of age, while the effect of age on state curiosity was minimal. Experiment 2 (N = 216) explicitly accounted for age differences in knowledge by orthogonalizing the effects of knowledge and age. We found that relative to younger adults, older adults experienced stronger curiosity for high-knowledge domains, whereas in other domains where younger adults were more knowledgeable, older adults experienced weaker curiosity relative to younger adults. Such age-related differences in curiosity were mediated by age-related differences in knowledge. These findings point to the important role of prior knowledge in curiosity across younger and older adults, extending theoretical notions on curiosity. Our results also suggest that previously observed age differences in state curiosity might be partly attributable to age-related differences in knowledge.

Epistemic curiosity refers to one's desire to seek new information and gain knowledge.

People often naively believe that it is primarily important for children and their development (Donnellan et al., 2022). However, there is a growing number of studies demonstrating that epistemic curiosity facilitates older adults' engagement in exploration and cognitively stimulating activities, which help them prevent cognitive and physical decline (for a review, see Sakaki et al., 2018). For example, a preference for novel stimuli was associated with a lower risk of Alzheimer's disease (Fritsch et al., 2005). Likewise, higher levels of curiosity in older adults were predictive of better survival rates (Swan & Carmelli, 1996). Furthermore, curiosity helps older adults to learn new information while protecting against false memories (Swirsky & Spaniol, 2024a). Given the crucial role in adaptive aging, it is important to understand whether and how old age leads to changes in epistemic curiosity.

Previous research shows that as people age, they report lower scores in questionnaires that assess dispositional tendencies to experience curiosity in everyday life (trait curiosity; Chu & Fung, 2022; Robinson et al., 2017; Wiegand et al., 2025). The brain regions crucial for epistemic curiosity also show age-related deterioration (Bunzeck et al., 2007; Schott et al., 2007). These results appear to suggest that older adults lose their ability to experience epistemic curiosity, even when they encounter potentially interesting stimuli. In contrast, experimental research suggests that normal aging does not necessarily impair curiosity (Galli et al., 2018; McGillivray et al., 2015; Sobczak et al., 2025; Swirsky et al., 2021; Yagi et al., 2023). Instead, research even showed that older adults report greater levels of curiosity in knowing the answer to specific epistemic questions (state epistemic curiosity) relative to younger adults in experimental settings (Fastrich et al., 2024; Galli et al., 2018; Ozono et al., 2021; Whatley et al., 2025). Why does epistemic state curiosity, defined as momentary curiosity elicited by learning materials, increase with age despite a decline in trait curiosity?

To address this apparent puzzle, the present study focuses on state epistemic curiosity and proposes the importance of considering prior knowledge to examine age-related enhancement in state epistemic curiosity.

Knowledge is information acquired and accumulated through learning and experience (Murayama, 2022). In line with the concept of crystallized intelligence, it is gained through a long-term accumulation process, and maintained across one's lifespan once it's acquired (Cattell, 1963). Thus, as we age, our knowledge base should increase (Hartshorne & Germine, 2015). One way this has been examined is by asking older and younger adults to answer general knowledge questions, with older adults typically outperforming younger adults (Coane & Umanath, 2021). Similarly, older adults show better performance in vocabulary tasks, such as production tasks (e.g., providing a dictionary-like description for words) and multiple-choice tests (e.g., choosing a synonym or description among alternatives; see Verhaeghen, 2003). Furthermore, previous research showed that older adults rely more on their knowledge when they engage in cognitive processes (Peters et al., 2007; Umanath & Marsh, 2014). For example, relative to younger adults, older adults rely more on prior knowledge when evaluating misinformation (Brashier et al., 2017; Umanath & Marsh, 2012) and use their prior knowledge to compensate for age-related declines in episodic memory (Badham & Maylor, 2015; Castel, 2005; Matzen & Benjamin, 2013).

We argue that the age-related enrichment in general knowledge may lead to enhanced state epistemic curiosity in old age (Donnellan et al., 2022). In fact, several theoretical accounts suggest that one's prior knowledge in a specific domain plays a key role in inducing epistemic curiosity (Gottlieb et al., 2013; Loewenstein, 1994; Murayama, 2022; Murayama et al., 2019; Oudeyer, 2007). For example, according to the information gap theory (Loewenstein, 1994), curiosity arises when people become aware of a gap in their knowledge: a discrepancy between what is known and what is unknown. Critically, the theory also posits that as individuals gain relevant knowledge, the saliency of information gaps increases, leading to higher levels of epistemic curiosity.

More recent theories also suggest that prior knowledge in a specific domain enhances curiosity. The region of proximal learning theory proposes that curiosity peaks when we have the feeling of 'almost knowing' a piece of information (Metcalfe et al., 2020). This feeling is considered to arise when our knowledge is incomplete but close to the boundary between what is known and unknown, which is highly related to the notion of the information gap proposed by Loewenstein (1994). Furthermore, the reward learning framework of curiosity argues that individuals perceive more knowledge gaps when they can connect a new piece of information with what they already know (Murayama, 2022). In contrast, they would not perceive knowledge gaps for a standalone fact in a domain with which they are not familiar, because it cannot be connected to their existing knowledge. Thus, the framework again predicts that a larger amount of knowledge is predictive of stronger curiosity.

These theories are also consistent with research findings in younger adults (Fastrich et al., 2024; Reichardt et al., 2023; Sakaki et al., 2024; Wade & Kidd, 2019; Witherby & Carpenter, 2022). In Witherby and Carpenter (2022), for example, participants who were more knowledgeable about American football showed stronger levels of curiosity for questions relevant to American football; in contrast, prior knowledge about American football was not predictive of curiosity for other questions related to cooking. Similarly, Reichardt and colleagues (2023) showed that after reading short texts on a randomly selected topic, participants reported stronger curiosity for questions related to the text, but not questions irrelevant to the text. In addition, Fastrich and Murayama (2020) revealed that when participants read information about unfamiliar countries, their interest in the country increased as they gained more knowledge.

In summary, the literature suggests that prior knowledge plays an important role in state epistemic curiosity and that older adults have more general knowledge, and rely on it more heavily, than younger adults. These results point to the likelihood that older adults' stronger state epistemic curiosity (Fastrich et al., 2024; Galli et al., 2018; Ozono et al., 2021; Whatley et al., 2025) is driven by their enriched knowledge. However, previous studies on state curiosity and aging did not systematically control for prior knowledge; thus, little is known about the role of prior knowledge in older adults' epistemic curiosity.

Present Study

In the present study, we examine the role that prior knowledge plays in state epistemic curiosity in older and younger adults. We hypothesize that prior knowledge positively affects epistemic curiosity, regardless of age. We also hypothesize that the age-related differences in epistemic curiosity are at least partly due to age-related differences in knowledge; thus, we expect that older and younger adults show similar levels of epistemic curiosity after controlling for knowledge. To address these hypotheses, we conducted two experiments. In both experiments, older and younger adults were presented with trivia questions from one domain in which they had high prior knowledge and another domain in which they had low prior knowledge.

Experiment 1 used learning materials characterized by large population-level differences in prior knowledge, but limited variation across age groups. Specifically, we tested participants from the United Kingdom (UK) and the United States (US), and asked them to indicate their curiosity about questions in two different domains: soccer and American football. American football has been the most popular sport to follow for American people across all age groups (Jones, 2024); in contrast, soccer (football) is the most popular sport for British people to follow, irrespective of age (YouGov, n.d.). Thus, British people are expected to know more about soccer than American football (and vice versa for US people), irrespective of age. Based on our hypothesis that prior knowledge is a key factor responsible for age differences in curiosity, we expected a significant interaction between participants' nationality and knowledge domain in curiosity, irrespective of age; in other words, British participants should report stronger curiosity for questions about soccer than American football (and vice versa for US participants). Experiment 2 aimed to extend Experiment 1 by treating knowledge and age as orthogonal factors. Specifically, we used questions about two groups of celebrities: a) those known well by younger generations but not by older generations, and b) those known well by older generations but not by younger generations. We expected that older adults, relative to younger adults, would show higher levels of curiosity about celebrities famous to older generations. In contrast, the pattern should be reversed for the other celebrities (those famous for younger generations), such that older adults should show lower levels of curiosity relative to younger adults.

Experiment 1

In Experiment 1, we recruited older adults and younger adults from the US and UK and presented them with a series of questions from two domains: soccer and American football. For each question, participants first rated their curiosity to learn the answer, and then their interest in the answer once they saw it. As described earlier, we expected that participants from the UK would report higher curiosity ratings for soccer than American football, while participants from the US would report higher curiosity ratings for American football than soccer. To test this prediction, we selected learning materials that showed large knowledge differences based on country of origin, but only limited age-related differences.

While it still remained plausible that older adults generally possessed more knowledge than younger adults and, consequently, reported higher state curiosity, we expected that the size of this main effect should be smaller than the effect size of the interaction. To ensure that participants knew more about a sport domain famous for their country than the other domain, we asked all participants to generate as many teams as possible for American football and soccer (thereafter called a listing task). Given that younger adults outperform older adults in this type of fluency task due to the age-related cognitive declines (Lanting et al., 2009; Tomer & Levin, 1993), this task is not suited to examine age-related differences in knowledge.

However, it still allowed us to perform a within-person comparison to ensure that US participants know more about American football than football (and vice versa for UK participants). Lastly, we included the Epistemic Curiosity Scale (ECS; Litman, 2008) to examine previously observed age-related decline in trait curiosity (Chu & Fung, 2022; Robinson et al., 2017; Whatley et al., 2025)

Methods

Transparency and Openness

We pre-registered our sample size, criteria for data exclusions, experimental manipulations, and measures. The hypotheses and analyses of this experiment were also preregistered on the Open Science Framework (OSF; Abrahamse et al., 2024b). Additionally, data reported in the manuscript, the analysis code and output, and the materials for the trivia question paradigm are available on OSF (Abrahamse et al., 2026). ChatGPT was used to assist with refining the text (OpenAI, 2023). Data were analyzed using R, version 4.3.2 (R Core Team, 2023), in which ANOVAs were performed using the anovakun() function developed and updated by Iseki (2025) (https://riseki.cloudfree.jp/?ANOVA%E5%90%9B).

Participants

Participants included 216 adults. The sample size was determined by an a priori power analysis using G*Power (Faul et al., 2007) to achieve a 95% statistical power to detect smallsized effects of knowledge on curiosity (Cohen's f = 0.1) observed in previous studies (Wade & Kidd, 2019; Witherby & Carpenter, 2022). This sample size also ensured a 95% power to detect a small-to medium-sized effect (Cohen's f = 0.19) of age on trait curiosity and working memory.

A total of 248 participants recruited through Prolific (http://www.prolific.com/) completed the experiment in 2024. Following six preregistered exclusion criteria reported in the OSM, 32 were excluded from the final analysis. This exclusion resulted in the following four groups (N = 54 per group): UK younger adults (Mage = 28.2 years, SD = 4.8, range 18 to 35), UK older adults (Mage = 68.6, SD = 3.2, range 65 to 85), US younger adults (Mage = 28.7, SD = 4.4, range 18 to 35) and US older adults (Mage = 70.1, SD = 4.8, range 65 to 85). Each group had an equal number of males and females (see Table S1 in OSM for additional participant information). All participants gave informed consent and were compensated £7.00 per hour for their participation.

Potential participants were screened to make sure that they were both citizens and residents of the UK or the US, aged between 18 to 35 (for younger adults) or 65 and 85 (for older adults), and are able to name at least one team either for NFL (National Football League; a professional league for American football in US) or Premier League (a professional league for soccer in UK; see the Procedure section for more details). Additionally, older adults were screened for cognitive impairment using the online version of the Cognitive Telephone Screening Instrument (Haas et al., 2022); those who scored lower than a cut-off value of 21.74 (Alexopoulos et al., 2021), or reported cheating on the test in a questionnaire after participation (after their compensation was assured) were not invited to the study.

All participants gave informed consent before participation, and the experiment was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Economics and Social Sciences of the University of Tübingen.

Materials and Measures

We used 20 soccer-related and 20 American football-related questions (see Table S2 for the questions and answers). Questions about American football concerned the NFL (National Football League in the US); some of them were based on questions used in Witherby and Carpenter (2022), but modified to ensure that answers were not outdated or incorrect, and that neither questions nor answers included measurement systems that differed between the UK and the US (e.g., meters and yards). Other questions were newly generated.

Questions about soccer concerned the Premier League in the UK. We conducted a pilot study with 40 younger adults and 38 older adults to select the 40 questions that elicited relatively low accuracy and sufficient curiosity (see OSM for details).

Additionally, the Epistemic Curiosity Scale (Litman, 2008) was used to assess trait curiosity (α = .85). The scale consisted of 10 items (e.g., "I enjoy exploring new ideas") with a Likert scale from 1 (almost never) to 4 (always). A short version of the Operation Span (OSpan) task was also used to assess working memory (Oswald et al., 2015) (see OSM for the procedure). This measure served as a check to verify whether the previously reported agerelated decline in working memory span was replicated in our sample (Jaroslawska & Rhodes, 2019; Verhaeghen et al., 2003).

Procedure

The experimental procedure was programmed and implemented using lab.js (Henninger et al., 2022) and hosted by a server of the University of Tübingen to which the authors are affiliated. All participants first completed a screening session. Here, participants were asked to list as many teams from both leagues as possible in one minute each ("a listing task"); the league for which they started listing the teams was randomly assigned. Next, older adults completed the online version of the eCOGTEL (Haas et al., 2022). Participants who were deemed eligible based on performance in these tasks were, within two days of its completion, invited to take part in the main experiment.

In the main experiment, participants completed the trivia question paradigm and the ECS, with the task order randomized for each participant. The trivia question paradigm included 40 trials; after the 20th trial, participants were allowed to take a break. The order of the trials was pseudo-randomly determined for each participant, so that participants would not see questions from the same domain in no more than three consecutive trials.

Each trial of the trivia question paradigm started with a trivia question (see Figure 1).

Participants were instructed to provide their best guess for every question and were assured that their best guess was appreciated, even when they were uncertain about the answer. They had 20 sec to report their guess; when they could not think of any guess within 20 sec, they could leave the answer box blank. Once they submitted their guess or did not provide it within

Knowledge

We expected that British people know more about soccer than American football (and vice versa for US people), irrespective of age. To confirm if this was the case, we first performed a 2 (age: young vs. old) x 2 (nationality: UK vs. US) and 2 (domain: soccer vs.

This analysis showed a significant domain-by-nationality interaction, F(1, 212) = 241.67, p < .001, η²G = .30. Follow-up analyses revealed that people from the UK listed more teams from the Premier League (M = 9.41, SD = 3.65) than from the NFL (M = 3.31, SD = 3.56), t(107) = 14.66, p < 0.001, d = 1.41, whereas people from the US listed more teams from the NFL (M = 7.49, SD = 5.15) than from the Premier League (M = 3.20, SD = 3.78), t(107) = 7.87, p < 0.001, d = 0.76. These findings are consistent with our expectation that participants had more knowledge about their domestic league compared to the foreign league. There was also a significant age effect, F(1, 212) = 17.12, p < .001, η²G = .05, with younger adults listing more teams (M = 6.73, SD = 5.39) than older adults (M = 4.97, SD = 4.13), which is in line with previous findings on verbal/semantic fluency and aging (Lanting et al., 2009; Tomer & Levin, 1993).

Domain and Age Effects on State Curiosity and Interest

Next, we investigated the effect of knowledge and age on state curiosity using a similar 2 (age) x 2 (nationality) x 2 (domain) Mixed ANOVA. There was a significant domain-by-nationality interaction (see Figure 2), F(1, 212) = 147.54, p < .001, η²G = .16. As expected, follow-up t-tests showed that people from the UK reported higher curiosity ratings for soccer (M = 6.01, SD = 2.25) compared to American football (M = 3.89, SD = 2.38), t(107) = 9.72, p < 0.001, d = 0.94, while the opposite was true for people from the US (soccer: M = 5.00, SD = 2.38; American football: M = 6.77, SD = 1.98), t(107) = 7.56, p < .001, d = 0.73. This interaction was not significantly qualified by age (ps > .23), indicating that the domain-by-nationality pattern was consistent across age groups. These results are in line with our expectation that prior knowledge is a key factor driving epistemic curiosity irrespective of age.

It should be noted that we also found a significant main effect of age, F(1, 212) = 3.99, p = .047, η²G = .01, with older adults (M = 5.68, SD = 2.59) reporting higher curiosity than younger adults (M = 5.16, SD = 2.38). However, as expected, the effect size for age group (η²G = .01, 95% CI [.01, .06]) was substantially smaller than the effect size for the domain-bynationality interaction (i.e., the effect of prior knowledge; η²G = .16, 95% CI [.12, .21]). To directly compare these effects, we bootstrapped the difference between their generalized etasquared values across 1,000 resamples. The resulting 95% bootstrap confidence interval for the difference in effect size did not include 0 (Δη²G = .15, 95% CI [0.09, 0.20]), suggesting that the knowledge effect is significantly larger than that of age group. Taken together, our results are in line with the hypothesis that prior knowledge is a key factor for epistemic curiosity across age groups.

Mediation Analyses

Results presented so far showed the country-by-domain interaction in knowledge assessed by the listing task and in curiosity. These results are in line with our hypothesis that knowledge serves as a key predictor of curiosity. To test this hypothesis more directly, we ran two additional mediation analyses that we did not preregister, reported in full in the OSM: one for state curiosity and one for interest. In separate regression analyses, we found that nationality predicted performance in the listing task, which in turn significantly predicted curiosity and interest. We also revealed significant mediation effects of listed teams for the effect of nationality on state curiosity, ab = 1.33, SE = 0.17, 95% CI [1.01, 1.66], and for the effect of nationality on interest, ab = 1.23, SE = 0.16, 95% CI [0.93, 1.56] (see Figure S1).

Together, these findings indicate that knowledge about the leagues at least partially accounts for nationality differences in both curiosity and interest, further supporting the role of knowledge as a predictor of curiosity.

Trait Curiosity and Working Memory Span

We also investigated the effect of age on trait curiosity and working memory span. A 2 (age) x 2 (nationality) ANOVA on trait curiosity showed no significant effect of age (young: M = 2.67, SD = 0.50; old: M = 2.54, SD = 0.53), F(1, 212) = 3.80, p = .053, η²G = .02. Neither the effect of nationality, F(1, 212) = 2.25, p = .14, η²G = .01, nor the interaction effect between age and nationality, F(1, 212) = 0.33, p = .56, η²G = .00, was significant.

The working memory span was also not significantly different between younger (M = 10.20, SD = 2.48) and older adults (M = 9.65, SD = 2.37), F(1, 214) = 2.83, p = .09, η²G = .01.

To further explore age differences in cognitive functioning, we compared response times to answering the trivia questions between the age groups in a non-preregistered analysis. Older adults (M = 12.70s, SD = 2.81) answered significantly slower than younger adults (M = 11.38s, SD = 3.02), F(1, 214) = 11.14, p < .001, η²G = .05, consistent with age differences in cognitive functioning (Fricker, 2005; Ratcliff, 2008; Yan & Tourangeau, 2008).

Experiment 2

The results from Experiment 1 indicate that prior knowledge positively predicts epistemic curiosity across older and younger adults. While we also found a significant main effect of age on curiosity, its effect size was significantly smaller than the effect size for the interaction between nationality and domain. These results are in line with our hypothesis that age-related changes in prior knowledge serve as a key in explaining age-related differences in epistemic curiosity. However, there are also several limitations. First, we focused on one particular domain (football) and excluded participants who failed to list any teams from the NFL or the Premier League. Therefore, the results may be specific to individuals who are interested in sports, limiting generalizability. Second, because we did not treat age and knowledge as orthogonal factors in our design, it was unclear whether the differences in state curiosity between older and younger adults could be attributed to knowledge differences.

Experiment 2 aimed to address these limitations by introducing several changes. First, we used a new set of materials, namely, questions about celebrities. We created stimulus sets comprised of two groups of celebrities to examine the effects of knowledge driven by age: those primarily known by the younger generation and those primarily known by the older generation.

This enables us to treat age group and knowledge level as orthogonal factors, allowing us to isolate the effects of knowledge from the effects of age. Second, we obtained more detailed measures of participants' prior knowledge about each stimulus by explicitly asking all participants to rate their familiarity with each celebrity. People should feel higher levels of familiarity for celebrities they know well than those they know little about (Reder et al., 2013). Therefore, this allowed us to check if our manipulation worked as intended (e.g., if older adults are familiar with one group of celebrities over the other) and examine whether prior knowledge predicts curiosity on a trial-to-trial basis. Lastly, we incorporated a behavioral measure of epistemic curiosity (i.e., information seeking) to complement subjective curiosity ratings.

Similar to Experiment 1, we predicted knowledge to positively affect state curiosity and information seeking across age. Specifically, we hypothesized that for celebrities known by older generations, older adults would show higher levels of curiosity and information seeking behavior than do younger adults; in contrast, for celebrities known by younger generations, older adults should show lower levels of curiosity and information seeking behavior than younger adults. Lastly, we predicted that younger adults would score higher on trait curiosity compared to older adults.

Methods

Transparency and Openness

We pre-registered our sample size, exclusion criteria, experimental manipulations, and measures prior to data collection. The hypotheses and analyses were preregistered on OSF (Abrahamse et al., 2024a). Additionally, data reported in the manuscript, the analysis code and output, and the materials for the trivia question paradigm can be found in the OSM (Abrahamse et al., 2026). ChatGPT was used to assist with refining the text and the creation of trivia questions and celebrity fun facts (OpenAI, 2023). Data were analyzed using R, version 4.3.2 (R Core Team, 2023), in which ANOVAs were performed using the anovakun() function (Iseki, 2025) and mixed effects models using the lmerTest package (Kuznetsova et al., 2017).

Participants

Participants included 248 adults who took part in the experiment in 2025. Data from 32 participants were excluded because they met one or more of six preregistered exclusion criteria (see OSM). The final sample included 216 participants; the sample size was sufficient to detect the hypothesized age-by-domain interaction with a power of 95% (see Participants Section in Experiment 1 for the power analysis). The participants were citizens and residents from the UK, divided into two groups: younger adults (n = 108; Mage = 28.1, SD = 4.5, range 18 to 35; 55 males and 53 females) and older adults (n = 108; Mage = 68.6, SD = 4.16, range 65 to 85; 57 males and 51 females) (see Table S1 in OSM for additional participant information). As in Experiment 1, participants were recruited through Prolific, gave informed consent, and were compensated £7.00 per hour for their participation. Older adults were screened using the eCOGTEL in an identical manner as in Experiment 1. Based on this task, 18 participants were not invited to the main experiment. The experiment was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Economics and Social Sciences of the University of Tübingen.

Materials

We used a new set of questions focusing on 20 celebrities (musicians and actors) from the UK and US (see Table S4 for a list of questions and answers). These celebrities were equally divided into two domains: known-by-young and known-by-old. We conducted a pilot study with 20 younger and 20 older adults that demonstrated a significant age-by-domain interaction on familiarity ratings of celebrities (see OSM for details), indicating age-specific knowledge levels for the celebrity domains. The topics of the trivia questions were matched across the two groups of celebrities. For example, in the 'known-by-young' domain, one question was "What is John Cena's catchphrase, often shouted while taunting opponents in the WWE ring?", while the corresponding question in the 'known-by-old' domain was "What was Jerry Lewis's catchphrase, often delivered in a goofy voice in movies like The Ladies Man?".

To further reduce potential item effects, we created two questions for each celebrity, resulting in a total of 40 questions. For each participant, one of the two questions for each celebrity was randomly selected and presented during the experiment. To assess information-seeking behavior, we also included a 'fun fact' about each celebrity, consisting of 16 words (±1), that participants could learn (see Table S5).

Procedure

Older adults completed a screening phase prior to the main experiment, in which they completed the eCOGTEL. Those who were considered eligible after the screening session were invited back to the main experiment within two days.

During the main experiment, participants completed a trivia question paradigm; the procedures were similar to those in Experiment 1, with the following exceptions (see Figure 1b). First, in addition to a trivia question, a picture of the celebrity was displayed on the screens (for copyright reasons, Figure 1b shows placeholder icons instead of the actual pictures). Second, instead of the post-answer interest rating, participants were given a choice to see additional information about the celebrity. If they chose 'yes', they waited 2 seconds before seeing the information, which they could read at their own pace. If they chose 'no', they proceeded to an intertrial screen for 2 seconds before the next trial began. The task included 20 trials in total; participants were allowed to take a short break after the 10th trial.

Prior to the task, participants completed three practice trials about athletes. The trivia question paradigm was followed by a rating task, where participants were shown the name and a photo of each celebrity used in the task again; for each celebrity, they rated their familiarity on a scale from 1 (no knowledge at all) to 7 (extensive knowledge). As in Experiment 1, the order of the trivia question paradigm and ECS (Litman, 2008; α = .85) was randomized. All participants completed the OSpan task at the end of the experiment.

Mediation Analysis

To further examine whether prior knowledge explains age differences in epistemic curiosity, we conducted a non-preregistered mediation analysis examining whether the effect of age on state curiosity was mediated by familiarity ratings. Here, familiarity was used as a proxy for prior knowledge and was operationalized as a within-subject difference score: the average familiarity rating for celebrities in the known-by-old domain minus the average familiarity rating for celebrities in the known-by-young domain for each participant, i.e., Δ familiarity. State curiosity was operationalized similarly: the average curiosity rating for known-by-old celebrities minus the average curiosity rating for known-by-young celebrities for each participant, i.e., Δ state curiosity. Consequently, positive values reflected relatively greater curiosity or familiarity for known-by-old compared to known-by-young domains, whereas negative values reflected the opposite pattern. Age group was effect coded (1: old, -1: young).

As done in Experiment 1, we followed the guidelines of Baron and Kenny (1986). We first conducted a regression analysis and found significantly effects of age on Δ state curiosity, b = 1.17, SE = 0.10, t(214) = 11.85, p < .001, confirming that older adults were on average more curious about the known-by-old domain than the known-by-young domain (M = 1.30, SD = 1.39), and vice versa for younger adults (M = -1.04, SD = 1.51). Second, we confirmed significant effects of age on Δ familiarity, b = 1.38, SE = 0.06, t(214) = 24.55, p < .001, such that older adults on average showed higher levels of familiarity for the known-by-old domain than the known-by-young domain (M = 1.15, SD = 0.77), and the opposite was true for younger adults (M = -1.61, SD = 0.87). Third, a regression analysis predicting Δ state curiosity from Δ familiarity, controlling for age group, showed a significant positive effect of Δ familiarity, b = 0.58, SE = 0.11, t(213) = 5.09, p < .001, confirming that higher prior knowledge in one domain predicts higher curiosity for the domain. Consistent with the first regression, the effect of age group on Δ state curiosity also remained significant, b = 0.37, SE = 0.18, t(213) = 2.03, p = .043. Finally, we tested the indirect effect of age group on Δ state curiosity through Δ familiarity ratings using a similar approach as for the mediation analysis in Experiment 1. Results revealed a significant indirect effect (see Figure 5), as the 95% CI did not include zero, ab = 0.80, SE = 0.16, 95% CI [0.49, 1.12]. These findings suggest prior knowledge mediates age-related differences in epistemic curiosity. Trait Curiosity and Working Memory Span

To examine the effect of age on trait curiosity, a Between-Subjects ANOVA was conducted with age (young vs. old) as the independent variable and the score on the ECS as the outcome variable. In line with previous findings (Robinson et al., 2017; Whatley et al., 2025; Wiegand et al., 2025), we found a significant effect of age, F(1, 214) = 2.43, p = .003, η²G = .04, reflecting the fact that younger adults (M = 2.60, SD = 0.49) reported higher trait curiosity than did older adults (M = 2.39, SD = 0.56).

There were no significant differences in working memory span between younger adults (M = 10.69, SD = 3.04) and older adults (M = 10.93, SD = 2.61), F(1, 210) = 0.40, p = .53, η²G = .00. As in Experiment 1, we also compared response times to answering the trivia questions between the age groups in a non-preregistered analysis, which showed that older adults (M = 15.13s, SD = 2.79) responded significantly more slowly than younger adults (M = 13.33s, SD = 3.18), F(1, 209) = 19.03, p < .001, η²G = .08.

General Discussion

Given the importance of curiosity in old age, a growing number of studies investigated factors that affect epistemic curiosity in older adults (Sobczak et al., 2025; Swirsky et al., 2021; Swirsky & Spaniol, 2024b; Yagi et al., 2023). Nevertheless, existing research does not provide a clear idea about how and why older adults report stronger levels of state epistemic curiosity for a specific piece of information relative to younger adults. The present study aimed to address this issue by focusing on the role of prior knowledge. Across two experiments, both older and younger adults reported higher curiosity ratings for highknowledge domains than for low-knowledge domains. This effect of knowledge was significantly larger than the main effect of age in Experiment 1. In addition, in Experiment 2, we found that older adults experience stronger epistemic curiosity only for high-knowledge domains; in other domains, where younger adults were more knowledgeable, older adults experienced weaker curiosity relative to younger adults. Furthermore, we demonstrated that age differences in curiosity across those domains were partially mediated by familiarity ratings. These results are in line with our hypothesis that age-related differences in state curiosity might be partly attributable to age-related differences in prior knowledge.

Previous studies have repeatedly indicated that older adults experience stronger levels of epistemic curiosity relative to younger adults (Fastrich et al., 2024; Galli et al., 2018; Whatley et al., 2025). Researchers have also identified the similarities and differences in the mechanisms underlying curiosity across age groups (Chen & Castel, 2026; Chu & Fung, 2022; Fastrich et al., 2024; Sobczak et al., 2025; Swirsky et al., 2021; Swirsky & Spaniol, 2024b; Yagi et al., 2023). Yet they do not explain the age-related increase in state curiosity observed in the literature. In addition, despite prior work clearly describing the role of prior knowledge for curiosity (Fastrich & Murayama, 2020; Gottlieb et al., 2013; Murayama, 2022; Murayama et al., 2019; Wade & Kidd, 2019; Witherby & Carpenter, 2022), as well as older adults' cognitive processing in general (e.g., Badham & Maylor, 2015), these studies did not systematically control age-related differences in knowledge. One exception is Chen & Castel (2026), although their measure did not clearly capture the well-established age differences in prior knowledge. The present study extends these prior studies by examining state epistemic curiosity across both low and high prior knowledge domains. We found that the effect of prior knowledge on state curiosity occurs across older and younger adults, different materials (those on football and those on celebrities) and measures (curiosity ratings and information seeking).

We also demonstrated positive associations between curiosity and multiple proxies of prior knowledge, including confidence and familiarity, although these relationships weakened at higher levels of both measures.

The role of knowledge in explaining age differences in state curiosity may also be relevant in interpreting findings from another study on curiosity and aging; Fastrich et al. (2024) reported that older adults' information-seeking style is more specific (i.e., exploring facts within one topic), whereas that of younger adults is more diverse (i.e., exploring facts by switching between topics). Building on the reward-learning framework of knowledge acquisition (Murayama, 2022), one would expect individuals to be more curious about a topic, and to search for more information about it, when their knowledge increases. Similar to how increasing knowledge in older adults may account for the apparent age differences in state curiosity, it may also evoke a change in information-seeking styles; as knowledge in certain areas increases with age, information-seeking might shift from more diverse to more specific in those areas.

In addition, in line with previous studies (Robinson et al., 2017; Whatley et al., 2025; Wiegand et al., 2025), we found that older adults scored lower on trait curiosity than younger adults. This age-related decline in trait curiosity is consistent with several lifespan theories of human motivation, which suggest a decline in acquiring knowledge with increasing age. For instance, socioemotional selectivity theory suggests that, in older adulthood, less priority is given to goals such as knowledge acquisition and more to emotionally meaningful pursuits (Carstensen, 2021; Carstensen et al., 1999). Similarly, the selective engagement hypothesis argues that older adults become increasingly selective in their engagement of cognitive resources because the costs of cognitive engagement increase with age (Hess, 2014). The suggestions from these theories, together with previous findings on age-related changes in trait curiosity and our own results, make the findings on state curiosity even more intriguing: in the same participant groups in which trait curiosity decreases with age, state curiosity appears to increase (Whatley et al., 2025).

Our findings may provide insights into this contrasting age-related pattern of state and trait curiosity. Specifically, this pattern may be explained by differences in how state and trait curiosity are operationalized. Measures on trait curiosity include questions on participants' information-seeking behavior in their everyday life; in contrast, state epistemic curiosity was typically assessed by asking the subjective feeling of curiosity in response to a specific learning material (e.g., trivia questions). In Experiment 2, we included a behavioral measure of information seeking for each celebrity. Here, we found that older adults searched for less additional information than younger adults. Swirsky and Spaniol (2024) already showed agerelated differences in the correlation between curiosity and information seeking. Our study extends these previous findings and suggests that age affects self-reported measures of curiosity and information seeking differently, which might explain why older adults score lower on trait curiosity questionnaires compared to younger adults. This lower rate of information seeking among older adults is consistent with the socioemotional selectivity theory (Carstensen, 2021) and may be due to the age-related increases in cognitive costs associated with such behavior (Hess, 2014). The higher levels of state curiosity observed among older adults in the literature (e.g., Fastrich et al., 2024; Galli et al., 2018; Whatley et al., 2025) may be explained by the nature of the study setting: trivia questions often concern general knowledge, which tends to increase with age, and satisfying curiosity does not require substantial effort to access the answer.

Our results also provide insights into the relation between curiosity and interest, two constructs that are highly related to each other. As observed in previous studies, we found that curiosity and interest were highly correlated across participants when we assessed them for each question in Experiment 1. However, our item-level analysis showed that they were more strongly related for the high-knowledge domain than for the low-knowledge domain (see OSM). These results are in line with previous notions that interest is more relevant to knowledge than curiosity (Hidi & Renninger, 2020). Future research needs to examine the dynamic interplay among these factors across the adult lifespan.

Limitations

There are a few important limitations with the present study. First, one concern is the potential generalizability. Although we attempted to address this issue by conducting two experiments using different sets of materials, both sets assessed only one topic, namely, football and celebrities. The generalizability of the findings may therefore be restricted to similar types of content or to participants who are particularly interested in these topics, as participants were aware of the topics before choosing to participate. We also only focused on participant groups from the UK and the US. Furthermore, the ecological validity of the trivia question paradigm may be limited. In real-world settings, satisfying curiosity usually requires effort and occurs in an open-ended fashion where one can freely explore (Gottlieb et al., 2013; Murayama, 2022; Murayama et al., 2019; Schumacher et al., 2026). Future research needs to examine the role that knowledge plays in curiosity across adulthood in more naturalistic settings.

Another important question concerns our measures of prior knowledge. In the present study, we used two domains, where we expected a clear group difference in the amount of prior knowledge to clearly observe its effect on state curiosity. This experimental manipulation was complemented by the listing task (in Experiment 1) and familiarity ratings (in Experiment 2) that aimed to assess participants' knowledge of the two domains. In Experiment 1, US participants were able to list more NFL teams than Premier League teams (and vice versa for UK participants). In Experiment 2, participants reported stronger curiosity and chose to engage in information seeking more for celebrities that they felt more familiar with than other celebrities. Our mediation analyses further revealed that both of these knowledge measures predicted curiosity. Given this comparable pattern across the two different measures of knowledge, the effects of knowledge observed in the present study were unlikely due to the specific measures we used. However, it is difficult, if not impossible, to fully assess one's knowledge and therefore these tasks likely have assessed only a part of one's knowledge. In addition, neither of the measures directly assessed the amount of knowledge gaps, which should predict curiosity according to theoretical notions (Loewenstein, 1994; Murayama, 2022). Future studies should develop fine-grained measures of prior knowledge to reliably assess one's knowledge and to assess how it affects curiosity across the adult lifespan.

Lastly, we did not find significant differences in working memory performance between younger and older adults. Although the effects of age on working memory were not always observed (Jaroslawska & Rhodes, 2019; Verhaeghen et al., 2003), younger adults typically outperform older adults in such tasks (Alloway & Alloway, 2013; Hartshorne & Germine, 2015). The lack of significant differences may be due to several factors. First, we excluded older adults with signs of cognitive decline during the screening phase. While this helped us to ensure that all participants had the ability to consent, we may have had a sample of older adults with potentially, on average, higher working memory spans. Second, because of time constraints, a shortened version of the OSpan task was used to assess working memory (Oswald et al., 2015), which may not have been sensitive enough to detect age differences compared to the full version or to using multiple shorter versions (Foster et al., 2015; Oswald et al., 2015). Third, older adults on platforms like Prolific might have higher cognitive abilities than those who would participate in lab-based experiments. However, it should be noted that we replicated previous findings that older adults show poorer performance in a fluency task in Experiment 1 (Jaroslawska & Rhodes, 2019; Verhaeghen et al., 2003), and that older adults responded more slowly than younger adults in both experiments (Fricker, 2005; Ratcliff, 2008; Yan & Tourangeau, 2008), suggesting that there were age-related declines in cognitive abilities even in our participants. Nevertheless, it is important for future research to examine whether and how the results are replicable in other samples who show clear age-related declines in working memory capacities.

Conclusion

We often associate curiosity primarily with children and hold a negative stereotype that curiosity decreases in adulthood. However, our study indicates that both younger and older adults report strong curiosity for something unknown as long as they have relevant prior knowledge. Given that older adults generally have a richer knowledge base than younger adults, these results suggest that older adults may, in general, experience even higher curiosity in life relative to younger adults. By highlighting the role of accumulated knowledge over the lifespan in sustaining epistemic curiosity, our findings offer a positive view of curiosity in later adulthood and highlight the importance of knowledge as a key driver of curiosity across the lifespan.
