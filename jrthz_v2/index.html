Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Abstract

Digital technologies make it possible to adapt the level of support to students' behavior. However, determining what level is optimal for each student is methodologically challenging: If adaptations depend on prior behavior, adaptivity decisions are not fully randomized, which complicates causal inference. We present a methodological approach based on sequential experimental designs and directed acyclic graphs (DAGs) to evaluate adaptivity decisions. We illustrate this approach in a mobile intervention promoting frequent vocabulary study. Students received planning prompts during a 20-day first-stage intervention, followed by an adapted prompting frequency in the second stage. Using a DAG, we formalized causal assumptions, identified confounding, and adjusted for it. The results illustrate the importance of this methodological approach to obtain valid causal conclusions: an unadjusted analysis would have led to the incorrect conclusion that scheduling more prompts leads to reduced study activity. This underscores the importance of making adaptivity decisions in an evidence-based way.

Educational relevance statement

Adapting support to students' needs is a promising way for educational interventions to promote learning, but designing effective interventions requires evidence about which adaptations actually work. This study introduces a methodological approach that helps researchers obtain such evidence, even in complex, real-world settings, and demonstrates it in a mobile intervention that adapted planning support to students' learning behavior. We found that students who studied less frequently particularly benefited from receiving more mobile prompts encouraging them to formulate plans. Moreover, students who received fewer prompts early on benefited from increased support later, whereas those who had already received many prompts benefited more from reduced prompting. These findings show how educational technologies can be designed to provide the right amount of support to students at the right time. & Snow, 1977), examining whether relatively stable learner aptitudes moderate the effectiveness of different intervention options. However, identifying aptitudes that reliably predict which intervention options will be most effective has proven difficult.

An alternative approach shifts the question from how an intervention should be adapted based on who students are to how it should be adapted based on how students have responded to the intervention so far (response-to-intervention; e.g., Fuchs & Fuchs, 2006). Adaptivity decisions can thus be made repeatedly as new information about students' behavior or performance becomes available.

To illustrate this approach, imagine a class of students using a mobile learning application (app) designed to promote more frequent vocabulary study. The app teaches students how to make effective plans that support behavior change (Sheeran et al., 2024) and regularly prompts them to apply this strategy. After several weeks, clear differences in study behavior emerge: Some students study very frequently, whereas others do not. Based on these differences, the app adapts its support, providing more frequent planning prompts to students who have not yet established a regular study routine and fewer prompts to those who have.

Researchers who wish to design and evaluate such adaptive interventions, however, face a methodological challenge: How can we determine whether students actually benefit from receiving intervention options adapted to their ongoing behavior or performance? Experimental designs addressing this question must contend with the complexity inherent in sequencing intervention options over time (Hedges, 2018).

For instance, adherence often declines over an extended intervention period.

Moreover, later intervention options are contingent on prior behavior or performance and thus not fully randomized. How can adaptivity decisions be rigorously evaluated under these conditions?

The primary aim of this study is to introduce a methodological approach that addresses the challenges of evaluating adaptivity decisions when intervention options are assigned sequentially. In the following, we outline how combining sequential experimental designs with directed acyclic graphs (DAGs) makes causal assumptions explicit and allows researchers to identify which effects can be estimated without bias and how. We then demonstrate this approach in an empirical application examining whether adapting the frequency of planning prompts in a mobile planning intervention can support students' vocabulary study.

1.1 Adaptive educational interventions

Educational interventions vary in their effectiveness for different students.

Consequently, a longstanding goal of education has been to personalize learning (Bernacki et al., 2021; Dockterman, 2018). Adaptive educational interventions all follow the basic idea of adapting the duration, frequency, amount, type, or delivery modality of intervention components to students' unique and changing needs (Nahum-Shani & Almirall, 2019). In more technical terms, an adaptive intervention is defined by the modification of intervention options based on decisions informed by real-time or previously assessed learner data (Almirall et al., 2018; Plass & Pawar, 2020).

Numerous approaches have been developed to adapt educational interventions, differing in what to adapt to and on what timescale. Mastery learning approaches, for example, are grounded in the principle that students should progress at their own pace, advancing to new material only after achieving a high level of competence in the preceding content (Keller, 1968). Other approaches emphasize adapting instruction to students' individual aptitudes, such as prior knowledge or achievement motivation (Cronbach & Snow, 1977). In special education, Response-to-Intervention (RTI) methods have been established in which at-risk students initially receive general education, their responsiveness is continuously monitored, and instruction is intensified using a multi-tiered approach (Fuchs & Fuchs, 2006). In computerized adaptive learning systems, many individual characteristics (e.g., motivation, knowledge) are continuously assessed and updated during task performance. Instructional strategy is adapted based on discrepancies between students' current state and learning objectives (Vandewaetere et al., 2011).

Across these traditions, educational effectiveness hinges on how information about the student is translated into decisions about how to adapt intervention components. The central question is how these decisions can be made in an evidence-based way.

1.2 How to make adaptivity decisions in an evidence-based way

Developing an adaptive intervention requires identifying which intervention options are most beneficial for particular students at a particular time (Nahum-Shani & Almirall, 2019). The traditional approach to this question has been experimental research on aptitude-treatment interactions (ATIs; Cronbach & Snow, 1977). The idea is that different learners benefit from different interventions depending on relatively stable learner characteristics (e.g., prior knowledge, motivation).

Methodologically, ATI research aims to identify statistical interactions between these learner characteristics and the intervention options to which learners are randomly assigned (Cronbach, 1957). However, with few exceptions (Tetzlaff et al., 2025), it has proven difficult to identify robust ATIs. This difficulty has been explained by the fact that intervention effects likely depend on the interplay of multiple factors rather than a single learner characteristic (Cronbach, 1975). Detecting higher-order interactions is often infeasible with standard regression-based approaches. This limitation has motivated the development of alternative methodological approaches for identifying treatment heterogeneity, including methods such as latent profile analysis or machine learning approaches that can accommodate complex and potentially nonlinear effects (Edelsbrunner et al., 2025; Knaus, 2021). These efforts underscore the need for methodological strategies that produce robust causal evidence under conditions of high complexity.

In addition to the development of statistical methods for identifying complex ATIs, another line of research treats adaptivity as a sequential decision-making problem, in which intervention options are repeatedly randomized and evaluated as new information about the learner becomes available. In recent decades, approaches originating in health research have entered the educational toolkit, most notably the sequential multiple-assignment randomized trial (SMART; Chow & Hampton, 2022; Nahum-Shani & Almirall, 2019) and the micro-randomized trial (MRT; Breitwieser et al., 2024). Both are experimental designs specifically intended to inform the development of adaptive interventions (Nahum-Shani, Dziak, et al., 2022) and can be viewed as extensions of the classic RCT through their use of sequential randomization at multiple decision points.

In an MRT, students are repeatedly randomized to receive or not receive an intervention or an intervention option. For instance, randomization could occur each evening and determine whether a student receives a mobile reminder. This design is particularly well suited for developing technology-enhanced interventions that adapt to fast-changing, time-varying factors, such as day-to-day fluctuations in students' motivation or affect (Nahum-Shani, Dziak, et al., 2022). MRTs allow researchers to estimate heterogeneity in within-person causal effects, thereby identifying which students benefit more from one intervention option versus another. In addition, when combined with repeated assessments of time-varying factors (e.g., motivation), MRTs make it possible to examine when a given student is more or less likely to benefit from a specific intervention option (Breitwieser et al., 2023). Thus, MRTs can address questions about which intervention options work best at which time points.

The SMART, in contrast, typically operates on slower timescales (e.g., weeks, months). It is used when adaptation decisions are based on students' progress over the course of an intervention, such as early response status or adherence (Nahum-Shani, Dziak, et al., 2022). Compared to MRTs, SMARTs involve fewer stages and therefore fewer randomization points; the minimal SMART consists of two stages.

For example, in a two-stage SMART, students may initially be randomized to different intervention options (e.g., different types of instructional strategies). After a couple of weeks, students' response to the initial intervention is assessed, and subsequent intervention options are then re-randomized conditional on response status. Non-responders may receive intensified or alternative support, whereas responders may continue with the initial intervention or receive it at a reduced intensity to increase efficiency. At the end of the second stage, SMARTs allow researchers to address questions such as which initial intervention is most effective overall and which follow-up interventions are most beneficial for responders versus non-responders.

Taken together, MRTs and SMARTs are methodological approaches that support evidence-based adaptivity decisions. However, they also introduce substantial methodological complexity, as outlined in the next section.

1.3 Challenges for causal inference in sequential experimental designs

The sequential nature of SMARTs and MRTs creates challenges for causal inference that do not arise in the same way in conventional RCTs. One could naively assume that SMARTs and MRTs can be treated like conventional RCTs, where randomization typically yields unbiased estimates. This assumption overlooks that SMARTs and MRTs differ from conventional RCTs in that later intervention stages are influenced by the previous intervention stages. This dependency is easy to overlook because, when each decision point is considered in isolation, it resembles a conventional RCT. However, in sequential designs, some of the effects of interest are inherently confounded by design (Yeaton, 2024) and a failure to adjust for this confounding would lead to biased estimates.

To illustrate this point, consider first the example presented in Figure 1. It shows a sequential experimental design in which, during a first stage, all students receive an intervention. At decision point t, students are categorized as either responders or non-responders, based on their behavior or performance. During the second stage, students are randomized to either a condition in which the intervention dose is adapted based on their responder status (i.e., either decreased or increased) or to a control condition in which the intervention dose remains unchanged. Note that this experimental design is not a full SMART because students are not randomized to different first-stage intervention options. Nevertheless, it captures the essential feature of a sequential adaptive design: a later intervention decision is contingent on information generated during an earlier stage. We use it as a minimal example to illustrate the challenges that arise in sequential designs. In this design, some estimates are unbiased whereas others are not (see Yeaton, 2024). Within each responder stratum, the second-stage intervention is randomized. Thus, comparisons between Groups A versus B and between Groups C versus D can provide unbiased estimates of the effect of the assigned second-stage intervention. However, suppose we are interested in the overall causal effect of second-stage intervention dose across students. An approach that compares all groups in an ANOVA or regression model seems straightforward, but would, without further adjustment, not yield an unbiased causal estimate. The reason is that assignment to the second-stage intervention is conditional on students' behavior or performance during the first-stage intervention (determined at decision point t). This first-stage response is itself a post-treatment variable and is likely to be associated with behavior and performance during the second stage. Consequently, differences between the groups can reflect both the effect of the second-stage intervention and systematic differences between the students. Furthermore, suppose we are not only interested in the causal effect of the assigned intervention dose in stage 2, but also in the effectiveness of the intervention dose students actually received. In this case, issues of adherence must also be considered. That is, the intervention dose students receive during the second-stage intervention may depend not only on the randomized group assignment but also on whether students adhered to the study protocol or were available at the time the intervention was supposed to be delivered. This situation is analogous to conventional RCTs, in which the implemented intervention often deviates from the intended protocol. Although randomization allows researchers to estimate the causal effect of assignment to an intervention condition, estimating the causal effect of actually receiving the intervention requires additional assumptions and appropriate methods to account for confounding. Issues related to adherence are often exacerbated in naturalistic contexts and in digital interventions where students need to engage with a digital technology to receive the intervention (Nahum-Shani, Shaw, et al., 2022). Given the sequential nature of the design, it is plausible that adherence during the first-stage intervention is correlated with adherence in the second stage, which introduces an additional source of potential bias that needs to be taken into account.

Taken together, these considerations illustrate that sequential experimental designs can involve multiple sources of potential bias. Some arise from the sequential nature of the design itself, where treatment assignment at later stages is conditional on information generated at earlier stages. Others arise when the research question is about the effectiveness of the intervention as received rather than the effect of the assigned intervention, which can be confounded by additional variables. The next section introduces a methodological approach for systematically reasoning about these different sources of bias and identifying analytic strategies for obtaining unbiased causal estimates.

1.4 Identifying sources of bias in DAGs

Directed acyclic graphs (DAGs) provide a methodological approach for systematically representing the assumed causal relationships among variables. The example in Figure 2 constitutes such a DAG. Even for educational researchers who are not familiar with DAGs, this representation may appear familiar due to its resemblance to structural equation models or path models. Indeed, DAGs are generalizations of simple path models (Pearl, 2009). In recent years, DAGs have gained increasing attention in psychological research (Rohrer, 2018) and in educational technology research (Weidlich et al., 2024). While DAGs are primarily discussed and used for causal inference in observational studies (Rohrer, 2018), they are also highly informative for guiding analysis strategies in experimental research.

Furthermore, we assume that learning behavior during the first stage influences learning behavior during the second stage (arrow 3). This is because there are likely unobserved student characteristics, such as conscientiousness, self-regulated learning skills, or related traits, that exert causal influence on learning behavior across both stages. For the purposes of illustration, we represent these shared influences by a direct arrow from first-stage to secondstage learning behavior. Finally, we also consider the issue of adherence. In both intervention stages, the intervention dose students receive is not only dependent on the scheduled intervention dose but also on students' adherence to the study protocol (arrows 4 and 5). For instance, we could imagine that the intervention was delivered via a digital application (app). Thus, adherence could mean that students need to engage with the app as intended to receive the intervention. It is likely that adherence is also related across stages, for instance due to stable individual differences in app engagement. A comprehensive treatment of DAG methodology is beyond the scope of the present article, and clear and accessible overviews are available elsewhere (see, for example, Rohrer, 2018). For our purposes, we focus on the central concepts of back-door paths and confounding. We assume that most educational psychologists are already familiar with the general idea of confounders. In DAGs, confounding is defined in terms of paths rather than individual variables. Back-door paths are "paths that start with an arrow pointing to the independent variable and end with an arrow pointing to the dependent variable", such paths "indicate that there might be a common factor affecting both the treatment and the outcome" (Rohrer, 2018, p. 31).

Open back-door paths introduce bias and thus must be blocked to obtain unbiased causal estimates. A confounder, in this framework, can be understood as a variable on such a path that -when appropriately conditioned on -"blocks" the path (i.e., removes the association transmitted along that path). The second open back-door path arises through adherence across the two intervention stages. Specifically, stage-2 intervention dose received is influenced by stage-2 adherence, which is influenced by stage-1 adherence. Stage-1 adherence, in turn, influences stage-1 intervention dose received, which affects stage-1 learning behavior and, subsequently, stage-2 learning behavior. This path can also be blocked by conditioning on stage-1 learning behavior. In the simplified example shown in Figure 2, therefore, conditioning on stage-1 learning behavior is sufficient to obtain an unbiased estimate of the total causal effect of stage-2 intervention dose received on stage-2 learning behavior. However, note that this need not be the case, as different designs or causal assumptions may imply different adjustment sets. For instance, in some cases it may be plausible to assume that there are learner characteristics that affect both stage-2 adherence and stage-2 learning behavior. In this case, conditioning on stage-1 learning behavior alone would not block this additional back-door path. We return to this possibility and explore its implications in the empirical study.

As should be evident by now, identifying which variables should be conditioned on can quickly become complex. Fortunately, software tools are now available to support this process, including R packages specifically designed for working with DAGs (Textor et al., 2017). Such tools can help identify appropriate adjustment sets and clarify which variables must be conditioned on to obtain unbiased estimates.

Our aim is not to exhaustively catalog all possible sources of bias in sequential experimental designs, or in experimental designs more generally. This task would be infeasible given the diversity of designs and research questions. The strength of DAGs lies precisely in their flexibility as a general methodological approach that educational researchers can adapt to their own study designs and research questions. Thus, they enable principled reasoning about causal inference across a wide range of empirical contexts. One such context is digital prompting interventions (Breitwieser et al., 2026; Thomann & Deutscher, 2025), in which students are prompted to use self-regulation or learning strategies. These interventions are particularly well suited to adaptive designs because the type and intensity of support may need to be tailored to students' changing levels of competence. One promising target for such adaptive digital prompts are implementation intentions, a planning strategy that can help students translate learning goals into action.

1.5 Implementation intentions: A planning strategy requiring adaptive support?

Planning is an integral part of self-regulated learning (SRL; see Zimmerman, 2002). In recent years, a specific type of planning, prominent in the health and behavior change literature, has been introduced to SRL research: implementation intentions (Gollwitzer, 1999). Implementation intentions link a situational cue to a goal-directed action, typically using an if/when-then structure (e.g., "When I have finished lunch, then I will study vocabulary"). This cue-action link increases the accessibility of the situational cue and the automatization of the specified action, similar to habits (Gollwitzer, 2014).

Meta-analytic findings show the effectiveness of implementation intentions in helping individuals, including children, achieve their goals (Breitwieser & Reinelt, 2026; Sheeran et al., 2024). In academic contexts, implementation intentions have been used successfully to help young students develop regular study routines (Breitwieser et al., 2023). However, other studies in the multimedia learning context have produced mixed results (cf. Hoch et al., 2020; Stalbovs et al., 2015). Furthermore, in implementation intention research, individuals are typically provided with a well-formulated if-then plan they are supposed to enact (see Breitwieser & Reinelt, 2026). However, to use them as an SRL strategy, students should learn to formulate their own plans and to apply this strategy flexibly to their goal striving. It is reasonable to expect that this is much more challenging than enacting a prespecified plan, particularly for younger students.

External prompts provide additional support by reminding students to engage in this planning process. In our own research in the context of a mobile intervention, students received repeated mobile prompts reminding them of an implementation intention they had formulated at the beginning of the intervention. These prompts increased the likelihood that students engaged in vocabulary study on the days on which they received a prompt (Breitwieser et al., 2023). Thus, repeated prompting can provide an external aid that helps students enact an implementation intention.

Research on how such prompts should be designed is sparse, as most studies are about one-off planning (Breitwieser & Reinelt, 2026). In our previous study, students were always reminded of the same plan, which may strengthen this plan's cue-action link (Breitwieser et al., 2023). However, there are also good reasons for giving students the opportunity to decide for themselves whether they stick with a plan or adapt it to changing circumstances and to their own experience with what works for them. For example, a plan that is suitable for a school day may not be appropriate on a weekend, or students may discover that a particular situational cue or study time is ineffective. Revising a plan can therefore help establish a cue-action link that is better suited to students' current circumstances.

Repeated prompts can thus provide opportunities to reaffirm or revise implementation intentions as needed.

The rationale for adapting the intensity of such planning prompts follows from the idea that the need for external support may differ between students and change over time. Prompts can provide external support for behaviors that students do not yet initiate independently (Bannert, 2009). Students who have not yet established effective study routines and who do not yet independently use implementation intentions to establish these routines may therefore benefit from more frequent prompts. These prompts could help them gain experience in initiating strategy use and practicing the formulation of implementation intentions. Conversely, students who already use implementation intentions on their own and have established effective study routines may no longer require external support. In line with the literature on scaffolding and fading, support should be gradually reduced as learners become more capable of independently performing the target behavior (Van de Pol et al., 2010). If prompts are provided to students who no longer need them, they could lead to habituation (Muench & Baumel, 2017), over-reliance (Nobbe et al., 2024), or expertise reversal effects (Nückles et al., 2010). Thus, the intensity of support should be adapted to students' changing needs. To our knowledge, adapting intervention intensity in the context of implementation intentions has not yet been systematically investigated.

1.6 The present study

In the present study, we apply the methodological approach developed above -using DAG-guided reasoning to evaluate adaptivity decisions in sequential experimental designs-to an empirical case involving a mobile planning intervention designed to support students' vocabulary study. The experimental design followed a two-stage sequential structure. In the first stage, all students received mobile planning prompts with a fixed probability. The prompts asked students to formulate an implementation intention for their vocabulary study on the following day. They could either formulate a new plan or re-enter a plan they had formulated previously.

Students' study activity during this stage served as the tailoring variable for the second-stage intervention. Based on their observed behavior, students were classified as either high or low in study activity.

In the second stage, students with high and low study activity were randomized to either a condition with reduced or increased prompting probability, respectively, or a condition in which the prompting probability remained unchanged.

Due to the probabilistic assignment, there was both between-and within-group variation in the frequency of assigned prompts. Furthermore, it is important to note that the sequential two-stage intervention was embedded within an MRT. On each day, and for each student, randomization determined whether a planning prompt was delivered or not. The present study focuses on the between-student effects of this adaptive intervention. For the results of the MRT see Schaaf et al. (2025).

The complex sequential design of this study, which involves two intervention stages and probabilistic assignment of intervention dose, as well as its naturalistic context provide an ideal test case for examining the challenges involved in estimating the causal effects of an adaptive educational intervention. The target of the intervention is to support students in regulating their vocabulary study through the use of implementation intentions. The adaptive component of the intervention is the frequency of external prompts providing this support. We thus address the following research questions (RQs): RQ1: Does the frequency of planning prompts in stage 1 affect study activity in stage 1?

RQ2: Does the frequency of planning prompts in stage 2 affect study activity in stage 2? RQ3: Does the effect of received prompt frequency in stage 2 on study behavior during stage 2 depend on prior intervention history or prior study behavior?

Thus, RQ1 and RQ2 address effects within each intervention stage, whereas RQ3 addresses core adaptivity questions by examining effects across intervention stages. To address these questions, we distinguish between effects of the assigned prompt schedule and effects of prompts actually received. This distinction yields six specific causal effects of interest (see Table 3), which we elaborate further using the DAG-guided approach.

2.1 Participants

We recruited German-speaking fifth and sixth graders in the spring of 2023.

Prior to the official start of the study, interested families were offered a one-month premium subscription to the cabuu vocabulary app (https://www.cabuu.app/) to see if they enjoyed using it and to reduce the novelty effect of the app. Of the 577 students who activated this trial subscription, 444 later provided informed consent for the study. Students were included in the study if they used both the vocabulary app and our intervention app at least once during the first 20 days of the study period, and if they were in grade 5 or 6 (n = 359). Two students were excluded due to technical difficulties with the intervention app, leaving us with a final sample of 357 students (53.78% female; one student identified as diverse). The mean age was 11.63 years (SD = 0.69), with 206 students in Grade 5 (57.87%) and 150 in Grade 6 (42.13%); for one student, both grades were reported. Students were rewarded with a gift card to an online store, the value of which ranged from 50 cents to 25 euros, depending on the level of participation. The use of the vocabulary app was not incentivized. Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of DIPF | Leibniz Institute for Research and Information in Education (DIPF_EK_2023_12, 17th of April 2023) and the study was conducted in accordance with the human subjects guidelines and principles.

2.2 Design and procedure

The study involved two smartphone apps: an intervention app developed by the authors and a vocabulary learning app. The vocabulary app supported English-German vocabulary learning using evidence-based techniques (e.g., retrieval practice and adaptive repetition). Students studied three vocabulary lists over three 21-day intervention stages. At the end of each stage (i.e., days 21, 42, and 63), the students were instructed to complete a vocabulary test in the vocabulary app. The test data are not part of the current analyses. A more detailed description of the functionality of the vocabulary app, including screenshots, is provided in Schaaf et al. (2025).

The intervention app delivered study instructions, daily surveys for experience sampling (data not part of the present study), and the experimental manipulation, which consisted of videos and scheduled prompts encouraging students to formulate implementation intentions (Gollwitzer, 1999). Specifically, students were prompted to specify in the app when and where they would use the vocabulary app the following day. The then-part of the plan was prespecified as "then I will study with cabuu!"

Figure 3 shows the study procedure. The study started with an onboarding session during which students watched instructional videos on the importance of regular, distributed vocabulary practice and on the planning strategy of implementation intentions. They were prompted to create an initial plan specifying when they would use the vocabulary app the following day. Furthermore, students were instructed to use the intervention app daily after 6 p.m. Each day, students were sent a push notification, prompting them to open the intervention app, where they then completed a short survey on procrastination and motivation (data not part of the present study). Afterwards, if scheduled, they received the planning prompt, asking them to create an implementation intention for the next day, followed by a brief activity to internalize this intention. Students could either create an entirely new plan or re-enter the plan they had formulated previously. This gave students the flexibility to adapt their plans to changing circumstances as needed. If the intervention app was not opened on a given day, the prompt was not delivered. App logs recorded whether a student actually viewed the prompt on a given day.

Planning prompts were delivered probabilistically via daily microrandomization. During Stage 1 (days 0-20), all students had a probability of .50 of receiving a planning prompt on any given day. At the end of Stage 1, students were classified based on their observed study behavior in the vocabulary app. The decision point after 20 days was chosen for pragmatic reasons, balancing the need to observe students' study behavior over a sufficiently long period and to keep the overall study duration manageable. Students who studied vocabulary on 18-20 days were classified as having high study activity, whereas those with fewer days were classified as low study activity. The threshold of 18 days was chosen based on the median number of days with study activity observed across all participating students during Stage 1, thus providing a data-driven basis for the adaptive assignment. At the beginning of Stage 2, students were then randomized to one of two prompt schedules: either they maintained the prompt probability of .50, or the probability changed to .15 for students with high study activity or .85 for students with low study activity. The specific probabilities were chosen pragmatically to create a large and practically feasible contrast in prompting intensity between the conditions. Following Stage 2 (days 21-41), a follow-up phase (days 42-62) began during which the intervention app was no longer used and no planning prompts were delivered, although students were encouraged to continue using the vocabulary app (data not part of the present study). Specifically, we expected that students with low study activity in stage 1 benefit from receiving more prompts in stage 2, whereas students with high study activity in stage 1 do not benefit from additional prompts and may achieve a comparable study activity even when the frequency of prompts is reduced.

Despite their relevance, such experimental approaches have only recently begun to enter educational research (Chow & Hampton, 2022). One reason may be the limited number of examples demonstrating how these designs can inform concrete adaptivity decisions. Moreover, their methodological complexity may hinder adoption, leading researchers to rely on more familiar approaches such as ATI.

Although analytic strategies for MRTs and SMARTs are well documented in the health sciences (Nahum-Shani et al., 2012; Qian et al., 2020; Yeaton, 2024), they remain relatively unfamiliar in educational research.

DAGs are already familiar to many researchers in education and psychology, particularly in the context of observational data (Rohrer, 2018; Weidlich et al., 2024).

However, they are less frequently applied in experimental research, perhaps because randomization is assumed to guarantee unbiased causal estimation. In complex experimental settings, this assumption can be misleading, as our empirical study has demonstrated. DAGs have the advantage of being a flexible analytical tool that can be applied across a wide range of experimental designs. The present study, for example, did not follow a classic SMART design but combined a multi-week twostage design with an MRT, potentially representing the first instance of this particular design combination. DAGs can be readily used to examine its causal structure, thus allowing other researchers to scrutinize, question, or refine these assumptions rather than leaving them implicit in statistical models.

DAGs help ensure that conclusions are supported by the experimental data rather than based on biased estimates. For instance, unlike in the medical and health sciences, researchers in education and psychology often do not clearly distinguish between intention-to-treat and exposure effects (e.g., Breitwieser & Reinelt, 2026). In practice, researchers may attempt to estimate exposure effects by excluding participants who did not adhere to the intervention protocol. This approach can introduce selection bias, as adherent and non-adherent participants are likely to differ systematically (Torgerson & Torgerson, 2003). In our study, such individual differences were represented by a latent factor we termed App engagement tendency. Including this factor in the DAG made its confounding role explicit, and analyses informed by this structure helped avoid biased estimates.

In sequential designs, confounding can also arise when estimating intentionto-treat effects. A clear illustration of this issue can be seen in our study for the intention-to-treat effect of stage-2 prompts scheduled on stage-2 study activity. Due to the sequential nature of the design, this effect is confounded by stage-1 study activity. A failure to control for stage-1 study activity in the statistical model (i.e., leaving stage-2 prompts scheduled as the only predictor) leads to a highly statistically significant negative effect estimate (b = -0.05, SE = .01, p < .001). This would suggest that more prompts scheduled in stage 2 are counterproductive, whereas the unbiased estimate is slightly positive and not statistically significant (see Model 3 in Table 5). This example highlights how explicitly modeling the causal structure of sequential interventions is essential for obtaining valid causal conclusions.

These remarks should not be taken to imply that DAGs guarantee correct or precise causal effect estimates. The validity of a DAG-based analysis depends critically on the correctness of the underlying causal assumptions, for example, the assumption that there are no other unmeasured confounders influencing both the number of prompts received and study activity in stage 2. If such assumptions are violated, the resulting estimates may still be biased despite careful adjustment.

Furthermore, DAGs are non-parametric: they encode assumptions about causal structure, but they do not impose assumptions about functional form or model specification. For instance, a DAG does not determine whether an interaction effect should be modeled additively or multiplicatively (Attia et al., 2022). Such decisions must be made at the level of statistical modeling and require additional substantive and methodological judgment. Thus, while DAGs clarify which variables need to be adjusted for to identify a causal effect, they do not specify how the effect should be parameterized.

Even when appropriate adjustment sets are identified, obtaining precise and unbiased estimates also depends on the reliable measurement of key variables, including tailoring variables (Chow & Hampton, 2022). In our case, we relied on comparatively objective, high-resolution log data capturing students' study behavior within the vocabulary app. Nevertheless, even such data are not immune to potential issues, such as data transmission errors that result in incomplete logging.

Taken together, DAGs do not eliminate broader challenges related to model specification and measurement quality. However, they clarify the assumptions required to identify unconfounded causal effects. Thus, they support rigorous evaluations of adaptive interventions, such as the one examined in this study.

4.2 Recommendations for planning and analyzing sequential experimental designs

Based on our experiences in the present study, we offer five recommendations for planning and analyzing sequential experimental designs. First, the causal structure of the design should be specified before data collection, so that researchers can establish which effects the planned design allows them to estimate and which it does not. In the planning stage, non-identifiable effects could still be made identifiable, for instance by including the assessment of additional variables. If, for instance, we had not logged students' use of the intervention app, we would have had no measure of availability.

This would have made it impossible to answer research questions 1b and 2b. Second, assigned intervention options should be distinguished from received intervention. Particularly when interventions are tested in naturalistic contexts and delivered via technology, adherence to the assigned intervention is typically less than perfect. Researchers should therefore be explicit about which effect they seek to estimate and represent assigned and received intervention options as distinct variables in the causal structure. Third, and relatedly, the assumed causes of non-adherence such as participant availability or engagement should be represented in the causal graph. Doing so reveals which comparisons are confounded and clarifies why excluding non-adherent participants is not a valid alternative.

Fourth, adjustment sets should be derived formally to determine which variables must be conditioned on to obtain valid causal estimates, based on the assumed causal structure. In sequential designs, the relevant confounders are rarely obvious, and conditioning on inappropriate variables can introduce bias rather than remove it.

Fifth, the assumptions underlying the estimates should be reported alongside the analyses and results -for example in the form of the DAG. This allows readers to identify which assumptions the conclusions depend on and to assess the implications if a particular assumption does not hold.

4.3 Implications for the development of an adaptive planning intervention

Beyond its methodological contribution, the present study also offers substantive insights into how mobile planning support should be adapted to student needs. Previous research suggests that planning with implementation intentions can be an effective self-regulation strategy for children (Breitwieser & Reinelt, 2026).

However, emerging evidence indicates that such strategies are not universally effective. Specifically, analyses of within-person effects from the micro-randomized component of the present study showed that planning prompts were beneficial only when students generated plans of sufficient quality (Schaaf et al., 2025). When plans failed to meet theory-informed criteria -for example, when they were formulated too vaguely -students were no more likely to study on the following day than when they had used the intervention app without receiving a planning prompt.

The current analyses of the sequential two-stage design extend this perspective to the between-person level. Here, we did not examine the immediate effect of receiving versus not receiving a prompt, but rather the impact of differences in prompt frequency across students and across intervention stages. The findings of the present study provide important insights for the development of an effective and adaptive mobile planning intervention beyond what the MRT analyses alone revealed.

We found that students benefited overall from receiving a higher number of prompts in stage 2. However, this association depended on prior exposure: Students who had received relatively few prompts in stage 1 benefited from receiving more prompts in stage 2. In contrast, students who had already received many prompts in stage 1 benefited more from fading out support. One possible explanation is that, beyond a certain point, prompts become disruptive rather than helpful: Students may have already practiced plan formation enough to develop a consistent study routine, such that additional prompts would provide limited benefit and could even interfere with their established plan. This interpretation is consistent with the expertise reversal effect, which shows that instructional support that benefits less experienced learners can become redundant or counterproductive as learners become more proficient (Kalyuga, 2007; Nückles et al., 2010). For example, Nückles et al. (2010) found that gradually fading prompts supported more independent strategy use than providing them permanently. Similarly, scaffolding research emphasizes that external support should be adapted to learners' developing capabilities and gradually withdrawn as they become more capable of managing tasks on their own (Van de Pol et al., 2010). However, our study did not directly assess changes in independent strategy use and SRL competence, so this interpretation remains tentative.

The findings of the moderation analyses further suggest that not all students require the same level of support. Specifically, the positive effect of prompts in stage 2 was stronger for students with lower study activity in stage 1; for students with already high study activity, prompts had slightly diminishing returns. This pattern is consistent with the rationale that external prompts may be particularly useful for students who have difficulty independently initiating the target behavior (Bannert, 2009). For students who already engage in frequent study, additional prompts may offer less incremental benefit because the target behavior is already relatively well established. In contrast, students who did not initially exhibit strong study routines appeared to benefit from more frequent prompting to engage in planning. This finding aligns with prior research showing that mobile planning interventions are particularly effective when planning is combined with frequent plan reminders (Breitwieser et al., 2023). Taken together, the results suggest that adaptive planning interventions may be most effective when higher intervention doses are targeted toward students who demonstrate lower baseline study activity.

Furthermore, our findings clearly underscore the importance of developing measures to increase engagement with the mobile intervention. This is an issue also extensively discussed in mobile health research (Nahum-Shani, Shaw, et al., 2022).

In the present study, low engagement with the intervention app substantially influenced the relationship between assigned prompt frequency and actual exposure, thereby attenuating intention-to-treat effects. One could question whether an intervention is worth much practically if it is only effective under ideal circumstances.

Thus, our findings highlight that intervention development cannot focus solely on optimizing intervention components but must also address practical challenges of engagement. Increasing students' perceived usefulness of the app, its ease of use, and their intrinsic enjoyment in using it may be critical for fostering consistent engagement (Amaefule et al., 2023; Venkatesh et al., 2003). In school-based settings, teacher support may play an additional and important role, for instance by encouraging app use or by embedding the intervention within classroom routines.

Taken together, our findings suggest that digital planning interventions should adapt the intensity of support to students' study behavior over time, increasing external support for students who struggle to initiate the target behavior and reducing it for those who already engage in it. For example, teachers could periodically check in with students about their study routines and recommend tools such as the PROMPT app (https://prompt-lernplan.app/) to provide regular prompts for those who need additional support. However, low study activity may reflect different underlying barriers, from lack of self-regulated learning skills to a lack of motivation or misaligned goals. Teachers should therefore consider the reasons for students' low engagement and adapt support accordingly.

4.4 Limitations and future directions

While our study provides important insights for the development of adaptive interventions, several limitations warrant consideration. First, the theoretical basis for our adaptivity decision rule was preliminary. The logic of increasing dose for nonresponders was guided by prior empirical findings demonstrating the effectiveness of planning prompts (e.g., Breitwieser et al., 2023). Providing more of an effective intervention to students who appear to require additional support seems plausible.

However, it is equally plausible that the assumption "more is more" does not universally hold. Some non-responders may have been fundamentally disengaged, such that increasing prompt frequency alone would not address the underlying barrier. Future research should therefore examine barriers to engagement more systematically and derive adaptivity decision rules from a fully articulated theory of change (Nahum-Shani & Almirall, 2019). For example, if barriers are primarily motivational, adapting the type of prompt (rather than or in addition to dose) may prove more effective.

Second, the timing of the adaptivity decision (after 20 days) was primarily pragmatically motivated rather than theoretically informed. Determining optimal decision points remains an open challenge in adaptive intervention research.

Emerging methodological approaches allow for post hoc evaluation of alternative decision time points (Dziak & Nahum-Shani, 2026). Thus, future studies should investigate optimal decision timing more systematically. The same applies to the classification of students as having high versus low study activity: in the present study, we relied on a simple median split for pragmatic reasons, yet more nuanced and theoretically grounded approaches, such as tailoring variables that also take into account students' academic performance and SRL skills, may yield more precise and effective adaptation rules.

Third, the observed effect sizes were rather small. While modest effects are common in interventions implemented in naturalistic educational settings (Kraft, 2020), they underscore the need for cautious interpretation and replication. In particular, the interaction with stage-1 study activity was statistically significant but comparatively small. One could further argue that adapting to prior study activity may not be strictly necessary because simply providing a high number of prompts might yield benefits for all students, albeit to different degrees. However, it may still be advisable to avoid unnecessarily high prompting frequencies, as our other results suggest that additional prompts could be counterproductive once students have already received a high number of prompts.

4.5 Conclusion

This study addresses a problem at the heart of adaptive educational interventions: how to obtain valid causal evidence about adaptivity decisions. We introduced a methodological approach that combines sequential experimental designs with directed acyclic graphs (DAGs). Sequential designs provide the experimental basis for evaluating adaptivity decisions, while DAGs make the causal assumptions underlying such designs explicit, allowing researchers to identify sources of bias, derive appropriate adjustment sets, and expose their assumptions to scrutiny rather than leaving them implicit in statistical models. 3.3 Adjustment sets for causal effect estimation

We now turn to our substantive research questions and identify the variables that must be controlled for to obtain unbiased causal effect estimates. To address the three overarching research questions, we distinguish six specific causal effects of interest, labeled RQ1a-RQ3b in Table 3. These distinguish between the effects of prompts scheduled and prompts received. The corresponding adjustment sets for each effect estimate are summarized in Table 3.

As shown in the table, only the intention-to-treat effect (i.e., the effect of prompts scheduled) within stage 1 (RQ1a) requires no adjustment, as treatment assignment at this stage is fully randomized. In contrast, the intention-to-treat effect within stage 2 (RQ2a) requires adjustment because randomization at this stage is conditional on students' study activity in stage 1; accordingly, stage-1 study activity must be included in the adjustment set.

To illustrate the logic of adjustment in more detail, consider RQ2b, which concerns the effect of stage-2 prompts received on stage-2 study activity. In this case, two back-door paths are open. First, the number of prompts received in stage 2 is influenced by the number of prompts scheduled, which is itself not fully randomized but depends on students' study activity in stage 1. Second, prompts received are also affected by stage-2 availability, that is, students' use of the intervention app as a prerequisite for receiving the scheduled prompts. To block these back-door paths, both must be closed through appropriate adjustment. This requires including one variable from each path in the adjustment set. Since both stage-1 study activity and stage-2 prompts scheduled lie on the same path, either variable can be used for adjustment.
