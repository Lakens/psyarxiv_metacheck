Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Social influence is widely recognized as an important determinant of food consumption, yet evidence on reducing animal-based food consumption is dispersed across disciplines, study designs, and social influence concepts. This mixed-methods scoping review provides a comprehensive overview of 124 studies assessing social influence on reducing or lower animal-based food consumption in upper-middle and high-income countries since 2000. We systematically searched seven databases for studies examining social influences on animal-based food consumption and the adoption or maintenance of reduced animal-based diets. Quantitative and qualitative evidence was synthesized separately and subsequently integrated.

This growing body of literature remains concentrated in specific countries and demographic groups. The quantitative synthesis (N = 73) revealed 10 studied social factors, of which social norms are most often studied, with stronger evidence emerging from observational than experimental research. Qualitative studies (N = 51) revealed the social processes and contexts through which dietary change occurs, including negotiation of conflicting preferences, practical and emotional support, social pressure, and the influence of shared meals and social contexts. The integrative synthesis suggests that social influence is best understood as a multifaceted and context-dependent phenomenon operating across interpersonal and broader environmental levels.

Future work should seek to better understand how multiple forms of social influence interact across contexts and populations, and should prioritize creating conceptual clarity when describing these phenomena. Doing so will likely require greater use of mixed-methods, social networks, and context-sensitive approaches capable of capturing the complex social realities through which dietary change occurs in daily life.

Social Influence on Reducing Animal-Based Food Consumption: A Mixed-Methods Scoping Review

What people choose to eat and drink impacts planetary and public health. The consumption of animal-based foods (i.e., meat or other animal-based foods such as dairy or eggs) is associated with substantial environmental impact, while red and processed meat consumption is also linked to adverse health outcomes (Clark et al., 2019; Ritchie et al., 2022; Scarborough et al., 2023; Springmann et al., 2018). Despite a growing awareness for the need to reduce animal-based food intake and increasing interest in plant-based diets (The Vegan Society, 2025), animal-based foods remain a substantial part of diets, particularly in middleand high-income countries (OECD & Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations, 2025). Therefore, effective strategies to reduce their consumption are urgently needed to promote public and planetary health (Willett et al., 2019).

Meaningful reductions in animal-based food consumption are unlikely to be achieved through modifying isolated, individual choices alone. It will likely require shifts in the broader social environment, as eating is fundamentally a social act (Cruwys et al., 2015; De Vaal et al., 2026; Higgs, 2015; Mata, 2026), determining what, how much, and how often individuals eat in general (Cruwys et al., 2015; Higgs, 2015; Higgs & Ruddock, 2020; Hunter et al., 2019; Salvy et al., 2012; Stok et al., 2016; van den Broek et al., 2020; Vartanian et al., 2015). An emerging number of studies indicate that social influence also plays a crucial role in reducing the intake of animal-based products, specifically (Alblas et al., 2023; Severijns et al., 2023 Severijns et al., , 2025)). Understanding the breadth of these social influence factors is essential for designing and testing interventions to encourage reduced animal-based food consumption (Hunter et al., 2019; Valente, 2012). However, so far, no comprehensive overview exists of the social factors that influence the reduction of animal-based food intake.

In this study, we present a scoping review of social influence factors involved in reducing animal-based food consumption, aiming to provide an overview of what is currently known, how this topic has been studied, and where important knowledge gaps remain. This approach is particularly appropriate compared to a systematic review, as the literature in this area is diverse, emerging, and conceptually complex rather than focused on a single welldefined social factor or dietary outcome (Arksey & and O'Malley, 2005; Levac et al., 2010; Munn et al., 2018; Peterson et al., 2017). This review adopts a mixed-methods approach that synthesizes and integrates both quantitative and qualitative evidence, combining evidence on quantified social factors associated with reducing animal-based food consumption with insights into the social processes and contexts through which such reductions occur. This scoping review provides a comprehensive overview of studies assessing social influence on reducing or lower animal-based food consumption in upper-middle and highincome countries since 2000. To obtain an overview of the included quantitative and qualitative studies, we assessed the study characteristics (i.e., research design, country, and year of publication), participant characteristics (i.e., sample size, age, gender distribution, and dietary pattern), and type of food intake studied as the outcome in the included studies. We furthermore assessed which social influence factors were studied (for included quantitative studies) and which social processes, mechanisms, and/or contexts were studied (for included qualitative studies). Collectively, the aim was to identify the available evidence and aid the identification of gaps in the available knowledge and evidence.

Method Design

The protocol for this scoping review was registered on the Open Science Framework (OSF) on the July 3, 2025 (https://osf.io/hqwj8). The protocol was informed by the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) methodological guidance for scoping reviews (Peters et al., 2024) and adheres to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analysis extension for scoping reviews (PRISMA-ScR; Tricco et al., 2018), see Supplemental Table A.

Search Strategy

As recommended (Mak & Thomas, 2022), the search strategy was constructed with the help of a librarian and validated using three pre-selected articles (Alblas et al., 2023; Severijns et al., 2023 Severijns et al., , 2025)). A three-step search strategy was used (Peters et al., 2024). First, an initial, limited search on PsycINFO and Web of Science was undertaken to identify articles on the topic. The words in the titles and abstracts, and the index terms used to describe relevant articles were used to develop a full search strategy for PsycINFO. Second, the search strategy was adapted for each database (i.e., Web of Science (via Clarivate), PsycINFO (via Ovid), Medline (PubMed, via Ovid), Embase (via Ovid), Sociological Abstracts (via ProQuest), IBSS (via ProQuest), and ERIC (via Ovid)). Supplemental Text A shows the full search strategy, which was conducted on the June 18, 2025. Third, a forward and backward citation search was conducted in November and December 2025, where reference and citation lists of included sources were inspected for additional studies (Hirt et al., 2024).

Study Selection

Exported references from the seven databases were imported in Rayyan (https://rayyan.ai; Ouzzani et al., 2016) for deduplication and screening (Janka & Metzendorf, 2024; McKeown & Mir, 2021). Two pilot tests were conducted in May and June 2025, respectively, to test the screening tool and improve interrater agreement on a random sample of 100 and 50 articles, respectively (Polanin et al., 2019). Following the pilot test, titles and abstracts were screened in July and August 2025 for eligibility against the inclusion criteria using the pilot-tested final title and abstract screening tool. Screening of abstracts was done by four reviewers (NB, RS, JL, MR), with one reviewer (MR) screening 100% of the articles independently and the other three reviewers (NB, RS, JL) screening a randomized sample of 50%, 33% and 17% respectively, resulting in each title and abstract being screened by two independent reviewers. The overall weighted interrater agreement was k = .65 across all dyads, corresponding to "substantial" agreement (Cohen, 1960; McHugh, 2012). Conflicts were resolved by discussion between the two independent reviewers. A summary of the conflicts, discussions, and resolved outcomes, as well as the interrater agreement scores for each dyad separately can be found in the Supplemental Excel File A.

The remaining sources were retrieved in full-text and imported into Rayyan for fulltext screening, which took place in August and September 2025. One reviewer (MR) screened 100% of the articles independently, and three other reviewers (NB, RS, JL) screened a random sample of 33.33% each. Interrater agreement was substantial across dyads: k = .70 (Cohen, 1960; McHugh, 2012). Conflicts in inclusion choice and in exclusion reasons were resolved by discussion between the two independent reviewers. A summary of the discussions as well as the interrater agreement scores for each dyad separately can be found in the Supplemental Excel File A. The two pilot test versions and the title and abstract version of our screening tool can be found in our OSF protocol. The final screening tool used for the full-text screening can be found in Supplemental Text B, reflecting our inclusion criteria for population, concept, context, and evidence source (Peters et al., 2024).

Data Extraction

Data from the included papers were extracted using a data extraction form and accompanying guidance form (see Supplemental Text C). Informed by existing guidance (Peters et al., 2024; Pollock et al., 2023; Robson et al., 2019), data extraction was performed by three reviewers (MR, NB, and RS). First, a pilot study was conducted in October 2025 in which data of 4 randomly selected studies (2 qualitative and 2 quantitative) were extracted by MR and NB. Conflicts were discussed and resolved, and relevance and feasibility of the outcomes and forms were discussed, after which the extraction form and instruction form were improved. Second, studies were approximately equally divided between the three extractors and data extraction took place from October 2025 to January 2026. Conflicts and uncertainties were resolved through discussion between the three extractors. Supplemental Excel File B shows the extracted data.

We deviated from our protocol by not contacting authors from included papers in which details (e.g., age (range) or gender) were missing to conserve resources. In line with the scoping review guidance of JBI and the PRISMA-ScR (Peters et al., 2024; Tricco et al., 2018), critical appraisal of individual sources of evidence was not conducted.

Data Analysis

Following data extraction, the extracted information remained heterogeneous and required further organization before it could be synthesized and integrated, as is common in scoping reviews (Pollock et al., 2023). The analysis proceeded in four parts: 1) descriptive synthesis of study and participant characteristics, 2) synthesis of findings from quantitative studies (3) thematic synthesis of findings from qualitative studies, and 4) integration of quantitative and qualitative evidence.

First, study and participant characteristics relevant to the pre-registered review questions were selected for synthesis. These included research design, country, year of publication, sample size, participant age, gender distribution, and dietary pattern of participants. Additionally, for the quantitative studies, measures of animal-based food consumption were further categorized into broader categories where appropriate, according to a predefined guidance form (Supplemental Text D).

Second, we synthesized the social influence factors investigated in the quantitative studies to develop a comprehensive overview of the social factors examined in relation to animal-based food consumption. Due to substantial variation in the operationalization, measurement, coding, and/or manipulation of social influence across studies, the extracted descriptions of these measures were subjected to qualitative content analysis (Hsieh & Shannon, 2005) in ATLAS.ti (web version 9.26.0). NB and MR grouped conceptually similar measures into broader social factor categories, presented in Supplemental Text D and described in the Results section.

Third, findings from the qualitative studies were synthesized using a method inspired by thematic synthesis (Thomas & Harden, 2008). NB and MR coded extracted findings and authors' interpretations relating to social influence processes, mechanisms, and contexts in ATLAS.ti (web version 9.26.0). Consistent with the aims of evidence synthesis, and deviating from Thomas and Harden (2008), coding was conducted on extracted study findings and authors' interpretations rather than primary qualitative data. Themes were developed iteratively through repeated discussion and refinement until they adequately captured the diversity within the extracted data. The final themes are presented in the Results section, and the coding tree is provided in Supplemental Text E. Fourth, findings from the quantitative and qualitative syntheses were integrated using a convergent segregated approach (Stern et al., 2020). After presenting the two evidence streams first separately (as reflected in analysis stage 2 and 3), findings were integrated during interpretation to identify areas of convergence, complementarity, divergence, and expansion (Fetters, 2020).

Social Influence Measures and Findings

The content analysis resulted in 10 main categories of social factors (see Figure 6, Supplemental Text D, and Table 1a). In the following section, we describe the content of these categories and their respective sub-categories, ordered by prevalence, and provide a concise narrative synthesis of their associations with animal-based food intake.

Social norms.

The largest group of quantitative studies focused on social norms; in about half of the studies (n = 37; 50.7%) social norms were assessed or manipulated. Social norms are defined as average or aggregated estimates or perceptions of food-related expectations, behaviors, or patterns of a referent group, including descriptive norms reflecting the group's perceived food intake or dietary patterns (e.g., "18-25-year-olds eat meals containing meat"; Sharps et al., 2024) and injunctive norms reflecting the group's perceived approval or disapproval of such behaviors or patterns (e.g., "18-25-year-olds think that other 18-25-year-olds should eat meat"; Sharps et al., 2024). Descriptive norms (n = 33) were studied more often than injunctive norms (n = 14), particularly in experimental studies. Social norm perceptions were either assessed (as delineated in the examples by Sharps et al., 2024) or manipulated using messages to measure their effect on food choice (e.g., "Some people limit how much meat they eat"; Sparkman & Walton, 2017).

Other relevant distinctions in social norms are not included as sub-categories in Figure 6. For example, studies differed with regard to the target food intake behavior reflected in the norm. The social norms were formulated to capture or manipulate a wide range of foodrelated behaviors and patterns in participants' social environments. Specifically, norms referred to pro-meat (e.g., Aasen et al., 2024), pro-milk (e.g., Szczepanski et al., 2025), pro-legumes (e.g., Carfora & Catellani, 2022), pro-veg*n (i.e., vegetarian and vegan) food (e.g., Griesoph et al., 2021), pro-plant-based food (e.g., Lim et al., 2021), or pro-vegetables (e.g., Çoker et al., 2025) intake behaviors of others, or meat reduction (e.g., de Gavelle et al., 2019), meat avoidance (e.g., Wolfswinkel et al., 2024), meat replacement (e.g., Sparkman et al., 2020), or the prevalence of veg*ns (e.g., Dhont & Ioannidou, 2024) or meat eaters (e.g., Hielkema & Lund, 2021) in the referent group. Furthermore, the referent group mentioned in the social norms varied considerably, including friends (e.g., Sparkman et al., 2020); family (e.g., Rosenfeld et al., 2020); colleagues (e.g., Çoker et al., 2025); people in the local community (e.g., Rosenfeld et al., 2020); people in the same study, research institute, food outlet, or country (Reinholdsson et al., 2023; Sparkman & Walton, 2017; Zorell et al., 2024); other customers (e.g., Griesoph et al., 2021); people participants know (e.g., Hielkema & Lund, 2021); peers of the same age (e.g., Sharps et al., 2024); or the wider circle of friends and extended friends (Dhont & Ioannidou, 2024). The importance of this referent group is highlighted in studies that vary and compare referent groups within the same study (i.e., setting-dependent norms; e.g., Sharps et al., 2024). In one study, participants customized the assessed norms with personally relevant referent groups (Wolfswinkel et al., 2024). Furthermore, descriptive norms varied in their operationalization, with studies assessing either the perceived proportion of people in a referent group who engage in a behavior (e.g., "Most people I know eat red meat"; Vatn et al., 2022) or the perceived frequency with which the behavior occurs within a referent group (e.g., "How often do your friends eat meals containing meat"; Sharps et al., 2021). Another distinction found is that norms could be dynamic, static, or guessed. Most norms were static, describing the current proportion of people or frequency of a behavior (e.g., "Some people limit how much meat they eat"; Sparkman & Walton, 2017). Fewer studies examined dynamic descriptive norms, where changes in behavior or its prevalence over time are emphasized (e.g., "more and more people are reducing their consumption of meat"; Carfora et al., 2022). One study asked participants to guess the social norm rather than to show or assess it (Griesoph et al., 2021).

Other ways of customization or adaptation of norms, in this case by the researcher, were studies in which participants received feedback on norms (e.g., "above average" or "below average", depending on participants' own meat consumption relative to the average meat consumption of all participants; Alblas et al., 2023). Narrative summary of key findings: Observational studies generally found clear associations in the expected direction, such that lower norms around meat consumption were associated with lower intake or a higher likelihood of eating less meat. Studies reporting nonsignificant findings more often examined injunctive norms or norms communicated by specific social referents, although some studies also reported findings in the unexpected direction for descriptive norms. Experimental studies evaluating norm-based messages provided less consistent evidence. While some studies found that norm-based messages promoted meat reduction or increased plant-based choices, several experiments reported no effects on food choice or intake, or even effects in the opposite direction.

Reported eating behavior of others. This factor includes assessments of the eating behavior or dietary pattern of specific others in daily life (e.g., a family member, best friend, classmate). The reported eating behavior of others was measured in 16 studies (21.9%), including social network studies. In contrast to social norm measures, these measures referred directly to the diet of specific others. The measures differed on two dimensions. First, either food intake (e.g., friends' and best friends' dairy intake; Bruening et al., 2012) or dietary pattern (e.g., number of vegetarians in network; Bègue & Treich, 2019) was assessed. Second, either participants self-reported on the eating behavior of others (e.g., "How often does your parter consume cow's milk?" ; Szczepanski et al., 2025), as is common in egocentric social network studies (Severijns et al., 2023), or the social relationships reported on their own eating behavior (as shown by the example by Bruening et al., 2012), as is typical in sociocentric social network studies (e.g., Severijns et al., 2025). This resulted in four subcategories: self-reported food intake (n = 2 studies), self-reported dietary pattern (n = 9 studies), relationship-reported food intake (n = 4 studies), and relationship-reported dietary pattern (n = 2 studies). Narrative summary of key findings: Observational studies assessing the reported eating behaviors of others generally found positive cross-sectional and longitudinal associations with participants' own food intake or dietary pattern. One exception was a longitudinal social network study finding no association between college friends' animalbased food intake levels. Similar to the pattern of findings for social norms, however, experimental evidence was more limited and mixed. Some studies found no effect of providing information about specific others' eating behavior on subsequent food choice or intake, whereas others did.

Social presence. Social presence, defined as eating or choosing food in the physical presence of others, was examined in 10 studies (13.7%), with most using experimental designs (N = 7). The most-studied form of social presence was commensality (n = 5 studies), involving eating in the physical presence of others, such as friends, family, or colleagues (Horgan et al., 2019), household members (e.g., Biermann & Rau, 2020), guests (e.g., Biermann & Rau, 2020), or a romantic partner (Wang et al., 2023). Two studies examined observability, whereby food purchases or choices, such as selecting a food voucher, were either visible to others or kept private (Dannenberg et al., 2024; Dannenberg & Weingärtner, 2023). In the remaining studies, social presence involved another person's dietary pattern (n = 1) or specific food choice (n = 2). Experimental manipulations included the presence of a confederate identified as vegetarian during the choice of meat-and plant-based foods (Hammami et al., 2023, study 1) or by tracking the meal choice of the person ahead in line when ordering a meal (Christie & Chen, 2018; Hammami et al., 2023, study 2). While in most cases social presence was studied in-person, one study took place online (Wang et al., 2023). Narrative summary of key findings: Overall, studies on social presence indicated that eating in the company of others was associated with greater animal-based food intake (mostly meat) compared to eating alone. Experimental studies examining more specific forms of social presence yielded more mixed findings: while being observed by others did not affect food choice, another person's previous food choice or dietary pattern sometimes (counterintuitively) predicted participants' subsequent choices. One experimental study further found lower meat intake when participants were in the online presence of their romantic partner, suggesting that the effects of social presence may depend on the type of social relationship and the specific form of social presence. Social support. Social support was assessed in six studies (8.2%). Four observational studies examined the importance of social support, in which participants described the degree to which they feel a (lack of) support in (changes in) food intake patterns (e.g., a vegetarian or vegan diet; Dhont & Ioannidou, 2024). In the other two (experimental) studies, social relationships were involved when participants aimed to reduce animal-based products (Leon & Schobin, 2026; Severijns et al., 2024). Narrative summary of key findings: Overall, findings suggest that greater self-reported social support was associated with more (sustained) reductions in animal-based food intake and adherence to meat-free diets. Experimental findings were mixed; involving family and friends in an intervention was associated with a longer maintenance of decreased animalbased food intake, whereas another experiment involving friends versus non-friendship groups did not affect changes in meat consumption.

Social pressure. Social pressure was examined in five studies (6.8%) that were mainly cross-sectional observational (n = 4). It involves anticipated or experienced social reactions to (non-)compliance, such as stigmatization (Mayrhofer et al., 2024) or teasing (Liobikienė & Brizga, 2025), contingent to a specific intake behavior (e.g., eating less meat). Almost all studies examined this type of negative social pressure regarding reducing animal-based food intake; only one assessed positive social pressure ("Most of my family, friends or colleagues appreciate it when I eat little or no meat"; Liobikienė & Brizga, 2025). Narrative summary of key findings: Overall, findings suggest that negative social pressure may act as a barrier to reducing animal-based food intake or adopting a vegetarian diet, although this was often not tested for significance. As only one study examined positive social pressure and did so using a measure that combined social pressure with other social factors, no conclusions can be made about the specific effects of positive social pressure.

Self-reported influence. Five cross-sectional, observational studies (6.8%) assessed self-reported influence. In all studies, participants reported on whether sources of influence (e.g., family or friends; Andersson & Thondhlana, 2025) affected their food intake. In the study of Roy et al. (2021), participants also self-reported on the degree to which they listened to advice from others. Narrative summary of key findings: Participants often perceived their social relationships as a source of influence for both reducing and increasing animal-based food intake. Findings regarding differences across dietary groups were mixed: while one study found no differences between groups in perceived social influence, other studies reported that the types and sources of influence varied, with vegans reporting greater influence from nutrition and health experts and food influencers, and meat eaters reporting greater influence from family, friends, and celebrity cooks.

Social identity. Social identity was assessed in four studies (5.5%). Two of these studies assessed whether participants feel part of their specific dietary identity group (e.g., "I feel a bond with others who are [own dietary group]"; Cruwys et al., 2020) (social identification). In the other two studies, social identification with the ingroup (same dietary identity group) versus outgroup (other dietary identity group) was manipulated (Thurmer et al., 2024) or assessed as a barrier to eating more plant-based foods (Stancu et al., 2025). Narrative summary of key findings: Overall, observational studies found significant but mixed associations: while in one study stronger social identification was associated with greater adherence to a veg*n diet and lower animal-based intake, in the other study social identification motivations were revealed to be linked to less dietary adherence. The two experimental studies found no ingroup-outgroup effects on consumption.

Impression management.

Impression management was measured in three studies (4.1%) which assessed to what extent behavior is influenced by concerns about how participants will appear to others (e.g., "I eat what I eat because it makes me look good in front of others"; Mussig et al., 2022, both studies). This construct thus reflects social image and self-presentation, not social rules or approval. Narrative summary of key findings: Impression management was not found to be associated with dietary pattern or changes in animal-based food intake.

Social communication.

In three observational studies (4.1%), social communication was studied. This concerned assessing the amount of social interaction or receiving information on food-related topics (e.g., on the negative impacts of eating meat, dairy and/or eggs; Bryant et al., 2023) with or from others, such as veg*ns (Bryant et al., 2023) or "people around me" (de Gavelle et al., 2019), assessed in two studies. Two studies assessed persuasion, which refers to a more directive form of social communication, in which social relationships actively convince participants to (change) eat(ing) in a certain way (e.g., by asking participants about the number of people who had tried to convince them to change their eating habits over the past year; Lund & Halkier, 2024). Narrative summary of key findings: The few studies regarding social interaction did not indicate an association between exposure to vegetarians and vegans and subsequent dietary change. Findings on persuasion were more mixed: while perceived persuasion attempts from others to reduce meat consumption were reported less frequently by omnivores than meat reducers, persuasion was not linked to meat intake behavior.

Other.

Three "other" sub-categories did not fully fit with the main social factors categories, consisting of 8 studies (11.0%). These sub-categories were social ease (n = 2) reflecting the perceived (lack of) easiness of making a specific consumption choice or adopting a dietary pattern (e.g., "in most social situations, it is easiest to eat meat"; Hielkema & Lund, 2021). We also coded (the lack of) others' openness (n = 4) reflecting the extent to which others are open to eating less animal-based foods (e.g., "how willing are the people in your environment to reduce their meat consumption?" ; Stewart et al., 2022). Finally, motivation to conform (n = 4), refers to the motivation to comply with others (Linville et al., 2022), the desire to adhere to the expectations of others (Mussig et al., 2022) or follow social trends (Hodson & Earle, 2018), which were often combined with a social norm measure. Narrative summary of key findings: Overall, (mostly observational) findings were mixed, with some evidence that lower openness, lower ease, and higher motivation to conform were associated with dietary patterns or intake, whereas several studies found no differences or associations. The one experimental study did not find an association between openness and meat intake changes.

Part 3. Qualitative Study Results

Next, we report on findings from the qualitative studies (n = 51). We developed four themes with associated subthemes capturing various recurring social influence processes and contexts across the included qualitative studies (see Figure 7). The results reveal experiences of social influence on both daily food choices and the adoption and maintenance of a reduced animal-based diet (RABD), in this review defined as dietary patterns characterised by reducing or avoiding animal-based food consumption (e.g., a flexitarian or vegan diet).

Theme 1: Information, Social Support, and Community

The first theme reflects how the social environment can inspire reduced animal-based food consumption and can encourage and facilitate the adoption and maintenance of an RABD by providing information, demonstrating alternative food practices, and offering emotional and practical support. Conversely, limited access to social support can impede dietary change and maintenance.

Information and demonstration of feasible dietary change.

Information exchanged in the environment supportive of reduced animal-based food consumption can influence both daily food choices and the adoption of an RABD. Studies described that supportive information received through conversations and recommendations can influence daily food choices and can support participants to try new things (Bogueva et al., 2022; Gibson et al., 2023; Wendler, 2023). Media can inform individuals that an RABD can be healthy and tasteful which was described as a trigger for questioning established food practices andconsequently -for making reduced animal-based food choices or adopting an RABD (Larsson et al., 2003; Wendler, 2023; Williams et al., 2023; Yang et al., 2025). Aside from providing information, other people can demonstrate that an RABD is feasible in daily life.

Seeing someone in the environment or on social media successfully reducing animal-based food consumption, sharing practical strategies, or showing how to prepare tasty animal-based reduced dishes can help individuals feel empowered and capable of making a dietary change (Kemper, 2020; Lawo et al., 2020; Mylan, 2018; Sundet et al., 2023; Waters, 2022; Williams et al., 2023). Additionally, a social environment that is open and accepting towards reduced animal-based eating was described as making adoption easier (Kemper & White, 2021).

Experiences of social support. Beyond these initial triggers for adoption of an RABD, support is important for maintenance of an RABD and individuals describe a desire for a community. Participants described 6 receiving social support from a variety of sources, including family (e.g., Vezovnik & Kamin, 2024), friends (Williams et al., 2023), colleagues (e.g., Yoo & Yoon, 2015), like-minded (e.g., vegan) individuals (e.g., Cherry, 2015), and online communities (e.g., Lawo et al., 2020). Participants described actively seeking out supportive communities when support was perceived to be lacking, for example by engaging in an animal-rights activities group (Cherry, 2015; Larsson et al., 2003), Additionally, Cherry (2015) described that discussions with family and friends could transform initially indifferent or unsupportive individuals into sources of support. Social support was not described as a single type of interaction, but rather as an interpretation of various social situations (e.g., acceptance or approval of an RABD by the environment, accommodation of dietary preferences, sharing resources) as supportive and beneficial for maintaining their RABD. As illustrated by the quote below, these supportive interactions can provide individuals with both emotional and practical support: "This community brings me security. Knowing I'm not alone is reassuring, and I feel more comfortable with those around me. In terms of my vegetarian practices, no doubt about that.

We meet up to eat together, if someone discovers a new restaurant that serves vegetarian dishes they let me know, or even a shop that sells stuff for us. For me, YES it's super important, first of all for moral support but also the practicality. […] I count on them to choose the restaurant, bring ingredients back from the supermarket, chat and be open about our practices." (quote from participant; Gilles Séré de Lanauze & Sirieix, 2022) Emotionally, social support can provide a sense of belonging, security, reassurance, and can help individuals cope with normative pressure (Gilles Séré de Lanauze & Sirieix, 2022; Williams et al., 2023). Interacting with like-minded individuals through shared meals or other activities helps them feel understood and less alone in their dietary choices (Cherry, 2015; Williams et al., 2023). These interactions were described as helping retain enjoyment around food and eating, while supporting dietary maintenance (Cherry, 2015; Gilles Séré de Lanauze & Sirieix, 2022).

Practically, social support can make an RABD more convenient and feasible.

Participants described receiving information on products, recipes, restaurants, and cooking practices, from others following an RABD and through social media or other online resources (Gilles Séré de Lanauze & Sirieix, 2022; Kemper, 2020; Lawo et al., 2020; Menzies & including exact participants quotations as well as author's descriptions and interpretations of participants' accounts, rather than on raw participant data. The phrasing does not imply that the synthesis was based only on direct participant accounts.

Sheeshka, 2012). Aside from sharing information, close others can support the maintenance of an RABD by adapting recipes, accommodating reduced animal-based dietary preferences, modifying their own eating behaviour, or transitioning to an RABD together (Twine, 2014; Wehbe et al., 2022). Similarly, the availability of vegan options in school and workplace cafeterias was reported to be important for those wanting to adopt and/or maintain an RABD (Larsson et al., 2003; Yoo & Yoon, 2015).

Although communities were described as supporting the adoption and maintenance of an RABD, studies also identified downsides of participation in these social communities, time investments, tensions with commitments to other social groups (e.g., family), differing philosophies among community members, and concerns about unreliable information (Gilles Séré de Lanauze & Sirieix, 2022; Larsson et al., 2003). Some participants also viewed community membership negatively or did not perceive reduced animal-based eating as involving belonging to a community (Gilles Séré de Lanauze & Sirieix, 2022).

Experiences of a (perceived) lack of social support. In contrast, a perceived lack of social support can hinder the adoption and maintenance of an RABD. Described experiences of a lack of support include others making negative comments, failure to accommodate dietary preferences, encouragement of animal-based food consumption, or attempts to prevent dietary change (e.g., Cherry, 2015; Markowski & Roxburgh, 2019; Twine, 2014; Zou et al., 2024). Limited practical support can increase the effort required to maintain an RABD, for instance when not being able to divide food preparations as others are unwilling or unable to prepare suitable meals (Menzies & Sheeshka, 2012; Ostermann et al., 2024; Twine, 2014).

Participants consequently reported increased difficulty adopting or maintaining an RABD, adapting to others' more animal-based eating practices, or returning to previous eating habits that included more animal-based food consumption (Barr & Chapman, 2002; Wehbe et al., 2024; Zou et al., 2024). Many of these experiences, such as social pressure and failure to accommodate dietary preferences, are discussed in the upcoming themes.

Theme 2: Family Practices and Household Transitions

The second theme captures how household members' dietary preferences, their varying influence over food preparation, and practical constraints within the household can collectively shape food practices. These factors can influence both daily food practices, as well as the adoption and maintenance of an RABD. As a result, changes in living situations can create opportunities or challenges for dietary change.

Household influences on food practices. Household food practices are shaped by dietary preferences and needs of household members, and hence by a complex system of taste preferences, family habits, opinion on climate change, or health-related dietary needs (Hesselberg et al., 2024; Markoni et al., 2023; Markowski & Roxburgh, 2019). This in turn can influence the amount of animal-based foods consumed within the entire household (Groen et al., 2025; Markoni et al., 2023; Yang et al., 2025). Illustratively, a study described that households with a member following an RABD consumed less animal-based food products and another study described a participant increasing their animal-based food consumption once their partner became less strict in their RABD (Markoni et al., 2023; Yang et al., 2025).

How conflicting preferences are managed in specific eating situations is further discussed in Theme 3.

In households with children, meals were often adapted to children's actual or perceived preferences and needs, which could either increase or reduce animal-based food consumption (Birch & Memery, 2020; Groen et al., 2025; Hesselberg et al., 2024; Sundet et al., 2023). These adaptations were described to occur despite parents' own dietary preferences. Parents following an RABD sometimes prepared animal-based meals for their children because of children's taste preferences or beliefs about their nutritional needs, whereas non-vegan parents similarly sometimes prepared vegan food for their children (Groen et al., 2025; Larsson et al., 2003; O'Neill et al., 2019; Salonen & Helne, 2012; Sundet et al., 2023; Wendler, 2025).

Beyond shaping everyday food choices, household members could also influence the adoption and maintenance of RABD within the household. Children' dislike of an RABD could lead households to return to more animal-based meals (Groen et al., 2025; Hesselberg et al., 2024). Similarly, partners or other family members' preferences for meat could also constrain dietary change (Groen et al., 2025; Mylan, 2018; Sundet et al., 2023; Wehbe et al., 2022). For some families, such preferences for animal-based foods can be rooted in their family values and their attachment to meat (Markoni et al., 2023; Markowski & Roxburgh, 2019; Mylan, 2018). Parents could also influence children's dietary change by actively promoting animal-based food consumption or prohibiting dietary change (Racey et al., 2017; Twine, 2014). However, household members could also facilitate a dietary change within the household by adopting an RABD themselves. Studies described household members wanting or needing to follow an RABD as instigators to reduce the households' animal-based food intake (Csutora & Mózner, 2025; Markoni et al., 2023; O'Neill et al., 2019), for example by prompting reconsideration of the household food practices (Hesselberg et al., 2024; Vezovnik & Kamin, 2024), or by slowly adapting to the reduced animal-based diet of a household member (Hesselberg et al., 2024; Veen & Smaal, 2025), as illustrated by the following quote: "Meat has slowly been phased out. In the beginning, our oldest brother cooked a separate vegetarian dish, while the rest of us ate meat, and then slowly when my middle brother also became vegetarian, we simply ate less and less meat and then it was easy for me to become vegetarian too […] and half a year after our oldest brother became vegetarian the family only ate meat once a month, as I remember" (quote from participant; Hesselberg et al., 2024) The influence of household members on household food practices can depend on who is responsible for food preparation. Studies reported that as those in charge of cooking have greater influence over other household members' diets (Koning et al., 2026; Zhao et al., 2026), the other members may feel dependent on or constrained by their cooking practices (Groen et al., 2025; Sundet et al., 2023). Family hierarchies similarly gave parents greater influence over children's eating, making shared family meals harder to change than meals with friends, and making dietary change within the family home more difficult (Koning et al., 2026; Twine, 2014; Zhao et al., 2026). However, having greater influence over food preparation does not always translate into a meal in line with the preparator's own dietary preferences, as individuals often take into account other household members' preferences or perceived needs (Groen et al., 2025; O'Neill et al., 2019; Salonen & Helne, 2012; Sundet et al., 2023; Wendler, 2025).

Practical constraints on household food practices. Across these social dynamics, practical constraints can also shape household food practices. Single parents and households with (older) children reported greater difficulty reducing animal-based food consumption and less intention to do so, as limited time resulted in fewer opportunities to experiment with dietary change (Sundet et al., 2023; Zhao et al., 2026). Time pressure and financial considerations were perceived to favour animal-based meals because of their perceived convenience and the perceived cost of preparing separate meals (Groen et al., 2025; Salonen & Helne, 2012; Wehbe et al., 2022). Conversely, the availability of plant-based foods in the home was described to encourage their consumption (Birch & Memery, 2020; Pluck & Morrison-Saunders, 2022).

Collectively, studies mention that a complex system of social and practical dynamics shape the food environment within the household. This can have implications later in life, as it is reported that exposure to particular foods and practices within the household during childhood can affect familiarity and taste preferences (Gibson et al., 2023; O'Neill et al., 2019).

Changing households.

The role of household dynamics in food practices also became apparent in studies describing changes in the household. Changes in living situation were described as opportunities to reconsider food practices, try new things, and make a dietary change, marking the beginning or end of an RABD (Godin, 2023; O'Neill et al., 2019; Sedova et al., 2016). Specifically, moving away from the parental home was described as a moment to establish new food practices, as illustrated by the quote below: "I officially started being firmly flexitarian because I moved away from my parents; I went to school two hours away. That was when I started to cook for myself. I decided that at my apartment I wouldn't eat meat, but at my parents' house, on weekends when I went back up there, they cooked meat." (quote from participant; Godin, 2023) Moving away may create an opportunity for dietary change, supported by being surrounded with other like-minded individuals who are perceived to be more accepting and open to trying new things compared to their family household or parents (Cherry, 2015; Godin, 2023; Kemper & White, 2021; Sedova et al., 2016; Vezovnik & Kamin, 2024).

Others described moving in with a veg*n partner or housemate as facilitating animalbased food reductions or adoption of an RABD (Menzies & Sheeshka, 2012; Pluck & Morrison-Saunders, 2022; Twine, 2014). Similarly, when moving (back) into a household with meat-eaters or when a veg*n household member moves away, animal-based food consumption may be resumed (Barr & Chapman, 2002; Godin, 2023; Hesselberg et al., 2024; Menzies & Sheeshka, 2012). This may be due to the perceived ease of (sharing) food preparations when food preferences are aligned within a household (Menzies & Sheeska, 2012).

Theme 3: Navigation of Conflicting Dietary Preferences during Shared Meals

The second theme revealed that household members may have different dietary preferences which can influence what food is being prepared and consumed. The third theme captures how conflicting dietary preferences regarding animal-based foods are navigated during shared meals while balancing a complex system of various related goals, including maintenance of dietary preferences, social relationships and commensality. The ways in which these differences are navigated can vary depending on the eating context and social roles of those involved.

Adapting to dietary preferences of others.

When eating together with individuals with conflicting dietary preferences, people may adjust their eating behaviour to the preferences of others. For instance, individuals following or striving to follow an RABD, may consume animal-based food products despite their own intention not to (e.g., Kemper & White, 2021; Salmivaara et al., 2022; Sundet et al., 2023). Adaptation may also consist of making (smaller) compromises (Docherty & Jasper, 2023; Yoo & Yoon, 2015), for instance by not eating meat but eating dairy or eggs when following a vegan diet (Docherty & Jasper, 2023). How such adaptations were experienced varied, with some participants describing distress and discomfort deviating from their diet, while others viewed compromises as necessary and acceptable part of social life (Yoo & Yoon, 2015). Those not following an RABD may choose a vegetarian dish when eating with someone following an RABD or adjusting meals to make sure there is something for everyone to eat (Bogueva et al., 2022; Pluck & Morrison-Saunders, 2022; Sundet et al., 2023; Wendler, 2023).

Adapting to others' dietary preferences was motivated by practical and social considerations. Beyond convenience (Pluck & Morrison-Saunders, 2022), participants described adapting to maintain social relationships, for example to be polite, avoid being a burden, to fit in with others, or to avoid conflict and other negative social interactions (Bogueva et al., 2022; Godin, 2023; Jabs et al., 2000; Pluck & Morrison-Saunders, 2022; Rosenfeld & Tomiyama, 2019; Sedova et al., 2016; Wehbe et al., 2022 Wehbe et al., , 2024;; Wendler, 2025; Yoo & Yoon, 2015). Those newer to an RABD or following an RABD for health compared to ethical reasons were reported to be more inclined to adapt to others' preferences (Jabs et al., 2000).

Choosing to adapt to others' preferences may depend on the context of the meal and their role within it. Studies reported participants modifying or making exceptions to their dietary practices during special occasions (e.g., family gatherings, celebrations, business gatherings), or when going out for a meal (Markoni et al., 2023; O'Neill et al., 2019; Rosenfeld et al., 2020; Sedova et al., 2016; Zou et al., 2024). When being a guest following an RABD, participants may compromise their dietary preference and eat what was served (Jabs et al., 2000; Ostermann et al., 2024; Rosenfeld & Tomiyama, 2019; Wendler, 2025; Zou et al., 2024). Similarly, when being a host following an RABD, participants described adjusting meals to include animal-based food products when catering for guests, this partially related to perceptions of what was appropriate to serve guests (see Theme 4; Wendler, 2023; Zou et al., 2024).

The extent of adaptation to others' food preferences during moments of shared eating may also depend on the food being served, with one study describing a participant eating meat when visiting someone when it was integrated into a dish, but avoiding it whenever possible (Wendler, 2025), as illustrated in the quote below: "When visiting someone, especially my in-laws, who eat quite a lot of meat, sometimes the meat is sort of integrated into the dish… In those cases, I'll eat meat. But if they make a roast and some sides, then I'll just eat the sides. I think it's because, I mean, I don't want to cause a stir, if that makes sense" (quote from participant; Wendler, 2025) Maintaining dietary preferences. Rather than deviating from one's own preferences and adapting to the dietary preferences of others, participants following an RABD also described strategies to maintain dietary adherence. One way of doing so was to avoid the shared eating situation, with participants describing eating before attending a social event, or in some cases, avoiding a social gathering altogether to avoid conflict (Cherry, 2015; Veen & Smaal, 2025). To maintain both commensality and dietary adherence, alterations may be made in the organisation of shared meals to accommodate the preference for reduced animalbased food. Studies described the preparation of two dishes, having meat-based and plantbased options, leaving out the animal-based component for those following an RABD and individuals bringing their own food (Groen et al., 2025; Jabs et al., 2000; Koning et al., 2026; Veen et al., 2023; Veen & Smaal, 2025; Yoo & Yoon, 2015). Alternatively, the amount of animal-based products in a meal could be negotiated, or a plant-based meal could be prepared for everyone, allowing those following an RABD to participate in the shared meal without requiring a separate meal (Koning et al., 2026; Veen et al., 2023). When going out for a meal, the choice of the food outlet can be negotiated to offer food options to both veg*ns and nonveg*ns (Pluck & Morrison-Saunders, 2022).

However, these type of arrangements can be experienced as inconvenient, difficult, time-consuming, wasteful, more expensive (Groen et al., 2025; Pluck & Morrison-Saunders, 2022; Twine, 2014; Wehbe et al., 2022; Yoo & Yoon, 2015) and require creativity, particularly when eating with meat-loving partners or in settings where available food is predominantly meat-based (Hoek et al., 2017; Mylan, 2018; Sundet et al., 2023). As a result, some may experience these arrangements as a less desirable or impractical way of navigating conflicting dietary preferences, and when good alternatives are unavailable, individuals may have to endure unappetising meals (Wehbe et al., 2022; Yoo & Yoon, 2015).

Maintaining dietary preferences also involved navigating whether and how to discuss dietary differences with others. Some participants choose to disclose their dietary pattern before eating with others (Jabs et al., 2000), while others described framing dietary practices as individual choices and avoiding broader discussions about meat eating, vegetarianism and ethical considerations, in order to avoid conflict (Veen et al., 2023). Beyond maintaining social relationships and/or dietary adherence, some participants mentioned using their dietary choices to actively challenge the existing normative behaviour by demonstrating the possibility to deviate from the standard (Koning et al., 2026; Salmivaara et al., 2022; Zhao et al., 2026).

Theme 4: Social Expectations and Pressure

The fourth theme reflects that social expectations and pressure surrounding animalbased food consumption could encourage or constrain dietary change by making reduced animal-based eating more or less acceptable, expected and/or difficult. These influences were described across various social contexts, including within households and during shared meals, as described in previous themes. Individuals experienced and managed these influences in different ways, varying between social contexts.

Experiences of social expectations and pressure.

Expectations of others regarding meals and (reduced) animal-based food consumption can affect individuals' food choices and RABD maintenance. Studies reported that participants mentioned listening and adapting to the opinion or expectations of others, which can either encourage or hinder reduced animalbased eating, depending on the expectations held by the others (Bogueva et al., 2022; O'Neill et al., 2019; Wehbe et al., 2024). When meat consumption was perceived as appropriate or expected for the meal, participants described experiencing difficulty modifying a meal to contain less animal-based foods or disregarded available vegetarian options (Koning et al., 2026; Sundet et al., 2023). Social expectations could also encourage reducing animal-based food consumption. In some social groups, following or striving for an RABD was described as "the social norm" or "an ideal" (Sedova et al., 2016; Sundet et al., 2023). Participants reported normalization of choosing plant-based options and being influenced by perceptions that reducing meat consumption was trendy or cool (Ford et al., 2023; Ostermann et al., 2024). Such perceived social norms and expectations regarding reduced animal-based food consumption can influence daily food choices due to shame surrounding meat consumption, and can positively affect adoption of an RABD (Sedova et al., 2016; Sundet et al., 2023). Such perceived expectations were also described in close relationships, with one study describing participants reporting eating veg*n food to align with the expectations of their partner (Bogueva et al., 2022).

Beyond more implicit, perceived expectations of others, participants also described experiencing explicit social pressure to eat animal-based food products where others actively "pushed" individuals towards animal-based food consumption (Markowski & Roxburgh, 2019; Ostermann et al., 2024; Racey et al., 2017; Rosenfeld et al., 2020). Individuals experienced social pressure through negative or cynical comments, teasing, or negative attention in general, from others in response to their choice not to eat animal-based foods and accusations of disturbing the social harmony (Cherry, 2015; Csutora & Mózner, 2025; Markowski & Roxburgh, 2019; Merriman & Adams, 2010; Wehbe et al., 2022; Wendler, 2023; Yoo & Yoon, 2015). Others faced criticism for proposing an alternative meal for special occasions (e.g., a Christmas dinner), for discussing animal suffering during meals, or in response to (the taste of) veg*n food itself (Docherty & Jasper, 2023; Koning et al., 2026).

Participants also described anticipating fear of social disapproval and being mocked for eating less or no animal-based food products (Bogueva et al., 2022; Docherty & Jasper, 2023; Markowski & Roxburgh, 2019; Wehbe et al., 2024), with the anticipation of negative comments sometimes being based on what they observe in their social circles (Bogueva et al., 2022), e.g., when eating a vegan burger: Although this pressure often increased the difficulty of maintaining an RABD (e.g., Ostermann et al., 2024), some participants were described maintaining their diet despite opposition from others or having conversations with others to reduce the negative attention they receive (Cherry, 2015; Merriman & Adams, 2010). Alternatively, some studies also described experienced social pressure to reduce meat consumption from friends, housemates, and social media (Kemper, 2020; Ostermann et al., 2024). For instance, a household member adopting an RABD was described to pressure others in the household to join them in this dietary change, through parental authority or by providing incentives (Zhao et al., 2026).

Contextual expectations and pressure. Social pressure and expectations regarding (reduced) animal-based food consumption can vary across social groups as described above, but may also differ depending on occasion, location, and social role. Studies described meat being perceived as particularly important or expected during special occasions, such as during celebrations or family gatherings, for enjoyment of the meal for others (Markoni et al., 2023; Mylan, 2018), as well as in settings where meat is the norm, such as meat-centred restaurants (Sundet et al., 2023). In these occasions, some individuals reported to occasionally deviate from their diets (see Theme 3; Rosenfeld et al., 2020).

Additionally, studies described specific expectations regarding appropriateness of foods and eating behaviour when individuals occupy the role of a guest or host during a meal.

Participants described perceptions of proper hospitability to include serving foods accommodating the guests' needs and preferences and making sure there is something for everyone to eat (Sundet et al., 2023; Wendler, 2023). Furthermore, studies described that vegetarian meals were not seen as appropriate meals for guests and hosts not wanting to "push their own values" on their guests (Wendler, 2025; Zou et al., 2024). In turn, when being a guest following an RABD, some may perceive bringing food themselves or asking the host to cook something else as inappropriate (Sundet et al., 2023) Perceived stigma and (avoiding) social identification. Experiences of social pressure could also shape how individuals perceived and presented their dietary choices to others. Studies describe perceptions of a stigma associated with veganism, with participants reporting concerns about being stereotyped as "a vegan" or negatively judged for reducing animal-based food consumption (Docherty & Jasper, 2023; Markowski & Roxburgh, 2019; Wehbe et al., 2024; Yoo & Yoon, 2015). Participants described apologising for their dietary choices or concealing their reduced animal-based food consumption to distance themselves from a veg*n identity and/or maintain their identity as a meat eater, or attempting to provide other helpful service to give a good impression (Bogueva et al., 2022; Wehbe et al., 2024; Yoo & Yoon, 2015). In other cases, concerns about stereotyping were associated with compromising dietary practices, such as consuming animal-based food products to avoid being singled out, or including non-meat animal-based food products in meals to increase identification with the meat-eating group and reduce perceived stigma (Docherty & Jasper, 2023; Wehbe et al., 2022). Thus, the stigma against veganism can hinder individuals in adopting and maintaining an RABD and may hinder non-veg*n individuals in making animalbased reduced food choices out of fear for stigmatization (Docherty & Jasper, 2023; Markowski & Roxburgh, 2019). The extent to which social pressure shaped dietary choices may vary between individuals, with participants discussing aspects such as self-confidence, personal importance of fitting in and belonging, and the desire for social validation as relevant factors for the impact of social pressure (Wehbe et al., 2024).

Part 4. Integration Quantitative and Qualitative Study Results

We now reflect on the quantitative and qualitative results' convergence, complementarity, divergence, and expansion using illustrative examples.

Convergence. Both our qualitative and quantitative results show that reducing animalbased food intake is socially embedded in a variety of social influence mechanisms. Across both strands of literature, close social relationships, including family members, household members, friends, and romantic partners, emerge as important for shaping food intake and dietary patterns. Likewise, both highlight the relevance of others' presence, behaviours, and (perceived) expectations and pressure. The findings also converge in demonstrating that social influences are context-and relationship-dependent rather than uniform across context and time. More specifically, the qualitative results reflect how influence varies across social relationships, occasions, locations, and social roles, and the quantitative results indicate this through the use of different referent groups and setting-specific measures. Nevertheless, the qualitative findings suggest that more attention to the role of specific social relationships and contexts may further strengthen quantitative and experimental research.

Complementarity.

While quantitative results show that social support facilitates dietary change, the qualitative results reveal the multiple forms this support can take, including emotional reassurance, practical accommodation, recipe sharing, food provisioning, and participation in communities of like-minded individuals. Similarly, whereas quantitative results link social pressure to difficulties in reducing animal-based food consumption, qualitative results illustrate how such pressure is experienced in practice through teasing, questioning, perceived expectations, criticism, and difficulties refusing food in social situations. The qualitative results also enrich the quantitative evidence on social norms by demonstrating how norms are encountered and negotiated in everyday life, during family meals, social gatherings, host and guest roles, workplace eating occasions, and household food practices. Furthermore, while quantitative studies often incorporated context through the use of specific referent groups and settings, they reveal limited insights into why these contexts matter. The qualitative literature complements this work by showing that family meals involve obligations and long-term relationships, special occasions are often accompanied by stronger expectations surrounding meat consumption, hosting and guest roles create distinct social expectations, and household members must navigate practical constraints such as time, costs, and competing dietary preferences.

Divergence. Some divergence between the two strands of results can also be observed.

For instance, social norms are among the most frequently studied factors in the quantitative literature, yet are rarely addressed explicitly in qualitative accounts, where expectations, obligations, and shared understandings are embedded within richer narratives about social dynamics. Additional divergence emerges in the complexity and perceived consequences of social influence. Quantitative studies often examine single social influence factors in isolation, while qualitative studies described social influence as involving tensions and tradeoffs, in which maintaining relationships, adhering to dietary preferences, fitting in, and navigating practical constraints simultaneously shape food choices, depending on the situation. More broadly, context appears to play a different role across the two evidence streams. In the quantitative studies, context is typically incorporated as a single, often rather static, characteristic of the social influence being studied, such as through a one-time measure of a particular referent group or setting. In the qualitative findings, variations in context are central to understanding consumption; the same individual can experience and enact social influence differently as a parent, partner, friend, colleague, host, or guest, and the same social relationship could simultaneously provide support, exert pressure, create practical barriers, and encourage dietary change depending on the occasion or location. Furthermore, while social support is shown to be robustly associated with beneficial dietary outcomes in the quantitative literature, the qualitative results also introduced potential downsides, including tensions within communities, conflicting commitments to different social groups, and the burden of maintaining community involvement.

Expansion.

Finally, the qualitative results expand the quantitative results by drawing attention to social processes and contexts that received relatively little attention in quantitative research so far. Household transitions (e.g., leaving parental home, moving in with partners, changing household composition), negotiations of conflicting dietary preferences, expectations in different social roles, food logistics (who cooks, shops, decides) and the balancing interpersonal relationships and dietary adherence emerge as recurring themes in qualitative studies but are rarely represented in quantitative work. Furthermore, particularly the qualitative literature emphasizes that social influences do not operate in isolation and direct, linear effects on consumption, but are intertwined in a complex system with broader contextual factors such as ease, time, availability, price, and taste preferences, which may facilitate or constrain reductions in animal-based food consumption. Conversely, some social influence factors that receive attention in the quantitative literature are largely absent from the qualitative results. Most notably, quantitative studies frequently examined more distal referent groups, such as similarly aged peers, colleagues, customers, local communities, institutional groups, and broader populations, whereas qualitative studies predominantly focused on close interpersonal relationships. Similarly, forms of social presence studied experimentally, such as observability and the presence of strangers or unfamiliar others during food choice, received little attention in the qualitative literature, possibly due to the more unconscious nature of these influences.

Discussion

Study and Participant Characteristics

The aim of this scoping review was to provide a comprehensive overview of the Mapping the participant characteristics revealed that these were often only partially reported. Sample sizes were relatively large, although considerable variability existed. Most studies focused on emerging adults and/or adults, whereas adolescents and older adults were comparatively underrepresented. In addition, women were overrepresented in many samples, and qualitative studies frequently focused on individuals who had already reduced or abstained from animal-based food consumption, whereas quantitative studies more often examined mixed dietary populations or meat eaters. Collectively, the mapped characteristics suggest that while the literature is rapidly growing, it remains concentrated in specific countries and demographic groups.

Quantitative Findings

Through content analysis, we identified ten overarching social factors that are currently measured in the quantitative studies, as presented in Figure 6. Our synthesis revealed that the quantitative literature to date is heavily concentrated on social norms, while other potentially relevant social factors have received substantially less attention. Our study further highlights considerable conceptual diversity in how researchers have studied social influence, not only between but also within factors. Within the social norms factor, for instance, norms referred to different referent groups and consumption types. Across social factors, characteristics of the social environment were generally associated with animal-based food consumption in the expected direction.

At the same time, the review revealed a notable distinction between observational and experimental evidence; whereas observational studies frequently reported associations between social influence factors and dietary outcomes, experimental studies yielded more mixed findings, including non-significant and occasionally counter-intuitive effects.

Furthermore, the examined evidence for impression management and persuasion was relatively limited, suggesting that extrinsically controlling techniques (from others or oneself) to reduce animal-based intake may be less effective. One final notable observation was that quantitative studies predominantly focused on meat consumption and broader animal-based food intake measures, whereas other more specific animal-based food categories (such as fish and dairy products) were examined much less frequently. Collectively, these findings provide an overview of the current scope of quantitative research and may be a helpful starting point for comparing, integrating, and building on social influence factors.

Qualitative Findings

In the qualitative studies, it is reflected that reducing animal-based food consumption is deeply embedded within social relationships and everyday social contexts. Across the four identified themes (see Figure 7), social influence emerged not as a single mechanism, but as a multifaceted, complex process involving support, belonging, negotiation, expectations, pressure, and relationship management that depend on occasion, location, group, and social role. Participants described how family members, partners, friends, and communities could facilitate dietary change by providing information, encouragement, practical assistance, and shared identities. It was also highlighted that social relationships could hinder change, through (perceived) social obligations, conflicting preferences, lack of support, expectations, and pressure to conform. Depending on the social context, relationship dynamics, and priorities at a given moment, individuals either maintained their dietary practices or adapted them to accommodate others and preserve social harmony. Household transitions, changes in living arrangements, and evolving family or partner relationships were often identified as moments in which dietary practices could change. Collectively, these findings suggest that reducing animal-based food intake is often shaped through ongoing social interactions and should therefore be understood as a socially negotiated rather than a purely individual behavior.

Mixed-Methods Integrated Implications

The integration of the qualitative and quantitative findings revealed areas of convergence, complementarity, divergence, and expansion. Based on this integration, we formulate several implications for research and interventions, conceptualization, and methodology.

Research and intervention implications. First, our integrated findings suggest that social influence should not be understood as a single, isolated interpersonal factor, but as part of a broader social-ecological system shaping animal-based food consumption. While much of the quantitative literature focused on influences from referent groups and the qualitative literature primarily described interactions within families, households, friendships, and communities, both bodies of evidence also pointed to broader contextual or societal influences that shape these interpersonal dynamics. Across the reviewed studies, social influences were described to be intertwined with a multitude of personal and contextual factors such as convenience, time, availability, price, others' openness to dietary change, and taste preferences, which could either facilitate or constrain efforts to reduce animal-based foods. Furthermore, information on media, social or dietary trends, and the availability and visibility of foods within food environments may all influence what is perceived as normal, desirable, and feasible. Consistent with social-ecological perspectives, these findings suggest that social influence operates across multiple, interconnected levels, ranging from close relationships to communities, institutions, food environments, and wider cultures. Future research and intervention development may therefore benefit from adopting a complex systems perspective (Ewart et al., 2026; Griffiths et al., 2023; Nobles et al., 2022) and examining how influences across these levels interact to support or hinder dietary change.

Second, the quantitative research field has over-focused on the role of social norms as a standalone factor involved in reduced animal-based food intake. Quantitative studies have predominantly operationalized social influence through relatively narrow norm-based constructs and manipulations, often moving directly from observed correlation-based associations to intervention development. The qualitative evidence suggests, however, that consumption decisions are embedded within complex social processes involving support, expectations, relationships, household dynamics, and ongoing negotiation of competing situated goals. The limited and inconsistent effects of many social norm experiments, as is also evidenced by systematic reviews in the food context (Pollicino et al., 2025) and beyond (Papakonstantinou et al., 2025), may therefore reflect a mismatch between the complexity of social influences and the simplicity of current (norm-based) manipulations. Future research efforts may therefore benefit from shifting attention away from isolated norm messages towards intervention approaches that support people in navigating the social realities of meat reduction within their everyday social environments.

Conceptual implications.

Third, and related to the second, the strong focus on social norms may conceptually reflect a general preference for factors that are easily operationalized and experimentally manipulated. In line with this premise, it has been previously argued that behavioural science often prioritizes hypothesis-testing and experimental control over truly understanding complex phenomena in context (Diener et al., 2022; Scheel et al., 2021). The research field may thus require greater attention to more context-sensitive research that examines how social influences emerge, interact, and are negotiated in everyday settings.

Fourth, our review highlights the need for greater conceptual clarity in research on social influence and animal-based consumption. In the literature field to date, we observed considerable heterogeneity in the terminology used to describe social influence constructs. In the quantitative studies, the same label was often used for measures that appeared to capture distinct social influence processes, while in other cases studies used different labels for conceptually similar measures. For instance, some measures described as social norms appeared to capture other forms of social influence; when a measure referred to the eating behavior of a specific individual or referent group (e.g., a romantic partner; Szczepanski et al., 2025) we coded it as "eating behavior of others" rather than a "social norm". Similarly, measures labelled as social modeling (Christie & Chen, 2018; Hammami et al. 2023, study 2) or social facilitation (Horgan et al., 2019) were coded as social presence when they assessed the mere presence of others, without measuring the underlying mechanism. Differences in terminology were also evident across quantitative and qualitative findings, where qualitative participants often did not describe their experiences using the labels used by researchers in quantitative studies. These observations suggest the presence of jingle and jangle fallacies, complicating evidence comparison, synthesis, and accumulation (Hodson, 2021).

Consequently, our findings call for the importance of conceptual clarity in social influence research. Greater alignment between concepts, definitions and operationalizations facilitates clearer theory development and more meaningful synthesis of evidence across studies.

Although the 10 identified factors in our review can only be understood as a descriptive synthesis of existing measures rather than as a prescriptive conceptual framework, they provide a useful starting point for identifying inconsistencies between construct labels and operationalizations and for reflecting on how social influence constructs are conceptualized, measured, and distinguished from one another.

Methodological implications.

Fifth, future quantitative research may benefit from adopting longitudinal social network perspectives, which remain relatively uncommon in the field. Whereas most included studies focused on perceived influences from only a few specific individuals or single aggregated groups, social network approaches can capture broader patterns of interpersonal connections, social selection, and the diffusion of dietary behaviors through personal networks. For instance, social selection is a competing explanation of social similarity (i.e., people may be similar due to selection rather than influence), and only one study in our review controlled for that phenomenon. Social network approaches have been applied successfully in other areas of health behavior research (Valente, 2015) and may provide valuable insights into how dietary changes spread through social systems. In addition, social network interventions, which strategically leverage influential individuals or network structures to promote behavior change, represent a promising but largely unexplored avenue for encouraging reductions in animal-based food consumption (Hunter et al., 2019).

Sixth, our findings show the value of mixed-methods research. The quantitative and qualitative literatures provided complementary insights that would have remained incomplete in isolation. The continuation of combining (more intensive longitudinal) quantitative and qualitative methods may help identify not only whether social influence strategies work, but also how, why, and for whom they work. This will contribute to the development of interventions that are better tailored to the social realities in which daily consumption decisions are made.

Limitations

There are several limitations to be aware of when interpreting the findings of this review. First, when interpreting the results of the quantitative mapping, it should be kept in mind that studies potentially included other variables and intervention components on top of social influence in their models (e.g., in a COM-B model or TPB model), hindering precise comparison between studies. Where possible, we included the isolated social influence finding, but this was not always possible with the available information. Second, although our search covered numerous databases from diverse fields and performed backward and forward screening, it may not have captured all relevant papers, particularly grey literature and research published after the final search. Third, we focused on actual consumption behaviors and dietary patterns, excluding studies that assessed intentions only. While this strengthened the behavioral focus of the review, it may have reduced the inclusion of research examining how dietary changes develop over time. Fourth, for the qualitative synthesis, we relied on authors' interpretations and the quotes reported in the included studies. In addition, we extracted and coded only the information most relevant to the review rather than analyzing entire results sections line-by-line. Consequently, some relevant findings may not have been captured. However, given the large body of qualitative evidence included, we expect that the key social influences and processes were identified.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this review demonstrates that social influence plays an important role in reducing animal-based food consumption, but is more complex than often reflected in the existing literature. While quantitative research has predominantly focused on social norms, the qualitative evidence revealed a broader set of social processes involving support, expectations, negotiations, and relationship dynamics embedded within everyday social contexts. The integration of both bodies of evidence suggests that social influence is best understood as a multifaceted and context-dependent phenomenon operating across interpersonal and broader environmental levels. Future research should seek to better understand how multiple forms of social influence interact across contexts and populations, and how we can create conceptual clarity to describe these phenomena. Doing so will likely require greater use of mixed-methods, social network approaches, and context-sensitive approaches capable of capturing the social realities through which dietary change occurs in daily life.
