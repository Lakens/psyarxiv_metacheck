Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Matrix-reasoning assessments commonly use abstract geometric figures, although familiar content may change both performance and processing. This within-participants study compared accuracy and gaze-defined patterns of visual exploration for abstract and contextual items requiring corresponding relational rules. Participants were 56 children and adolescents recruited from a public school in São Paulo, Brazil, aged 11 to 15 years. Each participant completed six abstract and six contextual 3 × 3 matrices while gaze was recorded. Trial-level transition matrices were constructed from fixation sequences across nine matrix cells and the response alternatives, and k-means clustering identified two recurring patterns. Contextual items were answered more accurately than abstract items, with 2.44 times higher odds of a correct response. Completion time, fixation count, and fixation duration were similar between conditions. Cluster 1 was characterized by localized transitions involving the final row and response alternatives, whereas Cluster 2 involved broader, more sequential exploration across the matrix. Contextual items had lower odds of Cluster 1 classification, indicating that representational format modestly altered the distribution of gaze patterns. A significant condition-by-cluster interaction further showed that Cluster 2 was associated with higher accuracy for abstract items, but no cluster difference was detected for contextual items. These exploratory findings suggest that familiar content changes how matrices are explored, indicating that abstract and familiar matrix items should not be treated as interchangeable.

Introduction

Relational reasoning is the capacity to identify relations among representations, integrate multiple relations, and use their common structure to generate inferences or solve new problems.

It allows individuals to move beyond the properties of isolated objects and consider how those objects are connected. This capacity supports analogical reasoning, concept formation, knowledge transfer, and learning across academic domains (Alexander, 2016). Relational reasoning is also closely associated with fluid reasoning, broadly defined as the ability to solve novel problems for which a previously learned solution cannot simply be retrieved from memory (Carroll, 1993; Schneider & McGrew, 2012). Because it requires maintaining and manipulating multiple representations while resisting irrelevant information, relational reasoning is also closely linked to executive function (Diamond, 2013; Doebel, 2020; Miyake et al., 2000). Some authors have proposed that relational thinking should be considered an executive function in its own right (Starr et al., 2023), whereas others view it as the coordinated application of executive processes to structured information (Richland & Zhao, 2023; Zelazo et al., 2024). Regardless of its precise classification, relational reasoning lies at the interface between executive control and context-sensitive problem solving.

Matrix-completion tasks are among the most widely used measures of relational and fluid reasoning. A typical item presents an incomplete array of figures that vary along dimensions such as shape, number, position, color, or orientation. Participants must identify the relations governing changes across rows or columns, integrate these relations, and select the alternative that completes the matrix. These tasks are generally described as nonverbal, abstract, and culture-free because their rules are usually instantiated through geometric figures rather than language or familiar situations (Carpenter et al., 1990). Their apparent simplicity, however, conceals several cognitive demands. Solving a matrix can require encoding multiple visual features, determining which features are relevant, inferring one or more rules, coordinating information across cells, maintaining intermediate conclusions, and evaluating potential answers.

Correct performance consequently depends not only on whether participants can identify the relevant relations, but also on how they approach the problem. Different participants can reach the same response through distinct sequences of information search, comparison, rule inference, and answer evaluation (Laurence & Macedo, 2023). For example, participants may systematically compare complete rows and columns, repeatedly inspect particular cells, focus on elements adjacent to the missing entry, alternate between the matrix and response alternatives, or examine the alternatives only after developing a possible solution. These patterns differ in how information is selected, organized, and integrated, and they may vary in their effectiveness depending on the characteristics of the participant and the problem. Strategy use should therefore be understood as dynamic and sensitive to item difficulty, representational format, and task demands rather than as a fixed characteristic of the individual (Gonthier & Roulin, 2020; Laurence & Macedo, 2023).

Eye tracking provides a means of examining this strategic variation by revealing how participants distribute and sequence their visual attention while solving matrix problems. Gaze data can indicate whether participants systematically compare rows and columns, repeatedly inspect particular cells, focus primarily on elements near the missing entry, or alternate between the matrix and response alternatives (Laurence & Macedo, 2023; Vigneau et al., 2006). Whereas aggregate measures summarize how attention is distributed, sequential analyses preserve the order in which different areas are inspected and can therefore characterize the organization of information search across the course of a trial. Data-driven clustering can then identify recurring patterns of visual exploration without requiring each trial to conform to a strategy category defined in advance (Hayes et al., 2011; Kucharsky et al., 2020). Although eye movements do not provide a direct record of thought, they offer process-level behavioral evidence about how participants approach a problem that cannot be obtained from accuracy or response time alone.

Developmental studies reinforce the importance of examining these processes. Niebaum and Munakata (2023) used eye tracking to investigate matrix completion among 6-year-olds, 9year-olds, and adults. Across age groups, systematic scanning of matrix rows and columns predicted better performance, whereas earlier and more frequent consultation of potential answers predicted poorer performance. Patterns of visual exploration became more effective with age, and participants adapted how they inspected the problems as difficulty increased. Similarly, Gonthier et al. (2024) identified systematic developmental changes in strategy use from childhood through adolescence and showed that these changes accounted for part of the agerelated improvement in matrix performance. Neurocognitive evidence similarly indicates that the ability to integrate multiple relations continues to mature into adolescence (Crone et al., 2009).

Together, these findings indicate that developmental differences in matrix scores partly reflect changes in how participants search for, compare, and coordinate information, rather than changes in reasoning capacity alone. Importantly, however, the strategies available to children may depend not only on their developmental level, but also on how relational information is represented within the task.

Consistent with this possibility, research with younger children also suggests that matrix performance is sensitive to task presentation. Siegler and Svetina (2002), for example, used 2 × 2 matrices containing recognizable entities, such as mice and birds, which varied in size, color, and orientation. Their findings demonstrated substantial developmental and learning-related changes in the strategies children used to solve the matrices. More recently, Podjarny et al. (2022) developed a preschool matrix-completion task using familiar objects such as cars, balloons, cups, books, and mittens. The objects varied along two dimensions, identity and color, which children had to coordinate simultaneously. Four-and five-year-old children performed above chance on this modified task. The authors suggested that difficulties observed in earlier matrix and multiplicative-classification tasks may arise partly from demands that are not essential to relational integration, including the need to abstract the relevant dimensions.

These studies demonstrate that familiar objects can be incorporated into matrixcompletion tasks. They do not, however, establish whether familiar content improves matrix reasoning. Siegler and Svetina (2002) were primarily interested in developmental changes and learning, whereas Podjarny et al. (2022) simultaneously changed several properties of the original task, including the number of relevant dimensions, the instructions, and whether those dimensions were explicitly labeled. Neither study compared familiar-object matrices with relationally equivalent matrices composed of abstract geometric elements. Consequently, any effect specifically attributable to familiarity or contextual meaning cannot be isolated from other differences in task design.

A similar mixture of meaningful and abstract content is present in some standardized measures. For example, the earlier items of the KBIT-2 Matrices subtest include familiar objects connected by conventional associations, such as clothing and a washing machine, whereas other nonverbal intelligence tests rely primarily on novel geometric figures. Lancaster et al. (2025) argued that these differences influence the cognitive demands imposed by nominally nonverbal tests. Familiar stimuli may allow participants to use semantic or verbal knowledge, whereas novel stimuli may place greater demands on visual encoding and working memory. Their comparison demonstrates that nonverbal assessments differ substantially in stimulus familiarity and in the cognitive resources that can support performance. Nevertheless, because meaningful and abstract items differ in difficulty, order, and relational structure within these standardized tests, they do not provide a controlled test of contextualization. This distinction is theoretically important because reasoning does not occur independently of the situations in which problems are encountered. The surface and semantic characteristics of a problem can influence how it is represented, which information appears relevant, and which procedures become available. A classic demonstration comes from Carraher et al. (1985), who studied Brazilian children working as street vendors. The children solved 98.2% of arithmetic problems embedded in familiar commercial transactions, compared with 73.7% of school-like word problems and 36.8% of context-free calculations involving the same numbers and operations. Their verbal protocols further indicated that children used different procedures across settings. Equivalent numerical demands therefore elicited markedly different performance and processing depending on how the problems were presented. The authors argued that the physical presence of familiar objects did not itself reduce the required mathematical operations. Instead, participation in a meaningful activity supported the interpretation of quantities and the selection of procedures appropriate to the problem. Context can therefore alter how a problem is understood and approached without changing its formal structure. Rhodes et al. (2024) extended this argument into a broader critique of decontextualized assessments. From their perspective, problem-solving behavior emerges from the interaction between the reasoner's experiences, the sociocultural setting in which a problem is presented, and the researcher's assumptions about what constitutes an appropriate solution. A highly controlled test is therefore not context-free, but instead represents a particular context characterized by specific conventions, representations, and expectations. This argument is particularly relevant to matrix reasoning. Although abstract geometric figures are intended to minimize the contribution of previously acquired knowledge and emphasize relations that must be inferred within the item, unfamiliarity should not be equated with neutrality. Abstract items may increase the need to identify relevant perceptual distinctions and organize elements that lack an immediately available semantic structure. Moreover, familiarity with abstract representations and testing conventions may vary across individuals and sociocultural experiences. Incorporating familiar animals, foods, or everyday objects offers one way to ground matrix tasks more closely in participants' experiences while preserving their underlying relational structure. Familiar elements may make features and transformations easier to encode, maintain, compare, or describe verbally, thereby influencing how participants organize information and which strategies become available during problem solving. Comparing familiar and abstract matrix items can therefore reveal how representational context shapes both performance and cognitive processing, rather than treating observed scores as direct expressions of reasoning capacity alone.

For brevity, we refer to matrices composed of familiar objects as contextual items.

At the same time, contextualization need not invariably facilitate reasoning.

Developmental research on analogical reasoning indicates that young children initially focus on object attributes and only gradually come to prioritize relational structure (Gentner, 1988), and that attractive or familiar objects can distract them from relational matches, a vulnerability that decreases with age (Richland et al., 2006). Familiar objects may activate associations that are unrelated to the matrix rules or draw attention toward semantically salient but relationally irrelevant features. In some cases, abstract figures may make the formal structure of the problem more perceptually transparent precisely because they carry little prior meaning.

Contextualization could therefore facilitate performance by supporting the construction of a coherent representation, interfere with performance by introducing competing semantic information, or alter the strategy used without producing a uniform difference in accuracy.

Whether contextual content supports reasoning is thus an empirical question rather than an inherent property of familiar stimuli.

Despite its theoretical relevance, the specific role of familiar content in matrix reasoning remains poorly understood. Previous developmental tasks have shown that matrices can be constructed from familiar objects (Podjarny et al., 2022; Siegler & Svetina, 2002), and analyses of standardized tests indicate that stimulus familiarity may modify their cognitive demands (Lancaster et al., 2025). However, these studies have not compared contextual and abstract matrix items while holding their underlying relational operations constant. Most research on matrix reasoning has instead manipulated rule complexity, perceptual organization, feedback, or presentation format using abstract figures. It therefore remains unclear whether the same relational structure is processed differently when instantiated through familiar rather than abstract elements. It is also unknown whether such differences persist in early adolescence, when relational integration is still developing, but students have accumulated several years of experience with the abstract representations of formal schooling.

Performance measures alone cannot fully resolve this question. If contextual items are answered more accurately, this advantage could reflect more systematic comparison of matrix cells, less dependence on repeated consultation of the alternatives, or another pattern of visual exploration that is particularly effective for contextual material. Conversely, similar accuracy across conditions could conceal substantial differences in processing. Moreover, a given pattern of exploration need not be equally effective across representational formats. For example, an approach centered on the final row and the response alternatives might suffice when elements are easy to identify and label, but not when the relevant dimensions must first be abstracted from unfamiliar figures. Comparing eye movements across matched item types provides a means of determining whether participants approach the same relational operators differently when those operators are instantiated through meaningful or abstract elements.

To address this gap, the present study examined children and adolescents completing contextual and abstract matrix-reasoning items based on the same logical operators. Our first objective was to compare accuracy between contextual and abstract items. Our second objective was to identify recurring patterns of visual exploration from participants' fixation sequences and examine whether the frequency of these gaze-defined patterns differed across conditions. Finally, we tested whether the association between visual-exploration patterns and accuracy varied by item type. This analysis allowed us to examine whether particular patterns of processing were more or less effective depending on how the relational problem was represented. In addition, we explored whether age was associated with accuracy and scanpath classification and whether it moderated the effects of item condition.

We examined these questions in children and adolescents aged 11 to 15 years, attending the sixth to ninth school years of Brazilian elementary education. This period is of particular developmental interest for two reasons. First, the capacity to integrate multiple relations and the effective use of systematic matrix strategies are still developing during early adolescence (Crone et al., 2009; Gonthier et al., 2024). Second, these school years involve increasing exposure to formal, symbolic, and abstract representations in mathematics and science. If representational content continues to affect matrix performance and processing at this age, abstract matrices cannot be assumed to provide a neutral index of relational reasoning, even for students with substantial experience of formal educational tasks. Conversely, if differences between contextual and abstract items diminished with age within this range, this would suggest that reasoning over abstract representations is still consolidating during this period.

Based on evidence that meaningful contexts can support problem representation and strategy selection (Carraher et al., 1985), together with the argument that decontextualized assessments impose their own interpretive demands (Rhodes et al., 2024), we expected greater accuracy for contextual than abstract items. We also expected item content to influence patterns of visual exploration. However, because familiar content could support either efficient relational integration or attention to semantically salient but irrelevant information (Richland et al., 2006), we made no directional predictions about specific gaze patterns or about the interaction between item condition and scanpath pattern. As the study was not preregistered, these process analyses, as well as the analyses of age, should be considered exploratory. The process analyses were therefore intended to clarify why contextual and abstract matrix items might produce different levels of performance, without assuming that a contextual advantage must arise from a single strategy.

Methods

Study design and transparency

This study used a within-participants design, in which item content (abstract vs. contextual) varied across items completed by the same participants, to examine performance and visual processing in children and adolescents. Because the items were drawn from an existing standardized test rather than constructed for the study, the comparison should be considered quasi-experimental. Each participant completed both item conditions during a single assessment session and also a scale of Attention-Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder symptoms. The study was not preregistered. The project was approved by the University Ethics Committee (CAAE: 80473224.7.0000.0084).

Participants

A total of 82 children and adolescents from a public school in São Paulo, Brazil, participated in the study. To be eligible, participants had to be enrolled in the sixth through ninth years of elementary school, report no visual difficulties requiring corrective lenses, and have no known diagnosis or school-identified indication of a neurodevelopmental condition that could substantially affect performance on the reasoning task. Students who required eyeglasses were not included because reflections and partial occlusion produced by lenses or frames could compromise gaze-data quality. Eligibility regarding neurodevelopmental conditions was determined in consultation with the school's teachers and educational coordinators and did not involve a clinical assessment.

Three participants had no eye-tracking recordings because of equipment malfunction. An additional 23 participants were excluded from the eye-tracking analyses because no fixations were detected during the matrix-reasoning task, indicating unsuccessful calibration or inadequate gaze capture. These difficulties may have been exacerbated by the school-based data-collection environment, in which lighting conditions, participant positioning, and external movement could not be controlled as closely as in a laboratory setting. Data collection was conducted at the school using a portable remote eye-tracking setup. Although this setting allowed participants to be assessed within their educational environment, it presented challenges for calibration and stable gaze capture.

The final sample therefore comprised 56 participants, representing 68.3% of the initially assessed sample. The final sample included 28 girls (50.0%) and 28 boys (50.0%), ranging from 11 to 15 years of age (M = 12.77, SD = 1.16). Twelve participants attended the sixth school year (21.4%), 15 attended the seventh year (26.8%), 14 attended the eighth year (25.0%), and 15 attended the ninth year (26.8%). Data were collected between April and July 2025. Written informed consent was obtained from participants' parents or legal guardians, and all participants provided written assent before participating.

Materials

Matrix reasoning task: Matrix reasoning was assessed using an adapted selection of items from the Non-Verbal Reasoning Test for Children (Teste Não Verbal de Raciocínio Infantil, in Portuguese; TNVRI; Pasquali, 2005). The TNVRI contains abstract items composed primarily of geometric figures and contextual items depicting recognizable objects. The adapted task comprised 22 items, including 11 abstract and 11 contextual items. Items were selected in 11 abstract-contextual pairs, with both members of each pair requiring the same relational rule.

Thus, paired items shared their underlying relational structure but differed in whether that structure was represented through abstract geometric elements or familiar objects.

Five pairs consisted of 2 × 3 matrices with six response alternatives, whereas six pairs consisted of 3 × 3 matrices with six response alternatives. The 2 × 3 items constituted the practice set, and the 3 × 3 items constituted the experimental set from which behavioral and eye-tracking measures were obtained. The device supports five-and nine-point calibration procedures and permits limited head movement without requiring participants to wear eye-tracking equipment. Procedure Participants were assessed individually at their school during afternoon sessions conducted between April and July 2025. Each session lasted approximately 30 to 45 minutes.

The matrix-reasoning task was presented on a Dell DC15-I51334U-A50 laptop equipped with a 15.6-inch Full HD display (1920 × 1080 pixels). Participants were positioned approximately 65 cm from the eye tracker, consistent with the manufacturer's recommended operating distance.

The eye tracker was centered below the display and directed toward the participant's face.

Before the task, a nine-point calibration procedure was performed. Calibration results were inspected by the researcher, and the eye tracker was recalibrated whenever the initial results were considered unsatisfactory.

The matrix-reasoning task began with 10 practice items, comprising five abstractcontextual pairs presented as 2 × 3 matrices. These items familiarized participants with the task structure, response alternatives, and response procedure. Participants then completed the 12 experimental items, comprising six abstract-contextual pairs presented as 3 × 3 matrices. For each item, participants inspected the incomplete matrix and verbally indicated which of the six alternatives best completed it. Responses were recorded by the researcher.

Items were presented in a fixed order, identical for all participants, in which the two members of each pair appeared consecutively. Within each matched pair, either the abstract or contextual item could appear first, preventing one condition from consistently preceding the other.

Eye-tracking data processing

Fixations were mapped onto 10 areas of interest (AOIs): nine corresponding to the individual matrix cells and one encompassing all response alternatives (Figure 1). Trials containing fewer than five fixations were excluded because they provided insufficient sequential information for characterizing transitions among AOIs. This threshold ensured that each retained trial could contribute multiple transitions while limiting data loss associated with the schoolbased eye-tracking setting. For each remaining participant-item trial, the ordered sequence of fixated AOIs was extracted, and consecutive fixations within the same AOI were collapsed because they did not represent transitions between task components.

Following Kucharsky et al. (2020), each trial was represented by a transition matrix containing the number of transitions between every pair of AOIs. These trial-level transition matrices were submitted to k-means clustering to identify recurring patterns of visual exploration. The clustering analysis included both abstract and contextual trials and was conducted across trials rather than participants, allowing the same participant to exhibit different patterns across items and conditions.

A two-cluster solution was specified based on previous research identifying two broad patterns of visual exploration in matrix-reasoning tasks (Kucharsky et al., 2020; Laurence & Macedo, 2023 , 2025). Each valid trial was assigned to the cluster with the most similar transition profile. The resulting clusters were subsequently characterized according to their transition patterns and representative scanpaths.

Data analysis

Descriptive statistics were calculated for participants' sociodemographic characteristics, matrix-reasoning performance, and eye-tracking measures. Categorical variables are reported as frequencies and percentages, whereas continuous variables are reported as means, standard deviations, and ranges. Eye-tracking measures were aggregated at the participant-by-condition level and summarized separately for abstract and contextual items. These measures included the mean number of fixations, mean fixation duration, mean item-completion time, and percentage of trials assigned to Cluster 1. Paired-samples t-tests were used to compare these participantlevel measures between conditions.

Trial-level accuracy was analyzed using generalized linear mixed-effects models with a binomial distribution and logit link. The first model examined whether the probability of a correct response differed between abstract and contextual items. Item condition was included as a fixed effect, and a random intercept was included for participant to account for repeated observations. Abstract items served as the reference condition.

A second mixed-effects logistic regression examined whether scanpath classification differed by item condition. Cluster membership was represented as a binary outcome indicating whether each trial was assigned to Cluster 1. Item condition was entered as a fixed effect, with abstract items as the reference condition, and participant was included as a random intercept.

To examine whether the association between visual-exploration patterns and accuracy depended on representational context, a third mixed-effects logistic regression predicted triallevel accuracy from item condition, scanpath cluster, and their interaction. Abstract items and Cluster 1 served as the reference categories, and a random intercept was included for participant.

The condition-by-cluster interaction was evaluated through a likelihood-ratio comparison between models with and without the interaction. Estimated marginal means were used to compare clusters separately within each item condition.

Exploratory analyses examined whether age was associated with reasoning accuracy or scanpath classification and whether these associations differed between abstract and contextual items. Age was treated as a continuous, mean-centered variable. Accuracy was predicted from item condition, age, and their interaction using a mixed-effects logistic regression with a random intercept for participant. A separate mixed-effects logistic regression predicted Cluster 1 classification from item condition, age, and their interaction, also with a random intercept for participant.

All data processing and statistical analyses were conducted in R (R Core Team, 2026) version 4.6.0. Data organization and descriptive summaries were performed using the tidyverse package collection (Wickham et al., 2019), and generalized linear mixed-effects models were fitted using the lme4 package (Bates et al., 2015). Estimated marginal means and pairwise contrasts were calculated using the emmeans package (Lenth & Piaskowski, 2026). Model coefficients were exponentiated and reported as odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals.

Statistical significance was evaluated using an alpha level of .05.

Discussion

The present study examined whether representing the same general relational operations through familiar or abstract elements influenced matrix-reasoning performance and visual exploration among children and adolescents. Three main findings emerged. First, participants were more accurate on contextual than abstract items overall, in line with our primary hypothesis, although this advantage varied considerably across item pairs. Second, item condition predicted the frequency of the two gaze-defined scanpath patterns, indicating that participants explored contextual and abstract problems differently. Third, the association between scanpath cluster and accuracy depended on item condition. The broader, more sequential pattern represented by Cluster 2 was associated with higher accuracy for abstract items, but no cluster difference was detected for contextual items. Together, these findings suggest that representational context affected both performance and processing, as well as the effectiveness of particular patterns of visual exploration.

Participants had substantially higher odds of solving contextual items correctly than abstract items. Model-estimated accuracy increased from approximately 50% for abstract items to 71% for contextual items, even though paired items were selected to require the same general logical operator. At the aggregate level, this finding is consistent with the hypothesis that familiar content can facilitate relational reasoning when the underlying structure of the problem is preserved.

The item-level data, however, qualify this conclusion. The contextual advantage was not uniform across pairs: it was large for the two XOR pairs and for the size-progression pair, absent for the composition pair, and reversed for the quantitative-progression pair. In the XOR pairs, the abstract items were answered at near-chance levels (17.9% and 21.4%; chance = 16.7%).

Although abstract and contextual items did not differ significantly in normative difficulty overall, the normative IRT parameters of the TNVRI showed a similar pattern, with abstract items considerably more difficult than their contextual counterparts only in the XOR pairs, and normative difficulty differences closely tracked the advantage observed in the present sample.

Because these parameters were estimated from the same items, this convergence does not indicate that the contextual advantage is an artifact. Rather, it suggests that the effect of familiar content is replicable across samples but dependent on the relational rule. One possibility is that, in contextual XOR items, participants could identify the answer by tracking the identity of whole objects, whereas abstract XOR items required the cancellation of overlapping geometric components that are more difficult to individuate. The present findings should therefore be read as evidence that familiar content can substantially facilitate some relational operations, rather than as evidence of a general facilitation of matrix reasoning.

The overall contextual advantage is consistent with evidence that meaningful contexts can support problem representation and the selection of effective procedures. Carraher et al. (1985) showed that children solved arithmetic problems more successfully when the problems were embedded in familiar commercial activities than when the same operations were presented through formal calculations. Although matrix reasoning differs substantially from street mathematics, both findings indicate that formal equivalence does not guarantee equivalent cognitive demands. The representation through which a relation is presented can influence how readily its relevant features are identified, maintained, and integrated.

The present findings extend previous developmental matrix studies that incorporated familiar objects but did not directly compare them with relationally matched abstract items (Podjarny et al., 2022; Siegler & Svetina, 2002). They are also compatible with Lancaster et al.'s (2025) argument that variation in stimulus familiarity changes the cognitive resources that can support performance on nominally nonverbal assessments. Familiar animals, foods, and everyday objects may provide semantic labels that help participants encode and distinguish the elements, whereas abstract figures require relevant perceptual dimensions to be identified without an immediately available semantic structure.

Importantly, the contextual advantage was not accompanied by differences in completion time, number of fixations, or mean fixation duration. Participants therefore did not appear to solve contextual items by simply spending more time on them or inspecting them more extensively. Instead, contextualization may have changed how the available inspection time and fixations were organized. This distinction reinforces the value of examining sequential gaze patterns because comparable amounts of visual activity can conceal meaningful differences in how information is selected and coordinated.

These findings are consistent with Rhodes et al.'s (2024) argument that highly controlled assessments should not be treated as context-free. Abstract geometric figures constitute a particular representational context with their own conventions and interpretive demands. The contextual items may have reduced some of these demands by grounding the relational structure in recognizable elements. However, the present results should not be taken to imply that familiar content will invariably improve reasoning. Familiar objects could introduce irrelevant semantic associations in other tasks or populations. Rather, the findings suggest that abstract and familiar representations cannot be assumed to provide interchangeable measures of the same reasoning process.

Furthermore, the two clusters captured qualitatively different organizations of visual exploration. Cluster 1 was characterized by transitions concentrated in the final row and between that row and the response alternatives. Cluster 2 involved broader and more sequential exploration across the matrix, including frequent transitions between neighboring cells throughout all three rows. These patterns, especially Cluster 2, resemble the broader distinction between localized inspection and systematic relational comparison described in previous eyetracking studies of matrix reasoning (Laurence & Macedo, 2023; Niebaum & Munakata, 2023; Vigneau et al., 2006). Nevertheless, the clusters were identified empirically and should not be treated as direct measures of previously defined strategies.

The relative frequency of these patterns differed by item condition. Contextual items had lower odds of Cluster 1 classification and were therefore more frequently associated with the broader Cluster 2 pattern. This result supports our hypothesis that item content would influence visual exploration. Because mean completion time, fixation count, and fixation duration did not differ between conditions, the shift in cluster frequency appears to reflect a difference in the organization of visual search rather than a general increase or decrease in engagement.

One possible interpretation is that familiar objects made the distinct elements within contextual matrices easier to identify and compare, encouraging participants to examine relations across the complete matrix. Abstract figures may have increased uncertainty about which visual distinctions were relevant, resulting in greater reliance on the final row and response alternatives.

This interpretation is consistent with the proposal that familiar representations can support encoding and relational organization. However, gaze data cannot establish why participants adopted a particular scanpath. Cluster membership indicates how visual attention was sequenced, but it does not reveal whether participants verbally labeled the elements, consciously inferred a rule, or understood the relations being inspected.

The interaction between item condition and scanpath cluster is most consistent with the interpretation that contextualization altered the relationship between processing and performance. For abstract items, Cluster 2 trials had significantly higher odds of a correct response than Cluster 1 trials. Thus, when problems were represented through geometric elements, broader and more sequential exploration was more effective than concentrating transitions near the missing cell and response bank. This finding is consistent with developmental evidence that systematic scanning of matrix rows and columns predicts better performance (Niebaum & Munakata, 2023). Abstract items may require participants to inspect multiple cells systematically because neither the relevant dimensions nor the governing relations are semantically specified. Focusing primarily on the final row may provide insufficient information to distinguish the intended transformation from plausible alternatives. More complete scanning may support the identification and verification of relations across multiple rows or columns, reducing reliance on locally salient features.

In contrast, Cluster 2 did not confer a significant advantage over Cluster 1 for contextual items. A localized pattern was therefore not associated with the same performance cost when the matrix contained familiar elements. One possibility is that recognizable objects allowed relevant relations to be encoded more readily, making extensive rescanning less necessary. Participants may have been able to identify or maintain the relations represented in earlier cells and subsequently concentrate their gaze on the final row and alternatives. Under this interpretation, similar observable scanpaths may reflect different underlying cognitive operations depending on the representations being inspected.

The interaction also indicates that the contextual accuracy advantage cannot be explained simply by contextual items producing more Cluster 2 trials. Contextual items were more frequently associated with Cluster 2, but Cluster 2 was selectively advantageous for abstract items. In fact, the contextual advantage was concentrated in Cluster 1 trials, in which estimated accuracy increased from 44.0% for abstract items to 73.8% for contextual items, compared with an increase from 57.2% to 67.5% in Cluster 2 trials. Familiar content therefore appears to have made a localized search of the final row and the response alternatives effective, whereas the same search was considerably less successful with abstract figures. This pattern is consistent with the item-level results: in contextual XOR items, recognizing the identity of whole objects may allow the answer to be found without systematically scanning the entire matrix.

Representational format therefore appears to have affected both the probability of adopting a pattern and the conditions under which that pattern supported accurate performance. This finding accords with the view that strategy effectiveness is conditional on task demands rather than inherent to a strategy itself (Gonthier & Roulin, 2020; Laurence & Macedo, 2023).

Because the present analysis was associational, it does not establish that a scanpath pattern caused a correct response or mediated the effect of contextualization on accuracy.

Successful rule identification could itself produce a particular pattern of eye movements. For example, participants who quickly understood a contextual problem may subsequently have inspected only the cells needed to confirm their solution. Experimental manipulations that direct participants toward particular comparison sequences would be required to determine whether these visual-exploration patterns causally influence performance.

Regarding the developmental considerations, the exploratory analyses provided no evidence that age was associated with accuracy or scanpath classification or that the effects of item condition varied with age. The contextual advantage and difference in cluster frequency were therefore observed across the sampled age range. However, participants were between 11 and 15 years of age, and the study was not designed or powered to test developmental change.

These null associations should consequently not be interpreted as evidence that age is unrelated to matrix-reasoning strategies. Studies covering a wider developmental range have demonstrated changes in systematic scanning and strategy use from childhood into adolescence and adulthood (Gonthier et al., 2024; Niebaum & Munakata, 2023). The present results instead suggest that representational context remains relevant during late childhood and adolescence, even after children have acquired considerable experience with formal educational tasks. This pattern also contrasts with evidence that familiar objects can distract younger children from relational matches (Richland et al., 2006). By early adolescence, familiar content appeared to support rather than compete with relational processing, at least for the rules examined here, although direct comparisons with younger children are needed to test this developmental interpretation.

These findings also have implications for assessment. Several nonverbal tests, including the TNVRI, combine abstract and figurative items within a single score. The present results suggest that such items may not be interchangeable indicators of the same process: depending on the relational rule, familiar content can change both the difficulty of an item and the effectiveness of the strategies used to solve it. Item-level analyses that consider representational content alongside rule type may therefore be useful when interpreting matrix scores, particularly in educational contexts in which these tests inform decisions about students.

Several limitations should be considered. First, the final sample was relatively small and drawn from one public school in São Paulo. Eye-tracking data from a substantial number of initially assessed participants were unavailable or excluded because no fixations were detected on the task. Although this reflects the practical challenges of collecting eye-tracking data in school settings, it may have introduced selection bias and limits the generalizability of the findings. Second, the study included only six experimental pairs. Although paired items involved the same general logical operators, they could not be equated perfectly in perceptual complexity, discriminability, or semantic content. The normative IRT parameters indicated that paired items were closely matched in difficulty for four of the six pairs, but not for the two XOR pairs (Δb = 0.98 and 2.05). Differences attributed to contextualization may therefore partly reflect properties of the particular items. In addition, the statistical models included random effects for participants but not for items. Given the small number of pairs and the heterogeneity of the contextual advantage across them, the condition effect should not be generalized beyond the items examined here. Future studies should include larger item sets, independently assess visual complexity and stimulus familiarity, and model variation across both participants and items.

Finally, the contextual items contained recognizable images but were not embedded in authentic activities comparable to the commercial situations studied by Carraher et al. (1985). The study therefore manipulated stimulus familiarity and semantic meaningfulness rather than sociocultural context in its broader sense. Future research should examine whether participants' specific experiences with objects, testing practices, and educational representations moderate the effects observed here.

To conclude, the findings suggest that the representation of a relational problem matters.

Contextual items were solved more accurately than abstract items, despite being based on corresponding logical operators. They also elicited a different distribution of gaze-defined scanpaths. Most importantly, the effectiveness of a scanpath pattern depended on item condition: broad and sequential exploration was associated with better performance for abstract items, whereas the same advantage was not observed for contextual items. Matrix scores therefore reflect not only whether participants can identify and integrate relations, but also how the task representation organizes the search for those relations. Accounting for this interaction between relational structure, representational context, and cognitive processing is important for interpreting matrix-reasoning performance and for understanding how higher-order cognition is expressed across assessment contexts.
