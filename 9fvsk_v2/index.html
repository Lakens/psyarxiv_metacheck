Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. Some differences come from the ground truth: it lacks p-values with a correction name (“p Bonf = .061”), and counts partial eta squared written with its subscript after it (“η 2 p = .05”), “p = .05”, and some table notes and figure legends (“† p < .10, *p < .05”), which this module leaves out, as non-significant p-values. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. Abstract

The rapid adoption of AI creates both opportunities and risks for human well-being and flourishing, underscoring the need for an integrative interdisciplinary framework to guide its design and evaluation. We advance a flourishing-centered AI framework that draws together philosophical, behavioral, interactional, and technical perspectives. We conceptualize human flourishing as multidimensional, encompassing subjective well-being, psychological functioning, character virtues, and psychological richness. This broader conception of flourishing extends existing approaches by explicitly incorporating character and other goods, and by confronting the possibility that these goods cannot always be maximized simultaneously. Two cross-cutting tensions arise: a temporal tension concerning immediate versus longer-term benefits, and a selfcommunity tension concerning individual versus collective outcomes. We show how these tensions can be translated from behavioral objectives and measurable outcomes into interactional strategies and technical design choices. We conclude with implications for researchers and developers designing AI systems and for policymakers governing their use.

Flourishing-Centered AI: Designing AI for the Multiple Goods and Tensions of Living Well

Powered by recent advances in large language models (LLMs), AI chatbots have become widely used tools capable of human-like dialogue (Demszky et al., 2023). They are reshaping how people think, work, and relate to one another. These usage trends are rising: an estimated 62% of U.S. adults report interacting with AI several times a week, and 31% use it daily (Kennedy et al., 2025). Leveraging their scalability, immediacy, and low cost, AI chatbots are increasingly used to support domains directly connected to human flourishing, including mental health care (Vaidyam et al., 2019), coaching (Plotkina & Sri Ramalu, 2024), psychological interventions (Gual-Montolio et al., 2022; Xu & Ma, 2025), and even social companionship (De Freitas et al., 2025; Pani et al., 2024).

Crucially, these impacts extend beyond purpose-built systems. Many individuals are turning to general-purpose AI models (e.g., ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini) for flourishing-relevant needs: according to one study of almost 500 U.S. residents experiencing mental health challenges who had used LLMs in the past year, 48% reported using AI for therapeutic support, 63% sought personal advice, and 58% drew on AI for emotional insight (Rousmaniere et al., 2025). Given the stakes for human flourishing and the intensifying societal and regulatory scrutiny surrounding AI chatbots (Federal Trade Commission, 2025), empirical efforts to understand, evaluate, and guide their technical development and use cases have accelerated sharply.

While empirical evaluation and feasibility studies are crucial, the design and use of AI chatbots should also be informed by broader interdisciplinary perspectives on what it means for human beings to live well, given the potentially profound effects of these systems on individuals and society. Existing approaches provide important foundations. Positive Computing has sought to incorporate psychological determinants of well-being and human potential into technology design (Calvo & Peters, 2014), while Self-Determination Theory-based approaches emphasize supporting basic psychological needs such as autonomy, competence, and relatedness across different spheres of technology use (Ballou et al., 2022; Ballou et al., 2026; Peters & Calvo, 2023; Peters et al., 2018). These approaches have substantially broadened outcomes to consider human well-being in technology design.

However, identifying desirable psychological needs and well-being outcomes still leaves important dimensions of flourishing and the relationships among them less developed. First, flourishing may encompass goods that extend beyond feeling well or satisfying psychological needs. Many philosophical and psychological traditions understand flourishing to include the development and exercise of character virtues. In positive psychology, these include positive traits and qualities such as wisdom, courage, justice, compassion, and generosity that shape how people reason, choose, act, and relate to others (Fowers, 2008; Peterson & Seligman, 2004; Vallor, 2016). Flourishing also involves psychological richness. Recent work on this construct emphasizes the value of novel, complex, interesting, and perspective-changing experiences (Oishi & Westgate, 2022), while philosophical traditions have recognized goods such as knowledge, aesthetic experience, creativity, play, achievement, and engagement in worthwhile activities as potentially constitutive of a flourishing life (Finnis, 2011; Hurka, 1993; Nussbaum, 2000; Rice, 2013). These perspectives suggest that technology designed for flourishing should consider not only whether people feel good or have their psychological needs satisfied, but also whether it contributes to their capacities to act well and to experience life in rich, varied, and engaging ways.

Second, recognizing the multidimensionality of goods makes it apparent that the constituents of flourishing cannot always be simultaneously maximized. Desirable outcomes may come into tension, requiring choices about what an AI system should prioritize. These tensions become especially consequential for AI chatbots because such systems can continuously adapt their advice, responses, and behavior toward particular objectives. For example, promoting immediate subjective well-being or satisfying a user's preferences may conflict with the effortful processes involved in learning or character development (Fowers, 2008), or with opportunities for novelty or perspective-changing experiences that contribute to a psychologically rich life (Oishi & Westgate, 2022). Further, supporting individual autonomy, authenticity, or comfort may sometimes conflict with virtues and outcomes oriented toward other people and communities, such as justice, generosity, or social responsibility (Peterson & Seligman, 2004). Similarly, AI systems optimized around an individual's existing preferences may provide highly comfortable personalized experiences while reducing encounters with unfamiliar perspectives and ways of life that can enrich engagement with a diverse social world.

In short, our framework extends existing approaches to well-beingsupportive technology in two primary ways. First, it adopts a multidimensional conception of human flourishing encompassing subjective well-being, psychological functioning, character virtues, and psychological richness. In doing so, it incorporates previous concerns central to Positive Computing and Self-Determination Theory-informed technology design (Calvo & Peters, 2014; Peters & Calvo, 2023) while broadening the criterion of what it means for technology to support a life lived well. Second, rather than focusing only on identifying outcomes that technology should support, we foreground the tensions that arise when these desirable goods cannot be simultaneously maximized. We organize these tensions around two cross-cutting questions: (1) a temporal tension, concerning when flourishing is optimized -immediate versus longer-term flourishing; and (2) a self-community tension, concerning whose flourishing is optimized -the individual versus broader relational and collective flourishing. We examine how these broad tensions propagate from behavioral objectives to interactional strategies and technical implementation. Flourishing-Centered AI: Aspects of Flourishing, Core Tensions, and Sociotechnical Components

Human Flourishing

Human flourishing refers broadly to living well, but philosophical traditions differ in what they understand a flourishing life to involve (Lin, 2022). These views can be arranged along a continuum, from subjective theories centered on people's experiences and evaluations of their lives to more objective theories that specify goods -such as healthy functioning, virtue, and aesthetics -as valuable in their own right. Hedonic or subjective accounts emphasize how positively people experience and evaluate their lives, including pleasure, positive affect, and life satisfaction (Diener et al., 2018). Other accounts emphasize psychological functioning and the development and exercise of human capacities, including autonomy, agency, competence, purpose, personal growth, and meaningful relationships (Nussbaum, 2000; Nussbaum & Sen, 1993; Ryff & Keyes, 1995). Aristotelian approaches further emphasize the cultivation and exercise of character virtues, such as courage, temperance, justice, and generosity, through which people reason and act well (Crisp, 2014). Still other perspectives recognize the value of rich and engaging experiences. Recent work on psychological richness, for example, argues that a good life can derive value from experiences that are novel, complex, interesting, and perspectivechanging, even when they do not necessarily increase happiness or meaning (Oishi & Westgate, 2022). Related philosophical perspectives, including objective-list, perfectionist, and capabilities approaches, recognize goods such as knowledge, aesthetic experience, creativity, play, achievement, and engagement in worthwhile activities as potentially constitutive of a flourishing life (Finnis, 2011; Hurka, 1993; Nussbaum, 2000; Rice, 2013).

Although some of these theories are monistic -hedonism identifies pleasure as the sole basic good, and perfectionism explains diverse goods through the development of human nature (Lin, 2022) -the range of goods they collectively identify suggests that flourishing is multidimensional: living well is difficult to reduce to any single desirable psychological state or outcome (Hall & Tiberius, 2015; Scanlon, 1998). For purposes of translating these broad perspectives into AI design, we organize flourishing into four overlapping domains: subjective well-being, psychological functioning, character virtues, and psychological richness. Subjective well-being concerns how people experience and evaluate their lives (Diener, 2000; Diener et al., 2018). Psychological functioning concerns whether people function and develop well, encompassing constructs such as autonomy, competence, relatedness, purpose, agency, and personal growth (Ryan & Deci, 2000; Ryff & Keyes, 1995). Character virtues concern capacities for reasoning and acting well, including courage, compassion, honesty, justice, perseverance, and practical wisdom (Fowers, 2008; Peterson & Seligman, 2004). Finally, psychological richness concerns the extent to which life affords interesting, varied, complex, creative, aesthetically engaging, and perspective-expanding experiences (Oishi & Westgate, 2022). These domains are not intended as an exhaustive philosophical theory of flourishing, but as an organizing framework for translating major strands of thinking about the good life into behavioral targets that can inform AI design and evaluation. This multidimensional conception incorporates many of the concerns already central to well-being-supportive technology design. Positive Computing, for example, emphasizes designing technology to support well-being and human potential (Calvo & Peters, 2014), while Self-Determination Theory-informed approaches emphasize supporting autonomy, competence, and relatedness across different spheres of technology experience (Peters et al., 2018; Peters & Calvo, 2023). A flourishing perspective builds on these foundations but incorporates a wider range of goods, including character virtues and psychological richness. Critically, with different constituents of flourishing, we need to recognize that they cannot always be simultaneously maximized. An AI system designed to maximize immediate satisfaction, for example, may provide easy and agreeable experiences while reducing opportunities for challenge, exploration, learning, or character development. Similarly, optimizing exclusively around an individual's preferences may conflict with goods involving relationships, other people, or communities. This multidimensionality gives rise to a central design problem for flourishing-centered AI: when desirable aspects of flourishing conflict, what should an AI system optimize? We foreground two cross-cutting tensions that recur across these domains. The first, the temporal tension, concerns when benefits are realized: immediate versus longer-term flourishing. The second, the self-community tension, concerns whose flourishing is considered: individual versus collective flourishing. We do not claim that temporal and self-community tensions exhaust the tradeoffs involved in AI design. Important concerns such as autonomy versus safety, personalization versus privacy, convenience versus transparency, and openness versus control can arise in particular applications. We foreground the temporal and self-community dimensions because they pose two cross-cutting questions that recur across conceptions of flourishing and across behavioral, interactional, and technical design: when are benefits being optimized, and whose flourishing is being optimized? Therefore, these dimensions function as organizing lenses rather than an exhaustive taxonomy of AI design tradeoffs. Behavioral Components

Psychology and the social sciences more broadly have sought to contribute to the empirical assessment and formation of human flourishing -both at the individual and community level (VanderWeele, 2017). Behavioral sciences offer tools to operationalize flourishing (Lomas et al., 2025; Su et al., 2014) and delineate the processes for fostering it, ranging from the mitigative (American Psychological Association, 2012) to the promotive (Seligman et al., 2005; Seligman, 2011). Within the sociotechnical framework for AI chatbots, behavioral components therefore provide the empirical bridge between philosophical conceptions of flourishing and AI design. They translate broad goods of flourishing and the tensions among them into constructs that can be measured, modeled, and shaped through intervention (Deci & Ryan, 2001; Waterman, 2008). In doing so, behavioral science provides both measurable targets for designing AI chatbots and criteria for evaluating whether these systems contribute to human flourishing.

Following the philosophical framework developed above, we organize these behavioral targets into four broad and overlapping domains. These domains are not intended as a new or exhaustive taxonomy of flourishing but rather provide an organizing structure for translating multidimensional conceptions of the good life into empirically measurable outcomes (Table 1). First, subjective well-being concerns how people experience and evaluate their lives, encompassing evaluative dimensions such as life satisfaction and affective dimensions such as positive and negative emotions (Diener, 2000; Diener et al., 2018). Second, psychological functioning concerns the psychological resources involved in functioning and developing well, including autonomy, competence, relatedness, purpose, agency, personal growth, and positive relationships (Deci & Ryan, 2001; Ryff & Singer, 2000). Third, character virtues concern positive, emulation-worthy qualities that shape how people reason, choose, act, and relate to others, including strengths of heart (e.g., love, gratitude), mind (e.g., curiosity, wisdom), and will (e.g., bravery, perseverance), as well as other-oriented virtues such as justice and compassion (Peterson & Seligman, 2004). Finally, psychological richness captures the extent to which people encounter and engage with varied, interesting, complex, creative, aesthetically engaging, and perspective-changing experiences (Oishi, 2025; Oishi & Westgate, 2022). Together, these four domains provide behavioral criteria for asking not simply whether AI makes users feel better, but whether it helps them feel well, function and develop well, act well, and engage richly with life. These domains have distinct implications for AI chatbot design and evaluation. For subjective well-being, chatbots might be evaluated by their effects on positive emotions, distress, life satisfaction, and other subjective experiences. For psychological functioning, relevant outcomes include whether AI supports rather than undermines autonomy, competence, purpose, agency, and human relationships. For character virtues, AI systems might be evaluated by whether repeated interactions cultivate or inhibit qualities such as curiosity, perseverance, compassion, intellectual humility, and practical wisdom, potentially through reflective prompts, perspective-taking, or appropriately challenging guidance (Bohm et al., 2023; Li et al., 2025; Tang & Putra, 2025). Finally, for psychological richness, AI systems might be evaluated by whether they expand or narrow users' exposure to novel ideas, experiences, perspectives, creative activities, and opportunities for exploration. For example, highly personalized AI may increase immediate relevance and satisfaction by repeatedly providing content aligned with existing preferences while simultaneously reducing serendipity, novelty, and exposure to perspectives that could make users' experiences psychologically richer. Thus, behavioral evaluation requires attention to multiple goods of flourishing rather than optimization around any single outcome.

Importantly, operationalizing flourishing into multiple behavioral outcomes also makes the tensions among these goods empirically tractable. Rather than treating the temporal and selfcommunity tensions as psychological constructs or scales in themselves, we conceptualize them as dimensions along which the outcomes of AI use can diverge. Behavioral science allows these divergences to be measured by assessing outcomes across different time horizons and different levels of beneficiaries.

Temporal tension.

The temporal tension concerns when the effects of AI on flourishing are realized. Some behavioral outcomes can change rapidly. Emotional relief, positive affect, perceived support, and satisfaction with an AI response, for example, can be assessed immediately following an interaction (Bilquise et al., 2022). Other constituents of flourishing (e.g., competence, autonomy, relationships, habits, character virtues, and psychological richness) may emerge through repeated experiences and develop over substantially longer periods (Smith & Vickers, 2024). Similarly, single-occasion assessments (e.g., momentary emotion ratings) offer momentary snapshots, whereas longitudinal assessments reveal patterns of growth and change over time (Buecker et al., 2023). The relevant behavioral question is therefore not simply whether an AI interaction produces a beneficial outcome, but whether immediate and longer-term outcomes converge or diverge. Effective AI chatbot design needs to employ a range of measures to enhance immediate states while also contributing to longer-term trajectories of flourishing. A chatbot that immediately provides an answer may increase satisfaction while reducing opportunities to develop competence; one that consistently affirms a user's existing views may provide comfort while reducing opportunities for perspective change; and one that introduces reflective questions or constructive friction may produce short-term frustration while contributing to learning, autonomy, perseverance, or psychological richness over time.

Psychological pathways also clarify how AI chatbots can support positive change. Human motivational differences are critical to consider: hedonic motives (seeking comfort, pleasure, relief) guide immediate preferences, whereas eudaimonic motives (seeking purpose, growth, and virtue) promote deeper forms of flourishing over time (Huta & Ryan, 2009). Immediate human emotions and preferences are currently often the focus of human-AI interactions (Moerland et al., 2017); however, flourishing also requires support for eudaimonic motives (Gentzler et al., 2021). Complementary temporal dynamics also emerge in research on goals and habits (Wood, 2024; Wood & Neal, 2007). Goals operate through conscious intentions and outcome monitoring, offering flexibility and direction but often failing without planning, cues, or feedback. Habits, in contrast, develop gradually through repeated action in stable contexts, as contextual cues become directly linked to behavior and can sustain action independent of momentary motivation. AI chatbots can scaffold both pathways by supporting short-term goal articulation and planning while reinforcing context-consistent routines that foster durable habits, thereby aligning immediate benefits with longer-term flourishing. Accordingly, evaluation of flourishing-centered AI requires longitudinal designs capable of examining whether immediate benefits accumulate into, are unrelated to, or potentially undermine longer-term trajectories across the four domains of flourishing.

Self-community tension.

The self-community tension concerns whose flourishing is being assessed and optimized. Social scientific research has demonstrated that, in multiple scenarios, when individuals prioritize personal benefit over collective well-being, the outcomes can be detrimental to the group (Kollock, 1998; Van Lange et al., 2013). On the other hand, an emphasis on community benefit can inadvertently create greater marginalization of vulnerable members (e.g., population-level interventions) (Frohlich & Potvin, 2008). The same issue can arise across the four domains of flourishing. An AI system may increase an individual's subjective well-being while adversely affecting close relationships; support one user's autonomy while imposing costs on others; encourage personally advantageous behavior that conflicts with justice or generosity; or personalize experiences in a manner that limits unfamiliar perspectives and experiences.

Behavioral measurement must therefore extend beyond outcomes reported by the individual AI user. Collective outcomes can include aggregates and distributional indices of individual flourishing, such as the average and distribution of life satisfaction within a community (Tay et al., 2021). Yet, beyond distributional considerations, collective flourishing also entails emergent social properties that are not reducible to individual self-reports (Klein & Kozlowski, 2000), including social cohesion, prosocial norms, trust, and institutional legitimacy. These require alternative measurement approaches, such as referent-shift models (Chan, 1998), that capture how individuals perceive and experience the collective, rather than their own subjective well-being.

Distinguishing individual from relational and collective outcomes also makes explicit the normative choices embedded in AI evaluation. Decisions about what constitutes a desirable outcome inevitably involve questions about whose outcomes count, how benefits and harms are distributed, how responsibility is attributed between individual choice and structural constraints, and which forms of inequality are considered acceptable or remediable (Binns, 2020). As such, flourishing-centered AI requires evaluation at multiple levels and attention to both aggregate and distributional consequences. Rather than assuming that an AI system that benefits an individual user necessarily promotes flourishing more broadly, behavioral evaluation should test when individual, relational, and collective outcomes converge and when they come into tension. These behavioral objectives and measures, in turn, establish the outcomes that interactional and technical components of AI systems must be designed to support and balance (Stray, 2020; van der Maden et al., 2022).

Importantly, these should be assessed across both time horizons and levels of analysis, clarifying when immediate versus longer-term or individual versus collective outcomes converge or diverge.

Interactional Components

Interactional components are where the broader tensions of flourishing become concrete design choices in the user experience. Drawing from human-computer interaction (HCI), human-AI interaction, and user experience (UX) research, this layer concerns how system objectives are enacted through the interface and dialogue mechanics of AI chatbots (Amershi et al., 2019; Yang & Aurisicchio, 2021). Choices about how an AI frames its role, structures the pace of an exchange, personalizes responses, presents itself socially, and connects users with others can privilege different forms of flourishing. In this way, more specific interactional tradeoffs (e.g., comfort versus challenge, rapid assistance versus reflection, preference matching versus perspective broadening, and one-to-one support versus community engagement) instantiate the broader temporal and self-community tensions in our framework. Because interaction design also shapes user acceptance, trust, and engagement, it can determine whether the system's intended flourishing objectives are ultimately realized (Diederich et al., 2022).

Several interactional issues are particularly consequential for AI chatbot design, as summarized in Table 1. The first concerns interaction framing: how AI chatbots position themselves to users shapes expectations for the nature and purpose of the interaction. AI chatbots can be framed as sources of comfort, advice, or challenge that shape users' expectations for support and growth. This draws on broader research on AI chatbots demonstrating that users form distinct expectations based on how systems present themselves, with implications for engagement (Bickmore et al., 2005; Ngu et al., 2023), self-disclosure (Kim & Im, 2023; Wambsganss et al., 2025; Warren-Smith et al., 2025), and psychological outcomes (Meadows et al., 2024; Warren-Smith et al., 2025). Closely related is interaction pacing, or how the speed of interaction -or anticipated speed of a solution provided -is designed. Frictionless, rapid exchanges may promote efficiency and immediate relief, especially for younger individuals (Wang et al., 2025). However, designing slow technology (Hallnäs & Redström, 2001) by creating slower pacing, reflective prompts, and staged guidance can encourage reflection, meaningful interactions, and contemplation for growth (Au-Yeung et al., 2025; Britos Cavagnaro, 2024; Grosse-Hering et al., 2013; Hui et al., 2020). A third issue involves personalization. Highly personalized interactions may potentially enhance relevance and engagement by tailoring responses to individual preferences and needs (Shumanov & Johnson, 2021), but may also inadvertently reinforce narrower values or self-focused perspectives (Jacob et al., 2025). This distinction may provide more nuance, given that recent meta-analyses show mixed effects of personalization on psychological outcomes (Matthews & Rhodes-Maquaire, 2024). A fourth issue concerns the anthropomorphization of AI chatbots. Given the social interaction context, research shows that users naturally attribute human-like qualities to conversational systems (Nass et al., 1994) -but design choices (e.g., framing; visual design of avatars) can amplify or attenuate this (Lucas et al., 2014; Ma et al., 2025; Rafikova & Voronin, 2025). Though anthropomorphization may increase trust and engagement to enhance advice receipt and AI chatbot support (Park et al., 2023; Ta et al., 2020), it may also create unrealistic expectations of AI chatbots (Crolic et al., 2021) or overreliance that result in the growing worries that users may be substituting human connection (Fang et al., 2025; Moylan & Doherty, 2025; Zhang et al., 2025). A final issue is social interaction. Interaction designs that provide opportunities for interaction with others (e.g., group discussion, community-oriented interfaces) can enhance collective flourishing and greater concern for other perspectives. Research shows that AI-facilitated group discussions can help find common ground on divisive topics, creating greater perspective-taking and social cohesion (e.g., Argyle et al., 2023; Tessler et al., 2024). Questions remain on whether AI chatbots that facilitate human social interaction afford a sense of autonomy required for personal subjective well-being. Together, these interactional components show that the effects of AI chatbots on flourishing depend not only on the objectives they pursue, but on the interaction structures through which those objectives are realized. Interaction design therefore serves as the translation layer between behavioral objectives and technical implementation, determining how broader temporal and self-community tradeoffs are experienced by users in practice.

The interaction layer also illustrates how the two overarching tensions in our framework underlie more specific design tradeoffs. As Table 2 shows, choices such as comfort versus challenge, rapid assistance versus reflection, preference matching versus perspective broadening, connection versus relational boundaries, and one-to-one support versus community engagement can be understood in terms of when benefits are realized and whose flourishing is prioritized. These interaction-level tensions are therefore not separate from the temporal and self-community tensions; rather, they are concrete ways in which the broader tensions are instantiated through chatbot design. The mapping is not necessarily one-to-one: some interactional choices primarily implicate the temporal tension, others more directly implicate the self-community tension, and several can engage both simultaneously.

Temporal tension.

Interaction design directly influences whether AI chatbots privilege immediacy or longer-term development. Seamless, highly responsive interactions of AI chatbots can effectively support short-term subjective well-being by providing emotional relief and rapid assistance. Research shows that AI chatbots can reduce loneliness (De Freitas et al., 2025), but there are also findings demonstrating that AI chatbots for mental health improve immediate but not longer-term outcomes (He et al., 2023; Tong et al., 2025). Furthermore, an exclusive emphasis on immediacy may encourage overattachment and dependency (Vaidyam et al., 2019), cognitive offloading (Gerlich, 2025), or reliance on AI for quick solutions, leading to lower cognitive activity and future learning (Kosmyna et al., 2025). In contrast, interactional features such as deliberate pacing, reflective questioning, and follow-up prompts may introduce constructive friction that supports learning, self-regulation, and sustained growth. Recent work shows that students with access to standard AI chatbot conversations that provide immediate solutions to math problems -rather than learning hints or prompts -had greater performance than non-users; but performed worse than non-users once the AI chatbot was removed, though this was not the case for AI chatbots designed for longer-term learning (Bastani et al., 2025). These design choices make explicit the tradeoff between optimizing interactions for immediate comfort and solution, compared to structuring interactions to support longer-term learning, character development, and flourishing over time.

Self-community tension.

Interactional design also shapes whether AI chatbots orient users primarily toward personal benefit or toward benefiting the broader community and society. Personalization and one-to-one interaction can amplify individual preferences and satisfaction, but risk reinforcing self-centered perspectives or biases, such as the "chat-chamber effect" where AI sycophancy (Sharma et al., 2024) and hallucination can create a personal echo-chamber (Jacob et al., 2025), especially in long-term use. Conversely, interaction designs that surface social context, prompt consideration of others, emphasize shared norms, or facilitate interaction among users can enhance collective flourishing. Recent HCI research is increasingly moving beyond one-to-one chat toward multi-party conversational agents, AI-supported deliberation, and LLM-mediated conflict de-escalation (e.g., Argyle et al., 2023; Tessler et al., 2024), highlighting that interaction design can explicitly scaffold collective goods -not only individual satisfaction, but also mutual understanding and socially sustainable flourishing.

Summary.

Interactional components specify how flourishing objectives are translated into the user's experience of the chatbot. The more specific tensions typically discussed in interaction design can be understood as manifestations of the two broader tensions in Figure 2. For the temporal tension, choices such as comfort versus challenge and rapid assistance versus reflection determine whether an interaction prioritizes short-term relief and satisfaction or longer-term learning, growth, and character development. For the self-community tension, choices such as preference matching versus perspective broadening and one-to-one support versus community engagement determine whether the interaction is oriented primarily toward the individual user or toward relationships, other people, and collective flourishing. Framing, pacing, personalization, anthropomorphization, and social interaction thus provide concrete design levers through which these broader temporal and self-community tensions are navigated.

Technical Components

The technical architecture of AI chatbots fundamentally shapes their capacity to support human flourishing. While behavioral science provides the empirical foundations and interaction design guides user experience, the underlying computational systems determine what kinds of content and support AI chatbots can actually provide.

At their core, modern AI chatbots are built on LLMs trained through a process involving pre-training on vast text corpora, fine-tuning on conversational data, and reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) -a process in which human raters judge which model responses they prefer, and the system learns to match those preferences (Ouyang et al., 2022; Zhao et al., 2023). Training objectives determine what the system is optimized for, and current methods largely prioritize satisfaction and engagement (Stray et al., 2020; Ziegler et al., 2019), while memory architectures determine whether it can track user development over time.

Several key technical considerations emerge as critical for supporting human flourishing in AI chatbots. Training objectives represent a fundamental challenge as current methods optimize for user satisfaction and engagement metrics that are only one aspect of human flourishing (Stray et al., 2020; Ziegler et al., 2019). Newer approaches seek to consider multiple objectives simultaneously (Hayes et al., 2022), or use reinforcement learning from AI feedback (RLAIF), where the supervising AI is programmed with specific principles (Bai et al., 2022). Model transparency and interpretability are limited in LLMs, which can make it difficult to understand how they process complex human needs or to ensure they appropriately balance and weight different criteria (Lipton, 2018; Weidinger et al., 2022b). Memory and temporal modeling is another issue as AI chatbots need to maintain longitudinal understanding of user development over time to support flourishing, but current research suggests significant limitations in LLMs remembering over long-term multisession conversations (Wu et al., 2025; Xu et al., 2022). Finally, safety features need to ensure a balance between being overly restrictive and overly permissive (Qi et al., 2024; Röttger et al., 2024) and be carefully evaluated beyond single prompts to emulate multisession conversations over time (Weidinger et al., 2023).

The combination of these technical aspects produces profound temporal and selfcommunity tensions as predictable consequences of current design choices, and we argue that technical innovation is required in order to develop AI chatbots that differentiate between immediate satisfaction versus longer-term and collective flourishing.

Temporal tension.

It is critical to note that technical systems for training and deploying AI chatbots are structurally biased toward short-term optimization. The dominant training paradigm, RLHF, trains reward models on human preference judgments that are rendered within seconds (Casper et al., 2023; Sharma et al., 2024), which naturally favor hedonic, agreeable, and immediately appealing responses over eudaimonic needs that emerge through challenge and sustained effort (Deci & Ryan, 2001; Huta & Ryan, 2009). These judgments are distilled into a reward model that predicts immediate satisfaction rather than longer-term constructs such as character virtue, producing documented patterns of sycophancy that empirical work has traced to biases in preference data and shown to be amplified rather than corrected by post-training (Perez et al., 2023; Sharma et al., 2024). The resulting model is thus tilted toward responses that feel good now, and we argue that a clear-eyed integration of longer-term objectives is requiredsupported by transparency in training principles and evaluation criteria, so that alignment with human flourishing can be independently audited. Memory and temporal modeling point to a parallel need for a principled approach to human growth: the system must remember and forget with respect to developmental arcs, since the formation of character virtues is aspirationaloriented toward who a person is becoming rather than anchored to past behavior -and even frontier LLMs lag substantially behind human performance on multisession memory tasks (Maharana et al., 2024; Wu et al., 2025). With respect to safety, AI chatbots can pass individualprompt checks while gradually fostering dependence, eroding offline social ties, or reinforcing maladaptive coping; recent longitudinal evidence links higher daily chatbot use to greater loneliness, emotional dependence, and problematic use (Fang et al., 2025), while multi-turn redteaming shows that safety behavior itself degrades across extended conversations (Anwar et al., 2024; Li et al., 2024). Safety frameworks for flourishing-oriented AI must therefore be evaluated longitudinally rather than at the level of individual prompts.

Self-community tension.

Standard RLHF pools preference judgments across individuals into a single reward model (Casper et al., 2023), creating an inherent majority bias in which dominant cultural values disproportionately shape model behavior while minority conceptions of flourishing are diluted or erased (Chakraborty et al., 2024; Xiao et al., 2024). This is compounded by training corpora that overrepresent certain demographics and cultural contexts (Durmus et al., 2023; Santurkar et al., 2023), encoding or weighting particular perspectives about human flourishing universally. The opacity of these systems deepens the problem: because reward models and learned representations are not interpretable, neither developers nor users can readily audit whose values are being privileged, whether minority conceptions are being systematically downweighted, or whether the system is engaging in manipulation or deceptionignoring negative social externalities -to obtain more positive feedback (Williams et al., 2025). Memory and personalization architectures can intensify this individualistic orientation. By tracking and adapting to a single user without any representation of that person's relational or community embeddedness, they optimize for the individual in isolation and can reinforce rather than broaden existing views -as when introducing a more opinionated AI chatbot to address polarization instead entrenches people's prior stances (Jakesch et al., 2023). Addressing these issues requires technical mechanisms that represent value plurality without averaging away differences, architectures that incorporate collective constraints alongside personalized support, and distributional evaluation frameworks that assess fairness and equity that go beyond average user satisfaction (Sorensen et al., 2024).

Summary.

As shown in Figure 2, technical components specify how these behavioral objectives and interactional strategies are encoded, optimized, and sustained by the underlying AI system. Training objectives, transparency and interpretability, memory and temporal modeling, and safety mechanisms determine which outcomes the system prioritizes, over what time horizon, and for whose benefit. A flourishing-centered approach calls for moving beyond optimization of a single proximal metric, such as satisfaction, toward explicit multi-objective optimization of multidimensional flourishing outcomes across time and across individual and collective levels.

Implications for Policy, Research, and Technical Development

Flourishing-centered AI begins from a multidimensional conception of human flourishing and foregrounds two cross-cutting tensions that arise when different goods cannot be simultaneously maximized: the temporal tension between immediate and longer-term flourishing and the self-community tension between individual and broader relational or collective flourishing. Our sociotechnical framework makes these tensions actionable by tracing them across three interconnected components: behavioral science defines and measures the outcomes that matter, interaction design translates those objectives into concrete user experiences, and technical systems encode and optimize those objectives (Figure 2).

The central implication is that designing AI for flourishing requires not simply selecting desirable outcomes, but making explicit what is being optimized, over what time horizon, for whom, and through what design choices.

This perspective offers actionable guidance across policy, research, and technical development. Rather than treating AI chatbots as categorically beneficial or harmful to human flourishing, the framework helps identify which dimensions of flourishing are affected, where tradeoffs emerge, and under what conditions benefits in one domain may create costs in another. It also highlights where current approaches disproportionately emphasize immediate and individual-level outcomes while leaving longer-term and collective consequences comparatively underexamined.

Policy and Programs.

Identifying the fundamental tensions provides a principled foundation for governance. Current regulatory approaches often focus narrowly on harm prevention (e.g., content safety, data privacy) (Mittelstadt, 2019; Weidinger et al., 2022a) that do not fully address the ways AI chatbots can be leveraged to positively promote broad human flourishing at both the individual and collective level. A flourishing-centered approach expands the policy question from whether a system is safe or effective to what forms of flourishing it supports, whose flourishing is prioritized, and over what time horizon.

Effective policy must consequently move beyond binary assessments of whether AI is beneficial or harmful and examine the distribution and timing of its effects (Raji et al., 2021; Sen, 1999). This requires recognizing the tensions in human flourishing when making deployment decisions and evaluating AI chatbots (Selbst et al., 2019) that go beyond model performance (e.g., accuracy) (Birhane et al., 2022). For mental health applications, regulatory frameworks should distinguish between AI chatbots designed for immediate symptom relief versus those supporting sustained therapeutic progress and independence, with corresponding differences in oversight requirements (Palmer, 2025). Similarly, policies governing AI in education should evaluate systems not only on immediate, testable improvements (e.g., knowledge gains), but also on whether they foster or undermine the development of independent reasoning, character virtues, and moral capacities over time (Llano, 2025; Zagzebski, 1996).

The self-community tension further suggests that deploying AI chatbots at scale, whether in institutions or communities, requires community-level impact assessments. These should go beyond aggregated individual outcomes to also consider emergent social properties such as trust, social cohesion, and the quality of human relationships that may be displaced or enhanced by AI interactions (Putnam, 2000). This requires exploring new measures of collective constructs that are sensitive to societal concerns. For instance, high-SES friendships among individuals with low SES most strongly predicted upward income mobility, among different measures of community social capital (Chetty et al., 2022a (Chetty et al., , 2022b)). Such findings illustrate why the social consequences of AI deployment cannot be inferred solely from individual satisfaction or well-being.

Research. The framework also highlights critical gaps in current empirical approaches. Most evaluations of AI chatbots rely on relatively short-term designs assessing immediate responses and preferences (Casper et al., 2023). Although valuable, these designs provide limited evidence about whether AI contributes to flourishing over time. We know considerably less about whether sustained chatbot use promotes or undermines behavior change, psychological functioning, character virtue development, relational functioning, or other outcomes that may emerge only through repeated interactions.

Future research needs to adopt longitudinal designs that track flourishing indicators across extended timeframes beyond single sessions, such as ecological momentary assessments (Shiffman et al., 2008; Thapa et al., 2021). This includes not only repeated measures of immediate satisfaction but also assessments of experiential and relational aspects, character virtues, and moral capacities that may develop or worsen through sustained AI interactions. Further, beyond immediate well-being outcomes (Ng et al., 2023), experimental and observational comparisons of flourishing impacts from differently optimized AI chatbots can be made on the temporal (e.g., immediate comfort vs. growth) and/or self-community dimensions (e.g., individual vs. community).

Equally important is expanding measurement beyond self-reports or human feedback (Eichstaedt et al., 2021; Eichstaedt et al., 2015). The self-community tension underscores the need for multilevel research designs that capture both individual flourishing and collective outcomes (Klein & Kozlowski, 2000). These can include referent-shift measures, behavioral assessments, and social network analytic indicators. Such research can illuminate whether AI chatbots enhance individual flourishing while inadvertently eroding social cohesion, or whether careful design can promote both individual and collective good. This research also feeds directly back into the sociotechnical pipeline. Behavioral evidence provides the criteria for determining which outcomes technical systems should optimize and which interaction strategies successfully balance competing objectives. The framework calls not only for evaluating existing AI systems, but for comparative research on alternative design and optimization strategies.

Technical Development and Interactional Design.

Addressing the temporal tension requires fundamental advances in how AI systems are trained and evaluated. Current RLHF approaches optimize for immediate user approval (Ouyang et al., 2022; Zhao et al., 2023), creating a bias toward short-term satisfaction and sycophancy (Sharma et al., 2024). Multiobjective training approaches (e.g., adapting temporal difference learning for conversational contexts) (Hayes et al., 2022) may optimize simultaneously for immediate and longer-term outcomes, thereby striking a balance between them. However, this requires new memory architectures capable of maintaining longitudinal user models that track character development and flourishing indicators across extended timeframes (Packer et al., 2023; Wu et al., 2025).

One tractable intervention is RLAIF, which replaces human raters with an AI rater that evaluates responses against explicit principles (e.g., by directly encoding the hedoniceudaimonic tradeoff) (Bai et al., 2022; Lee et al., 2024). A deeper approach draws on recent work training AI to evaluate not just final answers but each step in a reasoning process (Lightman et al., 2024; Uesato et al., 2022). Applied to flourishing, this means evaluating the developmental quality of responses in an interaction: did the model's behavior encourage reflection, introduce appropriate challenge, maintain user autonomy, etc.? Such response-level signals can serve as proximal indicators of longer-term developmental processes and connect naturally to the behavioral constructs identified earlier.

To complement this, systems can also learn from the behavior of people who optimize for the long-term well-being of others -therapists, parents, coaches, and educators -whose per-turn decisions implicitly encode longer-horizon goals; clinical frameworks such as motivational interviewing (Miller & Rollnick, 2013) and the educational practice of scaffolding (Wood et al., 1976) formalize precisely this kind of present-action-for-future-growth reasoning. The most ambitious approach would maintain separate training objectives at per-response, per-session, and longitudinal timescales, requiring memory systems that maintain structured and evolving user representations.

Interaction design provides another important technical lever. Adaptive systems could calibrate challenge, reflection, and constructive friction based on individual readiness and growth trajectories (Isaacs et al., 2024; Li et al., 2025). Technical evaluation frameworks must also expand to include measures of delayed outcomes (e.g., sustained behavior change, skill development, enhancement of character and moral capacities) integrated into multi-objective training (Hayes et al., 2022). In this sense, the technical challenge is not merely better personalization, but personalization toward flourishing trajectories rather than a sequence of momentary preference satisfaction.

The self-community tension demands technical innovations that embed collective considerations directly into system design. Multi-agent training frameworks can optimize AI chatbots to balance individual user needs against broader social outcomes, as in work formulating recommendations as long-term social-welfare optimization across a coupled ecosystem of agents (Mladenov et al., 2020) and multi-agent studies of cooperation and defection in social dilemmas (Leibo et al., 2017). Value alignment methodologies must incorporate collective values and social norms -not merely aggregated individual preferencesrequiring advances in how training data represents community-level goods. Federated learning and privacy-preserving techniques offer pathways for AI systems to learn from collective patterns while respecting individual privacy (Abadi et al., 2016; Kairouz et al., 2021; McMahan et al., 2017), enabling optimization that considers both personal and community flourishing. Finally, technical evaluation must expand beyond individual user metrics to include measures of social impact, prosocial behaviors, and effects on human-to-human connections.

Across these approaches, the central technical implication mirrors the broader framework. AI systems should not implicitly collapse flourishing into a single reward signal. Developers should instead make explicit which dimensions of flourishing are being optimized, how competing objectives are weighted, over what time horizons outcomes are evaluated, and whose outcomes enter the objective function. Behavioral science can specify and validate these outcomes, interaction design can determine how they are instantiated in the user experience, and technical development can encode and optimize them.

Conclusion

The question of how to live well has animated human inquiry across cultures and throughout history. As people increasingly interact with and rely on AI chatbots for advice, learning, support, companionship, and everyday decision-making, the design of these systems can increasingly shape the conditions under which people pursue flourishing -both individually and collectively.

We have argued that designing AI for flourishing requires moving beyond any single conception of well-being or simple assumption that desirable outcomes can all be simultaneously maximized. Human flourishing is multidimensional, and its constituent goods can come into tension. We foreground two questions that cut across these tensions: When is flourishing being optimized, immediately or over the longer term? And whose flourishing is being optimized, the individual or that of the community?

Our flourishing-centered AI framework translates these questions into a sociotechnical agenda. Behavioral science identifies and measures the outcomes that matter; interaction design determines how those objectives are experienced and negotiated in human-AI interaction; and technical systems determine how they are represented, balanced, and optimized. The aim is therefore not to identify a single objective function for human flourishing, but to make these choices and tradeoffs explicit, measurable, empirically testable, and technically actionable.

Ultimately, the central design question is not simply whether AI chatbots improve wellbeing. It is what forms of flourishing they support, for whom, over what time horizon, and through what behavioral, interactional, and technical choices. Making those questions explicit provides a path toward AI systems that do more than respond effectively in the moment -they can be designed and evaluated in light of the richer and more difficult question of what it means to help human beings live well. Framing the chatbot as a source of comfort may prioritize immediate relief, whereas framing it as a coach or challenger may introduce effort or discomfort in service of longer-term learning, growth, or character development.

Framing can orient the interaction primarily toward satisfying the user's needs or toward considering responsibilities, relationships, and consequences for other people and communities.

Interaction pacing Rapid assistance vs. reflection/deliberation

Immediate answers can maximize efficiency and short-term relief, whereas guidance, reflective questions, and constructive friction can support autonomous reasoning, learning, and development over time.

Deliberative pacing may also create space for considering perspectives beyond one's own and the broader consequences of one's choices rather than responding only to one's immediate preference.

Personalization Preference matching vs. perspective broadening

Adapting to current preferences may increase immediate relevance and satisfaction, whereas personalization around growth may challenge existing preferences in support of longer-term development.

Highly individualized personalization may privilege user interests and reinforce existing views, whereas perspective-broadening personalization can incorporate other people, social norms, and collective consequences.

Anthropomorphization Connection vs. relational boundaries

Human-likeness may increase immediate trust, disclosure, and perceived support, but can contribute to longer-term dependence or overreliance.

AI companionship may benefit an individual user while potentially displacing human relationships.

Interaction component Example interaction-level tension Temporal tension: Immediate vs. longer-term Self-community tension: Individual vs. collective Social interaction

One-to-one support vs. community engagement Individual AI interaction may provide immediate convenience and support, whereas facilitating durable human relationships and social participation may yield benefits that emerge over longer periods.

Systems can optimize the experience of an individual user or can be designed to facilitate interaction toward shared goals and social cohesion.
