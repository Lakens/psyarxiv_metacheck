To fix Exact P-Values

What we found

Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (e.g., “decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

The APA manual states: Report exact p values (e.g., p = .031) to two or three decimal places. However, report p values less than .001 as p < .001. However, 2 decimals is too imprecise for many use-cases (e.g., a p value meta-analysis), so report p values with three digits.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 20 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

How Marginal Significance works

List all sentences that describe an effect as ‘marginally significant’.

The marginal module searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The list of terms is a subset of those listed in a blog post by Matthew Hankins. The module returns all sentences that match terms describing ‘marginally significant’ results.

Some of the terms identified might not be problematic in some contexts, and there are ways to describe ‘marginal significance’ that are not detected by the module.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 51 papers with 87 statements, this module correctly identified 38 statements (true positives) and incorrectly flagged 22 statements (false positives). It failed to detect 27 statements. Thus, among all statements flagged by the module, 63% were genuine cases (positive predictive value). However, the module missed 42% of all true statements (false negative rate). This validation was made with an earlier, looser pattern; the pattern now requires “significant” or “significance” to follow the trigger word closely (#695).

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. Full text

Abstract

Establishing shared meaning is widely considered the normative, almost ubiquitous trajectory of linguistic interaction, while moments of misalignment are typically viewed as temporary interruptions that individuals attempt to repair. However, it is not uncommon to experience communication breakdowns that actively escalate rather than resolve. This raises a fundamental question: does interaction inherently lead people toward shared meaning, or is conceptual alignment a reciprocal, socially contingent process? We conducted four experiments using a multiplayer word association task, where participants generated associations to cue words while interacting with either human or simulated partners varying in semantic typicality or alignment tendencies. Our results show that, in general, social interaction motivates individuals to produce more conventional associations to support alignment. However, this tendency depended on the partner's behavior: when interacting with an atypical or difficult-to-align-with partner, individuals actively reduced their own typicality and tendency to match the partner's associations, effectively sacrificing conceptual alignment. Such reciprocal misalignment persisted even when participants were incentivized to maximize alignment. Our findings suggest that conceptual misalignment may reflect a reciprocal, socially-motivated modulation of identity and differentiation, rather than solely a failure of coordination. This work sheds light on the interactional mechanisms underlying interpersonal distancing, communication breakdowns in neurodivergence, and potentially broader patterns of social polarization.

Introduction

Reciprocity can make or break human connection (Buunk & Schaufeli, 1999; Cosmides & Tooby, 1992; Gouldner, 1960; Perugini et al., 2003). Cooperative or generous behavior tends to elicit in-kind responses (Burger et al., 2009; Keysar et al., 2008), whereas selfish or aggressive behaviors often invite amplified reciprocal responses that may escalate conflict (Andersson & Pearson, 1999; Benard, 2015; Cardella et al., 2019; Keysar et al., 2008; Vandermeer et al., 2019). Despite this broad recognition that reciprocal behavior can not only bring people together but also set them apart, research on interaction in the linguistic domain typically views mutual understanding and convergence in linguistic expressions as the ubiquitous targets of interaction (Brennan & Clark, 1996; Clark & Wilkes-Gibbs, 1986; Fusaroli et al., 2014; Pickering & Garrod, 2006 , 2021; Stolk et al., 2016). However, while misunderstandings should, in theory, lead to repair (Albert & De Ruiter, 2018; Clark & Wilkes-Gibbs, 1986), it is not uncommon to experience misunderstandings that beget further misunderstanding. The current study aims to characterize reciprocity in conceptual misalignment by studying the process by which difficulty aligning with an interaction partner can lead to behavior that further undermines alignment.

Interactive alignment and repair in dialog.

Language provides a central domain in which coordination is systematically observed (Fusaroli et al., 2012; Garrod & Anderson, 1987; Pickering & Garrod, 2004; Rasenberg et al., 2020). This is because, rather than being an individual act, language use is fundamentally social and interactive (Beckner et al., 2009; Bohn & Frank, 2019; Clark & Murphy, 1982; Clark & Wilkes-Gibbs, 1986; Enfield, 2014; Rubio-Fernandez et al., 2025; Tanenhaus & Brown-Schmidt, 2008). In line with this view, dialog has been put forward as the central target of analysis in psycholinguistics (Pickering & Garrod, 2004 , 2021). Dialog research examines how interlocutors align linguistic behaviors across multiple levels, including phonology (Mitterer & Ernestus, 2006), lexical and referential expressions (Brennan & Clark, 1996; Brown-Schmidt, 2009; Hawkins et al., 2023) and syntax (Branigan et al., 2000; Weatherholtz et al., 2014; Schoot et al., 2019). Alignment across these different levels is often seen as the foundation for conceptual alignment, enabling interlocutors to build a similar representation of their immediate environment (Pickering & Garrod, 2013) as well as the world at large (Clark, 1996).

Crucially, in dialog research, alignment is implicitly treated as the normative, almost ubiquitous trajectory of interaction. Whether conceptualizing alignment as an automatic process based on priming (Pickering & Garrod, 2004), or an intentional process of building and monitoring common ground (Brennan & Clark, 1996) researchers primarily focus on the mechanisms underlying successful alignment rather than those undermining it. Furthermore, misalignment is typically interpreted as a temporary disruption in coordination that prompts adjustment or repair, rather than as an alternative stable mode of interaction (Clark & Wilkes-Gibbs, 1986; Pickering & Garrod, 2004).

Affiliative and identity motivations may modulate alignment.

Whereas the study of interactive alignment and common ground focuses on mechanisms of convergence, linguistic interaction need not always strive for convergence. Indeed, Communication Accommodation Theory (CAT) (Gallois et al., 2005; Giles et al., 1991; Giles & Ogay, 2007) conceptualizes linguistic adjustment as reflecting a strategic regulation of similarity and difference in response to identity concerns, power asymmetries, and relational goals. From this perspective, interlocutors can modify language use (e.g., emphasize an accent or a speech style; Doise et al., 1976) to assert distinctiveness, resist unwanted affiliation, or negotiate group boundaries.

These findings can be situated within a broader literature suggesting that coordination and convergence depend on affiliative and identity motivations. For example, individuals tend to abandon cultural tastes (e.g., certain clothes) when they are motivated to distance themselves from groups adopting these tastes (Berger & Heath, 2008). Similarly, individuals are less likely to mimic those they do not want to affiliate with or those they believe to hold different opinions from them (for a review see Chartrand & Lakin, 2013). Finally, Shared Reality theory posits that whereas people generally tend to align their worldviews with those of their conversation partners, this depends on the extent to which they are motivated to identify and connect with that partner (Figueroa-Grenett et al., 2025; Higgins, 1992; Higgins et al., 2021).

Together, these studies suggest that misalignment is not necessarily a failure of coordination but may rather constitute a socially motivated modulation of identity and affiliation that regulates interpersonal distinctiveness. Nonetheless, existing research falls short of characterizing how such social motivations contribute to the dynamic interactive alignment process by which interlocutors establish conceptual alignment. Indeed, while offering a comprehensive theory of motivated misalignment, CAT focuses on linguistic form, and particularly stylistic and paralinguistic features of speech (e.g., accent, speech rate, style; Gallois et al., 2005). Thus, a key open question is whether and when interactions may aim to produce or highlight misalignment at the conceptual level. Furthermore, conceptualizing alignment as an interactive process where interlocutors affect one another highlights a crucial, overlooked factor that may contribute to one's motivation to misalign -the extent to which their partner tries to align with them. Indeed, reciprocity may play a particularly crucial role in conceptual (mis)alignment, as indicated by the fact that the few studies that did examine reciprocity in linguistic alignment focused on linguistic form (i.e., syntax) and failed to find consistent results (Schoot et al., 2014 (Schoot et al., , 2019)). The current study aims to address these questions by characterizing reciprocal misalignment in conceptual representations.

The Current Study

Building on seminal models in semantic cognition, conceptual representations are defined here as the broad network of associations among concepts (Collins & Loftus, 1975; Günther et al., 2019; Kumar, 2021; Lambon Ralph et al., 2017; Reilly et al., 2025; Siew et al., 2019).

In dialog research, such conceptual representations usually define the baseline shared knowledge and beliefs between interlocutors (Clark, 1996), and are rarely studied because they are not assumed to change during interaction. Here, we focus on such long-term conceptual representations because recent evidence suggests that they can vary substantially across individuals, as an effect of personal experiences (Barsalou, 1999; Kidd et al., 2018; Wulff et al., 2022), learning history (Lupyan, 2012), and sociocultural context (Medin et al., 2002). For example, the concept 'risk' can evoke associations such as 'fun' for one person and 'danger' for another (Wulff & Mata, 2022), 'religion' can be tied with 'sacred' or 'fake' (De Deyne et al., 2019). Yet in other cases, individuals may come up with idiosyncratic associations that make their conceptual representations appear atypical and difficult to understand (e.g., the association 'because' to the concept 'knight'; Fradkin et al., 2025).

These discrepancies demonstrate why interacting with a partner with misaligned conceptual representations is expected to activate identity-related motivations that may, in turn, moderate one's motivation to align with that partner.

We examine this question using a multiplayer word association task in which individuals generate associations to shared cues and can then observe their partner's responses. Word associations are known to reflect semantic and conceptual representations (Barone et al., 2020; De Deyne et al., 2016; Fradkin & Eldar, 2023; Koll et al., 2010; Nelson et al., 2004; Schnabel & Asendorpf, 2013; Wulff & Mata, 2022). Furthermore, a recent study using a multiplayer word association task showed that individuals tend to produce more conventional associations in interaction versus alone, in support of mutual understanding (Tsang et al., 2024). Here we use a variant of the task where interlocutors were asked to synchronously report associations to the same cue (instead of responding to each other's associations as in Tsang et al., 2024). This task allows us to distinguish between individual-level contributions to alignment and alignment outcomes at the pair level. Alignment can be quantified as the extent to which partners produce identical or semantically similar associations. Conversely, individuals' role in shaping this process can be assessed by analyzing their responses, for instance focusing on response typicality as a clear strategy for promoting alignment (e.g., the association 'Apple' to the cue 'Orange' is more likely to promote alignment than the association 'Clockwork'; Fig. 1). This task also allows interlocutors themselves to easily track mutual alignment, making it well-suited for examining how individuals adjust their behavior when they see that their partner reports associations that undermine alignment.

Across four studies, we examine reciprocity in alignment-promoting behaviors. In Study 1, we examined reciprocity by testing whether individuals who play with typical partners also tend to report more typical associations. In Study 2, we aimed to establish causality by manipulating one participant in each dyad to report relatively atypical associations and examining the effects of this manipulation on their partner's typicality. In Study 3, we examined whether interacting with an atypical partner reduces alignment-promoting behaviors even when alignment is explicitly encouraged. Finally, in Study 4 we used an alternative measure of alignment-promoting behaviors and motivation by repeating cues and thereby allowing participants to align by repeating their partner's previous associations. Our hypothesis was twofold: while social interaction would naturally prompt individuals to report more typical associations to foster alignment, this behavior will depend on partner's behavior.

When interacting with a difficult-to-align-with partner, individuals would reduce alignmentpromoting behaviors and may even actively undermine alignment.

Study 1 Methods

Participants.

Participants were recruited via Prolific Academic, according to the following criteria: adults aged 18-65 years, with English as their first language, no reported language-related disorders, current residency in an English-speaking country, and a minimum approval rate of 95% from at least 10 previous studies and up to 10,000 submissions. Participants received £4.5 for their involvement in an approximately 30-minute experiment. Real-time pairing of participants was conducted using JATOS (Lange et al., 2015), based on the order of entry.

The final sample comprised 22 dyads, following the exclusion of 18 dyads in which one or both participants did not complete the task, or failed to follow instructions (e.g. wrote gibberish; Table S1). The substantial number of exclusions reflects the dyadic nature of the task, whereby if one participant dropped out or encountered technical issues, their partner could not proceed.

The final sample of 44 participants included 20 men (45.45%) and 20 women (45.45%), along with 2 participants who identified with other genders and 2 whose gender was unreported. The average age was 38.39 years (SD = 10.69). Most participants held a bachelor's degree or equivalent (N = 16, 36.36%), with some participants having only highschool education (N = 14, 31.81%) or a master's degree or higher (N = 10, 22.72%). All participants were native English speakers. Of these, 14 (31.81%) were multilingual, with English as their first language.

Materials and Procedure.

Participants engaged in a word association task in which each trial involved writing a single word association to a given word cue. The task comprised two phases. In the Alone phase, participants played by themselves. Subsequently, in the Interaction phase, participants were paired with a partner and could view each other's associations after submitting their own, along with an avatar representing their partner. To prevent participants' avatar choices from influencing their partners' impressions, participants were shown a randomly assigned avatar instead of the one their partner had selected. The Alone and Interaction phases comprised 84 and 109 trials, distributed across 2 and 3 blocks, respectively. After each block, participants rated their enjoyment of the task, whereas during the Interaction phase they also rated the extent to which they liked and felt in-sync with their partner.

Each trial began with a fixation cross displayed for 1000 ms, followed by a cue word (e.g., 'Orange') presented at the center of the screen. A previously validated procedure was employed to dissociate the time required to generate an association from the time required to type it (Fradkin & Eldar, 2022 , 2023). Specifically, participants were asked to press the spacebar as soon as an association came to mind and only then type in their association and press Enter. To discourage premature presses, participants were allotted a maximum of 15 seconds to press the spacebar (indicated by an on-screen stopwatch) but were given only 1500 ms to begin typing after pressing it. In the Interaction phase, after writing an association, participants could view their partner's association. To increase variability in perceived alignment (and encourage typicality matching), half of the dyads could not see each other's associations on 50% of the trials where they wrote the same association, whereas the other half could not see each other's associations on 50% of the trials where they wrote different associations.

Following both phases, participants reviewed the associations they and their partner wrote (without getting a reminder on who wrote what). For each association, they rated the extent to which the cue reminded them of the association (on a 0-100 visual analog scale), with an emphasis on subjective associative strength. Twenty cue-association pairs were repeated to test for reliability. An additional attention check required participants to choose a specific (e.g., leftmost) value when the cue 'Attention' was presented. Finally, participants completed several questionnaires, including an ad-hoc questionnaire (henceforth: Partner Evaluation Scale) measuring their judgments about their partners (e.g., "I felt like I had better associations than my partner", "I felt disconnected from my partner"; Table S2), and a demographic questionnaire.

Analysis.

Prior to analysis, we excluded trials that included gibberish, misspelled words, multiword responses, digits, as well as trials with no responses or those where participants copied the cue instead of writing an association. The mean proportion of excluded trials was 8% (SD = 4%). We subsequently lemmatized the valid associations to account for mild grammatical variations (e.g., to ensure 'Chair' and 'Chairs' are treated as equivalent).

We employed three measures of typicality. Normed typicality was calculated as the proportion of trials where participants produced normative associations, as indicated by previous norm studies (De Deyne et al., 2019; Nelson et al., 2004). Distributional typicality was calculated as the cosine similarity between the cue and the association using a pre-trained GloVe model (6B parameters, 50 dimensions; Pennington et al., 2014). To account for the differences in expected cosine similarity among cues (e.g., cues that are more frequent in language are characterized by greater similarity across associations), we also calculated a relative distributional typicality measure as the Z-score of cosine similarity with respect to the mean and standard deviation of the similarities between a cue and its normative associations.

Alignment was measured using three measures. Agreement was defined as the proportion of trials in which partners wrote the same association (either before or after lemmatization).

Distributional alignment was calculated as the cosine similarity between partners' associations, with same-association trials removed (since such trials would have resulted in a skewed (one-inflated) distribution of cosine similarity). We also used participants' ratings of their partners' associations as an additional measure of alignment.

All analyses were conducted using generalized mixed-effects models, with phase as the main fixed predictor, and random intercepts and slopes by subject within dyad, as well as by cue. To ensure convergence, models did not estimate correlations between random effects.

We used linear models for the distributional metrics, logistic models for normed typicality and agreement, and a Beta regression (with a log link) for ratings.

Results

Social interaction increases semantic typicality in support of alignment.

Following previous findings (Tsang et al., 2024), we hypothesized that interaction would increase the production of more typical associations and that this increase in typicality would facilitate alignment. Consistent with our hypothesis, participants produced more typical associations during Interaction than during the Alone phase. This effect emerged both for normed typicality, β phase = 0.10, 95% confidence interval = [0.03,0.18], Z = 2.58, p = .010, and for distributional typicality, β phase = 0.005, CI = [0.001, 0.01], Z = 2.46, p = .014 (Fig. 1b).

Interaction also increased alignment between partners. Relative to the Alone phase, participants more often agreed on associations, β phase = 0.24, CI = [0.05, 0.43], Z = 2.47, p = .013, produced associations that were more similar to one another, β phase = 0.01, CI = [7.43e-04, 0.02], Z = 2.09, p = .037, and received greater ratings from each other, β phase = 0.07, CI = [0.03, 0.12], Z = 3.14, p = .002 (although the latter was partially explained by agreement, as excluding trials where participants wrote the same association weakened this effect, β phase = 0.05, Z = 1.75, p = .078). Control analyses indicated that these effects could not be explained by the fixed order of the phases or the variation in cue sets across phases (Supplementary Results S1).

Typicality is reciprocal.

The results so far suggest that even minimal online interaction motivates individuals to increase semantic typicality, which effectively supports alignment. This increase in typicality may reflect either an automatic, ubiquitous effect of social presence or a context-sensitive response that depends on partner's behavior. To test this, we examined whether the typicality of a given participant is associated with their partner's typicality. Since cues differ in their expected level of typicality, we used a relative distributional typicality score here. This analysis revealed a strong correlation, suggesting that partners tend to converge on the degree of typicality. Crucially, this effect was specific to the Interaction condition (Fig. 1d), further supporting its interactional nature. This typicality matching effect may indicate reduced motivation to align with partners whose semantic representations are perceived as atypical. Alternatively, converging on lower typicality may reflect partner-specific alignment (e.g., a 'private language') or, indeed, higher-level alignment, where partners connect by implicitly 'agreeing' to generate unique or creative associations. To test these explanations, we calculated typicality matching (product of typicality Z-scores) and overall typicality (sum of typicality Z-scores) separately for each dyad and examined how these metrics correlate with actual and perceived alignment. Actual alignment, measured as agreement (to reduce metric-level shared variance), was strongly correlated with overall typicality (r(20) = .56, CI = [0.18, 0.79], p = .006), but not with typicality matching (r(20) = -.15, CI = [-0.54, 0.29], p = .499), suggesting that typicality matching is not accompanied by partner-specific alignment. To measure perceived alignment and connection, participants were asked to rate the extent to which they liked their partner and felt in sync with them, as well as the extent to which they enjoyed the task, all on a 1-5 scale. Discussion

The results of Study 1 replicate previous evidence that social interaction motivates individuals to produce more conventional semantic associations as a means to conceptual alignment (Tsang et al., 2024). Crucially, however, we found that this tendency is not ubiquitous, depending instead on the partner's typicality: individuals playing with relatively atypical partners also tended to produce less typical associations, effectively undermining alignment and its interpersonal benefits. However, this typicality matching effect relies on correlational evidence and a relatively small number of dyads (rendering dyad-level correlations inconclusive). Study 2 aimed to examine causal evidence for typicality matching by manipulating one participant in each dyad to produce less typical associations and measuring the effects of this manipulation on their partner's behavior.

Study 2 Methods

Participants.

Participants were recruited similarly to Study 1, using the same eligibility and exclusion criteria. The final sample consisted of 24 dyads, after excluding 18 dyads (see Table S1). The final sample consisted of 19 (39.58%) men and 28 (58.33%) women, with one unreported gender participant. Participants were 40.30 years old on average (SD = 10.51). The majority of participants held a bachelor's degree (20, 41.67%), or equivalent degrees (5, 10.42%), whereas a minority had attained only high-school education (10, 20.83%), or a master's degree or higher (12, 25.00%). All participants were native English speakers. Of these, nine (18.75%) were multilingual, with English as their first language.

Materials and Procedure.

The task was similar to Study 1, with the exception that we aimed to reduce associative constraint of one participant in each dyad by introducing a response signal procedure. This previously validated procedure involved asking participants to rapidly type the first word coming to mind as fast as possible on approximately 50% of the trials, instead of waiting for them to press the spacebar when they are ready. Response signal trials were visually salient, with the text input box highlighted in red (Fig. 2a).

The time limit for starting to type the association on response signal trials was 3 seconds. The timing of the response signal was determined using an adaptive algorithm, which ensured that response signals were presented (before the participant pressed the space bar) on approximately 50% of the trials. Notably, the participant in each dyad who did not undergo this manipulation was also unaware of the manipulation being applied to their partner.

Statistical Analysis.

Following preprocessing procedures used in Study 1, an average of 8.20% of trials were excluded per participant (SD = 4.3%). Here, we additionally excluded response signal trials where manipulated participants failed to initiate typing within the specified time limit, as well as trials allocated to the response signal condition, where participants pressed the space bar before it was presented. This led to the exclusion of an additional 16% of the trials (SD = 8%) in participants undergoing the response signal manipulation. Statistical analyses mirrored those of Study 1, with the exception of including response signal as an additional predictor.

Both categorical predictors were contrast-coded.

Results

We first examined whether the response-signal manipulation successfully reduced typicality during the Interaction phase. Here, we expected manipulated participants to produce less typical associations than their partners (who did not undergo the manipulation and were not aware of it), particularly on response signal trials. As expected, manipulated participants showed lower typicality (distributional typicality: β group = -0.01, CI = [-2.54e-02, -1.07e-04], Z = -1.97, p = .049; relative distributional typicality: β group = -0.08, CI = [-0.16, -0.01], Z = -2.21, p = .027; normative typicality: β group = -0.25, CI =[-0.48, -0.03], Z = -2.24, p = .025). In contrast to our predictions, manipulated participants exhibited similar typicality on trials with and without response signals (p's > .25), suggesting that the expectation for a response signal may have reduced constraint across all trials (Fig. 2b).

The typicality matching hypothesis suggests that participants interacting with partners who report less typical associations than they do should reduce rather than increase the typicality of their associations in interaction (versus alone). This hypothesis was supported in distributional typicality measures (distributional typicality: β phase = -0.01, CI =[-0.01, -0.001], Z = -2.32, p = .020; relative distributional typicality: β phase = -0.05, CI =[-0.08, -0.02], Z = -3.02, p = .002), but did not reach significance in normed typicality (β phase = -0.05, CI =[-0.14, 0.03], Z = -1.30, p = .194, see Fig. 2b).

Discussion

Study 2 provides further support for the idea that individuals interacting with an atypical partner may actually reduce their own typicality, effectively sacrificing alignment. While supporting the interactional causality of this effect, Study 2 did not directly compare individuals playing with an atypical partner to those playing with typical partners.

Furthermore, neither Study 1 nor Study 2 provided participants with explicit instructions concerning alignment. Thus, the difference between the two studies may reflect a tendency to infer the interaction goals from the partner's behavior, rather than the context-sensitive nature of conceptual alignment. To address these limitations, we conducted a pre-registered study where participants were explicitly instructed to maximize alignment, and then assigned to play with either a typical or an atypical partner. In Study 2 we used a modified wordassociation task where response signals (RS) were introduced to reduce the typicality of one participant (denoted by 'Player 1') in each dyad so as to examine its effects on the other participant ('Player 2') (a). The results suggest that reducing typicality in one participant indeed leads to a reduction in the typicality of their partner's associations (b). In Study 3 participants played with partners reproducing the behavior of either 'typical' or 'atypical' participants from Studies 1-2, where we hypothesized that participants interacting with atypical partners will produce less typical associations despite being encouraged to converge (thumbs-up sign) (c). We found a dampened increase in typicality in Interaction vs. Alone for the atypical partner condition ('Low') compared to the typical partner condition ('High') in Study 3 (d). A simulation indicated that this mitigated increase in typicality undermined a potential increase in alignment (e).

Study 3 Methods

Participants.

Participants were recruited similarly to Study 1, using similar eligibility criteria. Following our pre-registration, we recruited 30 participants per group, after removing 4 participants based on pre-registered exclusion criteria (see Table S1). This sample size was determined based on a bootstrapped power analysis for mixed-effects models, assuming a medium effect (Cohen's d = 0.6) detected in a small pilot.

The final sample consisted of 19 men (31.67%) and 37 women (61.67%), along with 2 participants who identified with other genders and 2 whose gender was unreported.

Participants were 39.79 years old on average (SD = 12.52). Most participants held a bachelor's (29, 48.33%) or equivalent degrees (12, 20%), while a minority had attained only high-school education (13, 21.67%) or a master's degree (4, 6.67%). All participants were native English speakers, with a small minority being multilingual (12 participants, 20%).

Materials and Procedure.

Participants took part in a word association task structured similarly to previous studies, with no response signal manipulation. Here, participants were instructed to maximize alignment, wherein trials in the Interaction phase where participants wrote the same association as their partner were followed by positive feedback in the form of a thumbs-up animation (Fig. 2c).

Crucially, instead of using live interaction for the Interaction phase, we re-used associations and response times of previous participants from Studies 1 and 2. Specifically, participants were randomly allocated to play with either a typical or an atypical partner. Here, a typical partner was sampled from the ten most typical participants from Study 1, while an atypical partner was sampled from the ten most atypical, manipulated participants from Study 2 (Fig. 2c). Each pre-recorded partner was used for 2-4 participants. While re-using previous responses, we reshuffled trial order, using random seeds that minimized expected differences in typicality between cues allocated to the Alone vs. Interaction phases.

The word association task was followed by two tasks designed to indirectly measure participants' perception of partner-specific alignment. First, participants reviewed their own association while attempting to estimate, for each association, the degree to which their partner would find it related to the cue. Next, participants were asked to choose between three hypothetical performance bonus options: a) one based on the closeness of their answers to their partner's associations, b) one based on the typicality of their associations (i.e., how many other participants gave the same associations), and c) one based on their partner's actual rating of the strength of their associations.

Statistical Analysis.

Preprocessing was similar to Study 1. Here, trial-level, preregistered exclusion criteria led to the exclusion of an average of 6.95% trials per participant (SD = 4.26%). The key hypothesis that playing with an atypical partner would reduce the extent to which people increase typicality in interaction was tested using mixed-effects models, with phase (Alone vs. Interaction) and condition (Typical vs. Atypical partner) as (contrast-coded) predictors of typicality. The hypothetical bonus preference question was analyzed using a Chi-squared test for independence.

Results

Selective Alignment under Explicit Alignment Goals.

First, we examined whether playing with atypical partners indeed reduced alignment. As expected, this manipulation worked (β condition = -0.21, CI =[-0.32, -0.10], Z = -3.91, p < .001).

Conversely, the similarity between associations in non-agreement trials (i.e., different associations) was not reduced by the manipulation (β condition = 0.001, CI = [-5.39e-03, 8.26e-03], Z = 0.41, p = .680), suggesting that agreement is a stronger indicator of alignment in this task.

Following a pre-registered analysis plan (https://aspredicted.org/44dm93.pdf), we examined whether participants interacting with an atypical partner showed a smaller increase (or even a decrease) in typicality. As hypothesized, whereas encouraging participants to converge with their partner led to greater typicality in interaction versus alone (distributional typicality: β phase = 0.01, CI =[0.006, 0.02], Z = 4.28, p < .001; relative distributional typicality: β phase = 0.06, CI =[0.03, 0.09], Z = 4.08, p < .001; normed typicality: β phase = 0.13, CI =[0.04, 0.21], Z = 3.05, p = .002), this increase was weaker among participants playing with an atypical partner in two out of three pre-registered measures (distributional typicality: β phaseXcondition = -0.004, CI =[-0.01, -3.32e-04], Z = -2.1, p = .035; relative distributional typicality: β phaseXcondition = -0.03, CI =[-0.06, -0.01], Z = -2.42, p = .015; normed typicality: β phaseXcondition = -0.06, CI =[-0.14, 0.03], Z = -1.37, p = .178). Simple effects analysis further indicated no significant increase in typicality among participants playing with an atypical partner (distributional typicality: β phase|atypical = 0.006, CI =[-5.83e-04, 0.01], Z = 1.79, p = .074; relative distributional typicality: β phase|atypical = 0.027, CI =[-0.01, 0.06], Z = 1.36, p = .173; normed typicality: β phase|atypical = 0.07, CI =[-0.04, 0.19], Z = 1.20, p = .230, see Fig. 2d).

These results suggest that interacting with an atypical partner reduces the tendency to increase typicality as a strategy to increase alignment, even when alignment is the explicit goal of the interaction. Yet, we did not find evidence for a reduction in typicality.

Accordingly, whereas we reported lower agreement with atypical partners (see above), this was not moderated by phase (β phaseXcondition = -0.04, CI =[-0.12, 0.03], Z = -1.12, p = .262), in contrast to our prediction. Therefore, we employed an exploratory simulation analysis to examine how the reduced tendency to increase typicality in the atypical-partner condition reduced alignment. This analysis suggested that increasing typicality further (particularly, to the average level of typicality of participants in the typical-partner condition) could have increased agreement in the atypical-partner condition, albeit to a considerably lower level than in the typical-partner condition (Fig. 2e). Thus, although increasing typicality is not as effective in increasing agreement with an atypical partner, the smaller increase in typicality observed in this group is detrimental to alignment, as defined by the task objective.

Reduced typicality does not indicate partner-specific alignment.

A dampened increase in typicality in the atypical-partner group (Fig. 2d) is consistent with the reciprocal misalignment hypothesis. One alternative explanation, also considered in Study 1, is that playing with an atypical partner may lead participants to attempt aligning with atypical partners by reducing typicality. To examine this possibility, we asked participants to estimate how their associations would be rated by their partner, and to indicate whether, were they to receive a bonus for performance, they would prefer the bonus to be linked to their partner's ratings of their associations. If participants in the atypical-partner group indeed emphasize partner-specific alignment at the expense of typicality, we expect them to provide higher estimates of partners' ratings (after controlling for typicality) and prefer partners' ratings as a performance criterion. This analysis did not show evidence for greater inferred partner's ratings in the atypical-partner group (β phaseXcondition = 0.001, CI = [-4.09e-02, 3.72e-02], Z = 0.09, p = .927) and in fact showed a non-significant trend towards a smaller preference to use partner's ratings as a performance criterion in this group (χ 2 (2) = 1.90, p = .380; Fig. S1). Moreover, participants playing with an atypical partner reported feeling less in-sync with their partner (95% Bayesian credible interval: [-1.08, -0.12]), suggesting that they experienced misalignment. Notably, however, we did not find evidence that atypical partners were less likable (CI = [-0.93, 2.31]), or that playing with an atypical partner led to a greater decrease in enjoyment (in comparison to playing alone; CI = [-1.53, 0.04]).

Exploratory cross-study analysis: Reciprocal misalignment may reflect an effort to reduce alignment.

Across three studies, we found that participants interacting with misaligned partners further contributed to misalignment. It remains unclear, however, whether this reflects only a reduced effort to adapt one's spontaneous behavior to promote alignment, or rather an active effort to reduce alignment. To examine this question, we conducted an exploratory analysis integrating the results of Studies 1-3, using reaction times (RT) as a proxy for effort. This analysis indicated that social interaction increased (log-transformed) RT regardless of the partner's behavior. Particularly, whereas participants in Study 1 became more typical in interaction and those who interacted with manipulated partners in Study 2 became less typical, both groups were slower in the Interaction phase (vs. alone; β phase = 0.11, CI = [0.08, 0.14], Z = 7.54, p < .001; β phaseXstudy = 0.01, CI = [-0.02, 0.04], Z = 0.75, p = .454). Similarly, participants in Study 3 were slower in interaction regardless of whether they played with typical or atypical partners (β phase = 0.09, CI = [0.05, 0.12], Z = 5.15, p < .001; β phaseXcondition = -0.01, CI = [-0.04, 0.02], Z = -0.65, p = .573). These findings suggest that reciprocal misalignment may reflect an effort to reduce alignment rather than disinhibition of spontaneous associations.

Discussion

These results further support the reciprocity of conceptual alignment, manifesting even when alignment is encouraged. Hitherto, evidence relied primarily on typicality matching, where producing atypical associations when interacting with an atypical partner effectively reduces alignment. These typicality reductions were not compensated for by actual or experienced partner-specific alignment. Yet, it remains possible that participants playing with atypical partners erroneously believed that matching their typicality to their partner's would increase alignment (reminiscent of the 'probability matching' bias; Newell & Schulze, 2016).

Study 4 was designed to test whether reciprocal misalignment indeed reflects a reduced motivation to align rather than a failed alignment strategy, while testing generalization to an additional type of alignment-promoting behavior. For this purpose, we used a word association task where each cue appears twice, enabling alignment promotion simply through repeating one's partner's previous associations (where partners consisted of artificial agents who were prone to repeat themselves). This tests reciprocal misalignment more directly, since participants are led to assume that partners can also repeat the participant's previous associations: by failing to 'repair' their alignment-undermining behaviors, atypical partners signal deliberate misalignment.

Study 4 Methods

Participants.

Participants were recruited similarly to Study 1, using similar eligibility criteria. Following our pre-registration (https://aspredicted.org/ni352y.pdf), we aimed to recruit up to 50 participants, such that approximately 30 of them would indicate they did not suspect to be playing with a bot, thereby ensuring a power of 80%, based on pilot results. The initial sample consisted of 60 participants, from which 10 participants were excluded based on pre-registered exclusion criteria (as detailed in Table S1).

The final sample consisted of 23 men (46%) and 27 women (54%), with an average age of 38.86 (SD = 10.63). Most participants held a bachelor's degree (n = 24, 48%) or an equivalent degree (n = 6, 12%), while a minority had attained only high-school education (n = 12, 24%), or a master's degree or higher (n = 7, 16%). All participants were native English speakers, with a small minority of multilinguals (n = 11, 22%).

Materials and Procedure.

We employed a modified word association task, wherein the generation of associations was decoupled from the selection of associations for sharing (Fig. 3a). Specifically, on each trial, participants were instructed to write two distinct associations (without initially pressing the spacebar). Following this, participants were asked to choose which of the two associations they preferred to share with their partner. Each cue was presented twice, in a random order. During the second presentation of the cue, participants were reminded of and could thereby select the association their partner had written during the first presentation of the cue. Participants had 15 seconds to generate and select associations for each cue.

Participants played the task with two partners, one after the other (in random order), both controlled by pre-programmed agents. The agents were designed to appear as human-like as possible by introducing occasional spelling mistakes (~8%) and using realistic rection times, for instance, faster reaction times for repeated cues. These partners consisted of a typical partner who shared typical associations on approximately 85% of the trials and an atypical partner with 30% typical associations, 45% loose associations, and 25% completely unrelated associations. Whereas typical associations consisted of those given by at least two people in previous norms, loose associations included those given by a single person in the same dataset (De Deyne et al., 2019). In turn, completely unrelated associations were those given by a single person (in the same dataset) in response to a different cue. This combination was designed to balance atypicality with human-like performance, based on pilot data suggesting that including only unrelated associations increased participants' suspicion that they were playing with a bot. The experiment began with a short training session (5 trials) without partners, where participants were simply asked to select the association they preferred.

Following the training session, participants produced 120 associations (30 cues, each presented twice, 2 associations per cue) with each agent.

Following an interaction with each partner, participants were asked to rate the extent to which they liked, felt in-sync with, and enjoyed playing with this partner, all on a 1-5 scale.

Participants also completed a shortened partner evaluation scale (where shortening was based on an exploratory factor analysis, see 'Social consequences of misalignment' below). After playing with both partners, participants were asked to provide a comparative response for each question, rating the extent to which they preferred one partner over the other, on a 1-8 scale.

The word association task was followed by a subjective associative strength rating task, in which participants were asked to rate the extent to which the cues reminded them of their partner's associations. This task was used to control for subjective associative strength in the examination of the effects of condition on the probability of repeating the partner's associations (see Fig. 3b, c). Following this, participants were asked to indicate the extent to which they believed at least one of their partners was actually a bot, followed by a demographic questionnaire.

Statistical Analysis.

Based on our preregistered plan, we employed logistic mixed-effects models to investigate the impact of condition (typical versus atypical partner) on the probability of selecting and/or generating a partner's previous association. All analyses focused exclusively on repeated cues. Notably, the primary analysis examining the probability of generating a partner's previous associations was employed after removing trials where participants repeated their own previous associations. In all models, we also explored the interaction between condition and the participant-rated subjective associative strength of the partner's respective previous association. If this interaction proved significant, we planned to conduct a simple effect analysis for highly-rated associations (specifically fixing ratings to the average rating of partners' typical associations), to ensure that reduced repetition of atypical partner's associations does not reflect only the fact that these associations were atypical. Condition was contrast-coded, and ratings were standardized prior to analysis. All models included random intercepts and random slopes for condition and rating for subject and cue.

Results

Reciprocal misalignment beyond typicality matching.

Our primary pre-registered hypothesis was that playing with an atypical partner would reduce participants' tendency to select the partner's previous associations. As expected, we found that participants were less likely to select associations previously shared by atypical partners (β condition = -0.37, CI = [-0.55, -0.20], Z = -4.04, p < .001), regardless of the subjective associative strength of these associations (β conditionXratings = 0.11, CI = [-0.04, 0.27], Z = 1.43, p = .153, Fig. 3b). This latter result suggests that reduced repetition reflects a reduced motivation to align with atypical partners, rather than a specific reduction in copying atypical associations.

Whereas choosing not to select a partner's previous association involves sending a social signal (since partners are assumed to see one's shared associations), we also set out to examine whether interacting with an atypical partner made participants less likely to generate (but not necessarily share) associations previously reported by their partner (but not by themselves). As hypothesized, we found that participants were less likely to generate associations previously shared by an atypical partner (β condition = -0.35, CI = [-0.50, -0.20], Z = -4.69, p < .001), regardless of the subjective associative strength of these associations (β conditionXratings = 0.01, CI = [-0.14, 0.17], Z = 0.18, p = .855, Fig. 3c). Whereas we used a variety of measures to make artificial partners appear human-like (see Methods), a significant proportion of participants indicated that they were either certain (10%) or almost certain (44%) that at least one partner was a bot. Crucially, however, a preregistered sensitivity analysis removing participants who suspected playing with a bot yielded similar results (see Supplementary Results S2).

Cross-study analysis: Social consequences of misalignment.

Finally, whereas our main aim was examining how interacting with misaligned partners affects alignment-promoting behaviors, this setting also enabled examining the social consequences of misalignment. Particularly, in addition to the block-level questions reported above, participants in all four studies completed the Partner Evaluation Scale, a custom questionnaire probing different aspects of affiliation, alignment, and specific perceptions concerning the partner (Table S2). We used the data of the first three studies (total N = 147) to shorten the initial pool of items (22 items) and examine the questionnaire's factor structure. Using established item removal methods reduced the questionnaire to 7 items, wherein exploratory factor analysis detected a single factor with good inter-item reliability (α cronbach = 0.78), with higher scores indicating more negative evaluation.

In these studies we found that partners who produced less typical responses, and those with whom the rater converged less received higher (negative evaluation) scores on the PES (normative typicality: r(145) = -.28, CI = [-0.42, -0.12], p corrected = .002; distributional typicality: r(145) = -.18, CI = [-0.33, -0.01], p corrected = .034; agreement: r(145) = -.22, CI =[-0.37, -0.06], p corrected = .017). Study 4 provided further, confirmatory, evidence that misaligned partners are evaluated more negatively (t (49) = -9.38, p < .001, Cohen's d = -1.33, CI = [-1.7, -1.07]). These results are consistent with the notion that reciprocal misalignment may reflect a motivation to distance oneself from a partner who is perceived unfavorably.

General Discussion

The central question motivating the current study was whether interacting individuals always strive to establish mutual understanding, or whether conceptual alignment is a reciprocal, socially contingent process. The findings support the latter. Whereas interaction often increased convergence, consistent with dominant accounts that treat alignment as a normative trajectory of dialog (Brennan & Clark, 1996; Clark, 1996; Pickering & Garrod, 2004 , 2006 , 2013; Rasenberg et al., 2020; Tsang et al., 2024), this tendency proved highly context-sensitive. When the partner's responses were atypical or difficult to align with, participants reduced their own alignment-promoting behavior, and in fact, selected responses that further undermined alignment.

Reciprocal misalignment was primarily supported by participants' tendency to report less typical associations when interacting with atypical partners. One alternative explanation we entertained is that such (a)typicality matching reflects an attempt to align with partner's atypical conceptual representations rather than reciprocal misalignment. Our findings do not support this interpretation. Reduced typicality was not accompanied by increased partnerspecific alignment, and participants were less likely to promote alignment with misaligned partners even when given a simple strategy that does not depend on typicality (Study 4).

Instead, the results are more consistent with a reduction in the motivation to align with misaligned or atypical partners, who were also evaluated more negatively.

This reduction in motivation provides a useful entry point for situating the findings within Communication Accommodation Theory (CAT). While most accounts of linguistic alignment treat convergence as a default outcome of dialog, CAT suggests that speakers strategically adjust language to meet social goals (Gallois et al., 2005; Giles et al., 1991). Thus, when the goal is to amplify social distance or assert a distinct identity, speakers may actively pursue linguistic divergence. Whereas classic CAT focused primarily on linguistic style (e.g., accent; Bourhis et al., 1975; Giles, 1973), the present findings extend this idea to alignment of conceptual representations.

More broadly, whereas most dialog research focused on alignment at the level of linguistic form, reference, or the conceptual representation of a specific task, the current study examined the dynamics of alignment in the conceptual representations that reflect individuals' long-term knowledge, worldviews, and beliefs. From this perspective, reciprocal misalignment may reflect an active form of differentiation, enabling individuals to distance themselves from partners with whom establishing common ground is undesirable because it may incur social or identity costs. Indeed, since aligning with atypical partners requires some sort of re-configuration of conceptual representations, doing so may inadvertently impair one's ability to remain aligned with their primary ingroup.

It is important to note that the current study primarily focused on alignment as an outcome at the dyad level, rather than the interactive process by which individuals may adapt their representations to one another (Brennan & Hanna, 2009; Brown-Schmidt, 2009; Hawkins et al., 2023; Pickering & Garrod, 2004). From this perspective, it could be argued that reciprocal misalignment may solely reflect a tendency to exhibit misalignment when interacting with a misaligned partner. In contrast to this interpretation, in Study 4 we found that participants were less likely to learn (i.e., interactively align to) the associations of misaligned partners. Still, understanding the dynamics of interactive conceptual alignment requires examining generalization patterns that go beyond simple repetition (Ji et al., 2025; Poncelet-Romaneli et al., 2025), highlighting the need for additional studies examining reciprocity in interactive conceptual alignment.

While reciprocal misalignment is primarily interpreted here as reflecting sociallymodulated motivation, an alternative explanation is that reciprocal misalignment reflects a strategic withdrawal under low expected return, as aligning with atypical partners may require greater resources while yielding less likely benefits (Lieder & Griffiths, 2020).

Consistent with this interpretation, Study 3 suggested that similar alignment-promoting behavior would have yielded significantly reduced returns when interacting with an atypical partner. On the other hand, we found no evidence that participants invest less resources when interacting (and misaligning) with atypical partners, suggesting that reciprocal misalignment at least partially reflects active distancing.

While the current study relied primarily on experimentally manipulated misalignment, unpacking the dynamics of reciprocal misalignment is crucial for understanding real-world contexts where conceptual representations systematically diverge. For instance, psychiatry research links schizophrenia to disruptions in communication and semantic cognition (Doughty et al., 2008; Doughty & Done, 2009; He et al., 2024; Kuperberg, 2010; Maher et al., 2005; Neill et al., 2014; Sharpe et al., 2026), and to social difficulties (Green et al., 2015; Newman, 2001; Okruszek et al., 2015). These accounts typically locate the source of difficulty at the level of the neurodivergent individual (but see Olarewaju et al., 2023; Schilbach et al., 2013). The present findings suggest that communicative breakdown may arise not only because one neurodivergent individual produces atypical or incoherent responses, but also because the neurotypical partner may reduce coordination in response.

Thus, whereas recent years have seen a surge in the use of natural language processing and large language models to analyze the language of individuals with schizophrenia (Alonso-Sánchez et al., 2022; Bedi et al., 2015; Fradkin et al., 2023; Palaniyappan et al., 2019), our findings suggest that analyzing the language of their interaction partners may be equally informative, highlighting the importance of studying the neurodivergent-neurotypical dyad (Dumas, 2022; Suter et al., 2025; Wadge et al., 2019). This might be particularly relevant for therapeutic contexts, where therapists' language is rarely analyzed.

Finally, these processes may scale to broader social contexts. Particularly, the current study is consistent with recent findings showing that exposure to opposing views can increase divergence rather than reduce it. For example, Bail and colleagues found that individuals exposed to opposing political perspectives on social media became more polarized (Bail et al., 2018). At a more structural level, similar dynamics have been proposed in accounts of polarization as an emergent property of interaction (Törnberg, 2022). The current study offers a specific mechanism by which such dynamics may occur, as well as a novel experimental paradigm to measure them.

Constraints on Generality

Considering extension to polarization research highlights an important limitation of the current study -the manipulation of misaligned partners relied primarily on the production of atypical associations. In many social contexts, however, misalignment is caused not by idiosyncratic conceptual representations, but rather by representations reflecting different, yet coherent, associations (e.g. 'religion' can be tied with 'sacred' or 'fake'; De Deyne et al., 2019). Future studies could extend the approach developed in the current study to generate artificial agents with coherent conceptual spaces that are tailored to differ from specific participants. Another limitation concerns ecological validity. While our word association task is designed to examine alignment at the conceptual level, it falls short of capturing alignment as it occurs in natural conversations. Relatedly, while studying avatar-mediated online interaction allowed us to focus on the social effects of semantic alignment, alignment motivations could differ in multimodal, face-to-face interaction, where the social costs of misalignment are arguably more salient. Finally, the study examined English-speaking participants from Prolific, and should be replicated in other languages and contexts.

Conclusion

Overall, our findings indicate that conceptual alignment operates as a reciprocal and socially contingent process, similarly to other types of social exchange (Andersson & Pearson, 1999; Benard, 2015; Cardella et al., 2019; Cropanzano et al., 2017; Cropanzano & Mitchell, 2005; Keysar et al., 2008; Vandermeer et al., 2019). Thus, when coordination breaks down, participants reduce their engagement in alignment-promoting behavior and even engage in behaviors that reduce alignment. This suggests that conceptual alignment is driven by social motivations. Accordingly, we found consistent evidence that conceptual misalignment leads to negative social evaluation, complementing previous debates concerning the social effects of syntactic alignment (Abrahams et al., 2019; Lelonkiewicz et al., 2021; Schoot et al., 2019). Although the societal implications remain open, this account connects moment-to-moment interactional dynamics with the emergence of wider patterns of social differentiation, distancing and polarization.
