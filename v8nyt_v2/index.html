To fix Exact P-Values

What we found

Show details

Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (e.g., “decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

P-Value Text
p’s > 0.05 No significant asymmetry was found for any of the mental health outcomes at posttreatment or at follow-up using Egger’s test as shown in Table S3 (p’s > 0.05; Egger et al., 1997).

The APA manual states: Report exact p values (e.g., p = .031) to two or three decimal places. However, report p values less than .001 as p < .001. However, 2 decimals is too imprecise for many use-cases (e.g., a p value meta-analysis), so report p values with three digits.

To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. Abstract

Background Access to mental health care is limited by financial, geographical, and resource constraints and sociodemographic alignments. Massive open online interventions (MOOIs), a form of online self-directed interventions, can address these barriers by providing cost-effective, evidence-based mental health support to large populations.

This meta-analysis and systematic review aimed to assess the effectiveness of MOOIs in reducing anxiety and depressive symptoms across broad populations.

Articles examining the effectiveness of mental health MOOIs were searched within PsycINFO, PsycARTICLES, and Academic Search Complete. Meta-analyses were conducted using randomeffects models. Person-and program-level moderators were examined with mixed-effect or meta-regression models.

203 articles (58,444 participants; M age =35.99 years) were included. MOOIs were more effective at reducing anxiety and depressive symptoms versus controls, with small effect sizes observed at post-treatment and follow-up (Range=1-54 weeks). Subgroup analyses indicated larger posttreatment effects on anxiety and depression when studies compared MOOIs to inactive controls (vs. active), required a mental health cutoff for inclusion (vs. no cutoff), and were conducted in Asia (vs. other continents). Meta-regression analyses revealed that younger mean sample age and shorter follow-up were associated with larger anxiety effects at post-treatment and follow-up, respectively. Regarding treatment components, gamification and rewards were associated with larger reductions in anxiety, and MOOIs without social connection, feedback, and suggestions reported larger reductions in depression. MOOIs without stories and examples showed larger reductions for anxiety and depression.

Further research should investigate how MOOIs can be tailored to address the needs of various sociodemographic characteristics and mental health symptoms.

Online Self-Directed Mental Health Interventions for Anxiety and Depression: A Meta-Analysis and Systematic Review

The high prevalence of unmet mental health needs globally stems, in part, from difficulty in accessing mental health care (World Health Organization, 2022). Common barriers to access include limited availability of providers and services, long waitlists, and gaps between existing service models and patients' needs (Murray & Knudson, 2023; Tran, 2024). Wait times are a significant barrier for those who require timely care, with times ranging from 6 weeks to 1 year in Canada and the United States, during which untreated symptoms may worsen (Moir & Esmail, 2025; Moroz et al., 2020; Sun et al., 2023). Other patient-level barriers include financial constraints, geographical considerations and proximity to services, and self-stigma related to help-seeking (Hicks et al., 2022; Lannin et al., 2016). Additionally, racism and discrimination within healthcare systems can discourage Indigenous and ethnic minority individuals from seeking out mental health support (Public Health Agency of Canada, 2019).

With rapid improvements in technology and increasing access to smartphones, tablets, and computers, electronic health (eHealth) interventions can offer access to private and remote mental health services for those impacted by institutionalized racism and other barriers to seeking care (Friis-Healy et al., 2021; Karyotaki et al., 2017; Wike et al., 2022; World Health Organization, 2022). Service providers have increasingly adapted to the changing technology landscape by using eHealth approaches to deliver mental health care for depression and anxiety, the most common mental disorders throughout the globe (Bouza et al., 2023; Byrne et al., 2021; Sultana & Pagán, 2023; World Health Organization, 2025a). eHealth programs can provide remote services through technology such as videoconferencing platforms, websites, and mobile applications, helping to reduce barriers related to waitlists, geographical constraints, limited resources, and provider availability (Lal & Adair, 2014). These approaches may also increase healthcare equity as they provide services at reduced or no costs, which may improve access for underserved populations, including racialized, ethnic, and sexual minority individuals and those living in rural and remote communities (LeBlanc et al., 2020; Schueller et al., 2019). However, this may also raise the concern of connectivity issues to for rural and remote communities, as limited infrastructure can contribute to poor internet and cellular data services (LeBlanc et al., 2020; Nelson et al., 2023). Since some eHealth services allow users to access support with greater anonymity, they may also help reduce stigma and shame associated with seeking out mental health services, which is a prevalent concern in Asian and Eastern European regions (Habeb et al., 2025; Lauber & Rössler, 2007; Nicholas et al., 2017). Despite the expansion of online interventions for depression and anxiety, the demand for care continues to exceed the capacity of available mental health workers (Haleem et al., 2021; Wiederhold, 2023). As a result, online self-directed mental health interventions have emerged as a promising approach to expanding access to scalable mental health support for anxiety and depression (Phillipi & Thomas, 2022).

Massive open online interventions (MOOIs) are a form of online self-directed interventions that are developed and informed by clinicians and can be used to provide costeffective mental health care without direct clinician involvement for large groups (Muñoz et al., 2016). They typically consist of modules that users can access independently and privately via mobile phone or computer, and provide therapeutic content such as psychoeducation, exercises, and/or homework (Saad et al., 2021). As of 2025, approximately 74% of people across the globe use the internet, and approximately 82% own a mobile phone, suggesting MOOIs could reach a large group of individuals with mental health symptoms (International Telecommunication Union, 2025). MOOIs may provide a more accessible, evidence-informed intervention to individuals who may lack the time or resources to access traditional in-person mental health care (Muñoz et al., 2016). MOOIs can also reach populations without a personal device, as they can be accessed via public (e.g., shelter, library) or loaned devices (DeLaCruz-Jiron et al., 2023; Polillo et al., 2021). There has been a call to action by Muñoz and colleagues for more research on the development and impact of MOOIs to ensure future research creates engaging and effective MOOIs that provide accessible mental health care for the general population (Muñoz et al., 2016).

Prior systematic reviews and meta-analyses on MOOIs found mixed results on their effectiveness in reducing anxiety and depressive symptoms, with some finding significant smallto-moderate effects, and others finding no significant effects (Babbage et al., 2022; Karyotaki et al., 2017; Klimczak et al., 2023; Saad et al., 2021; Thomson et al., 2025; Tong et al., 2024). This variability in effectiveness may be due to previous reviews being narrow in scope, focusing only on specific populations (e.g., adults, adolescents) or limited to therapeutic orientations (e.g., cognitive behavioural therapy [CBT], acceptance and commitment therapy [ACT]; Babbage et al., 2022; Karyotaki et al., 2017; Klimczak et al., 2023; Saad et al., 2021; Thomson et al., 2025; Tong et al., 2024). Given that MOOIs were designed to be implemented within the broader population, further research is needed to examine the extant literature collectively on the effectiveness of MOOIs for mental health (Muñoz et al., 2016).

Several program-and person-level characteristics may influence MOOI effectiveness.

Previous meta-analyses and systematic reviews found larger effects for MOOIs that were compared to inactive versus active controls in smartphone-based interventions, required individuals to meet a mental health cutoff for inclusion versus no requirement for university students, had human contact before the trial versus no contact for adults with depressive symptoms, and had adult participants versus children and adolescents when focusing on populations of any age with depressive symptoms (Firth et al., 2017; Harrer et al., 2019; Moshe et al., 2021; Tong et al., 2024). Other meta-analyses and systematic reviews found no significant effects of continental location or participant sex on MOOI effectiveness (Moshe et al., 2021; Pauley et al., 2023). Finally, mixed results were found for therapeutic orientation, treatment length, and modality, with some meta-analyses and systematic reviews reporting larger effects for CBT and ACT principles versus other approaches, short and medium length treatments versus longer treatments, and websites versus mobile applications, while others found no significant effects on MOOI effectiveness (Harrer et al., 2019; Heber et al., 2017; Karyotaki et al., 2017; Ma et al., 2021; Moshe et al., 2021; Noh et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2023). Given that previous research focused on these moderators within a pre-determined population (e.g., adolescents) or program type (e.g., CBT-based), further research is needed to examine MOOIs within a broader population to improve the effectiveness of future mental health focused MOOIs for the general population (Harrer et al., 2019; Karyotaki et al., 2017; Ma et al., 2021; Noh et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2023).

To improve our knowledge of MOOIs, specifically for anxiety and depression, a new meta-analysis and systematic review is needed to expand upon previous research and to address the following gaps: 1) Since the pandemic, there has been a major expansion and adoption of eHealth approaches to delivering mental health care (Bouza et al., 2023; Byrne et al., 2021; Ellis et al., 2021).

Given the significant increase in the literature, an updated metaanalysis is needed to synthesize the rapidly growing recent evidence with previous research on MOOI effectiveness for depression and anxiety (Güneyligil Kazaz & Doğan, 2026).

2) Previous reviews examining the effectiveness of MOOIs focused on a specific population or treatment format (Babbage et al., 2022; Karyotaki et al., 2017; Klimczak et al., 2023; Saad et al., 2021; Thomson et al., 2025; Tong et al., 2024).

Given that MOOIs are meant to be delivered for a broad population, an updated metaanalysis, that is inclusive of diverse populations and treatment formats for depression and anxiety, is needed to ensure findings are representative for those whom MOOIs are intended to be delivered.

3) There is a need to examine person-and program-level moderators to identify who is benefitting most and which program components are more (or less) effective when implementing mental health interventions (Crisp & Griffiths, 2014; National Academies of Sciences & Medicine, 2019). This is an extension from prior reviews that focused solely on specific groups and/or treatment components, and thus not allowing for between-group comparisons. Identifying which program components and for whom MOOIs are more (or less) effective will help inform the development of future MOOIs to improve anxiety and depressive outcomes.

The primary objective of the current meta-analysis and systematic review was to investigate the efficacy of MOOIs in reducing symptoms of anxiety and depression without stipulating inclusion criteria for a particular subsample, as MOOIs are intended to be delivered for the larger general population. Methods

This meta-analysis and systematic review followed the Preferred Reporting for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines (Page et al., 2021) and was registered on the Open Science Framework (https://osf.io/vk82c).

Study Selection

The databases PsycINFO, PsycARTICLES, and Academic Search Complete were searched on February 4, 2026, consistent with recommendations to use at least 3 databases to achieve a comprehensive search (Dagher & Khan, 2025). Articles were required to be published no earlier than January 1, 2000 to cover relevant and current eHealth technology. Search criteria identified articles with title, abstract, and/or keywords related to 'self-directed,' 'e-health,' 'mental health,' and 'randomized controlled trial.' No age limit was set for the sample. Keywords were developed in consultation with a librarian (see Appendix A for search terms used).

A team of three reviewers participated in weekly meetings with a fourth reviewer, who is a senior research trainee with expertise in conducting reviews and who provided training on article screening and data extraction. All articles retrieved via database searches were screened by the four reviewers, with each article being independently screened by two reviewers against inclusion/exclusion criteria. Studies were deemed eligible if they were: 1) original peer-reviewed empirical research, 2) written in English, 3) included a mental health intervention delivered in a fully online format (e.g., website, mobile application), 4) included a self-directed intervention (i.e., no direct clinician involvement), 5) used a randomized controlled trial (RCT) study design, 6) assessed anxiety and/or depression outcome, 7) presented quantitative data at post-treatment and/or follow-up. Grey literature (e.g., theses, dissertations, unpublished studies) were excluded to control for evidence quality. Articles were screened at the title and abstract level and subsequently at the full-text level. Disagreements were settled by a third reviewer at the title and abstract level and by consensus between all reviewers at the full-text level.

Data Extraction

Data from each article were extracted in duplicate by two independent reviewers from the review team, and discrepancies were resolved by consensus between all reviewers. The following data was directly extracted from eligible articles: 1) total sample size and sample size for each study arm, 2) description of the intervention and control groups, and 3) unadjusted mean and standard deviation [SD] (or variables needed to calculate these) for anxiety and depressive symptoms at baseline, post-treatment, and the last follow-up timepoint 2 . The following data was also extracted for moderator analyses: 1) control type (active, inactive) 3 ; 2) continental location (Africa, Asia, Europe, North America, Oceania, South America); 3) mental health cutoff required (yes/no); 4) therapeutic orientation (CBT [including third-wave] was at least one orientation vs. other orientations); 5) treatment format (web-based, mobile application), 6) mean age of the total study sample (continuous), 7) sex/gender (percentage female; continuousfoot_3 ), 8) time to last follow-up (in weeks; continuous), 9) treatment duration (in daysfoot_4 , continuous), 10) contact with research team prior to enrolment (yes/no), and 11) orientation session delivered prior to beginning the intervention (yes/no).

Additionally, we used inductive thematic analysis to identify treatment components (Braun & Clarke, 2006). Individual treatment components were collected from treatment descriptions within each study and further organized into broader categories by two authors.

Components were independently coded by two reviewers as present or absent within each treatment, with discrepancies resolved to consensus. If a study assessed multiple MOOIs, a treatment component was defined as "present" if at least one MOOI reported the component.

Risk of bias was assessed using the Cochrane Collaboration's Risk of Bias Tool (RoB 2) for RCTs (Sterne et al., 2019). The following criteria were assessed by the RoB 2 tool: 1) randomization procedure, 2) deviation from the planned treatment, 3) availability of outcome data, 4) outcome assessment, and 5) selective data reporting. Two authors independently completed the risk of bias assessment and disagreements were resolved by consensus by the review team.

Statistical Analyses

Prior to data analyses, means, SDs, and sample sizes were calculated using available data.

When more than one treatment or control arm were present in a study, we followed the Cochrane Handbook's guidelines and created a single pair-wise comparison by combining group data (Higgins et al., 2024). When a study analyzed the same outcome using more than one measure (e.g., Beck Depression Inventory and Patient Health Questionnaire to measure depression), we combined the data using the means of both outcomes to form an overall effect within Comprehensive Meta-Analysis (CMA) software (Borenstein et al., 2022).

Meta-analyses were then conducted using CMA Version 4 (Borenstein et al., 2022).

Random-effects models were used to calculate pooled effect sizes. Anxiety and depression outcomes were aggregated using mean differences. Effect sizes and 95% confidence intervals for each study were calculated using Hedges' g, which was interpreted as follows: g = .20 (small effect), g = .50 (medium effect), and g = .80 (large effect; Hedges, 1981). Effect sizes were reported with and without outliers, if applicable. Outliers, namely studies whose confidence intervals fell beyond the 95% confidence interval of the pooled effect, were calculated using CMA (Borenstein et al., 2022; Harrer et al., 2021). Heterogeneity was assessed using the I² index, Cochran's Q, and the prediction interval. The I² index assessed what percentage of variation across studies came from heterogeneity rather than chance and is interpreted as: 0-40% (heterogeneity may not be important), 30-60% (moderate heterogeneity), 50-90% (substantial heterogeneity), and 75 -100% (considerable heterogeneity; Borenstein, 2022; Higgins et al., 2024). Cochran's Q assessed the level of heterogeneity among studies, with higher scores suggesting greater result variation (West et al., 2010). Prediction intervals provide an estimate of the true effect, which allows for a clinical interpretation of heterogeneity (Borenstein et al., 2009; IntHout et al., 2016).

Categorical moderators were analyzed using subgroup analyses with mixed-effect models based on the following variables: control type, therapeutic orientation, mental health inclusion criteria, orientation session component, contact with research team before enrolment, treatment format, continental location, and treatment components (Fu et al., 2011). For continental location analyses, we compared studies from each continent against studies from all other continents. Subgroups with fewer than 4 studies each were not analyzed based on previous recommendations (Fu et al., 2011). Outliers were included in moderator analyses. Randomeffects meta-regressions were conducted for the following continuous variables: mean age of the study sample, percentage female, time to follow-up, and treatment duration (Higgins et al., 2024).

Publication bias was assessed using Egger's Test, visual inspection of funnel plots, and the Duval and Tweedie's trim-and-fill procedure resulting in an effect size estimate (Duval & Tweedie, 2000; Egger et al., 1997). Analyses were conducted with and without outliers to determine the impact of including outliers.

Discussion

We conducted a meta-analysis and systematic review examining the effectiveness of MOOIs in reducing anxiety and depressive symptoms compared to control groups across broad populations. This work helps to inform the development and improvement of future and existing MOOIs, with the potential to improve mental health outcomes for individuals who may otherwise face barriers to accessing services. Findings revealed small effects of MOOIs on improving anxiety and depression at post-treatment and follow-up. This review provides evidence supporting the efficacy of MOOIs, suggesting that they may help address limitations of in-person mental health services by offering an accessible and scalable intervention for the general population.

Results suggest MOOIs are effective in reducing anxiety and depressive symptoms, with small treatment effects observed at post-treatment compared to control groups. These findings are consistent with previous reviews demonstrating improvements in anxiety and depressive symptoms and extend the existing literature by suggesting that MOOIs are effective across diverse populations with varying mental health needs (Andrews et al., 2018; Edge et al., 2023; Klimczak et al., 2023; Ma et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2023). Similarly, small treatment effects observed at follow-up align with previous reviews demonstrating that the effects of internetdelivered programs on anxiety and depressive symptoms can be maintained over time (Buck et al., 2024; Păsărelu et al., 2017; Tong et al., 2024). However, none of the prediction intervals were found to be significant, which could suggest that in some settings MOOIs may not be as effective when compared to control groups (IntHout et al., 2016).

At post-treatment, larger effects on depression and anxiety symptoms were observed among studies that compared MOOIs to inactive controls, required participants to meet a mental health cutoff requirement, and were conducted within Asia. These larger effects observed relative to inactive controls are consistent with previous research on online interventions (Firth et al., 2017). This finding suggests that the relative benefit of MOOIs may be greater when compared with no or minimal intervention than when compared with active interventions, such as guided online therapy or face-to-face therapy (Firth et al., 2017). It also supports the potential utility of MOOIs as a source of support for individuals who are on waitlists or experiencing geographic factors that limit access to in-person mental health care and thus would not be receiving services otherwise (Hadjistavropoulos et al., 2022; Sun et al., 2023). Larger effects were also observed in studies requiring participants to meet a mental health cutoff requirement, suggesting those with greater mental health needs benefited more from MOOIs compared to those without clear mental health needs. However, this could also indicate a regression to the mean effect, as individuals with higher scores at baseline have more room for their scores to shift than those coming in with lower scores (Linden, 2013). Finally, studies conducted in Asia reported larger effect sizes than those conducted outside of Asia. Given that Asian regions have a lower median number of mental health workers compared to American and European regions, MOOIs and self-directed treatments may be particularly helpful in providing faster support to those living in regions with fewer clinician-delivered mental health resources (World Health Organization, 2025b). MOOIs also appear to be appealing for individuals with mental health stigma, a prominent concern in Asian regions, as they can allow users to remain anonymous which in turn may reduce shame associated with seeking mental health support (Lauber & Rössler, 2007; Nicholas et al., 2017).

However, further research is needed to determine if differences in mental health service availability or other cultural and contextual factors account for this geographic variation.

Larger effect sizes were found for anxiety outcomes in studies with a younger aged sample (at post-treatment) and had a shorter time to follow-up. Previous studies have found that younger individuals are more likely to adhere to MOOIs, which could reflect that younger populations being more comfortable navigating online resources than older populations (Batterham et al., 2008; Boucher et al., 2022). One way to support older individuals could be to involve clinicians in the delivery process to provide support with navigating the intervention (Rajappan et al., 2026). MOOIs also had larger effects when there was a shorter time to followup, which could reflect the fact that MOOIs have higher attrition rates over time compared to guided treatments, resulting in diminished effectiveness (Karyotaki et al., 2015; Linardon & Fuller-Tyszkiewicz, 2020). This finding highlights the importance of identifying strategies to maintain treatment gains following intervention completion. Future research could examine if approaches, such as booster sessions, could help sustain the effects of MOOIs long term (Jimenez-Vazquez et al., 2025).

MOOIs without stories and examples reported larger effects on anxiety (at post-treatment and follow-up) and depression (at follow-up) outcomes. This contrasts previous studies which report that narratives and testimonials are effective in improving health behaviours and acceptance of online mental health interventions (Apolinário-Hagen et al., 2021; Shen et al., 2015). However, it is possible that when MOOIs are implemented for a broad population, it may be more difficult to offer stories and testimonials that are relevant and users may prefer to have them tailored to their sociodemographics (e.g., age, gender, ethnicity, socioeconomic status, intersecting identities; Rooper et al., 2025).

MOOIs with gamification and rewards reported larger effects on anxiety outcomes at post-treatment. Components such as games and reward badges may incentivize users to remain engaged in the intervention, which in turn can improve its efficacy (Fleming et al., 2017; Lukka & Palva, 2023). This is also consistent with the literature on online interventions targeting substance use, where gamification and rewards have a larger impact on improving engagement and outcomes compared to interventions without gamification and rewards (Imran Ho et al., 2025; Oesterle & Bormann, 2026).

MOOIs that did not include feedback and suggestions (at post-treatment and follow-up) and social connection (at follow-up) reported larger effects on depression outcomes. Previous research has found that users prefer to receive suggestions and recommendations for activities (Pieritz et al., 2021). However, those users were under the impression that the app was personalized to their specific needs. Like our finding with stories and examples, this may suggest that when MOOIs are offered to general populations with varying needs, providing feedback and suggestions may be less effective when those features are not tailored to individual user's preferences. This could highlight an opportunity for MOOIs to incorporate artificial intelligence, with clinical oversight during its development and implementation, to offer more personalized treatment for individuals (American Psychological Association, 2026; Olawade et al., 2024).

Regarding social connection, our finding was unexpected, as opportunities to engage with other users has been found to be effective in promoting a sense of belonging and acceptance of one's condition (Marques & Queirós, 2021; Philippe et al., 2022). However, similar to stories and examples, implementing social connection features within a broad population may make it difficult for users of various characteristics and mental health concerns to find and connect with others with relatable lived experiences (Steiner & Farmer, 2024; Yuqing Ren et al., 2007).

Additionally, given that the MOOIs with social connection features in this review used an online group forum or discussion board (i.e., no in-person contact), it is possible that users may feel isolated if their posts remain unanswered, resulting in less engagement with the program (Berger et al., 2011; Lauder et al., 2015; Marshall et al., 2024; Morriss et al., 2021; Mouthaan et al., 2013; Ruehlman et al., 2012; Smith et al., 2011; Titov et al., 2010). It is also important to note that since only 6.73% of studies included a MOOI with a social connection feature, these findings should be interpreted with caution as they may not be representative of all MOOIs that focus on offering social connection.

Strengths and Limitations

Although this meta-analysis highlights important information regarding the effectiveness of MOOIs, some limitations must be noted. First, only articles published in English were included, which limits generalizability to non-English speaking countries. Second, some subgroup analyses had an unbalanced number of studies in each group, so results should be interpreted with caution, as the statistical power may have been reduced (Cujipers et al., 2021).

Third, most studies were identified as having some concerns of bias by the RoB 2 assessment, with only 11.33% of studies reporting low risk of bias, which is consistent with a prior systematic review and meta-analysis on online self-directed interventions (Sterne et al., 2019; Tong et al., 2024). We also did not exclude studies based on quality (i.e., higher than low risk of bias), which could overestimate treatment effects (Tong et al., 2024).

Key strengths of the current study include being the first meta-analysis to investigate

MOOIs of various formats across a broad range of populations for anxiety and depression outcomes. This broad scope provides insight into the efficacy of MOOIs across diverse populations and can inform the future development and improvements of MOOIs and large-scale eHealth services for the general populations (Muñoz et al., 2016). Additionally, with 203 included articles, we were able to provide a well-powered evaluation of the efficacy of MOOIs for anxiety and depressive symptoms. A further strength of this review is the systematic approach used by employing PRISMA guidelines (Page et al., 2021).
